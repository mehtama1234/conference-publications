We introduce the Generalization Spectrum, a framework that evaluates generalization as a function of transfer distance rather than a binary in/out split. exact recall of training instances (D0), implementation transfer across programming languages (D1) [8], context transfer under complete narrative reframing with preserved mathematical structure (D2), category-matched problems sharing algorithmic tags (D3), and an unpaired in-domain baseline (D4). If method A outperforms method B on transfer tasks but also shows higher training-set performance, is the transfer gain genuine or merely a side effect of better memorization? To isolate transfer efficiency—the ability to convert memorization into generalization—we propose matched-memorization comparison: selecting checkpoints where different methods achieve comparable D0 (exact recall) performance, then comparing their behavior at D1 and beyond. a single algorithmic problem can be expressed in different languages (D1) [8, 35], reframed with entirely different narratives while preserving mathematical structure (D2), or matched to problems requiring similar algorithmic techniques (D3). We then use the Spectrum to diagnose within-family variants—ICL content variants (abstraction, hints), SFT target-source variants including RFT [54], self-taught distillation (SDFT [20, 41, 59]), and a coding-adapted hint-assisted GRPO variant inspired by sparse-reward guidance methods [18, 22, 25, 29, 45, 57, 58]—asking whether they expand the generalization radius or merely shift local height, speed, or near-far concentration. The resulting profiles separate seed recall, local transfer, and far-transfer preservation, revealing that different post-training methods can change different parts of the generalization profile even when their aggregate gains may look similar.
