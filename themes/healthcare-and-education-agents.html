Problem

Clinical teaching is one-to-many: the agent must guide several trainees, keep track of individual misconceptions, preserve group progress, and avoid unsafe medical instruction.

Approach

ClinTutor-R1 builds a clinical teaching simulator, a Socratic dialogue dataset, and a multimodal agent that models individual belief states and group consensus.

Connecting read

This is an agent-evaluation problem because the quality lives in the interaction process, not just in a final answer.

FIRST PRINCIPLES

The core problem

A one-on-one tutor can optimize for one learner's confusion. A clinical instructor on ward rounds has to do something harder: listen to several trainees, infer who understands which part of the case, ask questions that reveal misconceptions, keep the group moving, and avoid turning uncertainty into unsafe medical guidance.

That makes healthcare education a useful stress test for agents. The agent is not merely answering medical facts. It is managing a social learning process in a high-stakes domain. Success depends on memory, role tracking, pedagogy, clinical caution, and interactive evaluation. Static benchmarks miss much of that because they remove the group dynamics that define the task.

SYNTHESIS

The paper's conceptual move

One-to-many alignment is not one-on-one tutoring repeated

clinical pedagogy

The central distinction is between dyadic instruction and group instruction. In dyadic instruction, the alignment target is one learner. In clinical group teaching, the target is a coordinated learning trajectory across multiple people, with individual scaffolding and collective progress both mattering.

ICML 2026ClinTutor-R1
ClinTutor-R1 builds ClinEdu, a multi-agent clinical pedagogical simulator; ClinTeach, a large Socratic teaching-dialogue dataset; and a multimodal tutoring agent designed for one-to-many clinical education. The agent uses an explicit internal thinking mechanism to model individual trainee belief states and group consensus. That matters because the failure mode is not only wrong answers. The agent can dilute context, overfocus on one trainee, mistake group consensus for real understanding, or fail to scaffold weaker learners while keeping the group engaged. The local note reports evaluation through both static benchmarks and in-situ interactive evaluation inside ClinEdu, with more than 20 percent improvement over base models and parity with proprietary reasoning models.
FIELD READ

What this theme says about ICML and ICLR

The broader pattern is that healthcare and education force agent evaluation to become process-aware. It is not enough to ask whether the model knows the medical fact or can produce a polished explanation. The system must behave well while interacting with learners, patients, tools, or biomedical data under domain constraints.

That connects to the conference gym work in a direct way. A good domain gym encodes the workflow: who the agent is interacting with, what state it observes, what actions it can take, and how success is verified. ClinTutor-R1 is the pedagogical version of that idea. MedAgentGym is the biomedical coding version, and CounselBench is the expert-evaluation version for mental-health QA. Together they point toward domain-specific agent environments where safety, learning, evidence, and interaction quality can be measured rather than assumed.
