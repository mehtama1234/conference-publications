Causality and causal discovery for production failure diagnosis

This page is about causality under operational pressure. In a microservice outage, correlation can tell us which services look abnormal together. Root-cause analysis asks a sharper question: which component most plausibly caused the failure, given partial structure, scarce failures, and expensive interventions?

FIRST PRINCIPLES

Why root cause is harder than anomaly detection

An anomaly detector can say that service A, service B, and service C all look strange during an incident. That is useful, but it is not enough for recovery. Operators need to know where to intervene first. In a coupled software system, downstream services often fail because an upstream service failed, and multiple symptoms can arrive at once. The causal question is therefore directional: which abnormal component best explains the observed failure pattern?

The difficulty is that production failures are rare, risky to reproduce, and only partially observed. A method that requires a complete causal graph or many post-failure interventions will not match the deployment setting. BRCD is interesting because it accepts this constraint as the starting point: use partial pre-failure causal structure, reason over possible directed graphs consistent with it, and infer root-cause candidates with posterior guarantees.

Partial structure

The system may know a CPDAG or dependency skeleton from normal operation, but not the exact directed causal graph under failure.

Scarce incidents

Failures are low-sample events. The method must remain useful without thousands of labeled outages or repeated interventions.

Actionable ranking

Operators need a root-cause ranking, not just a dense explanation. Top-k accuracy matters because investigation bandwidth is limited.

SUBTHEME

Nonparametric RCA with posterior guarantees

Bayesian Root Cause Discovery treats RCA as causal inference under graph uncertainty

operations causality

BRCD starts from a practical observation about cloud systems: engineers often have some pre-failure structural knowledge, but not a complete causal DAG. The paper uses a CPDAG learned during the pre-failure period, then performs Bayesian inference over root-cause candidates without enumerating every DAG inside each interventional Markov equivalence class. Uniform DAG sampling makes that posterior reasoning tractable enough to scale. The contribution is not only an algorithmic shortcut; it changes what evidence the RCA system is allowed to use. Instead of pretending the graph is known, it keeps uncertainty over compatible causal structures and turns that uncertainty into a posterior over likely causes.

The paper also reports identifiability and finite-sample posterior bounds for nonparametric RCA under an epsilon-vanishing approximation, then evaluates on synthetic benchmarks and three microservice systems: Online Boutique, Sockshop, and Petshop. The important deployment claim is low-failure-sample performance. In operations, failures are too costly to create casually, so sample efficiency is not a convenience feature. It is the condition under which causal RCA can be used at all.

CORE

Root cause is not the loudest symptom

A failed system usually produces many abnormal signals at once. Causal diagnosis asks which abnormal signal is upstream enough to explain the others.

Imagine a checkout service, payment service, inventory service, and recommendation service all showing errors during the same incident. A correlation-based monitor can rank all four as anomalous. That is useful for alerting, but it does not tell an engineer where to act first. If checkout depends on payment, and payment depends on a database, then the most visible symptom may be downstream of the real problem. The root-cause question is therefore a question about direction: which component changed in a way that would make the rest of this failure pattern likely?

BRCD treats that as a causal problem under uncertainty. It does not assume the full directed graph is known. It starts with partial pre-failure structure, then asks which directed causal worlds are still compatible with that structure. Across those possible worlds, it computes which candidate causes receive posterior support from the observed incident. The method is useful because it refuses two bad extremes: it does not collapse everything into undirected abnormality, and it does not pretend engineers already know the complete failure DAG.

ANATOMY

The BRCD object has four parts

A root-cause posterior is useful only when the uncertainty it summarizes is the right uncertainty.

BRCD is not merely "Bayesian" in the vague sense of assigning scores. It has a specific causal object. First, there is a pre-failure CPDAG: partial causal structure learned before the incident. Second, there is an interventional equivalence class: several directed graphs may still be compatible with the available structure. Third, there is an observed failure: the incident measurements that make some causes more plausible than others. Fourth, there is a posterior over root-cause candidates: the ranked belief about where intervention should start.

Those four parts explain why the method is useful in operations. Engineers rarely have enough failures to learn a full DAG after the fact, and they often cannot run many interventions during an outage. BRCD tries to spend the evidence that exists before the failure, preserve graph uncertainty instead of hiding it, and produce a ranked investigation target rather than a complete causal story.

METHOD

Why Bayesian reasoning belongs here

Bayesian RCA is useful because the system has uncertainty over causes and uncertainty over the graph at the same time.

A deterministic RCA method often wants one graph and one cause. That is attractive because it gives a clean answer, but production systems rarely provide enough evidence for that cleanliness. Several directed graphs may fit the pre-failure data. Several components may be plausible root causes. The honest output is therefore not only a name. It is a belief distribution over names, conditioned on the causal structures that remain possible.

The important simplification in BRCD is that it does not need to enumerate every DAG in every interventional Markov equivalence class. Exhaustive enumeration would be the mathematically direct approach but not the operationally useful one. Uniform DAG sampling lets the method approximate the causal uncertainty and still produce a ranking. That makes the paper a good example of first-principles engineering: preserve the causal object, then find the cheapest computation that still respects it.

TOPIC 01

Correlation finds symptoms; causality chooses interventions

During an outage, many services become abnormal together. The repair question is which one to touch first.

This is the difference between anomaly detection and root-cause discovery. An anomaly detector can tell operators that checkout latency, payment failures, inventory errors, and database load all changed. But if payment fails because the database failed, and checkout fails because payment failed, then treating all abnormalities as equal wastes investigation time. The root cause is the component whose change would explain the rest and whose repair would most plausibly stop the cascade.

BRCD matters because it makes that intervention meaning explicit. A root-cause ranking is not a list of loud metrics. It is a ranked set of candidate places where an intervention could change the incident trajectory. That is why the output has to be causal, not merely predictive.

TOPIC 02

A partial graph is evidence, not a finished story

Production structure is usually known enough to help, but not known enough to remove uncertainty.

A CPDAG is useful because it summarizes causal structure learned from pre-failure behavior while leaving some directions unresolved. That is an honest object. It says: these relationships are constrained by the data and assumptions, but multiple directed causal graphs may still be possible. In a live system, this is more realistic than pretending every edge direction is known.

The danger is collapsing the partial graph too early. If the method chooses one convenient DAG and hides alternatives, the diagnosis can look precise while relying on an arbitrary direction choice. BRCD's posterior reasoning keeps the unresolved directions alive long enough to ask which root causes remain plausible across compatible causal worlds.

TOPIC 03

The posterior is a disciplined form of doubt

A posterior over causes is useful because it shows how much the incident evidence actually supports each repair hypothesis.

Operators often want one answer, but causal evidence may not justify one answer. A posterior ranking gives a more honest interface: this component is most likely, this one is still plausible, this one is less supported. That ranking can guide investigation without pretending the system has solved the entire causal graph.

This also makes the evidence auditable. If the posterior changes a lot under different compatible DAG samples, then graph uncertainty is important. If two candidates remain close because the incident data is scarce, the method should say so. The posterior is valuable when it prevents weak evidence from becoming a confident story.

TOPIC 04

Top-k diagnosis matches human repair bandwidth

In operations, the first few investigation targets matter more than a complete ranking of every abnormal service.

A microservice outage is time-sensitive. Engineers need to inspect a small number of plausible causes quickly. That makes top-l accuracy a meaningful operational metric. If the true cause is near the top, the method can shorten the path to repair even if the full posterior is imperfect. If the true cause is buried under downstream symptoms, the method is not actionable.

This is why BRCD's causal object is practical. It does not need to produce an omniscient explanation of the whole system before helping. It needs to use partial structure and scarce failure evidence to rank where repair should start, while preserving enough uncertainty that engineers do not mistake a probability for a guarantee.

EVIDENCE

What would prove the diagnosis is not symptom chasing

The evidence must show that the ranking points toward causes under scarce failures, not just correlated alarms.

The local note gives the right evidence categories. Mathematically, BRCD claims identifiability and finite-sample posterior bounds under an epsilon-vanishing approximation. Empirically, it reports state-of-the-art top-l accuracy on synthetic benchmarks and three microservice systems: Online Boutique, Sockshop, and Petshop. Operationally, the important condition is low-failure-sample performance, because outages are rare and expensive to reproduce.

The failure checks are just as important. If the pre-failure CPDAG is wrong, if key service variables are missing, if the incident violates the assumed intervention model, or if the posterior approximation excludes important graph possibilities, the ranking can become falsely confident. A good RCA method should expose that uncertainty instead of turning weak structural evidence into a single neat story.

Top-l accuracy

Low-sample regime

Graph uncertainty

SYNTHESIS

How this connects to the larger conference picture

This one-paper page complements the broader causality page. Local Covariate Selection asks which local adjustment structure is enough for a target effect. Structure Learning CI asks which statistical primitive controls graph recovery. BRCD asks how much causal structure is enough to rank failure causes in a live software system. All three push against the same brittle assumption: that we must know the whole causal world before making a useful causal decision.

The deeper lesson is that causal ML is becoming decision support under imperfect structure. The system does not need omniscience before it can help. It needs to state the causal object, preserve the evidence contract, keep uncertainty visible, and produce a decision that can be checked. For BRCD, that decision is where to investigate first during an incident.
