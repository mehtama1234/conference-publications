Robustness, safety, privacy, and unlearning

A deployed model is safe only if its important constraints survive pressure. The pressure may be distribution shift, adversarial prompts, tool authority, private data, memorized content, unsafe fine-tuning, legal source limits, rare events, or a later request to forget.

Problem

Capability creates new failure surfaces: leakage, memorization, jailbreaks, hidden channels, unsafe tools, shortcut learning, brittle shifts, and deletion requests after training.

Approaches

Use red-team environments, privacy mechanisms, unlearning certificates, provenance controls, robust objectives, safety guidance, monitors, refusal preservation, and tail-risk estimates.

Connecting read

Delegation gives the model authority. Robustness and safety decide which constraints survive when that authority is stressed.

CORE

Safety is an invariant under pressure

A safety claim is weak until it names the pressure it survives.

Normal examples do not define the boundary. The boundary appears when a user asks indirectly, when the input shifts, when a tool can change state, when private training data can be inferred, when a model is fine-tuned by a customer, when a generated artifact needs provenance, when a clinical or legal decision has asymmetric cost, or when the system is asked to remove data after learning from it.

This is why safety, robustness, privacy, and unlearning belong together. They all ask whether a property remains true after the system leaves the clean training story. Robustness preserves behavior under changed inputs. Safety preserves forbidden boundaries under pressure. Privacy preserves facts that should not be revealed. Unlearning preserves the right to remove influence. Provenance preserves source accountability. These are deployment invariants.

The paper evidence fits this framing. SandboxEscapeBench, CyberGym, RedTeamCUA, SecFid, PLAGUE, Stable-GFN, and pressure-test work probe adversarial surfaces. Privacy, memorization, watermarking, Common Corpus, Copyright-Bench, and certified unlearning papers make data boundaries testable. CSPO, conformal policy control, rare-event analysis, and domain-safety work define what must hold when action cost rises.

First-principles line
Safety is not a vibe around model behavior; it is a set of properties that must remain true when the system is shifted, attacked, adapted, audited, scaled, or asked to forget.
ANATOMY

The paper claim anatomy

The concrete object is the boundary that should keep holding: a refusal boundary, tool permission boundary, private-data boundary, deletion boundary, source-use boundary, monitorable internal state, or domain harm boundary.

What must stay fixed is the boundary's meaning. A jailbreak paper must keep the forbidden capability forbidden, even when the request is transformed. A sandbox paper must keep the environment boundary fixed while the agent searches for escape. A privacy paper must keep the sensitive fact unrecoverable by the attacker it defines. An unlearning paper must keep the deletion target precise. A provenance paper must keep source eligibility inspectable. A domain-safety paper must keep the real harm model, not just a generic refusal label.

What may change is the pressure applied to the boundary: prompt form, modality, tool state, user role, fine-tuning data, retrieval source, generated artifact, sampling process, or deployment scale. The method works when the boundary still holds under those changes, or when the paper exposes exactly where it fails. The evidence is adversarial search, executable boundary tests, privacy attacks, certified deletion, provenance audits, monitor intervention, rare-event estimates, and domain-specific harm evaluation.

TOPIC 01

Robustness is preservation under shift

Robustness asks whether the system keeps the right behavior when the input, domain, interface, user, or environment changes.

A model can pass a benchmark while depending on a brittle shortcut. It can work in one GUI but loop in another. It can reason in a clean context but drift when irrelevant evidence appears. It can classify common examples while failing missing modalities, rare groups, or shifted distributions. Robustness is not one score; it is the survival of the task-relevant representation under changed conditions.

This connects back to representation repair. If the model preserved the wrong unit, shift exposes it. If it preserved the right causal or domain state, shift is less damaging. Robustness papers are therefore not separate from representation papers; they are the deployment test of representation quality.

Design principle
Name the shift that matters, then test whether the task-relevant unit still controls behavior under that shift.
TOPIC 02

Jailbreaks are boundary-search algorithms

An attack is not only a bad prompt. It is search over the places where the system's boundary is poorly represented.

PLAGUE makes jailbreaks procedural: prime the model, plan around refusals, and finish the unsafe request. Stable-GFN treats red teaming as diverse search. VJA and FlowGuard show that unsafe intent can cross modalities. Pressure-test papers show that model behavior changes when honesty, user satisfaction, compliance, self-presentation, and safety pull against each other.

The practical lesson is that refusal text is not the boundary. The boundary is the internal and external control system that keeps forbidden behavior unavailable under search. If the defense only blocks known strings, the attacker only needs a new representation of the same request.

Design principle
Evaluate safety against adaptive search over prompts, modalities, turns, and pressure, not only against fixed unsafe examples.
TOPIC 03

Agent safety is infrastructure safety

When the model can act through tools, the safety boundary includes the environment, credentials, files, browsers, sandboxes, and logs.

CyberGym and SandboxEscapeBench make this explicit. A security agent can generate executable artifacts, inspect targets, and pressure containment. RedTeamCUA shows that malicious instructions can live inside the interface the agent is trying to use. SecFid shows the hard tradeoff: ignore injected text and you may lose task fidelity; obey it and you may compromise security.

This follows directly from the delegation page. Tool authority needs containment. Containment needs tests. Tests need realistic execution surfaces. A language-only answer cannot prove that the operational boundary holds.

Design principle
Test agent safety at the same authority level the agent will receive in deployment.
TOPIC 04

Privacy is about what the model makes recoverable

Private data can leak through outputs, gradients, memories, client updates, examples that are too close to training data, or model families that reveal too much.

Privacy is not only whether the training set was public. It is whether sensitive facts can be inferred from the trained system or its adaptation process. Federated and personalized methods make this harder because the system tries to preserve local client structure while sharing useful global structure. Generative models add another failure: samples can look like generalization while being too close to the training set.

This connects to cost and scarcity because privacy is often bought with noise, restricted access, smaller updates, or less shared state. It also connects to state and memory: what persists can later leak.

Design principle
Treat privacy as a recoverability question: what can an observer infer from the model, memory, update, trace, or output?
TOPIC 05

Unlearning is deletion as a behavioral claim

To forget data is not merely to remove a file. It is to remove a claimed influence from the trained system.

Unlearning becomes hard because models compress data into weights, adapters, memories, retrieval stores, and downstream artifacts. A deletion request may apply to examples, users, concepts, copyrighted sources, private records, or derived behavior. The system needs a claim precise enough to test: prediction degradation, distributional closeness, certified removal, provenance removal, or memory deletion.

Gaussian certified unlearning is an anchor because it turns deletion into a certification problem. The deeper point is broader: every data lifecycle promise needs a measurable object. Otherwise "we forgot it" is only an operational story, not evidence.

Design principle
Define unlearning by the residual influence that is no longer allowed, then choose the evidence that can certify that claim.
TOPIC 06

Provenance and legality are model constraints

Source eligibility is not metadata decoration. It controls what the system may train on, retrieve, quote, generate, and use commercially.

Copyright-Bench is useful because it puts source-use decisions inside agent workflows. Common Corpus makes legal usability a pretraining constraint. Watermarking and provenance work ask whether content origin remains detectable after generation, editing, compression, or distribution. These are not merely governance topics; they change the feasible method.

A system that cannot track source eligibility cannot reliably obey source-use policy. This links data governance to tool use: retrieval, browsing, summarization, and generation all need provenance boundaries when source rights matter.

Design principle
Preserve provenance whenever source eligibility can change what the model is allowed to do.
TOPIC 07

Monitoring must look before the final answer

Output filters see the end of the process. Many safety failures begin earlier.

Activation Oracles and robust harmful-feature work ask whether unsafe tendencies are visible inside activations. Trojan-Speak and invisible-channel work show that surface text can hide unsafe processing. Weak-to-Strong Monitoring and monitorability papers treat oversight as a workflow problem: the agent may act, reason, hide, or evade before the final output exists.

Monitoring is delegated authority too. A monitor only matters if it can stop, route, ask for evidence, restrict tools, or force review. Otherwise it is a dashboard that discovers harm after the system has already acted.

Design principle
Monitor the earliest observable signal that can still change the route before harm becomes irreversible.
TOPIC 08

Safe adaptation preserves constraints while changing behavior

Fine-tuning and personalization are useful only if they do not erase the boundaries that made the base system deployable.

The adaptation page framed change as controlled plasticity. Here the same question becomes safety-critical. Buffer-and-Reinforce, Surgery, and adversarial fine-tuning papers ask whether customization can weaken refusal, implant hidden behavior, or preserve dangerous capability. Federated and LoRA papers ask whether local adaptation can preserve privacy and avoid client interference.

Safe adaptation needs a before-and-after contract: what may improve, what may specialize, what must not move, and what verifier proves the boundary survived.

Design principle
Every adaptation path should specify which safety, privacy, and provenance constraints are invariant under the update.
TOPIC 09

Rare failures need tail evidence

A failure absent from ordinary testing may still be common enough to matter at deployment scale.

Rare Event Analysis and tail-risk papers make the sampling problem explicit. If a harmful output occurs once in a million queries, a small benchmark will miss it while a large service may see it constantly. Safety evaluation therefore needs event definitions, stress samplers, importance sampling, confidence intervals, and clear escalation thresholds.

This connects to uncertainty and decision risk. The right question is not "did we see the bad event?" It is "what can we infer about the probability of the bad event after the sampling process we used?"

Design principle
For costly rare harms, evaluate the tail directly instead of treating absence in ordinary samples as evidence of safety.
TOPIC 10

Domain harm defines the safety boundary

Safety is domain-specific because harm is domain-specific.

A clinical assistant can be unsafe by sounding helpful while giving unauthorized medical advice. A legal assistant can be unsafe by overstating certainty or source authority. A tutoring agent can be unsafe by optimizing answer speed over learning or welfare. A robot can be unsafe by succeeding on average while crossing physical constraints. A recommender can be unsafe by creating bias through adaptive exploration.

CounselBench, TRECA, CSPO, PACT, fairness verification, and causal decision papers all show that safety depends on the downstream decision. A generic refusal policy cannot capture every domain harm. The system needs domain evidence, domain escalation, and domain-specific invariants.

Closing line
A safe system is not one that avoids every action; it is one whose constraints survive the specific pressures created by its domain and authority.
