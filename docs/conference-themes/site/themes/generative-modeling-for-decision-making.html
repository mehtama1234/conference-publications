Problem

Decision spaces can be too large or structured for direct policy search. But unconstrained generative proposals can leave the feasible region, overfit offline logs, or require too much environment interaction.

Approaches

The papers use flow-matched latent policies, diffusion policy adaptation, generative planners, offline evaluators, and constrained policy search.

Connecting read

The shared idea is proposal plus control: use generation to explore candidate actions, then use constraints and evaluators to decide which proposals are safe enough to trust.

READ

The Big Picture

Generative modeling for decision-making treats actions as objects that can be sampled. That is attractive when the action space is combinatorial, continuous, high-dimensional, or only partially covered by offline data. The generator can propose diverse candidates; the decision system then needs a way to score, constrain, and adapt those candidates before acting.

The three papers here show that generation alone is not the decision. Latent Spherical Flow Policy learns expressive stochastic policies in a compact latent space for combinatorial action problems. ExDM adapts diffusion policies after reward-free pretraining with limited interaction. AIGB-Pearl combines generative bidding planners with offline trajectory evaluation and constrained policy search. In each case, the useful system is the loop around the generator: sample, evaluate, constrain, and adapt.

CORE

A generated action is only a proposal

A generative model can make many possible actions, but decision-making starts only after the system asks which of those actions is feasible, supported, and worth executing.

This is the central distinction. Generating an image or sentence can be judged after the artifact appears. Generating an action is different because the action changes the environment, spends budget, risks violating constraints, or leaves the support of past data. A sample that looks high-scoring may be infeasible. A sample that is feasible may be unsupported by the offline logs. A sample that explores may cost too much interaction.

The three papers solve different parts of this problem. Latent Spherical Flow Policy uses a solver so generated latent samples become valid combinatorial actions. ExDM adapts diffusion policies with limited environment interaction after reward-free pretraining. AIGB-Pearl wraps generative bidding proposals in offline trajectory evaluation and constrained search. All three say the same thing: generation expands the menu, but control decides what can be served.

TOPIC 01

Generation expands the menu, not the permission set

A generator can propose more actions than a hand-designed policy would consider, but it does not automatically make those actions allowed.

This is the core idea behind the page. A generated action is a candidate. It still has to pass the decision problem's boundary: valid combinatorial structure, reward improvement, logged-data support, budget limits, risk limits, and interaction limits. Latent Spherical Flow Policy uses generation to explore a compact action space, but a solver has to turn latent samples into valid actions. AIGB-Pearl can generate bidding plans, but offline evaluation and constrained search decide which bids are supportable.

The deeper principle is that generation is useful because it widens search. It becomes dangerous when widened search is mistaken for evidence. The permission to act has to come from a verifier, solver, evaluator, rollout, or constraint, not from sample plausibility.

TOPIC 02

Feasibility is a hard boundary

A generated action that cannot be executed is not a bad decision. It is not a decision at all.

Combinatorial action spaces make this obvious. The allowed action is often a structured object: a route, assignment, subset, ranking, allocation, or bid configuration. A neural sampler can easily propose something outside the legal set. Latent Spherical Flow Policy handles this by separating expressive stochastic proposal from the solver that enforces validity. The solver is not an implementation detail; it defines the boundary between sample and action.

This lesson carries to robotics, bidding, and planning. High-level generation is only useful if the downstream system can cash it out as feasible motion, feasible spending, feasible allocation, or feasible interaction.

TOPIC 03

Interaction is a scarce resource

A policy can look sample-efficient in a paper only if the adaptation loop respects the real cost of trying actions.

ExDM is about this constraint. Reward-free pretraining gives the diffusion policy a broad behavior prior before the final reward is known. That is useful because real environment interaction can be expensive, risky, or slow. But the promise only holds if a small amount of later feedback can steer the pretrained behavior toward the task. If adaptation still needs many trials, the pretraining did not solve the decision bottleneck.

The first-principles object is not just the policy after training. It is the path by which the policy is adapted: how much interaction it consumes, what feedback it uses, and whether the learned behavior remains inside safe or useful regions while improving.

TOPIC 04

Offline data defines what can be trusted

In offline decision-making, the model cannot freely test every generated action in the real world.

AIGB-Pearl sits exactly on this boundary. Auto-bidding systems have logs, but logs are not a simulator of all possible bids. A generated bidding trajectory may look profitable because the offline evaluator is weak or because the policy moved into a region the logs cannot support. That is why constrained policy search matters. It limits how far generation can move away from evidence.

The deeper rule is support accountability. If the data cannot tell whether an action is good, the system should not present that action as proven. Offline improvement is credible only when the candidate remains connected to trajectories, propensities, constraints, or evaluators that can actually support the claim.

TOPIC 05

The proof is closed-loop value

For decision-making, sample quality is intermediate evidence. The final evidence is whether acting from the sample improves the loop.

A flow policy may have elegant samples, a diffusion policy may imitate diverse behavior, and a bidding planner may produce plausible trajectories. None of that is enough. The action has to improve reward, respect constraints, adapt with limited interaction, or produce a better outcome under the decision environment. This is why these papers are closer to control and reinforcement learning than ordinary generation.

The reusable concept is proposal plus proof. Generate candidates, pass them through the right boundary, and then measure the decision consequence. Without the consequence, the system has only produced action-shaped artifacts.

METHOD

Three different safeguards around generation

Each paper puts a different control surface around a generative policy.

Latent Spherical Flow Policy handles feasibility. It does not ask the neural generator to directly output arbitrary combinatorial actions. It samples in a compact latent space and relies on a solver to map those samples into valid structured decisions. The solver is the guardrail that keeps stochastic generation inside the allowed action set.

ExDM handles interaction scarcity. Reward-free pretraining can learn a broad behavior space before the final reward is available, but the later adaptation phase must be careful because real interactions are limited. The diffusion policy is useful when it can reuse pretraining structure and improve with a small amount of task feedback.

AIGB-Pearl handles offline-deployment risk. A bidding planner can generate attractive trajectories, but static ad-system logs do not justify arbitrary extrapolation. Offline evaluation and constrained policy search ask whether a generated bidding decision is plausible and safe enough under the evidence available.

INSIGHT

Decision generation needs a trust boundary

A generator is powerful because it can imagine actions the current policy would not try. That is also why it needs a boundary.

In decision problems, novelty is useful only when it remains tied to what the environment, solver, data, or evaluator can justify. Too little generation leaves the policy stuck in old behavior. Too much unconstrained generation creates unsupported actions. The deep principle is not "generate more." It is "generate candidates, then prove why this candidate is allowed to become an action."

EVIDENCE

What would prove generation became decision-making

The evidence has to show that the generated candidate survives the decision constraint, not only that the generator produces diverse samples.

Feasible actions

For Latent Spherical Flow Policy, generated latent samples must map through the solver into valid combinatorial actions, and those actions must improve reward rather than merely look diverse.

Useful adaptation

For ExDM, reward-free pretraining must reduce the amount of later environment interaction needed, while still letting the diffusion policy move toward the task reward.

Supported offline gains

For AIGB-Pearl, generated bidding plans must improve under offline trajectory evaluation and constrained policy search without exploiting evaluator artifacts or leaving logged-data support.

Constraint survival

The strongest result keeps the same operational constraint after optimization: combinatorial validity, limited interaction, budget limits, risk limits, or deployment rules are not relaxed after the generator appears.

Closed-loop value

A proposal is useful only if executing or adapting from it leads to better decisions in the loop the paper actually studies, not only better likelihood or prettier samples.

Ablation of the boundary

The control layer should matter. Removing the solver, interaction-efficient adaptation, offline evaluator, or constraint should make the system worse or less safe.

CLUSTERS

The Main Research Move

Generation proposes; decision logic filters

The papers share a practical architecture. A generative model creates candidate actions or policies, but another layer must keep those candidates useful. Flow matching supplies expressive stochastic policies for structured actions, diffusion policies provide adaptable behavior after reward-free pretraining, and offline evaluators plus constraints control risk when the domain is a deployed bidding system. This is the same broad pattern as agent planning: generation expands the search space, while verification and constraints keep it from becoming arbitrary.
