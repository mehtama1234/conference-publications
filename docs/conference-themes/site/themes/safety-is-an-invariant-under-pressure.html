CORE

The safety claim is what remains true after attack

A safe system should preserve the protected behavior when the surrounding conditions become hostile or tempting.

Pressure changes behavior. Fine-tuning can preserve capability while weakening refusal. Hidden channels can carry harmful content through benign-looking text. A multi-turn attack can build context before the final request. A monitor can miss intent if it sees only actions. Internal regularization can help when the unsafe route is visible before the final output.

Pressure Reveals Character tests models across pressure scenarios. Trojan-Speak studies harmful fine-tuning that evades surface classifiers. Invisible Safety Threat shows unsafe decoded content can hide inside safe-looking interactions. PLAGUE turns jailbreaks into multi-turn planning. Surgery regularizes attention-sink drift during fine-tuning. Monitoring Monitorability asks when chain-of-thought makes misbehavior more visible than action logs alone.

Core rule
A safety claim is strong only when it survives the pressures that deployment will create.
FIRST PRINCIPLE

Safety is the behavior that must not change

A safety property matters only when it remains true under the pressure the system will actually face.

A calm prompt does not test safety. It tests ordinary behavior. Safety begins when something tries to bend the system: a user insists, an attacker hides the goal, fine-tuning shifts the model, a tool gives power to an answer, a multi-turn dialogue builds a trap, or a monitor sees only part of the process. The safety claim is the invariant: the rule that should stay true while everything around it changes.

This is why the protected behavior must be named before the defense. Refusal is not always safety. More caution is not always safety. A monitor score is not safety. The real claim might be that the model will not reveal a secret, will not execute a harmful action, will not convert hidden content into unsafe instructions, will not lose honesty under social pressure, or will keep oversight possible while acting. Each claim needs a different pressure test.

First-principles test
Name what must remain true, then apply the strongest realistic pressure that tries to make it false.

METHOD

Why safety cannot be measured only at the output

The final answer may hide the process that created risk.

Some safety failures are visible in final text. Many are not. A model can plan unsafe steps and then phrase the final answer cautiously. It can use a tool in a risky way while its explanation sounds benign. It can carry a hidden trigger through internal state. It can respond safely in one turn and become unsafe after earlier turns have shaped context. This is why the evidence surface matters.

A good safety method compares surfaces: final answer, action log, intermediate trace, internal signal, fine-tuning change, and monitor judgment. The point is not to worship traces or probes. The point is to find the surface where the protected behavior can actually be checked. If the chosen surface cannot see the failure, the defense may only hide the problem.

FAILURE MAP

How safety claims become weak

Vague invariant

The page says safe without naming the behavior that must remain true.

Calm tests

Evaluation checks friendly prompts while deployment creates pressure.

Surface mismatch

The monitor watches final text while the risky work happens in tools, traces, or hidden state.

Capability collapse

The defense increases refusals broadly instead of preserving useful safe behavior.

Single-turn blindness

The attack is spread across context, but the defense treats each prompt alone.

Access mismatch

The defense assumes prompt attacks while the threat arrives through fine-tuning or data.

CONNECTION

Why this idea cuts across the atlas

Safety is the invariant form of evaluation, boundaries, and agent control.

Evaluation asks whether the test reproduces the real task. Safety asks whether the test reproduces real pressure. Boundaries name what the system is allowed to touch. Agent trajectories show why safety has to be checked across the path, not only at the final text. Privacy and unlearning give one concrete protected object: recoverable information.

This is also why safety pages should avoid broad language like robust, aligned, or trustworthy unless the protected behavior is explicit. Those words can hide the hard part. The hard part is deciding what must remain true, which pressure is allowed to act, and what loss of useful capability would make the defense unacceptable.

The deeper rule is simple: a safety claim has content only when we know the protected behavior, the pressure that attacks it, and the evidence surface that can reveal whether it held.
