CORE

The question is what can still be inferred

A privacy or deletion claim is not a feeling about secrecy. It is a statement about what an observer can reconstruct from outputs, weights, gradients, memories, signatures, or behavior.

Differential privacy limits how much one person can change what is released. Unlearning asks whether a removed example still affects the model. Provenance asks whether a model or output can be traced back to its source. These are one family because all of them test residual information.

SlaClip adapts DP-SGD clipping without extra private gradient queries. IHM places private regression noise through Hessian geometry. Secret Memory Lower Bounds proves privacy can require more streaming memory. LLM DNA reconstructs model lineage from behavior. Ellipse Signatures uses logprob geometry as an origin signal. Gaussian Certified Unlearning belongs here because deletion must be checked by what information remains after removal.

Core rule
Ask who observes the system, what they can recover, and which guarantee limits that recovery.
FIRST PRINCIPLE

The protected thing is information that might still be recoverable

Privacy, unlearning, and provenance all ask what can be inferred from a trace.

A system can remove a row, hide a name, compress a memory, release only outputs, or claim a model was cleaned. None of that settles the question. The question is what another party can still recover. Can they infer that a person was in the data? Can they reconstruct a sensitive value? Can they tell which model produced an output? Can they detect whether a deleted example still shaped behavior?

This is why the observer must be named. A user, attacker, auditor, model owner, downstream deployer, and data subject do not see the same surface. One may see outputs, another may see log probabilities, another may see gradients, another may compare model behavior before and after deletion. A privacy claim without an observer is incomplete because recovery always happens from somewhere.

First-principles test
Name the observer, name the trace they see, and measure what protected source can still be recovered from that trace.

METHOD

Why deleting the visible object is not enough

The model may still carry the source after the source is no longer visible.

Training turns examples into many traces: weight changes, decision boundaries, memorized strings, gradients, behavior patterns, log probability shapes, and downstream outputs. Removing the original file does not remove those traces. Adding noise does not automatically protect the right source. Marking a model output does not prove provenance under adaptation. Every claim has to follow the source into the remaining observable surface.

The method lesson is to compare worlds. What changes when one user is present or absent? What changes before and after unlearning? What origin can be recovered from outputs after paraphrase, distillation, or fine-tuning? What memory cost is forced by a privacy guarantee? The answer is never just “less information.” It is a measured bound on what a named observer can still infer.

That bound is the substance of the claim. Without it, privacy and deletion are promises about intent rather than limits on recovery.

FAILURE MAP

How protection claims become false

Row deletion

The source is removed from storage but remains in model behavior.

Wrong observer

The guarantee is tested against a weak attacker while real users see stronger traces.

Surface mismatch

Outputs look safe while logprobs, weights, or gradients still reveal the source.

Noise blindness

Noise is added without understanding which directions carry utility and risk.

Lineage leakage

Adapted models still reveal ancestry through behavior or probability geometry.

Cost hiding

The privacy claim ignores the memory or accuracy cost needed to make recovery hard.

Final rule
Privacy, deletion, and provenance are all claims about what information survives and who can recover it.
CONNECTION

Why this idea cuts across the atlas

Recoverable information is the privacy version of grounding and safety.

Safety names the behavior that must remain true under pressure. Privacy names the information that must remain unrecoverable under observation. Data pressure explains how private examples become model changes. Compression asks what survives a smaller representation; privacy asks whether too much survived. Theory matters here because the guarantee is often a bound, not a benchmark score.

The same lens also keeps provenance honest. A signature, lineage test, or watermark matters only if it survives the transformations an observer will actually face: sampling, paraphrase, distillation, fine-tuning, compression, or partial access. Provenance is not a label attached to an output. It is a recovery claim under attack.

The simple question for every paper in this family is: after training, release, deletion, adaptation, or compression, what protected source can still be recovered?
