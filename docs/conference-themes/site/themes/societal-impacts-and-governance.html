Theme 64 - ICLR 2026: 1

Societal impacts and governance as information power

This theme asks how a system can shape collective decisions without lying. Governance is not only about blocking falsehood; it is also about who controls the order, timing, and framing of true information.

Problem

Information mediation can steer people and agents even when the mediator is transparent, does not fabricate evidence, and does not hide available facts.

Approach

Model the mediator as a planner that shapes sequential Bayesian belief updates and decisions under transparency constraints.

Connecting read

The governance issue is subtle: a system can be honest in content while still exercising power through selection, sequencing, and presentation.

FIRST PRINCIPLES

The core problem

Governance usually focuses on deception, bias, censorship, or privacy. This page points at a different mechanism: steering through truthful mediation. If a platform, recommender, assistant, or planner decides what information appears first, which signals are highlighted, and when agents observe evidence, it can change group outcomes without ever making a false statement.

That matters for AI systems because many deployed models are not just answer machines. They are information intermediaries. They summarize, rank, recommend, schedule, notify, and explain. Even when those actions are individually truthful, the system can change welfare by shaping the path through which users update beliefs and make decisions.

CORE

Truthful information can still steer

A system can satisfy a narrow truthfulness test and still control the decision process by choosing the order and structure of evidence.

Imagine several people trying to decide what action to take. Each person receives true signals, updates beliefs, and observes some earlier choices. A mediator does not need to lie to influence the final outcome. It can choose which true signal arrives first, which comparison is made salient, which uncertainty is delayed, and which user's decision becomes visible before others act. The content remains truthful, but the social learning path changes.

This is why governance needs a broader object than "did the assistant say something false?" For recommender systems, assistants, feeds, search results, social platforms, and advisory agents, the system often controls the evidence path. That path can shift beliefs, herd behavior, and welfare even under transparency constraints.

SYNTHESIS

Governance is source-to-output accountability

The shared first-principles problem is not only whether a model tells the truth. It is whether the system can account for the origin, permission, transformation, ownership, and downstream use of the information it handles.

An AI system touches information at many points. A document enters a corpus. A training run compresses it into weights. A fine-tune or distillation step changes lineage. A generator emits text, image, audio, video, or code. An agent chooses which source to use under workflow pressure. A mediator orders truthful evidence for a group of decision-makers. Governance asks whether the accountability record survives those transformations.

This connects the nearby pages. Data governance asks what evidence may enter training and what rights it carries. Open science infrastructure asks whether the pretraining corpus is inspectable and lawful. Robustness, safety, privacy, and unlearning asks whether provenance, deletion, and source-use boundaries survive pressure. This page adds the social layer: even if the facts are true and the sources are legal, the system can still steer outcomes through ordering and mediation.

TOPIC 01

Truthfulness is not the same as neutrality

A system can show only true information and still shape what people believe and do.

Steering the Herd is the clean anchor because it removes the easy cases. The mediator is not lying. It is not hiding evidence in the simple sense. The agents update rationally. Yet the mediator can still affect welfare by choosing the order and structure of truthful signals. That makes governance sharper than ordinary factuality checking.

The concrete object is the belief-update path, not one isolated answer. What must stay fixed is truthfulness and the transparency condition in the model. What may change is sequencing, salience, and information structure. The method works because sequential decisions are path-dependent: early evidence shapes early actions, and later agents learn from those actions as well as from their own signals.

TOPIC 02

Source rights are part of the technical object

A dataset cannot support a governance claim if its origin, permission, and filtering path are not inspectable.

Common Corpus belongs in this governance page because it treats lawful reuse and provenance as infrastructure. A pretraining corpus is not just a token distribution. It is a record of sources, rights, language coverage, code coverage, filtering choices, and reproducibility constraints. If those records are weak, the model may perform well while the governance claim remains unverifiable.

The fixed object is the source record. What may change is the mixture, filter, deduplication rule, and balance across domains. Evidence would show that another party can inspect what entered the corpus and why it was allowed. The failure mode is source laundering: data looks open or clean because the governance evidence disappeared before training.

TOPIC 03

Provenance must survive transformation

Ownership and origin signals are useful only if they remain checkable after the artifact changes.

Watermarking, signatures, and lineage papers all face the same first-principles problem. Generated content is edited, compressed, cropped, paraphrased, filtered, and reposted. Models are fine-tuned, distilled, quantized, merged, and served behind APIs. If the provenance signal works only on the untouched original, it is not strong enough for deployed governance. Catch-22, Spherical Watermark, Semantic Watermark Fingerprints, Ellipse Signatures, and LLM DNA each protect a different part of this chain.

The fixed object is the claim being authenticated: origin, ownership, model lineage, or generated status. What may change is the encoding, signature route, fingerprint domain, or verification method. The method works when the signal remains detectable under realistic transformations without ruining the artifact or creating easy forgery.

TOPIC 04

Permission can be input-specific

A model may be allowed to generate from one input and not another, even when the architecture is the same.

GoodDiffusion makes this concrete for generation. The right governance boundary is not always "this model may generate" or "this model may not generate." Sometimes the boundary is attached to a particular input, signature, source, user, license, or authorization path. Copyright-Bench gives the workflow version: an agent must choose sources lawfully while still trying to satisfy a user's creative request.

The fixed object is the permission boundary. What may change is the prompt, source set, signature, generation route, or final artifact. Evidence would show that the system preserves authorization under realistic user pressure. The failure mode is policy knowledge without policy behavior: the model can recite the rule but still uses disallowed sources when the task rewards convenience or quality.

TOPIC 05

Governance needs records, not vibes

A system is not accountable because it sounds compliant. It is accountable when the relevant object can be checked.

Across these papers, governance becomes operational only when there is an artifact to inspect: corpus provenance, source-use trace, watermark detector, output signature, lineage fingerprint, authorization signature, mediation policy, or welfare analysis. Without that artifact, governance collapses into claims about intent. The model may be truthful, helpful, or policy-aware, but an outsider cannot verify the chain.

The fixed object is the audit trail attached to the information lifecycle. What may change is the representation of that trail. The evidence would prove that the record survives the transformation being governed. The failure mode is policy without evidence: the system claims compliance while no one can reconstruct what sources, transformations, permissions, or ordering choices produced the outcome.

EVIDENCE

What would prove governance survived the transformation

The evidence has to show that the accountability object remains checkable after data collection, training, adaptation, generation, workflow pressure, or social mediation.

Corpus auditability

For Common Corpus and related data work, the proof is source documentation, licensing clarity, filtering records, coverage reporting, and reproducible corpus construction.

Lineage after change

For LLM DNA and semantic fingerprints, ownership or ancestry signals must survive fine-tuning, distillation, quantization, filtering, and realistic model adaptation.

Output authentication

For Ellipse Signatures and watermarking papers, a verifier should identify origin after ordinary edits without relying on hidden platform trust or fragile ideal assumptions.

Permission boundary

For GoodDiffusion and copyright workflows, authorization has to remain tied to the input, source, policy, and generated artifact under realistic user pressure.

Workflow trace

For Copyright-Bench-style tasks, the evidence should include which sources the agent selected, why they were allowed, and how the final output used them.

Mediation sensitivity

For Steering the Herd, the proof is a formal or empirical account of how truthful ordering changes belief updates, decisions, welfare, and who benefits.

FAILURE MODES

Governance fails when the accountable object changes silently

The dangerous failure is not always obvious illegality or obvious deception. Often it is a broken chain.

Source laundering

A corpus looks open or useful, but the source rights, filtering, contamination, or provenance records are too weak to support the model claim.

Transformation loss

A watermark or ownership signal works on the original artifact but disappears after fine-tuning, quantization, editing, compression, or filtering.

False authentication

A signature verifies under ideal assumptions but can be forged, removed, or confused with ordinary output variation.

Workflow pressure

An agent knows the policy in the abstract but chooses disallowed sources when the task rewards speed, quality, or user satisfaction.

Truthful steering

The mediator does not lie, but it changes beliefs and welfare through sequencing, salience, or social-learning path dependence.

Policy without evidence

The system claims compliance but lacks the records, traces, signatures, or tests that would let an outsider verify the claim.

METHOD

Why Bayesian social learning is the right lens

The paper needs a model where people update from evidence and from one another, because that is where mediation power appears.

If each person made an isolated decision from the full evidence set, information order would matter less. Social learning changes that. Earlier decisions become signals for later agents. A user may not only ask "what evidence did I see?" but also "what did others choose after seeing their evidence?" Once decisions are sequential, the mediator can shape which beliefs become public at which moment.

Bayesian updating is useful here because it removes a common escape hatch. The result is not only about irrational users or manipulative lies. Even rational agents can be steered when the structure of truthful observation is chosen strategically. That makes the governance claim stronger: neutrality cannot be inferred from truthfulness alone.

INSIGHT

Transparency is not neutrality

A transparent mediator can still choose an objective, and that objective can decide who benefits from the information path.

This is the practical lesson for AI systems. Search ranking, feed ordering, retrieval summaries, educational hints, medical triage explanations, investment dashboards, and public-interest recommender systems all mediate evidence. They may disclose sources and avoid falsehood while still optimizing for engagement, welfare, profit, speed, safety, or a regulator's target. Those objectives are not interchangeable.

The governance question becomes: whose welfare is the mediator optimizing, what path of belief updates does it create, and how sensitive are decisions to that path? That is a deeper audit than checking individual statements for correctness.

The central idea is that transparency is not the same as neutrality. If all information is observable but arrives through a mediator, the mediator can still affect decisions by controlling the structure of observation. That gives governance a sharper object: not only whether information is true, but how a system shapes the sequence of belief updates.

ICLR 2026Steering the Herd
Steering the Herd shows that information mediation can shift welfare even with no lying, no cherry-picking, and full observability. The paper models a planner shaping sequential agents' Bayesian belief updates and decisions. Its importance is that it isolates a clean governance problem: an AI system can influence collective behavior through truthful structure. That makes audit questions more precise: what objective is the mediator optimizing, who benefits from the ordering, and how sensitive are downstream decisions to the presentation path?

FIELD READ

Why this belongs beside safety

The safety pages ask whether systems stay bounded under attack, privacy pressure, and distribution shift. This governance page asks a complementary question: even when a system stays within the rules, what social objective is it optimizing through information flow?

That is why governance cannot be bolted on after deployment. The mediation policy is part of the system design. It determines who sees what, when they see it, and how much the system can steer collective outcomes while remaining formally transparent.

In plain terms, the paper says: do not only ask whether the model lied. Ask what sequence of true things it chose to show, what decisions that sequence made more likely, and who gained or lost from that path.
