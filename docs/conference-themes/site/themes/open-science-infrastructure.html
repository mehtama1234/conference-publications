Open science infrastructure starts with the data substrate

This page is about a basic dependency that often disappears behind model names: if the pretraining corpus cannot be inspected, reused, licensed, or audited, then the model built on top of it is not a fully reproducible scientific object. Common Corpus treats open, lawful pretraining data as infrastructure, not as an incidental dataset release.

FIRST PRINCIPLES

Why data provenance is scientific infrastructure

A language model is not only an architecture plus weights. It is also a compressed consequence of the data it saw. If that data is legally ambiguous, unavailable, heavily skewed toward a few languages, or filtered by undocumented rules, then downstream researchers cannot fully explain, reproduce, compare, or safely deploy the model. The corpus becomes a hidden experimental condition.

Open science infrastructure is therefore not just code release. For large-model research, the pretraining corpus is a shared laboratory. It defines what experiments others can repeat, what regions of language are represented, what legal constraints attach to reuse, and what claims can be made about model behavior. Common Corpus is important because it moves the research artifact upstream: before the benchmark score, before the fine-tune, before the agent scaffold, there is the question of whether the training substrate itself can be examined and reused.

Legal reuse

If a corpus depends on copyrighted or proprietary material, research may be difficult to reproduce and commercial use may carry unresolved risk.

Provenance

Dataset assembly, filtering, source domains, time periods, and license categories become part of the scientific method, not an administrative afterthought.

Coverage

Multilingual and code coverage determine who can build on the corpus and which capabilities the resulting models can realistically learn.

CORE

The corpus is part of the experiment

For large language models, the training dataset is not a replaceable background detail. It is one of the main experimental conditions.

Two models with the same architecture and training budget can behave differently because they saw different mixtures of languages, code, books, web pages, licenses, filters, dates, and duplicates. If the corpus is closed or legally ambiguous, other researchers cannot fully repeat the experiment. They can copy the optimizer and model size, but not the evidence that formed the model.

Common Corpus matters because it moves open science upstream. It says that reproducibility does not start at the model checkpoint. It starts at the data substrate: what sources entered, what rights they carry, what filtering changed, which languages are present, and whether another lab can legally train from the same material.

TOPIC 01

Open science is a reproducibility contract

The point of open science is not that a file exists on the internet. The point is that another researcher can repeat, inspect, challenge, and extend the claim.

For small experiments, the reproducibility contract usually means reporting the dataset, the preprocessing, the model, the optimizer, and the evaluation. For large language models, the contract becomes harder because the pretraining corpus is enormous, assembled from many sources, filtered by many rules, and governed by many legal conditions. If that corpus is missing, the model is only partly open. The weights may be downloadable, but the experiment that made the weights cannot be reconstructed.

Common Corpus makes this simple: the corpus is the experiment's raw evidence stream. If researchers cannot inspect that stream, they cannot cleanly ask whether a result came from architecture, scaling, data mixture, code proportion, multilingual coverage, leakage, filtering, or source rights. Open science therefore starts before training. It starts with the question: can the evidence stream itself be reused and audited?

TOPIC 02

A corpus is a method, not just material

A pretraining dataset is not passive input. It decides what distinctions the model can learn.

Every corpus makes choices. It includes some languages and misses others. It includes some domains and excludes others. It keeps or removes duplicates. It filters some text as low quality, unsafe, private, synthetic, spammy, or legally unusable. It gives different amounts of mass to books, web pages, code, references, and low-resource language material. Those choices become training pressure. They shape what the model treats as common, rare, formal, conversational, factual, executable, or out of distribution.

That is why Common Corpus is a technical paper, not only a governance paper. Its central object is a lawful, documented, multilingual and code-rich substrate for pretraining. The method is the construction of a reusable evidence base: source selection, legal filtering, provenance tracking, curation, deduplication, coverage balancing, and suitability testing through model training. The model trained later inherits these choices.

TOPIC 03

Provenance turns data into evidence

A data point is more useful scientifically when you know where it came from, why it was included, and what rights or assumptions travel with it.

Without provenance, two datasets with the same token count can be scientifically different in invisible ways. One may contain duplicated benchmark-like text. One may be mostly high-resource web pages. One may include code under incompatible licenses. One may have aggressive filtering that removes dialects, informal writing, or minority-language material. One may contain older documents while another includes recent sources. These differences are not bookkeeping details. They are experimental conditions.

Common Corpus teaches that provenance is a form of measurement. It measures the path from source material to training substrate. Good provenance lets later researchers audit contamination risk, compare language coverage, inspect license categories, test filtering bias, and reproduce mixture choices. Bad provenance turns the corpus into a black box even if the final file is public.

TOPIC 04

Reuse requires interfaces, not just release

A released artifact becomes infrastructure only when other people can use it without reverse-engineering the hidden decisions behind it.

For a corpus, the interface is not a graphical UI. It is the set of handles that let researchers reason about the data: language labels, source categories, license categories, filtering metadata, code subsets, documentation, data statements, training recipes, and benchmark checks. These handles let a lab ask controlled questions. What happens if we change the language mixture? What happens if we remove one source family? Which results survive stricter deduplication? Does code data improve reasoning tasks or only programming benchmarks?

This is the first-principles difference between a dump and infrastructure. A dump is something you can download. Infrastructure is something you can build experiments on. Common Corpus is aiming at the second category: a substrate that makes downstream comparisons more meaningful because the data side is visible enough to control.

TOPIC 05

Evaluation context must travel with the artifact

The corpus has to be judged by more than size, because size alone does not show whether it is lawful, representative, clean, or useful for training.

The evidence for a pretraining corpus has several layers. Legal evidence asks whether the sources can be reused. Provenance evidence asks whether the assembly path is inspectable. Coverage evidence asks whether the corpus serves more than a narrow slice of language and code. Quality evidence asks whether filtering and curation remove enough noise without erasing useful variation. Training evidence asks whether models trained on the corpus behave competitively enough to make the corpus worth using.

Common Corpus uses small-model training results as a practical suitability check. That does not prove every larger-scale use case. It proves something narrower and important: the corpus is not merely a governance ideal. It can support actual model training at enough quality to be a serious research substrate. Future evidence would need to test larger scales, more languages, more contamination checks, stronger filtering audits, and downstream behavior across domains.

METHOD

Why lawful data is a technical constraint

Legal status is not separate from ML method design when the dataset is the thing that creates the model.

A closed or legally uncertain corpus limits what claims can be repeated. A lab may report a benchmark score, but another lab cannot test whether the score came from architecture, optimizer, data quality, source duplication, leakage, or a hidden language mixture. A model can be open in weights while still being scientifically hard to audit if the data path is missing.

Common Corpus treats lawful reuse as part of the artifact. That does not mean licensing solves every quality issue. It means the corpus crosses the first gate: researchers can inspect it, reuse it, and ask sharper questions about data mixture, coverage, contamination, and capability formation.

EVIDENCE

What would actually prove the contribution

A corpus paper cannot be proved by token count alone. The evidence has to show that the dataset is lawful enough to reuse, documented enough to audit, broad enough to matter, and clean enough to train models.

Scale

The paper reports about two trillion tokens, which puts the corpus in the range where pretraining experiments are meaningful rather than toy demonstrations.

Rights

The source material is described as uncopyrighted or permissively licensed, making legal status part of the technical artifact.

Documentation

Provenance, filtering, and curation details are the audit trail. They tell later researchers what experiment was actually run.

Coverage

The corpus includes many languages, including low-resource languages, plus substantial code, so openness does not mean only one narrow text slice.

Training check

Two small language models trained on Common Corpus perform comparably to other models of similar size, which is an initial suitability test.

Remaining risk

The paper still has to be read against license-audit details, contamination checks, language quality, filtering bias, and whether small-model evidence scales.

INSIGHT

Provenance is evidence, not decoration

A provenance record tells the reader what experiment was actually run.

In ordinary small-data science, researchers report the dataset, sampling procedure, inclusion criteria, and preprocessing. Large-model pretraining needs the same discipline, just at larger scale. Source categories, license classes, language coverage, filtering steps, and code inclusion all change the resulting model. Treating those records as optional paperwork makes the model harder to explain.

The first-principles lesson is that open science requires control over the evidence stream. If the evidence stream is hidden, the model is not fully reproducible even when the weights are available.

SUBTHEME

Open LLM pretraining data

Common Corpus makes the pretraining dataset the main research object

data governance

Common Corpus introduces a roughly two-trillion-token open dataset for LLM pretraining. The central claim is not merely size. The paper argues that model development is constrained by the legal and scientific status of the data substrate: common pretraining corpora often include copyrighted, proprietary, or otherwise ambiguous material, which complicates research transparency and deployment under emerging AI regulation. Common Corpus assembles data that is either uncopyrighted or available under permissive licenses, documents provenance, applies filtering and curation, and covers a wide range of languages including low-resource languages that are often underrepresented in pretraining datasets. It also includes substantial code data, making it useful for models that need programming capability.

The evaluation claim is deliberately pragmatic. The authors train two small language models on the corpus and report performance comparable to other models of similar size, using that as an initial suitability check for multilingual pretraining. The deeper contribution is that this makes the dataset a reusable piece of the research ecosystem. If the corpus is open, documented, and legally easier to reuse, then labs can ask sharper questions about data mixtures, filtering, contamination, multilingual transfer, and downstream behavior without treating the training data as an unknowable private asset.

FIELD READ

How this fits the 2026 conference picture

Across the conference notes, data is increasingly treated as an intervention surface. Other papers study data selection, privacy risk from pretraining overlap, reasoning data recipes, and dataset difficulty. Common Corpus sits at the governance layer of that same story. It asks whether the raw material for LLM development can be shared and audited before we argue about the best optimizer, post-training method, or benchmark.

This also applies beyond pretraining corpora. Benchmarks, agent environments, scientific datasets, and generated task suites need the same provenance discipline: where tasks came from, what licenses or usage constraints apply, what data was used to build them, what verifier defines success, and whether another lab can reproduce the artifact. Common Corpus is the pretraining-data version of a broader rule: the evidence substrate has to be governed before model claims can be trusted.
