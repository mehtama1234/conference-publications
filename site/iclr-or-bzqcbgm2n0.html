Beyond DAGs: A Latent Partial Causal Model for Multimodal Learning

Contrastive learning on images and text recovers the hidden shared factors that connect both modalities, allowing models to work well on new tasks with little data.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

When you pair images with text, the training process naturally finds what the two modalities share — the common ideas behind both. This paper explains why this happens mathematically and shows it produces cleaner, more reusable representations than standard methods. Those cleaner representations make models learn faster from a few examples, because each learned factor corresponds to one clear semantic concept so the model can adapt that factor in isolation, and they work better on new kinds of data because the model is not overfitting to the quirks of its training source.

Zero assumption version

Images and text do not come from the same source

When you pair an image with a text caption, they are created separately. A photo on the internet might be taken by a photographer; the caption is written by an annotator who never saw the photo process. Or an image is generated first, then text is added later. The two sources do not follow a single logical path.

A naive model would assume both the image and text are generated by one underlying cause — a single chain of decisions. But real data is messier: the image has its own quirks (lighting, background noise), and the text has its own quirks (grammar, sentence structure). A standard model built for single-cause data cannot capture this split.

Earlier work tried to fix this by assuming all multimodal data follows one directed graph of causes. But that assumption breaks down in the real world, where different datasets combine data created in different ways — some image-first, some text-first, some independent. The model cannot adapt to these different sources.

The paper's move: Instead of one cause, model the connection as an undirected link between the image's hidden factors and the text's hidden factors. Contrastive training on this model teaches the shared factors.

What it actually does

Model the setup

Describe how images and text are generated: each has shared hidden factors plus its own private quirks.

Build the causal graph

Draw edges between the image's hidden factors and text's hidden factors. This link is undirected, not a one-way cause.

Run contrastive training

Pair matching image-caption data. Pull matching pairs close in representation space, push mismatches apart.

Extract shared factors

Apply a technique called FastICA (Independent Component Analysis) to separate the learned representation into distinct factors.

Test on new tasks

Use the disentangled representations to train models on new tasks with very few labeled examples.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the assumption of a single directed cause with an undirected link between image and text hidden factors. Contrastive training then optimizes the representations to respect this link.

the object it moves  =  the causal structure connecting modalities
The hidden quantity

Disentangled factors: which hidden ideas are being shared

measured quantity  =  component-wise disentanglement score

The hidden quantity is how cleanly the learned representation separates into distinct, interpretable factors. One factor might capture the object in the image (dog, car, person); another might capture the style (bright, blurry, colorful). A disentangled representation has each factor independently carry one piece of meaning, with no redundancy or mixing between factors.

The paper estimates this by applying FastICA to the representations learned by contrastive training. FastICA measures how independent each factor is from the others — if the factors are truly separate, they should be statistically independent. The paper also validates this by checking which factors correlate with ground-truth attributes in test datasets (color, pose, expression, etc.).

Why it works

Why contrastive training recovers shared factors

Contrastive training pulls matching image-text pairs close while pushing mismatches far apart. If an image and caption truly share an underlying concept, the training signal will reward representations that encode that concept identically in both modalities. Any representation that captures only image-specific noise or text-specific quirks will contribute to misalignment, because a noise-based representation of the image will not help predict the text caption, and vice versa — the noise is different in each modality, so a model relying on noise will not align the two representations.

Over time, training pressure accumulates on the factors that appear in both modalities. The image encoder learns to encode these shared factors prominently because they help the image representation match the text representation. The text encoder does the same. The private quirks (image background, text grammar) are still learned by the model, but they occupy separate dimensions because they do not help the contrastive objective. This separation happens automatically.

The paper proves that under certain statistical assumptions — roughly, that the private factors of each modality are independent from each other and from the shared factors — the contrastive representation converges to the latent coupled variables. The proof uses the optimal global solution of the contrastive loss function to argue that only a specific structure of representations minimizes the loss.

Math after the picture

The contrastive loss equation

First in words:

The loss sums over all pairs of images and captions. For each image, it tries to make the representation of the matching caption as close as possible, and the representations of all wrong captions as far away as possible.

Then the compact form:

L = -log( exp(sim(x_i, t_i) / tau) / sum_j exp(sim(x_i, t_j) / tau) )

Every symbol:

x_ithe representation vector of image i
t_ithe representation vector of the matching text caption
t_jthe representation vector of any other caption in the batch
simsimilarity: the dot product of two vectors divided by their lengths
tautemperature: a scaling factor (smaller value makes correct matches more important)
loglogarithm: the standard math function
A tiny worked example
Say the similarity between image 1 and its matching caption is 0.9. The similarity between image 1 and 3 wrong captions are 0.5, 0.4, 0.3.
Compute exponentials with tau=0.1: exp(0.9/0.1)=2.441e4, exp(0.5/0.1)=1.649e2, exp(0.4/0.1)=55, exp(0.3/0.1)=20.
Fraction = 2.441e4 / (2.441e4 + 1.649e2 + 55 + 20) = 0.993. Loss = -log(0.993) = 0.007. The model strongly rewards the correct match.
If the representations were random (all similarities ~0.5), the fraction would be 0.25, loss = 1.386. The penalty is 197x higher. Contrastive training uses this huge difference to steer encoders toward alignment.

The loss compresses representations of matching pairs and pulls apart mismatches. Over time, only the shared factors survive as stable alignment signals because they help both the image and text encoder minimize the loss consistently, while private factors are too noisy to help the contrastive objective — image-only noise does not align with text, and text-only noise does not align with images — so they segregate into separate dimensions where they cannot harm the alignment.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Data from one true causal source only. If all image-text pairs come from a single, simple causal process (e.g. images and text both generated by a fixed pipeline with no independent variation), the latent partial model assumptions break. The method works best when heterogeneous data is actually present.
Shared factors are weak or absent. If image and text encode almost no overlapping information (e.g. random image paired with random caption), there is no alignment signal. Contrastive training cannot extract shared factors that do not exist.
Private factors outnumber shared factors. If the image has 20 distinct visual quirks and the text has 20 distinct linguistic quirks, but only 2 truly shared semantic factors, the method may struggle to isolate the shared 2 among the noise.
Heterogeneous data at scale. The method works best with large datasets that genuinely mix different causal sources (images from photography, images from digital art, images from synthetic 3D, etc.). At scale, the statistical assumptions are more likely to hold.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a contrastive model (e.g. SimCLR or the multimodal version used here) on a dataset of images paired with text captions. Then apply FastICA to extract independent factors from the learned image representation and text representation. Measure which of those factors correlate with ground-truth attributes in a test set (e.g. facial features like age, gender, expression; object properties like color, pose). Compare to a model trained without contrastive learning or without the assumption of multimodal coupling.

disentanglement_score = (fraction of ground-truth attributes that each learned factor correlates with strongly) / (average number of factors each attribute correlates with)

The demo must show that contrastive training on multimodal pairs produces factors where each attribute (dog vs. cat, bright vs. dark, frontal vs. profile) lives in exactly one or two factors, not scattered across many — that is the signature of disentanglement.
