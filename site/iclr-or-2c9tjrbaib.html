InfoSeek: Agentic Search via Hierarchical Constraint Synthesis

A framework that turns web exploration into constraint puzzles, generating synthetic training data that teaches AI agents to search better.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When an AI agent searches for information across many webpages, it has to decide which link to follow next and know when it has found what it needs. Training agents to do this well is hard because real research tasks are complex and most datasets do not capture that complexity. This paper introduces a method that builds training data by imagining research as a constraint-satisfaction puzzle — a set of problems nested inside each other, where solving the whole thing means satisfying constraints at multiple levels.

Zero assumption version

Why agentic search training fails today

An LLM-agent trying to research a topic needs to visit webpages, read them, and decide what to search for next. The agent has to learn two things: which pages matter, and when it has gathered enough information to answer the original question. Today's training data either has simple lookup tasks (find one fact on one page) or fake research tasks that do not match how real research actually works. A naive approach tries training agents on random webpage collections, but that teaches them nothing useful — they learn to browse randomly, not to build a coherent research strategy.

Real research requires satisfying nested goals. You need facts from multiple pages, but some facts only make sense after you have found other facts. A dataset of tasks where everything is on one page, or where any page will do, misses this entirely.

The problem in one sentence. Existing datasets for training agentic search do not capture the nested, multi-source nature of real research tasks, so agents trained on them search badly.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a seed webpage.

Pick any real webpage, call it the starting point.

Diffusion: expand outward.

Follow outgoing links, creating a tree of neighboring pages. Record constraints that connect each page to the goal.

Retrospection: sample a subtree.

Pick a subset of the tree and add backtracking constraints — false paths or misleading links.

Blur and label.

Mix genuine and fake links, label which pages truly satisfy the nested goals.

Generate a training instance.

Output a task, a set of webpages, and a relevance score for each based on the constraints.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

InfoSeek generates training data by conceptualizing the search process as a constraint-satisfaction problem. Instead of collecting real research behavior or writing synthetic tasks by hand, it builds tasks algorithmically by unrolling a webpage tree and labeling pages by how many constraint levels they satisfy. This shifts the problem from hand-labeling tasks to automatically extracting the structure of real webpages.

the object it moves  =  training data generation mechanism
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: constraint satisfaction depth

measured quantity  =  constraint satisfaction depth

Constraint satisfaction depth is a count of how many nested goals a single webpage can help you solve: if a page directly answers the top-level question, its depth is 1; if it only helps after you have already found other facts, its depth is 2 or higher. This matters because pages that satisfy multiple levels of constraints are the ones an agent must learn to find and rank by importance to solve complex research tasks.

InfoSeek estimates this depth by tracing backward through the webpage tree. It builds a tree by expanding outward from a seed page (the diffusion phase), then samples a subtree and counts how many levels of constraints each page satisfies (the retrospection phase). Pages that satisfy constraints at multiple levels get higher depth scores.

Why it works

Why the method works: the causal chain

The mechanism has three parts. First, real research has a tree structure — each query or sub-question leads to new webpages, which in turn suggest other searches. By building a tree of webpages from a seed page (diffusion), InfoSeek captures that structure. Second, not all pages are equally important. Some pages answer the core question, others provide context, and others are distractions. By labeling pages with their constraint satisfaction depth — how many levels of nested sub-goals they help solve — InfoSeek teaches an agent what matters. Third, real datasets contain both signal and noise. Pages that are easy to find are not always relevant; pages that are truly useful might require knowing what to search for. By adding backtracking constraints and mixing genuine and misleading links (retrospection), InfoSeek creates an agent-training challenge that matches reality.

The result is training data where each task reflects the actual complexity of multi-level research. An agent trained on this data learns two skills together: it learns which pages to trust by reading their content, and it learns to stop searching when it has satisfied the nested constraints. This is more realistic than random-link datasets (which do not teach a strategy) or single-page datasets (which do not teach multi-step reasoning).

The constraint satisfaction formula

First in words:

relevance score for a page equals the number of constraint levels it helps satisfy, divided by the total depth of the tree, scaled by how many other pages also satisfy those constraints.

Then the compact form:

relevance_page = (depth_satisfied / max_depth) * (1 - conflict_factor)

Every symbol:

relevance_pagea score from 0 to 1 indicating how important the page is for solving the task
depth_satisfiedhow many nested constraint levels this page helps satisfy (1, 2, 3, etc.)
max_depththe maximum depth of any constraint chain in the research tree
conflict_factora small penalty (0 to 0.3) if the page contradicts constraints at other levels
A tiny worked example
Consider a research tree with max depth of 3. The main question (depth 1) asks for facts about neural networks.
Page A directly answers the main question, satisfies depth-1 constraint: relevance_A = (1 / 3) * (1 - 0) = 0.33
Page B gives historical context needed to understand Page A, satisfies depths 1 and 2: relevance_B = (2 / 3) * (1 - 0) = 0.67
Page C is about a related but different topic, satisfies only depth 3 partially: relevance_C = (1 / 3) * (1 - 0.2) = 0.27
Page B is ranked highest because it helps satisfy more levels of the nested research goal.

The formula gives higher relevance scores to pages that satisfy more levels of nested constraints, teaching an agent which pages matter most for the overall research goal.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Seed pages are not representative. If you start diffusion from a page that is unusual or off-topic, the generated tree will not reflect typical research. The constraints derived will be skewed, producing training tasks that do not match real research patterns.
Constraint labeling is noisy. Automatically determining which pages satisfy which constraints is imperfect. If the labeling is wrong, agents learn to weight irrelevant pages highly, and their search quality degrades.
Misleading paths are too obvious. If the backtracking constraints and false links are trivial to spot, agents do not learn to be skeptical. Real research requires reading carefully to distinguish signal from noise; if training is too easy, agents will not develop that skill.
Done right. When seed pages are chosen to represent common research domains, constraint labeling is accurate, and false paths are subtle and plausible, agents learn multi-source search strategies that work on new research tasks they have never seen before.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny research task: given a question like 'How do neural networks learn from mistakes?', generate a tree of 20 webpages (or snippets) using InfoSeek's diffusion phase. Manually label a few pages as satisfying depth-1, depth-2, and depth-3 constraints. Train an agent (a language model or retrieval system, for instance) on this synthetic task. Test it on a separate task with the same structure. Measure: (a) how many of the top pages the agent retrieves are truly central to the research goal (precision), and (b) how many of the actually central pages it finds (recall).

search_quality = 0.5 * precision + 0.5 * recall

The demo must show that agents trained on InfoSeek-generated data retrieve more central pages and use fewer searches to satisfy the nested constraints than agents trained on random webpages.
