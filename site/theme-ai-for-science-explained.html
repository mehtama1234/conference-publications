AI for Science

How can we train AI on past scientific data to predict, generate, or understand what happens next in biology, chemistry, physics, medicine, and neuroscience?

What this whole area is about

For centuries, science has worked the same way: scientists ask a question, design an experiment, collect data, and look for patterns. But some of the biggest challenges in science involve problems that are too big, too slow, or too expensive to solve this way. A drug that takes a decade to develop. A protein that takes weeks of computer time to simulate. A disease where the interactions are so tangled that no one can figure out which gene is really the culprit. AI offers a shortcut: instead of simulating every detail, train a machine to learn the patterns from past data and predict or generate what comes next.

The hard part is that science demands something AI usually struggles with: correctness. A weather forecast that is 5% wrong might be okay. A drug candidate with a 5% error rate could poison someone. Physics follows rules — energy is conserved, momentum is conserved — and those rules have to hold exactly, not just on average. So the challenge is not just training a model on data, but building models that respect the laws of nature, work with tiny amounts of data, explain their reasoning, and handle the uncertainty that always lurks in real experiments.

Researchers are tackling this from multiple angles. Some train neural networks to behave like differential equations — mathematical rules that describe how systems change over time. Others generate entirely new molecules and proteins from scratch, learning the recipe for building something useful. Some build foundation models on biology itself — trained on genomes and proteins — and fine-tune them for specific questions. Others combine AI with physics-based search and simulation to find the best drug or molecular design. Still others use AI to sharpen blurry images from microscopes, predict how a drug will behave without running expensive tests, figure out which genes cause disease rather than just correlate, predict weather by blending learning with physics, or decode what the brain is doing from its electrical signals.

This matters everywhere because AI can compress years of research into months: drug discovery that could cure diseases faster and cheaper because screening happens on a computer first, materials science that invents new alloys or batteries because we can test millions of compositions in simulation, climate modeling that gets forecasts right because we blend learning with physics, and neuroscience that lets paralyzed people control prosthetics with their thoughts because we can decode intention from their brain signals.

Subtheme

Solving equations and modeling systems

Physics and engineering describe how things change using equations — differential equations, to be precise — that say: given the current state, what is the next tiny step? A ball falls because gravity pulls it down. A chemical reaction speeds up as temperature rises because molecules move faster and collide more often. These equations are exact, but solving them by hand is impossible for most real systems. Scientists use computers to simulate step by step, but for large systems (fluids, weather, climate), even computers take days or weeks to run one simulation.

The bottleneck is that every tiny step costs computation time. So researchers ask: could we train a neural network to act like the equation itself? Feed it the current state, and it predicts the next state instantly — thousands of times faster than a real simulation, because a forward pass through a network is just arithmetic on the GPU instead of solving differential equations. The tricky part is that the network must obey the physics exactly: energy cannot disappear, momentum cannot create itself, and the same physical law must apply everywhere. Standard neural networks do not enforce these rules — they just fit the data, and they often break the laws of physics in ways that cascade into garbage predictions.

The papers in this cluster train neural networks in ways that respect physics. Some encode the equations directly into the network architecture so it cannot violate the laws. Others train on simulated data from known equations, then test whether the network really learned the equation or just memorized specific scenarios. Some networks learn to handle multiple time scales at once — a fast reaction coupled to a slow change — which is where most naive models fail. The goal is a surrogate model: a fake version of reality that is fast, accurate enough for the next discovery, and trustworthy because it respects the laws that we know are true.

Subtheme

Generating structures and sequences

A protein is a long chain of amino acids folded into a specific shape that determines what it does. A small molecule is atoms bonded together in a precise pattern. Both of these are sequences and structures that nature built to solve a problem: bind to a target, break down a poison, copy DNA, fight an infection. Drug discovery is about finding new proteins or molecules that solve a medical problem — but the space of possibilities is unimaginably huge. A hundred atoms can be bonded together in a trillion different ways.

Traditional drug discovery is to synthesize candidates in the lab one at a time and test each one. That is expensive and slow. What if we could generate promising structures on a computer first, test them in simulation, and only synthesize the winners? The challenge is that generation has to respect chemistry: atoms bond only to certain other atoms with certain angles and distances, and the resulting structure has to be stable — not blow apart in a solvent or fall apart in the body. Naive AI just generates gibberish atoms that violate chemistry entirely.

This cluster uses generative models — networks that learn the patterns in databases of known proteins, known drugs, known molecules — and generate new ones that are similar but different. Some generate them one step at a time, checking chemical validity at each step. Some learn a continuous space where similar structures are close together, so morphing between known proteins smoothly generates plausible new ones. Others combine generation with scoring: generate many candidates and rank them by computed toxicity or activity before synthesis. The papers here differ in how they handle the constraints: do they build chemistry rules in, or teach the model to learn them?

Subtheme

Understanding what AI learned from biology

Over the last decade, biologists have sequenced the genomes of thousands of organisms, imaged millions of protein structures, recorded billions of DNA and RNA sequences, photographed millions of cells, and captured brain activity from thousands of subjects. This is raw material. What if we trained a model on all of that data, and it learned the deep patterns in how biology works? Then when a researcher has a new question — does this mutation cause disease, or will this protein fold correctly — they could ask the model instead of running expensive experiments from scratch.

The challenge is that biological data is messy, diverse, and often involves rare events. A mutation might occur in one person in a million. A protein might only fold correctly if three things happen in sequence. The data comes from different labs, different organisms, different measurement methods — so the patterns are noisy and contradictory. A model trained on all this data could pick up false patterns or memorize quirks of specific datasets. It also needs to transfer: a model trained on human proteins should say something useful about a mouse protein, even if it has never seen that exact sequence before.

This cluster builds and releases foundation models — large models trained on vast biological datasets — and explores what they learned. Some models are trained only on DNA sequences and learn to predict mutations or genetic disease. Others are trained on protein sequences and 3D structures and learn to predict function or engineering challenges. Still others combine vision and biology: trained on cell images to detect disease patterns, or on brain imaging to predict outcomes. The papers explore how to train these models so they do not just memorize, how to adapt them to new tasks with small amounts of data, and how to check whether the model is really learning biology or just picking up statistical artifacts.

Subtheme

Optimizing through simulation and search

Drug discovery, materials design, and protein engineering all pose the same underlying problem: find the best solution in a gigantic search space. Does this change to a protein make it more stable? Does this new alloy conduct electricity better because of different atomic packing? Does this drug candidate hit the target and avoid side effects? Traditionally, chemists narrow the search space using chemical intuition, make a few candidates, and test them. But what if there is a much better solution that no human would think to try, because it requires a combination of properties that contradicts human intuition?

The space of possibilities is so enormous that brute-force testing is impossible. There are more possible small molecules with drug-like properties than atoms in the universe. Even if we train a model to predict drug activity, we still face the combinatorial explosion: which of a billion candidates should we actually synthesize and test? And how do we explore regions of the space that have never been tried, where the model is uncertain?

This cluster combines learning with search. Some use reinforcement learning — a technique where the AI learns to make decisions that maximize a reward (binding strength, stability, low toxicity) by trial and error. Others use active learning: generate many candidates, test a small number that the model is most uncertain about, and then retrain the model with the new data, focusing the search. Some interleave simulation and learning: run expensive physics simulations on the most promising candidates and use those exact results to refine the model. The common strategy is that the model is not trying to predict every property perfectly — it is trying to narrow down the search to the most promising few candidates, so that expensive testing focuses on winners.

Subtheme

Imaging and reconstructing hidden structures

A microscope image is always fuzzy. A medical scan — CT, MRI, X-ray — is always noisy and low resolution. Cryo-EM (a technique to see proteins) produces images so grainy they look like abstract art. This is not a failure — it is the cost of using safe radiation doses or capturing fleeting biological moments. But the loss of detail costs researchers: they miss tiny features, misdiagnose diseases, or cannot see exactly where a drug binds to its target.

Traditional approaches sharpen images by assuming specific models: assume the image is a blurred version of a sharp image, then deblur it using math. But biological structures are so varied that no single mathematical model works well. Cryo-EM images of proteins are determined by how they were randomly frozen and angled in the microscope, so the same protein looks different in each image. A tumor in a scan has texture and shape that varies from person to person.

This cluster uses neural networks to learn what sharp biological images look like by training on pairs: start with high-quality reference data (expensive, rare) and lower-quality versions (the actual scans we want to improve), and train a network to map from fuzzy to sharp. The challenge is generalizing: images from one microscope are different from another, data from one disease looks different from another. So papers here explore transfer learning (train once on a reference dataset, adapt quickly to new data), uncertainty quantification (the network should admit when it is guessing), and physics-informed networks (build in what we know about how imaging works). The payoff is microscopy and medical imaging that is clearer and cheaper because doctors can see tiny features without higher-end hardware, speeding up diagnosis and discovery because clearer images reveal disease earlier.

Subtheme

Predicting properties and interactions

When a drug company tests a new candidate molecule, they ask dozens of questions: Is it toxic? Does it stick to the target protein? Does it get absorbed in the gut? Does it cross the blood-brain barrier? Each test takes time and money — sometimes months and hundreds of thousands of dollars per molecule. Most candidates fail, so you want to kill the losers early.

The bottleneck is that chemists must choose which candidates to test first, usually based on intuition or pattern recognition from past failures. What if we could predict those properties directly from the molecular structure? A machine learning model trained on past test results could screen thousands of candidates computationally and flag the winners for expensive lab testing. This would save time, money, and animals, and accelerate drug discovery.

The papers in this cluster predict molecular properties from structure: predicting how toxic a drug is, how strong it binds to a protein target, whether it will be absorbed in the body, how it will be metabolized. The challenge is that molecules are complex 3D objects, and what matters is not just what atoms they contain but how those atoms are arranged and bonded. A naive model might ignore the 3D structure and fail on molecules that look similar on paper but fold very differently. Some papers use graph neural networks — a way to represent molecules as connected nodes — so the model understands bonds and structure the way chemistry does, which means it can generalize to new molecules it has never seen because it learned the rules of bonding rather than memorizing specific structures. Others use 3D coordinates directly to predict binding strength. Still others combine multiple models to predict a whole profile of properties at once, which is more reliable than predicting each property separately because errors in one property can lead to errors in another, so joint prediction catches those mistakes.

Subtheme

Reasoning about cause and effect

A person gets sick. A genome-wide scan finds a thousand genes that correlate with the disease. Which gene actually causes it? A treatment works in mice but fails in humans. Was it something unobserved that was different between the two? A study finds that people who take vitamins live longer. Is it the vitamin, or are they healthier for other reasons? These are questions about causation — what actually causes what — and they are notoriously hard to answer from data alone.

The challenge is that correlation is easy but causation is invisible. Two things can correlate because one causes the other, because the other causes the first, because both are caused by something hidden, or by pure accident. Machine learning usually finds correlations — patterns that predict one thing from another. It does not naturally distinguish between 'A causes B' and 'C causes both A and B.' In medicine and biology, this is critical: if you make a treatment based on a correlation that is actually due to confounding, it might be useless or harmful.

This cluster uses causal inference — techniques that try to untangle causation from correlation. Some use the structure of known causal relationships (a gene causes a protein, a protein causes a disease, but not the other way), then test whether that structure fits the data. Others use experimental tricks: if we can intervene on one variable (give people a medicine, edit a gene) and see what changes, that teaches us causation directly. Still others use reasoning about counterfactuals: what would happen if we changed this one thing and nothing else? Papers here combine machine learning with causal reasoning, building models that explicitly track what causes what, so they can predict the effect of an intervention, not just correlation with past data.

Subtheme

Forecasting weather and natural phenomena

Weather is driven by equations — the laws of fluid dynamics and thermodynamics. Current weather models solve those equations step by step on supercomputers. A five-day forecast takes hours to compute. Beyond ten days, the tiny uncertainties in the current state blow up and predictions become useless. Climate models try to project centuries into the future, but the compute and data requirements are staggering, and the models still have significant errors.

The bottleneck is that solving the equations is expensive. What if we trained a neural network on decades of past weather data and current observations to learn to predict the next day, or the next week, directly? A learned model could generate a forecast in seconds. The challenge is that weather obeys physics — storms do not just teleport — and a model trained only on data might learn fake patterns that work on test data but fail in the real world. It might predict a storm that violates conservation of energy, or forecast rain in dry air.

This cluster trains neural networks to forecast weather and climate, and deliberately infuses them with physics. Some networks are trained on historical weather data at global scale, learning to predict pressure, temperature, and precipitation days ahead without solving the equations. Others blend learned components with traditional physics solvers: use a fast learned model to do the bulk of the computation, then correct it with a physics-based step to ensure conservation laws hold. Still others use the learned model to improve initial conditions or to handle sub-grid phenomena (small-scale features too fine to resolve, like how clouds form in a single grid cell). The payoff is faster, more accurate forecasts and climate projections that respect physics because adding physics constraints prevents the model from predicting impossible storms or violating energy conservation.

Subtheme

Decoding brain signals and neural activity

When you think, your brain's neurons fire in patterns. When you move your hand, specific motor neurons activate. Researchers can record these electrical signals using electrodes (EEG, implanted sensors) or infer them from imaging (fMRI, PET). But the signals are noisy, and the brain is so complicated that no one has figured out a simple rule that translates the neural firing pattern into what you are thinking or wanting to do.

The challenge is that each person's brain is wired differently, the recording setup changes between sessions, and the same thought might activate different neurons at different times. A model that works for one person might completely fail for another. Yet the payoff is huge: if we can decode intention from brain signals, paralyzed people could control prosthetics with their thoughts, locked-in patients could communicate, and neuroscientists could understand how the brain encodes information.

This cluster trains neural networks to map from brain signals to behavior or intention. Some decode motor cortex recordings to predict which direction a person is planning to move, so a prosthetic arm can move before the person finishes the thought. Others decode visual cortex to reconstruct what someone is looking at or imagining. Still others use the signals to classify what a person is thinking about or predict their next decision. The technical challenge is working with small datasets (brain experiments are expensive and time-consuming), handling the unique wiring of each subject, and building models that can adapt to new people quickly. Papers here use transfer learning, personalization techniques, and carefully chosen network architectures that respect the structure of the brain.
