t-SNE Exaggerates Clusters, Provably

t-SNE can wildly distort how clustered your data is, making loose groups look tight and hiding how extreme outliers really are.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

t-SNE is a popular tool for visualizing data by squashing it from high dimensions down to 2D. Most people assume that if two groups look far apart on the plot, they were actually far apart in the original data. This paper proves that assumption is false. t-SNE can compress groups together, make loose clumps look dense, and obscure outlier positions so thoroughly that you cannot tell if the original data was well-organized or scattered.

Zero assumption version

What we trust t-SNE to show us

When you have a spreadsheet of thousands of data points, each with hundreds of measurements (like pixels in images, or test results on different drugs), you cannot plot them directly—your eyes only work in 2D or 3D. t-SNE solves this by squashing everything down to 2D while trying to keep nearby points near each other.

The unspoken promise is this: if two groups look tightly packed and far from each other in the t-SNE plot, then in the original high-dimensional space they really were separate and tight. If one point sits alone in the plot, it was genuinely far from the rest in the original data. But this paper shows the promise is broken.

A naive first attempt would be to just flatten dimensions randomly, but that destroys all structure. What you want is to keep similar points close while pushing dissimilar ones apart. t-SNE does this by measuring how likely each pair of points should be neighbors (based on distance in the original space) and then finding a 2D layout that reproduces those neighbor relationships. But the method has a hidden bias: it pumps up apparent clustering and suppresses extreme outliers.

The core problem. t-SNE cannot reliably tell you how clustered your original data was or how extreme the outliers really are—it systematically misrepresents both.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Assign neighborhood likelihoods

For each pair of points, compute how likely they should be neighbors based on distance in the original space.

Compress to 2D with a heavy-tailed probability curve

Find a 2D layout that matches those neighbor relationships. The curve used (t-distribution) allows rare, extreme distances in 2D more cheaply than the original space does, because distances grow more slowly in the tail. This makes it less costly to throw outliers back toward the center, since the penalty for a given separation shrinks as you go to the tails.

Nudge points iteratively

Adjust point positions step by step to reduce the mismatch between expected neighbors and plotted neighbors.

The asymmetry emerges

t-SNE exaggerates tight clusters and suppresses outliers because its loss function treats separation differently than it treats closeness.

You misread the result

Plot looks highly structured, so you believe the data was originally highly structured. But the visualization is the distortion, not the truth.

What the paper changes

What t-SNE moves

t-SNE changes the positions of all points in the 2D plane. But more fundamentally, it changes the *apparent structure* of your data by compressing clusters inward and pulling outliers toward the center.

the object it moves  =  point positions and apparent clustering
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: clustering strength

measured quantity  =  Silhouette score (how well-separated clusters actually are)

The paper measures how tightly grouped a cluster is by computing a 'silhouette score'—for each point, measure the distance to points in its own cluster versus the nearest different cluster. A score of 1 means perfectly isolated; a score of 0 means the point sits on the border; a negative score means the point is closer to a different cluster than its own.

The paper's key finding: two datasets with very different silhouette scores (one truly clustered, one scattered) can produce t-SNE plots that look similar. t-SNE fails to preserve this hidden quantity—the actual tightness of clusters in the original data.

Why it works

Why t-SNE exaggerates structure

t-SNE works by building a table that says how likely each pair of points should be neighbors in the original space. Then it finds a 2D configuration where a probability curve with heavy tails (the t-distribution, which allows rare extreme distances) matches those neighbor relationships as closely as possible, because the heavy tails mean that far-away points have less penalty in 2D than they would in the original space.

The distortion happens because of an asymmetry: when two points *should* be neighbors (high P), t-SNE penalizes them heavily if they are far apart in the plot. But when two points *should not* be neighbors (low P), the penalty for placing them close is mild. This is because the t-distribution's heavy tails make extreme distances in 2D cheap. So t-SNE pulls similar points together strongly, and only weakly pushes dissimilar points apart. Clusters compress inward. Outliers get reeled back in because the cost of pushing them further is low.

Additionally, t-SNE uses 'early exaggeration'—during the first phase of optimization, it multiplies all probabilities by 4 or so, making clusters look even tighter. This speeds convergence but also bakes in exaggeration. The paper shows that this bias is not an accident—the mathematics of the t-distribution guarantee it.

Math after the picture

The penalty t-SNE minimizes

First in words:

Total loss = sum over all point pairs of the KL divergence between the original neighbor likelihood P and the plotted neighbor likelihood Q under a t-distribution. The asymmetry is that when P_ij is high, the penalty for low Q_ij grows like log(Q_ij), which diverges as Q_ij goes to zero. But when P_ij is low, the penalty for high Q_ij is bounded.

Then the compact form:

L = sum_ij P_ij log(P_ij / Q_ij) where Q_ij = (1 + ||y_i - y_j||^2)^{-1} / sum_kl (1 + ||y_k - y_l||^2)^{-1}

Every symbol:

P_ijhow likely points i and j should be neighbors in the original space (based on distance)
Q_ijhow likely they are neighbors in the 2D plot (based on t-distribution)
y_ithe 2D coordinates of point i in the plot
log(P_ij / Q_ij)the divergence: penalizes Q_ij more when P_ij is high and Q_ij is low (because log diverges)
A tiny worked example
Suppose P_12 = 0.9 (points 1 and 2 should be neighbors). If Q_12 = 0.1, penalty = 0.9 * log(0.9/0.1) = 0.9 * log(9) ≈ 0.9 * 2.2 = 2.0.
Suppose P_34 = 0.1 (points 3 and 4 should NOT be neighbors). If Q_34 = 0.9, penalty = 0.1 * log(0.1/0.9) = 0.1 * log(0.11) ≈ 0.1 * (-2.2) = -0.22.
The first error costs 2.0; the second costs -0.22 (almost free). So t-SNE pulls 1 and 2 together hard but barely pushes 3 and 4 apart. This creates artificial tight clusters.

The asymmetry in the log-ratio penalty means t-SNE treats missing neighbors (bad) as far more costly than false neighbors (tolerable), so clusters get compressed and outliers get suppressed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

You assume tight clusters in the plot mean tight clusters in the data. They do not. t-SNE systematically exaggerates cluster tightness. A silhouette score of 0.3 (weak clusters) can look like 0.7 (strong clusters).
You trust the plot to identify outliers. Genuine outliers get pulled toward the center by t-SNE's loss. An extreme point might appear close to the main group, making you miss it entirely.
You compare two t-SNE plots of different datasets. Both are distorted in the same direction (exaggerating clustering). The comparison is meaningless. One plot might look tighter only because t-SNE exaggerated it more, not because the original data was tighter.
Tuning the neighborhood-scale value makes it worse. t-SNE has a setting (perplexity) that controls how many neighbors each point considers. Lowering it exaggerates clustering further. Raising it suppresses clustering. There is no setting that eliminates the distortion—it is built into the method.
Done right: interpret the plot as a rough visual guide only. Use t-SNE for intuition—to spot whether data looks high-dimensional or low-dimensional, to see gross structure—but never trust the exact clustering strength or outlier positions. Always verify with the actual distance distributions or other methods like PCA or UMAP.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate two datasets: one truly clustered (points sampled from well-separated gaussians, silhouette score 0.7) and one scattered (same points but with noise added, silhouette score 0.2). Add one extreme outlier to each. Apply t-SNE to both. Measure the silhouette score and outlier distance in the original data versus the plotted data. Show that both plots look similarly tight and both outliers appear similarly positioned, even though the inputs are very different.

Distortion = |silhouette_original - silhouette_plot| / silhouette_original. Also measure: |max_distance_original_outlier - max_distance_plot_outlier|.

The demo makes it impossible to deny: t-SNE hides the true clustering strength and pulls outliers back in because of the asymmetric loss penalties. Two radically different inputs look confusingly similar in the t-SNE output, proving you cannot safely read the plot as you thought you could.
