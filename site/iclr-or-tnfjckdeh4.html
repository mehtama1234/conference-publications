UnLoc: Depth Uncertainties for Floorplan Localization

Locate a camera in a floorplan by treating depth predictions as confidence ranges, not single guesses, and using pre-trained depth models that work everywhere.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot or phone needs to know where it is in a building. Traditional methods match what the camera sees to a database of images at known locations. This works, but requires building a custom depth model for each new building. UnLoc keeps the depth matching but treats each depth guess as a range with a confidence level, not a single number, and uses a pre-trained depth model that already works on any scene — no retraining needed.

Zero assumption version

Building custom depth models for every floorplan is slow and brittle

To find where a camera is in a building, one approach is to compare what the camera sees to images taken at known spots in that building. For each location in the building, store an image of that spot and its ground-truth depth map (how far away each part of the scene is). When you need to locate the camera, extract depth from the new image and match it against the stored depths.

The old way: train a separate depth model for each building. That is expensive and brittle — the model fails on any building it never trained on. The camera also gives depth predictions as point guesses (a single number per pixel), with no idea how confident it should be — when depth is ambiguous (glass walls, reflections, dark corners), the guess presents itself as certain even though it is unreliable.

The bottleneck. Every new environment needs a custom-trained depth network, and depth predictions ignore their own uncertainty.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Align and encode

Rotate the camera image to match gravity. Extract visual features using a pre-trained depth model.

Predict depth and spread

Pass features through the model to get a depth value AND a confidence range (aleatoric uncertainty) for each pixel.

Query the floorplan

Look up the pre-stored depth map at the location you are testing, also with its confidence range.

Score the match

Compare camera depth ranges to floorplan depth ranges. Big overlap means good match, tiny overlap means poor match.

Track over time

Fuse matches from multiple frames using the camera's ego-motion (how far it moved). Highest cumulative score is the location.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper treats each depth prediction as a probability distribution (a range with confidence), rather than a single committed guess. Instead of saying each pixel is exactly 3.7 meters away, it says likely 3.5 to 3.9 meters, with high confidence, or 2 to 5 meters with low confidence.

the object it moves  =  Depth predictions (from single numbers to confidence ranges)
The hidden quantity

The aleatoric uncertainty: how confident is each depth?

measured quantity  =  Aleatoric uncertainty (pixel-level depth confidence)

Aleatoric uncertainty is a measure of how sure the depth prediction is for each pixel. When a wall is solid and well-lit, the model is confident (narrow range). When the scene has glass, shadows, or reflections, the model admits uncertainty (wide range). The paper's depth network outputs both: a central depth value and a standard deviation (spread) that says how far the true value might wander.

The paper estimates this by training the model on real depth data with known ground truth. During training, the model learns to output not just a depth but also how far off that depth might be, measured against actual errors in the training set. At test time, ambiguous pixels naturally predict large uncertainty ranges, and certain pixels predict tight ranges. When matching against the floorplan, tight ranges are weighted more heavily because they carry more information.

Why it works

Why matching with ranges works better than point guesses

A single depth guess can be disastrously wrong in ambiguous regions (glass, shadows), yet the matching algorithm treats it as fact and bets the location on it. A range admits that ambiguity. When you match camera depth ranges to floorplan depth ranges, a pixel with high uncertainty in the camera depth contributes less to the final score — its wide range overlaps with many floorplan locations, so it does not narrow down the position as much. A high-confidence pixel with a tight range overlaps with only a few floorplan spots, so it precisely identifies where the camera must be.

The second innovation: use a pre-trained depth model. A depth model trained on millions of images (not just this building) learns generic visual cues — solid surfaces, edges, lighting, texture — that transfer to any new environment. By skipping per-building training, the system works instantly on unseen floorplans and adapts to changes in appearance (moving furniture, paint, lighting) without retraining.

Math after the picture

The loss: making predictions that match their own uncertainty

First in words:

Negative log-likelihood of depth and uncertainty. Penalizes: if the true depth falls outside your predicted range (with weight based on how confident you claimed), your loss climbs. The model learns to make tight ranges only when certain, and wide ranges only when uncertain.

Then the compact form:

L = sum_i [ (d_i - pred_mean_i)^2 / (2 * pred_std_i^2) + log(pred_std_i) ]

Every symbol:

d_itrue floorplan depth at pixel i
pred_mean_imodel's predicted central depth for pixel i
pred_std_imodel's predicted confidence (standard deviation) for pixel i
sum_iadd up the loss over all pixels in the image
A small worked example (one pixel)
True depth: 3.0 meters. Model predicts mean 3.2, standard deviation 0.5.
Error term: (3.0 - 3.2)^2 / (2 * 0.5^2) = 0.04 / 0.5 = 0.08
Confidence penalty: log(0.5) = -0.69
Total loss for this pixel: 0.08 - 0.69 = -0.61
Now pretend the model predicted the same mean 3.2 but claimed high certainty (std = 0.1).
Error term: (3.0 - 3.2)^2 / (2 * 0.1^2) = 0.04 / 0.02 = 2.0 (much larger penalty)
Confidence penalty: log(0.1) = -2.3
Total loss: 2.0 - 2.3 = -0.3 (worse than the honest-uncertainty case)
The model learns: only claim tight ranges when you are actually right.

The first term penalizes wrong predictions; the second term rewards admitting uncertainty when predictions are unsure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overconfident predictions in new scenes. If the pre-trained depth model was trained only on indoor scenes with specific lighting and materials, it may output tight ranges in a completely different environment (e.g., a warehouse with reflective floors), leading to wrong location matches. The range is tight but wrong.
Floorplan depth is noisy or sparse. If the ground-truth floorplan depth map has errors or missing regions, the match score becomes meaningless. The camera may be in the right location but fail to match because the reference is bad.
Rapid motion or occlusion. If the camera moves very fast or enters an occluded region (small room, basement corner), the ego-motion tracker loses the pose estimate, and the trailing frames cannot be fused correctly.
Done right. If the depth model is trained on diverse data (many buildings, lighting conditions, materials) and the floorplan depth is measured accurately, uncertainty ranges help localization ignore noisy pixels and focus on reliable ones. Diverse training data makes the model recognize which visual cues (solid walls, edges, textures) are trustworthy across different scenes, so it outputs tight ranges only when it sees reliable features.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 3D floorplan with known depth (e.g., a synthetic office or a real building scan). Record a sequence of camera frames from a known path. Train or use a pre-trained depth encoder. For each frame, predict depth and confidence ranges using the encoder. Compute the floorplan depth at several candidate locations (e.g., a grid of 1-meter spots). For each candidate, measure how well the camera's depth range overlaps with the floorplan depth range at that spot (e.g., Bhattacharyya distance or intersection-over-union of the predicted and ground-truth ranges). Fuse scores over a sequence of frames using ego-motion (from camera pose ground truth or visual odometry). The location with the highest fused score should match the true camera path.

localization_recall = (number of frames where top-1 predicted location is within 1 meter of ground truth) / (total frames)

The demo must show that depth ranges (with uncertainty) lead to fewer false positives in ambiguous regions compared to point-depth matching, and that the pre-trained model works on a floorplan it never trained on.
