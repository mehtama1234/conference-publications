GeoFAR: Geography-Informed Frequency-Aware Super-Resolution for Climate Data

To sharpen coarse climate maps into fine details, encode geographical location and separate the fast changes from the slow ones.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Climate scientists work with coarse weather maps—one measurement every 25 miles. To make farm-by-farm decisions, they need fine details: a 1-mile map showing where rain is heavy and where it is light. Neural networks can zoom these maps, but they keep missing the fast changes—the fine texture, the sharp edges between hot and cold. This paper fixes that by telling the network where on Earth each grid point sits, then explicitly teaching it to see the rapid wiggling layered on top of the slow patterns.

Zero assumption version

Coarse climate maps miss the fine details

A weather model produces maps at 25-mile resolution. You want 1-mile resolution to know which farms get rain. If you feed a neural network pairs of coarse-then-fine maps and train it to zoom, something strange happens: the zoomed output is blurry. It nails the broad patterns—the cool zones, the warm zones—but washes out the sharp edges, the sudden jumps in temperature, the fine texture of real terrain.

A neural network, when learning to upscale (make bigger pictures), has an inborn tendency: it finds the low-frequency patterns first because they are easier to learn—the broad smooth stuff. High-frequency details (the fine texture, the rapid wiggles) require much more adjustment during training to capture correctly, so the network's learning process gets stuck on the easy stuff. On top of that, climate data itself is dominated by low frequencies—huge flat plains and oceans with gentle slopes and transitions make up most of what the network sees during training, so the network's learning process spends most of its time on these smooth cases and barely adjusts itself to handle rough terrain.

In one sentence. The paper teaches the network geography (where each point sits and how high) and explicitly splits the learning into slow patterns plus fast wiggles.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies what information the neural network sees as input: instead of just feeding it the coarse climate map, it also passes in numbers encoding the geography (latitude, longitude, elevation), and it explicitly splits the coarse data into a low-frequency backbone and a high-frequency detail layer.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frequency content

measured quantity  =  High-frequency detail magnitude

Most of the climate data's energy lives in slow changes: the gradual temperature shift across the latitude, the gentle slope of air pressure. The fine texture—the sharp ridges of mountains, the rapid cold-air drops in valleys—adds up to only a tiny fraction of the data's total variation. This paper explicitly measures that fractional content and tells the network to focus on it.

The mechanism is a mathematical decomposition: you can break any map into a sum of smooth (low-frequency) + wiggly (high-frequency) components. During training, the network learns to estimate how much high-frequency detail belongs at each location, guided by the geography signal that tells it which spots are rough and which are flat.

Why it works

Why the method works: geography steers the frequencies

A neural network learning to upscale climate data faces a choice at every grid point: how much fine detail should appear here? The naive network has no way to know—it sees only the coarse pixels nearby. It defaults to guessing the smoothest answer (because smooth functions are easiest to learn) and misses the fine texture.

GeoFAR gives the network a guide. It encodes the location (latitude, longitude) and elevation of each point into a small vector (think: a set of numbers that describe the spot). A mountain location gets one set of numbers, a flat plain gets a different set. The network learns that mountains need sharp details and plains do not. At the same time, the paper explicitly decomposes the coarse input into low-frequency (smooth) and high-frequency (wiggly) pieces. This separation forces the network to build two separate pathways: one optimized to smooth changes, one optimized to detail. By guiding each pathway with the geography vector, the network learns to allocate high-frequency energy where it should go. The result: fine details appear where terrain is complex, smooth transitions appear where terrain is flat.

Math after the picture

The equation: geography-weighted frequency reconstruction

First in words:

High-resolution output equals low-frequency basis (smooth part) plus high-frequency basis (detail part) scaled by a geography factor that depends on location and elevation.

Then the compact form:

Z_hr(x,y) = Smooth(x,y) + alpha(Geo(x,y)) * Detail(x,y)

Every symbol:

Z_hr(x,y)the reconstructed high-resolution climate value at position x,y
Smooth(x,y)the smooth low-frequency component learned by the network
Detail(x,y)the high-frequency wiggly component learned by the network
alpha(Geo(x,y))a scaling factor: how much detail to add, based on geography
Geo(x,y)location (latitude, longitude) and elevation at position x,y
A tiny worked example
Flat plain: Geo signal says low-complexity. alpha(Geo) = 0.2. Smooth = 15°C, Detail = 3°C. Result: 15 + 0.2 * 3 = 15.6°C (mostly smooth, tiny detail).
Mountain peak: Geo signal says high-complexity. alpha(Geo) = 0.9. Smooth = 12°C, Detail = 8°C. Result: 12 + 0.9 * 8 = 19.2°C (significant detail added).
Same detail layer (8°C) but geography changed the scaling factor from 0.2 to 0.9—the mountain gets the fine texture, the plain stays smooth.

The geography signal does not invent details; it controls how much of the learned fine-texture layer to use at each location.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Geography signal insufficient or wrong. If the location/elevation encoding does not capture the real terrain complexity (e.g., a deep canyon is flattened to just elevation), the network cannot learn to predict the right amount of fine detail. Adding more geography information (terrain slope, roughness index, exposure to wind) tells the network which places really do have sharp features, fixing the problem.
Frequency split not aligned with the data. If the chosen cutoff between low and high frequencies does not match the actual data (e.g., high-frequency threshold set too low and includes broad weather patterns), both pathways become confused. The frequency split must match the physics of the climate process.
Fine details learned but overfitted to training region. The detail pathway learns sharply where training data is dense (over densely-sampled areas or coasts) but fails over sparse regions (remote mountains, deserts). Generalization requires diverse geography across the training set.
Done right. Encode detailed geography signals (latitude, longitude, elevation, slope, roughness index), split frequencies to match climate physics (synoptic scale vs. local), and train on data spanning all terrain types. The detail pathway then learns terrain-aware fine structure that carries to new regions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a real climate dataset (e.g., temperature from a weather model at 25-mile resolution). Degrade a 1-mile reference map to 25-mile, then try to upscale it back. Compare three outputs: (1) simple bilinear zoom (baseline), (2) a standard neural super-resolution network with no geography signal, (3) GeoFAR with geography and frequency split. Measure accuracy on mountains separately from plains. The demo should show that GeoFAR recovers mountain peaks and valleys (high-frequency detail) that the standard network smooths away, while keeping plains smooth as before.

Fine-texture error = mean absolute error on high-frequency component; test separately on mountain regions (high-complexity geography) vs. ocean/plain regions (low-complexity geography).

The demo must make impossible to miss: geography is the steering wheel. Without it, fine details either vanish or appear in the wrong places. With it, the model learns to place sharp edges where terrain is jagged and smooth transitions where land is flat.
