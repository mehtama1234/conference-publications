Reliable Probabilistic Forecasting of Irregular Time Series through Marginalization-Consistent Flows

A method that forecasts multiple time series together while guaranteeing that individual forecasts match what you'd predict alone.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you forecast multiple related time series at once (like stock price and trading volume), the model learns a joint pattern. But here is the problem: the predictions for each series individually can violate what the model itself predicts when asked about that series in isolation. MOSES fixes this by designing the forecasting model to be analytically marginalized—meaning individual predictions are mathematically guaranteed to come from the same model as the joint forecast.

Zero assumption version

The consistency trap in joint forecasting

When a forecasting model learns patterns in multiple related time series (like temperature and humidity), it learns how they move together. A naive approach: train one model on all the series at once, then use it to answer both 'joint' questions ('what are temperature and humidity together?') and 'marginal' questions ('what is just temperature?'). This seems logical but it breaks in a subtle way.

The issue is that the model's answers to single-series questions do not match what the model predicts when you ask it about that series in isolation. Imagine the model forecasts joint temperature-humidity as a cloud of possibilities. When you extract just temperature from that cloud, the resulting temperature predictions are not the same as what the model would produce if trained to forecast temperature alone. This inconsistency makes individual predictions unreliable and harder to interpret.

In one sentence. Standard forecasting models make joint and marginal predictions that contradict each other, destroying trust in individual predictions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define the model as a mixture

Start with K mixture components, each a normalizing flow (a learnable transformation).

Separate the core and the details

Each component has one shared latent Gaussian (the backbone) and one separate univariate transformation per variable.

Train on joint data

Learn the shared backbone and separate paths from examples of all variables together.

Compute marginals analytically

Drop a variable and integrate it out mathematically; no sampling, no approximation.

Deliver reliable individual forecasts

Both joint and marginal predictions come from the same model by design, not chance.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

MOSES changes how the model is built, not what it predicts. Specifically, it parametrizes the normalizing flow—the learned transformation that converts random noise into realistic predictions—using a separable structure: a shared multivariate core and independent univariate paths for each variable.

the object it moves  =  the normalizing flow structure
The hidden quantity

Marginalization consistency

measured quantity  =  consistency between joint and marginal predictions

Marginalization consistency is a check: when you remove a variable from the model's joint prediction, does the set of predictions for the remaining variables match what the model predicts when asked about those variables alone? In a consistent model, yes. In an inconsistent model, no. MOSES measures this by literally extracting the marginal (mathematically integrating out the removed variable) and comparing it to a single-variable forecast.

The paper estimates this consistency by computing log-likelihood on held-out test data. If the model is truly consistent, log-likelihood for individual variables should equal the likelihood of those variables extracted from the joint—they are the same object. MOSES achieves this by design: separable flows guarantee that marginals are always mathematically exact, never approximated.

Why it works

Why separable flows guarantee consistency

Here is why it works: if you design the model so that each variable has its own transformation path that does not interact with others (separable), then dropping a variable is just like never running that path. Mathematically, integrating out that variable gives you exactly the original model for the remaining variables. No contradiction, no approximation.

Technically, MOSES represents each component of the mixture as: a shared multivariate Gaussian (the latent backbone, shared across all variables), plus one univariate transformation (a normalizing flow) per variable that acts independently on its channel. When you want the marginal for a subset of variables, you integrate out the unwanted ones analytically. Because the shared backbone is Gaussian and the unwanted transformations are independent, integration is exact and closed-form. You never lose information or introduce inconsistency.

Why this works in practice: normalizing flows are flexible models that can learn many different shapes. By layering them (K mixture components), MOSES can learn complex joint patterns. The separable structure does not lose expressiveness for the joint—it only enforces a constraint that makes marginals reliable. In tests, MOSES achieves near-optimal joint predictions while guaranteeing marginal correctness.

Math after the picture

The separable flow equation

First in words:

The model is a mixture of normalizing flows. Each component combines a shared latent Gaussian density with independent univariate transformations. For each component k, the density factors as: the Gaussian density times the product of univariate transformations (one per variable), adjusted for volume changes during transformation.

Then the compact form:

p(x_1, ..., x_n | k) = N(z; mu_k, Sigma_k) * prod_i det(dT_i/dz_i) * prod_i p_i(T_i(z_i))

Every symbol:

x_1, ..., x_nthe observed variables (temperature, humidity, etc.)
zthe shared latent Gaussian random vector (the backbone)
N(z; mu_k, Sigma_k)Gaussian density for component k with mean mu_k and covariance Sigma_k
T_i(z_i)univariate normalizing flow for variable i (transforms latent to observation)
det(dT_i/dz_i)volume change factor for transformation i (Jacobian determinant)
p_ithe target univariate distribution for variable i (learned)
prod_iproduct over all variables i
kcomponent index in the mixture
A tiny worked example
Suppose two variables (temperature, humidity), 3 mixture components, shared latent z is 2D Gaussian.
Component 1 backbone: N(z; mu_1=[0,0], Sigma_1=I).
Temp path: T_1(z_1) = z_1^2 + 0.5, volume change = 2*z_1. Humid path: T_2(z_2) = exp(z_2), volume change = exp(z_2).
Joint density at sample (temp=1.5, humid=2.0) = Gaussian_1(z*) * 2*z_1* * exp(z_2*) * p_1(1.5) * p_2(2.0) where z* solves T_1(z_1*)=1.5, T_2(z_2*)=2.0.
Marginal for temperature: integrate humidity out. Since humid transform is independent, integration simplifies. Result: p(temp) depends only on temp-path, not humid-path—consistent with single-variable model.

The factorization into independent paths forces margins to be extractable exactly, avoiding the contradiction between joint and marginal predictions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Separability limits joint expressiveness. If the joint distribution has very complex correlations between variables (not captured by a shared Gaussian backbone), separable flows may not fit the joint as tightly as a fully expressive unrestricted flow. MOSES is designed to sacrifice some joint fit for marginal correctness.
Irregular sampling and missing data require careful handling. Real time series have gaps, irregular spacing, and missing values. MOSES must interpolate or encode these gaps into the model. If encoding is poor, both joint and marginal forecasts degrade.
Mixture size K must be chosen carefully. Too few components underfits; too many causes overfitting. The number of components controls expressiveness of the joint distribution, so K must be tuned for each dataset.
Done right. MOSES delivers marginally consistent predictions on irregular time series, trading small joint accuracy loss for reliable, truthful individual forecasts. This is the intended design: you accept slightly less joint fit in exchange for predictions you can trust and interpret.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic dataset: two time series with known correlation (e.g., sales and inventory, or temperature and power demand). Train MOSES and a baseline (unrestricted normalizing flow like ProFITi) on joint data. Then generate three sets of predictions: (1) joint forecast for both variables, (2) marginal forecast extracted from the joint, (3) single-variable forecast for each variable trained on that variable alone. Compute log-likelihood for each variable in all three settings. The demo must show that MOSES marginals (set 2) have log-likelihood very close to single-variable forecasts (set 3), while the baseline marginals diverge sharply from single-variable truth.

consistency_loss = sum_i |log_likelihood(variable_i | marginal_from_joint) - log_likelihood(variable_i | single_var_model)|

MOSES makes consistency_loss near zero; standard flows make it large. This gap is impossible to miss and proves the method works.
