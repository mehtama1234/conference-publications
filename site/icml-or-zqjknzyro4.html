Multi-Scale Action Generation with Probabilistic Consistency

A robot learns long-horizon tasks by planning at multiple time scales at once, staying consistent across them, so each level of detail supports the others.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots learn from human demonstrations, but long tasks trip them up: the robot must remember what it is trying to do overall while also getting the tiny hand movements right. This paper proposes a method that builds actions at multiple speeds at once—a coarse plan (what to do next step) and fine details (exactly how to move each joint)—and keeps them talking to each other so they do not contradict. Because both levels agree, the robot never wastes steps undoing its own actions, and it generalizes better to new situations.

Zero assumption version

Long-horizon tasks need two things that usually fight each other

A robot learns to do tasks by watching humans. A long task (say, reaching across a table, grasping a cup, moving it to a target) requires the robot to know both the big picture—where the arm is going, what happens next—and tiny details—exact joint angles at each instant. A naive robot that just learns action sequences token by token (predicting the next small action, then the next, then the next) forgets the global goal: it drifts, or fails when the environment is slightly different.

One fix: build actions at a coarse scale (big steps: reach, grasp, move) and then fill in fine details. But when you build coarse steps first and fine details later, the two levels often disagree. The coarse plan says move forward, but the fine details do the opposite, because the fine model did not see the coarse intent. Or the fine model contradicts itself across levels, making jerky, inefficient actions. The problem: the coarse and fine models are trained separately and never check whether they actually agree.

The method in one sentence. Train coarse and fine action generators together in one step, and make the fine generator sample actions that are mathematically consistent with the coarse generator, so both levels agree on what is happening.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encoded space

Compress the human's action sequence (joint angles over time) into a vector—a list of numbers capturing what happened.

Downsample by dropping time steps

The coarse scale sees every fourth frame; fine scale sees every frame. Both use the same encoded space, so fine detail is literally a refinement of coarse structure.

Neural model at each scale

A neural model (a network that learns patterns from data) reads the past and generates guidance (a condition) for what should happen next at that scale.

MeanFlow model distributes conditions

A lightweight generator takes both coarse and fine conditions and outputs a full action sequence, adjusted so coarse and fine agree.

Training enforces consistency

When training, the coarse condition and fine condition are checked against each other; if they conflict, the model learns to resolve it.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes how encoded conditions (compressed descriptions of what should happen) guide action generation. Instead of running coarse and fine models separately and hoping they agree, it builds a single continuous encoded space, samples at multiple time resolutions from that space, and trains a generator to respect conditions from every scale simultaneously.

the object it moves  =  condition flow across scales
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Cross-scale consistency measure

measured quantity  =  consistency score between scales

When you have two descriptions of the same sequence at different speeds—a coarse description (big steps) and a fine description (every joint angle)—the consistency score measures how well they agree. High consistency: the fine details are actually refinements of the coarse plan, not contradictions. Low consistency: the fine generator went rogue.

The paper estimates this by having the MeanFlow model output a probability distribution (how sure the model is) over actions. If the fine-scale and coarse-scale distributions overlap heavily, they are consistent; if one mode (likely outcome) contradicts the other, they are not. The paper enforces consistency by training the model to maximize this overlap.

Why it works

Why multi-scale consistency fixes long-horizon control

Long-horizon tasks fail when fine-grained control contradicts the coarse plan. A robot reaches toward a cup but the fine model does not know it is reaching for a cup, so it wastes steps rotating the wrist the wrong way. The solution: build both scales at once from a shared encoded space. Downsampling a continuous sequence automatically gives you a coarse version that the fine version refines, because they are mathematically the same vector—just sampled at different rates.

The transformer at the coarse scale generates a condition (a high-level guidance: 'move forward, grasp'). The transformer at the fine scale generates another condition (low-level guidance: 'rotate joint, extend'). The MeanFlow model then samples full action sequences, adjusted so that if you downsample the full sequence, the coarse version matches the coarse condition, and the fine version matches the fine condition. This enforces agreement: you cannot have a full sequence that is coherent at the fine scale but incoherent at the coarse scale, because the MeanFlow model simply will not output it.

Why this works: the model becomes incentivized to find action sequences that make sense at every level. A solution that contradicts itself at a different time scale is rejected, so the robot learns smooth, multi-level plans that do not fall apart when the world is slightly different.

Math after the picture

The consistency objective

First in words:

Total loss equals the generator loss (negative log likelihood of observed actions) plus a consistency weight times the disagreement between coarse-conditional and fine-conditional distributions.

Then the compact form:

Loss = -log p(actions | fine_cond) + lambda * KL(p(fine_cond) || p(coarse_cond))

Every symbol:

p(actions | fine_cond)probability of observed actions given fine-scale conditions
lambdaweight controlling how much consistency matters (tune this to balance fine detail vs coarse agreement)
KL(A || B)divergence (how different distributions A and B are; 0 means identical)
A tiny worked example
Example: at one moment, coarse condition says 'move forward' (let's score it as probability 0.8), fine condition says 'move forward' (score 0.8).
KL divergence is 0 (they agree). Consistency loss is 0.
Now coarse says 'move forward' (0.8), but fine says 'rotate wrist' (0.7 probability). They do not match.
KL divergence is nonzero. If lambda=10, this contributes +10 to the loss, penalizing the model.
During training, the model learns to avoid this penalty: it adjusts its fine transformer to say 'move forward' too, or adjusts its coarse transformer to expect wrist rotation.
Result: scales converge to agreement.

The loss forces the full action sequence to be simultaneously coherent at coarse and fine time scales, preventing contradictions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda too high: scales lock together, lose fine control. If the consistency weight (lambda) is too strong, the coarse and fine transformers converge to identical outputs. The fine scale becomes useless; you just get slow, coarse actions. The solution: tune lambda or use a schedule that starts tight and loosens to allow specialization, because initially tying the scales together prevents the fine model from overfitting to noise, then loosening lets it learn real fine details.
Lambda too low: coarse intent is ignored. If lambda is weak, the fine transformer ignores the coarse condition. The model falls back to learning actions one step at a time, and long-horizon coherence vanishes. The solution: validate on a long-horizon test set and increase lambda if needed.
Downsampling is too aggressive (e.g., every 8th frame). If the coarse scale skips too many timesteps, the coarse transformer misses rapid dynamics. A fast flick might be invisible at the coarse level, making the coarse condition useless. The solution: tune the downsampling factor; start at every 4th frame and experiment.
Done right. Careful tuning of the downsampling rate and lambda ensures both scales matter and both help the model learn, because the coarse scale stabilizes the high-level plan while the fine scale adds precision, and striking the right balance forces the model to solve both the broad-intent problem and the control-detail problem simultaneously, making it more general.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulated robot arm (six joints) tasked with reaching, grasping, and moving a cup from left to right. Train MSP on 100 human demonstrations. Test on 20 new scenarios with the cup at slightly different starting positions. Measure success (did the cup reach the target?) and action smoothness (no jerky reversals). Compare against a coarse-first baseline (coarse plan then fine details) and an end-to-end autoregressive baseline (predict one action at a time). The demo should show that MSP actions stay smooth and goal-directed even when the environment is slightly different.

Success rate = (number of cups reaching target) / (total trials); Jerkiness = average absolute acceleration difference between consecutive joint angles.

MSP should reach >80% success while keeping jerkiness below 0.15 rad/s² on unseen scenarios; baselines drop below 70% or spike jerkiness above 0.25.
