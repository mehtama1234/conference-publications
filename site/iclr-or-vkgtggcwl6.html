LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation

LLMs lose 39% accuracy in multi-turn conversations because they lock onto early assumptions and cannot recover when the conversation changes direction.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

When you talk to a chatbot, you often start by roughly describing what you want, then refine it over several back-and-forths. But LLMs handle this badly: they drop 39% in accuracy across real conversations, much worse than single one-shot attempts. This paper finds the hidden reason: LLMs guess at the goal too fast, then stubbornly stick to that guess even after you correct them.

Zero assumption version

The world before

Today, LLM benchmarks test one simple case at a time. You ask a question, the model answers, done. But real people do not talk that way — they explore, correct, and refine across multiple turns. They say 'write me a poem' and then 'make it shorter' and 'add more rhyme.' The evaluation toolbox ignores this completely.

If you try the naive approach — just testing one-turn answers — you miss a huge real-world failure: when a user's needs shift, LLMs get stuck. They picked a direction on turn 1, and they cannot unbeleive it.

The problem in one sentence. LLMs perform much worse in multi-turn conversation than single-turn, but we had no way to measure this or know why.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper does not propose a fix — it documents a failure mode. It changes how we evaluate LLMs: it adds multi-turn conversation to the benchmark suite alongside single-turn.

The hidden quantity

Unreliability: the assumption lock

measured quantity  =  assumption stickiness

The paper names and measures one hidden quantity: how much an LLM gets stuck on its early guess and resists changing direction. In plain terms: when an LLM makes an assumption in turn 1, how hard is it to convince that LLM to drop it in turn 3?

The paper measures this by running the same task with and without early 'wrong' guesses planted in the conversation, then tracking how often the LLM overcorrects or flip-flops. Aptitude (raw skill) drops only 10%, but reliability (consistency and direction-following) drops 30%+ because the LLM is fighting its own early assumption.

Why it works

Why it happens

Here is the causal chain. When an LLM reads turn 1, it uses the prompt to build a hidden mental picture — a starting guess at the task. This guess is baked into the hidden activations (internal numbers) that drive the model's output. On turn 2, new words arrive, but they must interact with those baked-in activations. The new words update the picture slightly, but the old one is sticky because it was computed with the LLM's full reasoning power applied to the original prompt.

When the user's correction is strong enough, the LLM detects the contradiction in its outputs. But instead of erasing the old picture and building a new one, the LLM tries to do both at once — it blends the old and new directions. This causes it to output something incoherent, or to randomly oscillate between the two interpretations. The LLM never truly backtracked; it just added noise on top of the old belief. To recover, the LLM would need an explicit 'reset' signal — an instruction to throw away the old picture. But users do not send such signals; they just add new information and hope the LLM figures out it has changed direction.

The measurement equation

First in words:

total accuracy drop equals aptitude loss plus unreliability loss. unreliability = baseline single-turn accuracy minus multi-turn accuracy on the same task when early wrong turns are inserted. aptitude loss = multi-turn accuracy on the true task minus the model's theoretical best if it had perfect recovery.

Then the compact form:

drop_total = loss_aptitude + loss_unreliability = (acc_single - acc_multi_true) + (acc_multi_true - acc_multi_wrong_turns)

Every symbol:

acc_singleaccuracy on the task answered in one turn
acc_multi_trueaccuracy on the task answered across turns with correct early prompt
acc_multi_wrong_turnsaccuracy when early turns contain a wrong guess that contradicts the correct answer
loss_aptitudedrop due to the task being harder across multiple turns
loss_unreliabilitydrop due to assumption stickiness and recovery failure
A tiny worked example
Suppose a task: write a haiku about joy. Baseline single-turn accuracy: 92%
Multi-turn (user says write poem, then says make it haiku): accuracy 62%
Total drop: 92 - 62 = 30 percentage points
Multi-turn with wrong early assumption (user says write a sad poem, then says make it about joy): accuracy 48%
Aptitude loss: 62 - 48 = 14 points (model got confused by multiple turns, not just wrong direction)
Unreliability loss: 92 - 62 = 30 points total, so unreliability = 30 - 14 = 16 points (the sticky assumption cost)
Real-paper finding: unreliability > aptitude across all tasks (same pattern as example)

The split reveals that LLMs do not just struggle with harder tasks; they actively fail to change their minds, making unreliability the larger problem.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overly strong early guess. If the LLM locks onto an interpretation with very high confidence early (because the first prompt was coherent but vague), later corrections become unreachable noise. The model treats turn 2 as a clarification, not a reversal.
No explicit reset mechanism. LLMs have no built-in 'erase memory' or 'restart task' instruction. Human instructions for reset ('start over', 'forget what I said') only work if the model recognizes them, which is unreliable.
Blending instead of switching. When two directions conflict, the LLM compounds them into its activations instead of choosing one. This produces outputs that are halfway between two incompatible tasks — neither right.
Done right. Future evaluations must include multi-turn conversation as a core benchmark, not an afterthought. Fixes (like special reset signals, conversation-aware training, or retrieval of earlier exchanges) should be tested against this rigorous setup.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple three-turn conversation loop. Turn 1: a vague instruction (e.g. 'write something about a journey'). Turn 2: a contradictory refinement (e.g. 'actually, make it about a friendship'). Turn 3: a verification question (e.g. 'what is the main theme?'). Have several LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Mistral) complete the loop. Record their outputs at each turn. Then measure: (1) Does the final output center on friendship, or does it confuse friendship and journey? (2) When you ask the model to summarize, does it correctly name friendship or does it list both themes? (3) If you insert turn 1.5 saying 'ignore the friendship angle, go back to journey,' does the model succeed or does it get more confused?

recovery accuracy = (times model correctly abandons old assumption after explicit correction) / (total correction attempts). coherence = (times output talks about the new topic only) / (total outputs). self-awareness = (times model admits uncertainty when confused) / (total turns).

The demo should make it impossible to miss: LLMs do not gracefully adapt to changing goals mid-conversation; they get tangled in contradictions and rarely admit when they are lost.
