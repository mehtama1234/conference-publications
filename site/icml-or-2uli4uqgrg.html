Mini-sized Contrastive Learning Pre-trained Models for Fine-grained Traffic Task

MiniTraffic learns from abundant road-level traffic data to predict lane-level traffic accurately using a tiny model by comparing similar and different time patterns and learning which roads and lanes move together.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Predicting traffic at the lane level (one direction of one road) is harder than city-level forecasts and demands computational resources that smartphones and roadside devices cannot spare. City-scale pre-trained models miss the fine details needed for lane guidance in navigation and autonomous vehicles. MiniTraffic solves this by building a lightweight model that learns from common road-level data, then fine-tunes on rare lane-specific data — achieving accurate predictions while keeping the model tiny enough to run on phones.

Zero assumption version

Lane traffic lives in the data desert, city models live on the island

Predicting traffic flow (how many cars move per minute on a road segment) at the city level is easy: you have millions of hourly snapshots, so you can build a giant model and train it thoroughly. But predicting at the lane level (one direction of one specific road) is different: each lane collects only a few years of data, and you need predictions that respect the unique rhythm of that particular stretch.

A naive approach: use a pre-trained city-level model and fine-tune it on lane data. This often fails because the city model learned urban-scale patterns (rush hour, day/night cycles) but missed lane-specific quirks (this northbound lane has a bottleneck at mile 2.3, so it backs up differently than adjacent lanes). Also, city models are enormous — they need GPUs to run, making them impractical for real-time guidance on phones or small roadside computers.

The core tension: you need deep learning (to capture nonlinear time patterns), but lane-level data is scarce and you cannot afford a giant model.

In one sentence. Build a tiny model that learns shared patterns from abundant road-level data, then specializes to individual lanes using contrastive clustering to capture which roads and lanes move together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Augment road time series in frequency domain

Split each road's traffic signal into slow (daily rhythm, weather) and fast (minute-to-minute bumps) components, randomly shift and remix them separately to create stable augmentations.

Create pairs of similar and dissimilar examples

For contrastive training: augment the same road traffic twice (similar pair), and pair roads that move together vs. unrelated roads (dissimilar).

Train a tiny encoder to recognize similarities

Push paired similar examples close in the model's internal representation (the embedding space, a compressed summary), pull dissimilar ones apart.

Cluster to find road-lane graph structure

After training, group roads and lanes by similarity; build a small graph connecting roads that co-move and lanes that align with them.

Fine-tune on target lane with few examples

Use the pre-learned graph structure and encoder as a starting point; train on your specific lane data until predictions match observed flows.

What the paper changes

The one thing that shifts

The paper changes the model size and its learned structure. Rather than scaling a generic deep network and training it from scratch on scarce lane data, MiniTraffic builds a small graph that encodes which roads and lanes correlate, then freezes that structure and fine-tunes only the edge weights (the strengths of the connections).

the object it moves  =  model structure and size
The hidden quantity

Correlation structure: which roads and lanes move together

measured quantity  =  road-lane co-movement correlation

The hidden quantity is the correlation structure — which pairs of roads exhibit synchronized traffic patterns, and which roads best predict which lanes. Normally this structure is invisible; the paper makes it explicit by learning a graph where edges connect correlated road and lane pairs.

The paper estimates this by training a contrastive model: pairs of augmented versions of the same road are pulled together in the embedding space (a compressed internal summary where similar time-series sit nearby), and augmented versions of unrelated roads are pushed apart. The learned embeddings reveal which roads are close (correlated) and which are distant (independent). The paper then clusters these embeddings to recover the graph structure: nodes are roads and lanes, edges exist between correlated pairs.

Why it works

Why: data abundance in roads lifts lane prediction

The paper works because of a simple fact: roads and their lanes move together. A road's total traffic and a specific lane's traffic on that road are correlated by physics (same drivers, same incidents, same weather). City-scale road data is plentiful; lane data is sparse. So learn from roads first.

Contrastive pre-training discovers road correlations: if two roads' traffic patterns are similar (both get congested at rush hour, both clear after incidents), the model learns to place their embeddings close. Then, when you fine-tune on a new lane, the model already knows which roads to attend to — the ones whose pre-learned embeddings are close to the lane's emerging embedding. This structure transfer (the graph connections learned from roads) lets a tiny model generalize from a handful of lane examples, because it has already absorbed the shared backbone from thousands of roads. Without pre-training, a small model has no structure to transfer and drowns in lane-data scarcity.

Frequency-domain augmentation (splitting signal into slow and fast) reinforces this by ensuring the model learns stable correlations (the ones that persist across noise). Roads that co-move in the slow component (day/night, weather, seasonal) are truly related; roads that only correlate in the noisy fast component are learning spurious patterns. By augmenting separately, the contrastive loss steers the model toward reliable co-movements.

Math after the picture

Contrastive clustering objective

First in words:

The training loss penalizes if embeddings of similar road pairs stay far apart and if embeddings of dissimilar pairs stay close. Formally: loss equals the average over all pairs of: (distance between similar pair) plus (penalty when dissimilar pairs are closer than similar pairs).

Then the compact form:

L = (1 / N_pairs) * sum_pairs [ D(emb_pos, emb_pos) + max(0, margin - D(emb_neg, emb_pos)) ]

Every symbol:

Ltotal contrastive loss, lower is better
N_pairscount of positive and negative example pairs
D(a, b)distance between embedding vectors a and b, e.g. Euclidean distance
emb_posembedding of a positively paired (similar) example
emb_negembedding of a negatively paired (dissimilar) example
marginminimum separation enforced between dissimilar pairs, e.g. 1.0
A tiny worked example
Example: Road A and Road B are correlated (positive pair), Road C is unrelated (negative pair).
Distance from Road A embedding to Road B embedding: D_pos = 0.2 (close, good).
Distance from Road A embedding to Road C embedding: D_neg = 0.8 (far, good).
Loss for this triplet: L = 0.2 + max(0, 1.0 - 0.8) = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4.
If Road C were closer (D_neg = 0.6), loss would be 0.2 + max(0, 1.0 - 0.6) = 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.6 (worse).
Training nudges embeddings to keep positive pairs close and negative pairs separated by at least the margin.

This loss drives the encoder to place correlated roads near each other in embedding space, encoding the co-movement structure that fine-tuning will exploit.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lanes that are genuinely different from any road pattern. If a lane has local quirks (a new bridge, construction, a unique traffic light pattern) unrelated to any pre-trained road, the model has no learned structure to transfer and will still require more data to specialize.
Seasonal shifts or regime changes. If a road's traffic pattern changes fundamentally (new highway exit opens, major employer relocates), the pre-trained correlations become stale. Fine-tuning will help adjust the graph weights, but the model may overly trust the old structure because it is constrained by the pre-learned graph edges, which can no longer reflect reality.
Poor choice of margin in contrastive loss. If the margin is too small, dissimilar roads cluster together (model learns nothing). If it is too large, the loss becomes too strict and the encoder wastes effort separating roads that are only weakly correlated.
Done right: regularly retrain on recent road data. Contrastive pre-training is fast (abundant road data, small model), so re-running every quarter keeps the learned correlations fresh as the city evolves.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that a tiny model pre-trained with contrastive learning on road data outperforms a full-size model trained only on a small lane dataset, because the pre-trained structure already knows which roads to attend to. Use a real traffic dataset with several roads and one or two target lanes. Split lane data into: a few weeks (fine-tuning set) and several weeks held-out (test set). Train a baseline large model on the fine-tuning set from scratch; it must learn correlations from scratch and so needs more lane examples to avoid overfitting. Train MiniTraffic: (1) pre-train a small encoder on all road data with contrastive loss, (2) cluster to recover the graph, (3) fine-tune on the same few weeks of lane data, (4) evaluate both models on the held-out lane data.

improvement = (lane_accuracy_MiniTraffic - lane_accuracy_baseline) / lane_accuracy_baseline

The demo must show that even with 5x fewer numbers to tune, MiniTraffic matches or exceeds the baseline's lane-level accuracy because the pre-trained correlation structure lets it learn from scarce lane data.
