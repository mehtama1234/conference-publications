ATLAS: Adaptive Transfer Scaling Laws for Multilingual Models

ATLAS predicts how model size and training data must grow when you add languages without losing performance on any language.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Today's biggest AI models serve billions of people speaking different languages. But research on scaling laws (the rules for how much bigger a model needs to be when you add more data) has looked almost entirely at English. This paper studies the real scaling laws for multilingual models—by running 774 experiments across 10 million to 8 billion parameters and 400 languages. The new law, ATLAS, reveals something counterintuitive: adding a new language actually makes the model worse at all languages unless you also grow its size. The paper gives you a formula to calculate exactly how much bigger the model needs to be.

Zero assumption version

Scaling laws only worked for English, not the real world

Scaling laws are recipes: they predict how much better a model gets when you give it more data or make it bigger, because both more data and bigger models let the model learn from more examples and store more patterns. Researchers have worked hard to find these recipes, but almost entirely for English. Meanwhile, every real AI system also serves people in Hindi, Spanish, Mandarin, and hundreds of other languages.

A beginner might think: if the scaling law for English says doubling the data makes accuracy better (because the model sees twice as many examples and can learn more patterns), then training a multilingual model with the same total data should work fine—just split the data among languages. It does not. When you add a second language to a model, something called the curse of multilinguality kicks in: the model gets worse at both languages, even with the same total computation, because the model capacity (the model's size) is now divided and each language has fewer weights to learn its patterns. No one had measured exactly how much worse or how much bigger the model needs to grow to stay even.

The problem in one sentence. We have no formula to tell us: when adding a new language, how much must the model grow to keep performance on all languages from dropping?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Run 774 experiments

Train models of different sizes and language mixtures to collect scaling data.

Measure the compute tax

For each new language added, calculate how much extra compute is needed to keep the same accuracy.

Fit a scaling law

Find a formula that predicts loss (error rate) as a function of model size, data volume, and number of languages.

Calculate transfer scores

Measure how much each language pair helps or hurts each other when trained together, because this shows which languages compete for the same model capacity and which share patterns.

Give practitioners a rule

A formula that answers: if I want to add K new languages, how much must I grow my model?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the model or training algorithm. Instead, it changes the scaling law itself—the formula we use to predict how loss behaves when you change three things at once: model size (N), training data volume (D), and the number of languages (K). Previous scaling laws only modeled N and D separately and only for English.

The hidden quantity

The compute efficiency tax

measured quantity  =  Compute Efficiency Tax

The hidden quantity is the compute efficiency tax: a number that tells you how much harder it is to train a multilingual model versus a monolingual one. When you have K languages, the tax is a multiplier—something like 1.23—meaning you need 23% more computation to keep the same accuracy compared to training on just one language.

The paper measures this tax by running pairs of experiments side by side: a monolingual model and a multilingual model, both trained with the same total compute budget, and comparing their final accuracy. The ratio of their accuracy curves gives the tax. Then the paper derives a formula that predicts this tax based on K, model size N, and data amount D.

Why it works

Why the formula works

The mechanism is capacity bottleneck and saturation. A neural network model (a list of numbers called weights) has a fixed capacity—how much information it can store. When you train on one language, all that capacity is used to store the patterns of that language. When you train on two languages, the same capacity is now split: part stores English patterns, part stores Hindi patterns. Each language gets fewer weights to work with, so both languages suffer.

But if you make the model bigger (more weights), you have room for more languages without each one shrinking its allocation. The paper's key insight is that this relationship is smooth and predictable: the loss curve for K languages follows the same power-law shape as the monolingual curve, just shifted upward by a constant factor that depends on K. That shift factor is the tax. By measuring the shift across many experiments, the paper can predict it for new language counts.

There is also a second effect: data saturation. Every language has a finite amount of unique text (the individual sentences available for training). When you train past that point, you are repeating the data, which hurts learning because the model is seeing the same patterns over and over and cannot extract new information. The paper measures this too and bakes it into the formula. A language like English with billions of text units saturates slowly because there is so much public data; a language like Swahili with fewer public texts saturates faster because the supply runs out. The formula accounts for both effects.

Math after the picture

The scaling law equation

First in words:

Loss for K languages equals A times K to the power phi times N to the power negative alpha, plus B times K to the power psi times D to the power negative beta, plus E.

Then the compact form:

L(K, N, D) = A·K^ϕ·N^−α + B·K^ψ·D^−β + E

Every symbol:

L(K, N, D)loss (error rate) for K languages, model size N, data amount D
Knumber of training languages
Nmodel size (number of weights)
Dtotal training data (number of tokens)
A, Blearned coefficients (fitted from experiments)
α, βscaling exponents (how fast loss improves with N and D)
ϕ, ψmultilinguality exponents (how much language count hurts loss)
Eirreducible loss (the entropy of natural text, unchangeable)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the exponent for model-size scaling is 0.07, for data scaling is 0.21, for language count in term 1 is 0.18, for language count in term 2 is 0.1, coefficients A is 0.5 and B is 0.3, irreducible loss E is 1.5.
For 1 language, 1 billion parameters, 100 billion text units:
Term 1: 0.5 times 1 to power 0.18 times (1 billion) to power minus 0.07 = 0.5 times 1 times 0.4 = 0.2
Term 2: 0.3 times 1 to power 0.1 times (100 billion) to power minus 0.21 = 0.3 times 1 times 0.25 = 0.075
Loss = 0.2 + 0.075 + 1.5 = 1.775
For 3 languages, same model size and data:
Term 1: 0.5 times 3 to power 0.18 times 0.4 = 0.5 times 1.21 times 0.4 = 0.242 (language tax: 21 percent loss)
Term 2: 0.3 times 3 to power 0.1 times 0.25 = 0.3 times 1.12 times 0.25 = 0.084 (small effect)
Loss = 0.242 + 0.084 + 1.5 = 1.826 (51 points worse, the curse of multilinguality)
To recover: grow to 1.5 billion parameters. Term 1 shrinks, loss approaches 1.78, near original.

Each language contributes a loss term scaled by K^ϕ and K^ψ; the exponents ϕ and ψ (typically 0.1-0.2) quantify how much language count hurts accuracy, and the formula tells you exactly how much model size you must trade to offset that hurt.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The formula needs enough data. If you train past the point where data repeats (saturation), the formula breaks because the power-law assumptions no longer hold. The paper bakes in a saturation correction, but it only works when you have enough unique text per language.
Unequal language pairs behave differently. The formula assumes all languages are equally resourced. In reality, English has billions of text units available; Swahili has millions. The tax formula captures average behavior but may miss the worst case for low-resource languages.
Pretrain-versus-finetune decisions depend on budget. The paper gives a crossover point: if you have under 144 billion text units of compute, finetune a multilingual base model; if you have over 283 billion text units, pretrain from scratch. Between those points, it is ambiguous and depends on exact hyperparameters.
Done right. Use the formula to estimate how much compute you need, run a small-scale validation experiment (a few sizes and language counts), and calibrate the formula's coefficients to your domain. The power-law exponents (how fast loss improves with size and data) are usually stable across different settings because the underlying mechanism—how much capacity is freed by adding weights, and how much information each new text example supplies—is universal across languages and domains.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should train three small models: (1) English only at a fixed size and data volume, measuring loss; (2) English plus one other language at the same size and volume, measuring how much loss rises (the curse); (3) English plus the other language, but with the model size scaled up by the tax formula's prediction, confirming that loss returns to near the baseline. The three runs should be small enough to finish on a laptop in an hour, using a standard language model architecture (GPT-2 sized or smaller) and two real languages with different data scales (e.g. English and Hindi).

Multilinguality Tax Ratio = (Loss with 2 langs, original size) / (Loss with 2 langs, scaled size); should be close to (Loss with 1 lang) / (Loss with 2 langs, original size)

When you grow the model according to ATLAS, the curse of multilinguality disappears—both languages stay at their original accuracy even after combining them.
