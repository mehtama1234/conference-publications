LIMSSR: LLM-Driven Sequence Reasoning for Incomplete Multimodal Data

When some video, audio, or text is missing during training, use an LLM to reason about the missing pieces instead of trying to recreate them.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Real-world data is messy: sometimes you have a video's audio and text but the video frames are lost, or you have frames and text but audio vanished. Most methods assume all pieces exist during training so they can learn from complete examples. This paper tackles the harder case: when pieces are missing from your training data itself, with no way to patch the holes. Instead of trying to fill in missing frames or audio, LIMSSR asks: what would an LLM infer is happening from what we do have? It treats the missing data as a question to answer, not a gap to patch.

Zero assumption version

Training with incomplete, broken data

In the real world, videos are missing audio. Sensor data is missing channels. Images have corrupted frames. Most learning methods assume during training you have complete examples: a full video with all its channels, so you can learn from the full picture. But if your training data was collected with broken sensors or privacy rules that strip certain data, you never get to see the complete version—not even once during training.

The naive approach: train on whatever pieces you have and hope the model fills in the gaps on its own. This fails because the model never saw a complete example to learn from. The alternative: use a separate complete dataset to teach the model what the missing pieces look like, then transfer that knowledge. But this adds cost and complexity, and the transfer often leaks information from that separate dataset instead of learning the task.

The method in one sentence. Instead of reconstructing missing data, feed what you have into an LLM as a prompt, and let it reason about what the missing pieces likely are based on their semantic meaning in context.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how missing data is handled during training. Standard methods try to reconstruct pixels or learn to ignore the gap. LIMSSR instead uses LLM reasoning to fill missing modalities at the semantic level, without pixel-level generation.

the object it moves  =  Missing modality handling during training
The hidden quantity

The semantic reasoning quality

measured quantity  =  How much to trust the LLM's guess about missing data

When the LLM reasons about a missing piece of data, it produces a semantic description—not a guess at pixel values, but a statement of what's happening in that modality. The quality of this reasoning determines whether it helps learning or misleads it, because better reasoning produces more reliable semantic meaning that the model can actually learn from. The paper calls this the inferred semantic content: how well the LLM captured what the missing modality would have conveyed if it existed.

The paper measures this indirectly by tracking which examples the model learns to weight as reliable (through a gating network) versus which it treats as uncertain. High quality LLM reasoning gets high confidence because the semantic meaning it infers aligns with what the visible data shows, giving the model a consistent story to learn from; when LLM reasoning hallucinates or contradicts the visible data, the model's confidence drops because the story becomes incoherent.

Plain reading. When a video of someone doing gymnastics is missing its audio track, an LLM can reason: this action demands balance, speed, and grace. That semantic understanding helps the model predict scores more accurately because the model now has a coherent story about what the missing audio would convey—it understands the action better, just like a judge watching video alone (no sound) but told 'this is a balance-heavy move' would score it more confidently than a judge seeing only the video with a blank audio channel.
Paper reading. The paper computes a learnable adaptive gating network that assigns a confidence weight to each imputed modality representation, allowing the model to learn whether to trust the LLM reasoning or rely on other present modalities. This is the Mask-Aware Dual-Path Aggregation: one path for present data (trusted fully), one path for imputed data (weighted by learned confidence).
Why it works

Why it works: reasoning instead of generation

Existing methods try to regenerate missing data (reconstruct audio waveforms or video frames from scratch). This fails when training data is incomplete because there is no ground-truth reconstruction to learn from. LIMSSR avoids this trap by asking: instead of generating pixels, what meaning is the LLM confident about? Because LLMs are trained on vast text, they already know that skateboarding tricks demand balance and momentum, that ballroom dance requires rhythm and partnership, that gymnastics routines have difficulty scores tied to specific moves. When you tell an LLM 'the video shows a skateboard trick, the judges saw this, but the audio is missing,' the LLM can reason semantically: what does that video most likely sound like? Not the actual waveform, but the semantic idea. This reasoning is grounded in the available evidence (present modalities), so it does not hallucinate as wildly as a blind reconstruction would.

The model then learns to weight these LLM inferences: if the LLM reasoning contradicts the visible modalities (e.g., claims the trick is easy but the video clearly shows a difficult move), the learned gating weight drops and the model ignores the bad imputation because the inconsistency signals the LLM is hallucinating. If the LLM reasoning aligns with the visible modalities and provides semantic context that tells a coherent story ('the video shows fast footwork and the audio likely has quick beats'), the weight stays high and the imputed data helps the model learn more reliably, because the model now has a unified understanding from multiple angles that all point the same way. This dynamic confidence assignment is key: it lets the model learn when to trust reasoning and when to reject hallucinations.

Math after the picture

The core equation: weighted fusion

First in words:

Score equals a weighted sum of role-specific semantic tokens, where each token captures one aspect of the action and the weights learn which aspects matter most.

Then the compact form:

z_main = sum_k w_role_k * t_k where t_k = [present_feats_k fused_with imputed_reasoning_k with gating weight lambda_m]

Every symbol:

z_mainthe fused semantic vector that predicts the action score
w_role_klearned importance weight for semantic role k (e.g., difficulty, execution, artistry)
t_ksemantic token k, combining present and imputed modality information
present_feats_kfeatures extracted from modalities that actually exist in the training example
imputed_reasoning_kdense representation of what the LLM reasoned about the missing modality
lambda_mlearned confidence gate: how much to trust the imputation for modality m (high when reasoning is reliable, low when it hallucinates)
gating weight1.0 for present modalities, lambda_m for imputed modalities
A tiny worked example
Example: action is a gymnastics routine. Video is present, audio is missing.
Extracted features: video shows front flip, precise hand position, smooth landing → present_feats = [0.9, 0.8, 0.7].
LLM reasoning: 'Audio of a flip landing is likely crisp, clear impact sound, no verbal cues; semantic importance of audio for difficulty judgment is high' → imputed_reasoning = [0.75, 0.8, 0.6].
Gating for audio: lambda_audio = 0.85 (model learned to trust LLM on audio in this task).
Fused token t_1 (difficulty aspect): 0.9 * 1.0 (video present, full weight) + 0.75 * 0.85 (audio imputed, gated) = 0.9 + 0.6375 = 1.5375.
Role weight w_1 (how much difficulty matters) = 0.6 learned from task.
Contribution to z_main: 0.6 * 1.5375 = 0.92. Repeat for other roles and sum.

The gating weight lambda_m is learned during training: if imputed audio contradicts visible video, the model pushes lambda_audio down, reducing its contribution.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

LLM hallucination on the missing modality. If the LLM is asked to reason about missing audio and invents sounds that do not match the visible video (e.g., claims loud applause when the video shows a solo private performance), the imputed representation is wrong. The gating network can learn to downweight this, but only after it sees several examples of the same modality being wrong.
Ambiguous prompt leads to inconsistent reasoning. If the prompt describing available modalities is vague ('the person is exercising'), the LLM may produce different semantic descriptions on the same missing modality across different examples, giving inconsistent imputed representations. The model then cannot learn a stable mapping.
Gating network becomes overconfident. If the LLM reasoning is universally poor for a specific modality, the gating network may still learn high confidence initially (before seeing enough examples to correct itself), misleading the model.
Done right: multiple modalities, learned weighting. When the LLM reasoning is grounded (describes available modalities accurately before inferring the missing one), and the gating network learns from examples showing when reasoning is helpful versus when it contradicts visible data, LIMSSR recovers most of the performance lost by missing modalities.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of three action videos with two modalities each (e.g., frames + audio, or frames + text). Hold out a different modality pair as the 'missing' case during training. Train LIMSSR with LLM reasoning. Train a baseline that tries to reconstruct the missing modality. Measure how close each gets to the ground truth quality scores when one modality is removed during training.

forgetting_rate = (baseline_accuracy_with_complete_data - baseline_accuracy_with_missing_modality) / baseline_accuracy_with_complete_data

If LIMSSR keeps accuracy above 80 percent of the complete-data performance while the baseline drops to 50 percent, the demo proves that LLM reasoning recovers missing modality information better than reconstruction because semantic reasoning is more stable than trying to guess pixels.
