Causal Structure in Latent Reasoning Steps

When an AI model thinks through problems with hidden steps instead of writing them out, some steps matter for the answer and some do not—this paper finds which ones and why.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models can solve hard problems by working through them step by step. Usually we see those steps written out (like showing arithmetic work). But newer models think through problems using hidden internal steps, like a thought happening inside your head that you never write down. The problem: we cannot tell if those hidden steps are actually helping, or just extra noise. This paper treats those hidden steps as a causal chain—a set of events where earlier events push later ones forward—and uses experiments to find out which steps really matter.

Zero assumption version

The invisible reasoning trap

When a model solves a math problem or answers a logic puzzle, we usually ask it to show its work—to write down each step. That is easy to check. But newer models are designed to do reasoning in the background, using hidden numbers instead of words. These hidden steps are like thoughts you have before you say anything out loud.

The trap: we cannot see if those hidden steps are real reasoning or just noise. If you ask the model how it got an answer, you get nothing—it is all locked inside. An engineer might guess: maybe the model needs 20 hidden steps to think, or maybe 5 would work just as well. Without a way to poke at the hidden steps and see what they do, you are flying blind.

In one sentence. This paper uses experiments where you pause the model mid-thought, change what it knows at that exact point, and watch what changes in the final answer—revealing which hidden steps actually steer the thinking.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper targets the hidden intermediate states inside models that solve problems using hidden internal thinking steps instead of writing them out. Instead of treating those states as opaque blobs, the paper creates a detailed causal map: which states feed into which other states, and which ones actually control the final answer.

the object it moves  =  hidden reasoning states and their causal links
The hidden quantity

The causal necessity score: which steps steer the answer

measured quantity  =  causal necessity: how much a step's hidden state controls the answer

The hidden quantity is the causal necessity score for each reasoning step. It answers: if you erase or flip this step's internal numbers, does the final answer change? A high score means the answer depends strongly on that step. A low score means the model can ignore that step and still get the right answer.

The paper estimates it by doing step-wise interventions—artificially modifying each hidden state one at a time and recording whether the answer stays correct or flips wrong. A step is causally necessary if changing it breaks correctness.

Plain reading. Imagine two students solving an algebra problem in their heads. After step 3, one student accidentally forgets a number. If they still get the right final answer, that number was not necessary. If they get it wrong, that number was essential. The causal necessity score is how often forgetting each step breaks the answer.
Paper reading. The paper models hidden internal steps as variables in a structural causal model and computes their causal effect on the output through do-interventions (a formal method in causal inference: an experiment where you artificially set a variable to see what happens). For each hidden step state, the paper performs counterfactual analysis: what happens to correctness when that state is perturbed, nullified, or re-encoded?
Why it works

Why it works: causal intervention as diagnosis

A hidden reasoning step could be important in two ways. First, it could be one of a long chain of steps where each one depends on the previous one, so breaking any link breaks the chain (like how one missing domino stops the whole fall). Second, it could be a shortcut that the model learned to take, so skipping it does not matter because the model already found the answer by another path.

By stepping in and artificially changing each hidden state, the paper breaks this ambiguity. If a step is truly necessary for the answer, the intervention will make the model fail. If the step is redundant or detoured around, the intervention will not hurt. This reveals the real dependency structure, not just the steps that happen to occur.

Math after the picture

Causal effect through step-wise intervention

First in words:

Causal effect of step i equals: probability answer is correct when step i is unmodified minus probability answer is correct when step i is modified

Then the compact form:

causal_effect_i = P(correct | step_i) - P(correct | do(step_i := perturbed))

Every symbol:

causal_effect_ihow much step i controls correctness
P(correct | step_i)probability of right answer when step i is left alone
P(correct | do(step_i := perturbed))probability of right answer when step i is artificially changed
perturbedmodified: nullified, flipped, or re-encoded
A tiny worked example
Problem: 5 + 3 equals what? Model uses 3 hidden steps.
Baseline (all steps normal): model outputs 8. Correct.
Intervention on step 1: erase step 1 hidden state, run steps 2-3. Model outputs 8. Still correct.
Causal effect of step 1: P(correct intact) - P(correct perturbed) = 1.0 - 1.0 = 0. Step 1 is not necessary.
Intervention on step 2: erase step 2 hidden state, run steps 1 and 3. Model outputs 5. Wrong.
Causal effect of step 2: P(correct intact) - P(correct perturbed) = 1.0 - 0.0 = 1.0. Step 2 is causally necessary.
Conclusion: the model needs step 2 to combine the numbers, but step 1 is just redundant bookkeeping.

The causal effect directly measures: if I break this step, does the answer break? That reveals which steps the model truly relies on.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Intervention method does not match how the model actually works. If you change a hidden state in an unnatural way (e.g. by erasing it completely instead of nudging it slightly), the model might behave strangely and the intervention does not tell you what would happen in real reasoning. The paper must be careful about how it perturbs each hidden state to keep the intervention realistic.
Hidden state entanglement. Multiple hidden steps might be packed so tightly together in the model's numbers that changing one automatically changes the others. Then the intervention does not isolate the causal effect of a single step—you are accidentally intervening on several at once. This is especially risky in deeply entangled neural networks.
The model learns to reroute around broken steps. If a hidden step is trained to be non-necessary, the model might learn to do the job through a different hidden step. So when you intervene on the first step, the model falls back to the second. The causal effect looks small, but that does not mean the original step was useless—it means the model is flexible and overbuilt.
Done right. The paper runs interventions on many different types of problems (math and logic puzzles) and compares models with hidden reasoning to models that write out their thinking step-by-step so that the causal map holds across multiple domains, not just one narrow case.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A convincing demo would take a latent chain-of-thought model trained to solve simple arithmetic (e.g., two-digit addition) and run the intervention experiment on every step. Solve 100 test problems with all steps intact, recording accuracy. Then for each step, zero out that step's hidden state and re-run on the same 100 problems, recording how many answers break. Plot the causal effect for each step as a bar chart.

causal_necessity_per_step = (accuracy_all_steps_intact - accuracy_with_step_i_zeroed) for each i

The chart should show a clear divide: a few steps (the tall bars) are causally necessary, most steps (the short bars) could be deleted without hurting the answer. That split proves the model is not equally dependent on all hidden steps, and reveals where to trim.
