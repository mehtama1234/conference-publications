CoMAS: Co-Evolving Multi-Agent Systems via Interaction Rewards

LLM agents improve themselves by learning from how well they work together, measured by scoring each other's contributions.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When you have multiple AI assistants working on a task together, they can teach each other to get better because each one sees the other's reaction in real time, which reveals what works and what does not. This paper shows how to measure the quality of their teamwork automatically (by having a third LLM score each turn) and use that signal to improve each agent's strategy, all without anyone writing reward rules by hand.

Zero assumption version

Agents learn the old way: from labels or dense scores

Today's AI assistants (large language models, or LLMs) are trained once and then frozen. A naive next step is to have them keep learning after deployment: improve on new tasks because each one must adapt to different real-world uses, get better at your specific job because the general model has not seen your exact setup, and adapt over time because the world changes.

Current methods either require a human to label every output as good or bad (dense external feedback), or they try to invent a reward signal by asking the LLM itself to score its own work. But humans do not actually learn this way: you learn by talking with others, seeing where they disagree with you, and refining your approach through that back-and-forth.

The paper's one-sentence method: Multiple agents discuss a problem together, score each other's contributions during the conversation (because each agent's words either helped or hindered progress, and a judge can spot that), and use those scores to improve their strategies without external labels (because reinforcement learning lets them learn from any reward signal, not just human approval).

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Setup the agents

Create two or more LLM agents, each with their own learned strategy for how to respond in a conversation.

Run a conversation

Agents take turns proposing ideas or critiques on a shared problem; each response is a concrete utterance.

Judge the interaction

After each turn, another LLM (the judge) reads the full conversation so far and scores how well that turn contributed to progress.

Assign rewards

Each agent's own move gets a score; that score is the intrinsic reward signal for that agent's action.

Improve the strategy

Each agent uses standard reinforcement learning (a method for rewarding good decisions) to nudge its weights so higher-scoring moves become more likely.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: internal agent weights driven by interaction scores

Every LLM is a collection of numbers (weights) that control what the model says next. This paper tunes those weights to maximize the score the agent receives during teamwork, instead of trying to score itself or waiting for external labels.

the object it moves  =  Agent weights via interaction-based rewards
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: contribution quality, measured live during conversation

measured quantity  =  Interaction score (quality of each agent's turn)

The paper needs a way to know whether an agent's move was good or bad in the moment, without a human saying so. It invents a measurement: after each agent speaks, a judge LLM reads the conversation and assigns a score to that turn—a number from, say, 0 to 10 indicating how much that move helped the team make progress.

This score is invisible in normal conversation but central to learning: the agent collects its scores over many conversations, and reinforcement learning uses them to steer future behavior. High-scoring moves (crisp clarifications, sharp critiques, unexpected suggestions) become more probable; low-scoring moves (repetition, irrelevant tangents) fade.

Why it works

Why it works: learning from live feedback on collaborative behavior

The mechanism has three steps. First, the conversation itself creates a natural signal: when two agents interact, one agent's move either helps or hinders the other because each utterance either adds clarity or adds noise. A clarifying question unblocks progress because the other agent can now act on solid footing; a contradictory statement causes confusion because the other agent must now reconcile conflicting instructions. This ground truth is observable in the conversation's trajectory because you can see whether the agents move forward together or loop back to explain things.

Second, the judge scores each move, grounding that observation as a number. Because the judge has read the full context of the conversation, it can recognize what actually moved things forward versus what wasted time.

Third, reinforcement learning works by rewarding actions that raise a score, which means the algorithm nudges the weights in whichever direction makes good actions more likely next time. Here, an agent that receives high scores for speaking clearly and building on others' ideas will gradually shift its internal weights to emit similar utterances in similar situations because each reinforcement step adjusts the weights slightly toward the pattern. Over many conversations, those high-scoring behaviors crystallize into the agent's strategy because the weights converge to a local peak where that style is most rewarded. The process mirrors how humans improve: you notice in conversation what lands well and what flops because you see the reaction, and you unconsciously shift your approach because your brain reinforces the moves that worked.

Math after the picture

Equation: updating weights by rewarding good turns

First in words:

Update each weight in a direction that increases the probability of high-scoring actions; the strength of the update grows with the score; penalize each update slightly to avoid wild changes.

Then the compact form:

grad_theta log(pi(a_t | s_t)) * R_t - beta * KL(pi_new || pi_old)

Every symbol:

grad_theta log(pi(a_t | s_t))which way to nudge the weight so the chosen action becomes more likely
a_tthe actual thing the agent said at turn t
s_tthe conversation state (everything said so far)
R_tthe judge's score for that turn (the interaction reward)
beta * KL(pi_new || pi_old)a penalty that prevents the updated strategy from drifting too far from the original
A tiny worked example
Suppose Agent A proposes an idea that the judge scores 8 out of 10.
The base update is: (nudge direction to make that utterance more likely) * 8.
The KL penalty is: 0.1 * (distance between the new and old strategy) to keep the strategy stable.
Net effect: Agent A's weight shifts to favor similar moves, but only by a reasonable margin (not a wild swing).
Suppose Agent A says something harmful, scored 2 out of 10. The update is (nudge) * 2, nearly flat—the weights barely move, so harmful moves stay rare.

The interaction reward from the judge is the key difference: it comes from the judge, not from the agent itself or an external labeler, and it scores the whole turn in context.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Judge gaming. If the judge uses a simple heuristic (e.g., rewards the longest responses), agents will learn to ramble instead of being concise. Misaligned scoring opens a loop where agents optimize the wrong thing.
Collapse to one style. If all agents converge to the same strategy (e.g., all adopt the same aggressive tone), they stop challenging each other and the feedback loop stalls. Diversity of agents is a hidden requirement.
Reward delay. The judge must score immediately or the connection between the agent's move and the score blurs. If scoring happens only at the end of a long conversation, the agent will not learn which specific turns were good.
Scalability risk (done right). The paper tests scaling to more agents and finds it works, but each additional agent means more compute for the judge. The overhead is linear, not exponential, so scalability is plausible if the judge is efficient.
Human alignment. The judge is an LLM, not a human. If what the judge considers good differs from what humans actually value, agents will learn the wrong thing because reinforcement learning optimizes for the reward signal it receives, whatever that signal measures. Regular audits of judge scores against human feedback help catch drift because you can spot when the judge's scores diverge from human judgment and retrain the judge if needed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple two-agent system on a concrete problem: for example, agents collaborate to write a plan or solve a logic puzzle. Each agent proposes a step, the other comments, they refine. A judge LLM scores each contribution. Run 100 rounds of conversation, collecting scores. Train both agents' weights using standard RL to maximize interaction rewards. Measure: (1) average judge score per turn over the 100 rounds; (2) task success rate (did they arrive at the right answer?) before and after learning; (3) the number of turns to convergence.

Learning success = (task_accuracy_after_learning - task_accuracy_before) + 0.1 * (avg_judge_score_after - avg_judge_score_before)

The demo must show that both agents improve their scores over time because reinforcement learning is steering their weights toward high-scoring behavior, and that tasks are solved faster and more accurately after learning because better collaboration strategies mean fewer false starts and dead ends.
