Generative Representation Learning on Hyper-relational Knowledge Graphs via Masked Discrete Diffusion

KREPE generates missing facts in knowledge graphs by treating each fact as a discrete diffusion problem, learning what hidden entities and relationships should be based on incomplete clues.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

A knowledge graph stores facts about the world as triples: (person, works-at, company). Most link-prediction methods assume two parts of the triple are known and only guess the third. But in the real world, you might know only the person, or have a fact with multiple blanks to fill, or need to generate entirely new facts. KREPE tackles this harder problem by learning to generate all missing pieces of a fact at once, using a method called masked discrete diffusion.

Zero assumption version

Why link prediction is too narrow

A knowledge graph is a database of facts, each written as a triple: (subject, relation, object). For example, (Alice, works-at, Google), (Gold, atomic-number, 79). The standard task is link prediction: given two parts of the triple, guess the third. So if you know Alice and works-at, you predict Google.

But real-world facts often have multiple missing pieces. You might see (?, works-at, Google) and need to generate all matching employees. Or (Alice, ?, Google) to infer the relationship. Or (?, ?, ?) to generate facts from scratch. The old methods break because they were built assuming only one blank.

Hyper-relational knowledge graphs are even more complex: each fact can have additional context attributes (time, location, confidence) attached to it. Filling in just one missing piece while ignoring the rest leaves incomplete, unrealistic facts.

The gap. Generate valid facts with multiple or all components missing simultaneously, respecting the graph's global structure and relationships.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

KREPE changes what the model generates. Instead of outputting one missing entity or relation, it outputs complete, multi-component facts even when multiple pieces are unknown. It learns a generative distribution (a probability model) over all valid fact components conditioned on the known pieces and the entire graph structure.

the object it moves  =  fact generation with multiple unknowns
The hidden quantity

The missing-component distribution

measured quantity  =  conditional probability of each unknown piece

KREPE learns a probability distribution: given some known parts of a fact and the global graph, what is the probability of each possible entity or relation for each unknown slot? For example, if we know (Alice, works-at, ?) the model learns the probability that Google, Facebook, Amazon, etc. fill the blank, incorporating the fact that Alice likely works at companies that have hired other people in the graph.

The paper estimates this distribution using a technique called discrete diffusion: it runs an iterative refinement process that gradually moves from random guesses toward real, valid fact components. At each step, the refinement uses contextual signals from the graph—nearby facts, entity types, relation patterns—to update probabilities for each blank. The process operates on discrete choices (which entity? which relation?) rather than continuous numbers.

Why it works

Why iterative refinement works

Generating an unseen fact is hard because you must satisfy many constraints at once: the entities must be real and of the right type, the relations must connect plausibly, and the entire triple must fit the graph's patterns. Trying to guess all blanks at once from scratch fails because there are too many bad combinations.

KREPE breaks this into small steps. It starts with random guesses and refines them iteratively. At each refinement step, the model sends information through the graph: each entity tells its neighbors what relations point to it, each relation broadcasts what entity types it typically links. Known pieces of the query act as anchors, biasing the refinement toward consistent completions. After many refinement steps, the random guesses have been gradually replaced with values that satisfy both the local query constraints and the global graph structure.

This works because each refinement step is much simpler than the full generation problem. Instead of guessing all blanks at once, the model simply asks: given what we know so far, which entities or relations are more likely here? The iterative process accumulates these local decisions into a coherent global fact.

Math after the picture

How each refinement step works

First in words:

probability score for entity e in slot i equals: (local signal from known fact parts) plus (global signal from graph neighbors) plus (alignment score between entity type and expected role), normalized to sum to 1 across all possible entities

Then the compact form:

P(e_i | known, graph) = exp(score_local(known_i) + score_global(neighbors_i) + score_type(e)) / Z

Every symbol:

e_ithe entity filling the i-th blank
known_ithe known pieces of the fact
score_local(known_i)signal from the known query parts, e.g., does e match the known relation type?
neighbors_ientities and relations connected to this blank by graph edges
score_global(neighbors_i)how often does e appear in those neighbors' facts?
score_type(e)is e the right entity type for this role?
Znormalization constant so probabilities sum to 1
A tiny worked example
Query: (Alice, works-at, ?). Known parts score 2.0. Graph neighbors (employees at Google) add score 1.5. Entity type (company) adds 0.8.
Candidate: Google. Total unnormalized score: exp(2.0 + 1.5 + 0.8) = exp(4.3) ≈ 73.7.
Candidate: Alice (wrong type). Total: exp(2.0 + 0.1 + 0.0) = exp(2.1) ≈ 8.2.
Candidate: TechCorp (less common, fewer neighbors). Total: exp(2.0 + 0.3 + 0.8) = exp(3.1) ≈ 22.3.
Normalize: P(Google) = 73.7 / (73.7 + 8.2 + 22.3) ≈ 0.73. Google is most likely.

Over many refinement iterations, these local probability updates propagate through the graph, gradually steering random noise toward valid, unseen facts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too few refinement steps. If you stop the diffusion early, masks are still partially noisy. The refinement process needs enough iterations to propagate signals from known anchors through the graph. Too few steps means incompletely refined blanks.
Disconnected graph regions. If a blank entity is far from any known pieces in the graph, the messages traveling through edges will dilute. The refinement may revert to random guesses for isolated slots. Link prediction works best on densely connected graphs.
Rare entity or relation types. If the correct completion is an uncommon entity, the type score and global neighbor scores may both be low. The model could mislabel a blank with a common entity instead. Rare facts are harder to generate.
Done right. Set refinement steps high enough, ensure query-relevant graph neighborhoods are dense, and handle rare entities with class balancing or separate training objectives.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small knowledge graph: 20 entities (companies, people, cities) and 5 relation types (works-at, located-in, founder-of, employee-count, established-year). Create 50 training facts. Train KREPE with 30 refinement steps. Then test three scenarios: fill one blank (old link prediction), fill two blanks (a new capability), fill zero blanks (generate a fact the model has never seen). Measure: how many generated facts are valid (correct entity types, realistic relation) and how many are unseen (not in the training set)?

validity = (facts with correct types and sensible relations) / (total generated facts); unseen_rate = (facts not in training set) / (total valid facts)

The demo must show that KREPE generates unseen, type-correct facts with multiple blanks, and that vanilla link prediction fails or produces gibberish when forced to fill multiple slots.
