Consensus Subspace Distillation for Time-Series Foundation Models

Compress a large time-series model by aligning a smaller model to the main geometric structure its layers naturally form, not by copying individual predictions.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Large models that work across many time-series tasks are expensive to run on phones and edge devices. This paper makes a smaller copy using a trick: instead of training the small model to mimic the big model's guesses, it aligns the small model to a shared underlying shape that emerges in the big model's internal layers—a low-rank consensus subspace. The result is a model 90% smaller that works just as well.

Zero assumption version

Big models work but are slow to deploy

Time-series foundation models—large neural networks trained on many different time-series tasks—can forecast, detect anomalies, and fill in missing data across dozens of domains without retraining. The problem: these models weigh gigabytes, making them too slow for phones, cameras, and factory sensors.

A naive approach is to train a small model from scratch on the same data. It fails because it never learns the complex shortcuts the large model discovered. Another approach: make the small model copy the large model's outputs on a batch of examples (output-matching). This works but has a fatal flaw: the small model is forced to mimic the large model's training quirks. If the large model was trained a certain way, the small copy gets stuck to those same peculiar choices and cannot adapt to new tasks or simplify cleanly.

In one sentence. Distill a time-series model by aligning a student to a geometric subspace all layers converge to, rather than to teacher-specific outputs.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Identify high-contribution layers

Measure each layer by removing it and watching the prediction error jump; keep the top layers.

Estimate the consensus subspace offline

Collect embeddings from those layers, compute their covariance, and extract the top few eigenvectors—the shared shape.

Project student and teacher into the subspace

During training, take student output, rotate it into the subspace basis, and measure how far it sits from the teacher's projected mean.

Align mean and covariance

Minimize the distance between student's projected mean/covariance and teacher's; add a frequency-domain uncertainty term to reduce subset bias.

Test on multiple tasks

Run student on forecasting, imputation, and classification; compare accuracy to the original large model.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which signals the student learns from. Instead of training the student to copy the teacher's internal numbers (the values each neuron produces) at every layer or every example, it trains the student to align its internal embeddings to a single, fixed, model-independent shape: the consensus subspace and its center statistics (mean and covariance).

the object it moves  =  the training target: from layer-wise feature copies to subspace alignment
The hidden quantity

The consensus subspace: a shared low-rank shape

measured quantity  =  Consensus subspace and its center statistics

Across different model sizes and architectures trained on time-series, the embeddings in high-contribution layers naturally land near a shared, approximately low-rank shape. This consensus subspace is a plane defined by the top principal directions extracted from the covariance (the average spread of embeddings around their center). Unlike teacher-specific parameters or outputs, this subspace is a model-agnostic object—a property of the task itself, not the teacher.

The paper estimates it offline: collect embeddings from the top-contributing layers, shrink-stabilize the covariance (a technique to make the estimate stable when you have fewer samples than dimensions), compute eigenvalue decomposition, and truncate to the top few directions. The center statistics are the mean embedding and its covariance structure within this subspace. During training, the student projects its embedding into this subspace and the loss measures how far the student's mean and covariance sit from the teacher's.

Why it works

Why aligning to a subspace works better than copying outputs

Output-matching distillation fails because it ties the small model to every idiosyncratic choice the large model made during training: its random initialization, its learning rate schedule, its batch size. The small model memorizes these as a package. When you try to use the small model on a new task or with different training settings, it breaks because it relies on details it never truly learned.

Consensus subspace alignment decouples the small model from these quirks. The subspace is a pure geometric fact: the directions in embedding space that actually carry task-relevant information, independent of how any one model was trained. By aligning to this stable object, the small model learns the essential structure and can generalize. Second, because the loss is a mean--covariance match (two global statistics), not a pointwise match, the small model has flexibility in how it reaches the subspace. It can discover its own simpler path instead of being forced to replicate the teacher's exact steps. Third, the paper uses only the high-contribution layers—the ones the task actually uses—avoiding noise from layers that barely affect predictions. This focus prevents the student from wasting parameters imitating useless layer outputs.

The alignment loss formula

First in words:

The loss is the sum of two parts: the distance between student and teacher projected means, plus the Frobenius norm (sum of squared differences) between their covariance matrices, scaled down by a factor that prevents overfitting to small batches.

Then the compact form:

L = ||mu_s - mu_t||^2 + ||Sigma_s - Sigma_t||_F^2 + lambda * KL(rho_freq_student || rho_freq_teacher)

Every symbol:

mu_sstudent embedding mean, projected into the subspace
mu_tteacher embedding mean, projected into the subspace
Sigma_sstudent embedding covariance within the subspace
Sigma_tteacher embedding covariance within the subspace
||·||_F^2Frobenius norm squared: sum of squared matrix entries
rho_freqcharacteristic-function spectral density (frequency-domain representation)
KLKullback-Leibler divergence: how different two distributions are
lambdauncertainty injection weight: controls how much to penalize subset bias
A tiny worked example with three students and one teacher
Teacher projects embeddings and computes: center at [0.5, 0.3], spread at [[1.2, 0.1],[0.1, 0.8]]
Student A projects to: center at [0.45, 0.32], spread at [[1.1, 0.08],[0.08, 0.85]]
Center-matching loss for A: distance-squared [0.5,0.3] to [0.45,0.32] = 0.05^2 + 0.02^2 = 0.0029
Spread-matching loss for A: sum of squared differences in covariance entries ≈ 0.04
Student B drifts more: center at [0.3, 0.1], far from teacher, center loss jumps to 0.08
Same parameter change produces smaller error for A; during training, adjustments push the student toward A's strategy.

The loss lets the student wander across the subspace freely as long as it lands near the right average location and spread; unlike pointwise matching, this gives the student room to simplify.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Layer selection is wrong. If you choose layers that do not contribute to the task (e.g., layers that are mostly dormant), the consensus subspace will be noisy or misleading. The paper uses drop-layer marginal loss to identify high-contribution layers, which works well as long as the layer-drop metric actually tracks task importance.
Covariance estimation becomes unstable with few samples. If you collect embeddings from only a small batch, the sample covariance is unreliable. The paper applies shrinkage stabilization, but if the shrinkage strength is set wrongly, the estimated subspace will still be biased.
Subset-induced bias: not all time series are equally represented. If your dataset has more examples of one type of time series (e.g., stock prices vs. sensor data), the consensus subspace will lean toward that type. The frequency-domain uncertainty injection partially corrects this but cannot fully compensate for a deeply skewed dataset.
Done right. Choose layers via drop-layer analysis, collect enough embeddings to estimate covariance reliably, use shrinkage stabilization, and apply frequency-domain uncertainty weighting to handle unbalanced data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal demo that (1) trains a large time-series model on a single task, (2) extracts the consensus subspace from its high-contribution layers, (3) trains a small student model using the consensus subspace loss, and (4) measures accuracy on a test set. Compare the student's performance against both a scratch baseline and an output-matching baseline. To make the trade-off visible, include a metric for model size reduction and how fast the model runs when answering on a CPU or phone.

retained_accuracy = (student_accuracy / teacher_accuracy) * 100%; compression_ratio = teacher_params / student_params; inference_speedup = teacher_latency_ms / student_latency_ms

The demo must make it unmistakable that a 10-times-smaller student can match a large teacher's performance across multiple time-series tasks—not by luck, but because it learned the stable shape, not the teacher's training accident.
