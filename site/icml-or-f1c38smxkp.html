Discriminative Anomaly Detection in Changing Graphs

Find rare bad events in constantly-changing networks by learning from a few labeled examples plus a lot of normal behavior.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Dynamic graphs are networks where connections between things change over time: for example, new messages in a social network or new data transfers in a computer network. Detecting anomalies (rare bad events like fraud or intrusions) is hard when you have almost no labeled examples to learn from. This paper proposes a method that learns a sharp decision boundary by combining three ideas: measuring how each interaction differs from its history, restricting where normal interactions live in the model's internal space, and using a flexible mathematical shape to separate normal from abnormal.

Zero assumption version

Three flawed paths, no good middle ground

You want to catch rare bad events (fraud, intrusions, equipment failures) in a constantly-changing network. Most of the interactions are normal, but a few are anomalies—and you need to detect them.

Unsupervised methods (learning from only unlabeled data) seem cheap, but they struggle to draw a sharp line between normal and anomalous. The boundary is blurry because the model has no examples of what anomalies actually look like.

Semi-supervised methods (using a handful of labeled anomaly examples plus lots of unlabeled normal data) can learn a sharper boundary, but they overfit: the model memorizes the few anomalies it saw and fails on new, different anomalies it has never encountered.

The gap: unsupervised methods are vague, semi-supervised methods are fragile. The paper learns a sharp boundary using only normal examples and whatever labeled anomalies you have, without overfitting to those rare examples.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure each interaction against its history

For each new edge (connection), compute how different it is from previous interactions between those two nodes.

Encode the deviation as numbers

Turn that difference into a vector (a list of numbers) that represents this interaction's anomaly-relevant signal.

Restrict normal interactions

Force the model to place all normal interactions inside a spherical shell (between two imaginary spheres centered at the same point).

Learn a flexible boundary

Use a normalizing flow (a flexible mathematical shape that bends to fit the data) to model where anomalies live outside that shell.

Optimize with labeled + unlabeled

Train the model to put labeled anomalies outside the shell and labeled normal ones inside, without memorizing.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves three levers at once: the representation (how interactions are encoded as numbers), the constraint on normal interactions (forcing them into a bounded region), and the decision boundary (how to distinguish normal from anomalous).

the object it moves  =  representation, constraint, boundary
The hidden quantity

The boundary between normal and anomalous

measured quantity  =  decision boundary shape

The paper measures where the dividing line between normal and anomalous should be. In older methods, this boundary was either a simple sphere (unsupervised) or a rigid surface fit to a few labeled examples (semi-supervised). This paper's key insight is that the boundary should be a flexible shape—not rigid, not vague, but learned to fit the actual pattern of how anomalies are distributed, so it catches new, unseen anomalies that look different from the labeled training examples.

A normalizing flow (a smooth, reversible mathematical transformation) is used to model this boundary. The flow learns to assign high probability to normal interactions and low probability to anomalies, but it does this without memorizing the few labeled anomalies you provided—because the normal representations are already constrained to a tight region.

Why it works

Why learning three things together prevents overfitting

The paper avoids overfitting by making overfitting mechanically harder. Here is why: if you just give the model a few labeled anomalies and say 'learn to reject these', the model can memorize them—it learns what those specific anomalies look like. Instead, this paper first constrains all normal interactions to live inside a spherical shell, which forces the model to agree on what 'normal' means. Once normal is tightly defined, the boundary between inside and outside that shell is mostly determined; the model cannot move it arbitrarily to fit the few labeled anomalies. The normalizing flow then learns the detailed shape of anomalies, but it does so under the constraint that the normal region is already fixed and compact.

The residual encoding (measuring deviation from history) amplifies the signal: a rare edge between two nodes that never usually interact shows up as a large deviation, making the model naturally suspicious of it even without seeing labeled anomalies. This helps because the model does not have to learn what anomalies look like from scratch; the anomaly signal is already baked into the encoding, so the constraint on normal interactions becomes easier to enforce and the boundary clearer.

Math after the picture

The restriction loss keeps normal interactions in bounds

First in words:

The total loss combines residual encoding loss, a restriction penalty that grows if normal interactions escape the spherical shell, and a flow-based anomaly scoring loss. The restriction term ensures that labeled normal examples and unlabeled data stay inside the shell defined by inner radius r_min and outer radius r_max.

Then the compact form:

L = L_residual + lambda_restrict * sum_i max(0, |z_i| - r_max)^2 + lambda_flow * L_flow_nll

Every symbol:

Ltotal training loss
L_residualloss from encoding interaction deviations
lambda_restrictweight controlling how strongly to enforce the shell constraint
|z_i|distance of encoded interaction i from the center of the shell
r_maxouter radius of the allowed normal region
L_flow_nllnegative log-likelihood from the normalizing flow on anomalies
Tiny worked example
Normal interaction 1: encoded distance 2.5 from center, r_max = 3.0 -> no penalty (already inside shell)
Normal interaction 2: encoded distance 3.5 from center, r_max = 3.0 -> excess 0.5, penalty = lambda * 0.5^2 = 0.25 * 0.25 = 0.0625
Anomaly interaction: distance 6.0 from center -> no restriction penalty (anomalies are not constrained to the shell), but flow loss tries to assign it high anomaly score
Result: normal interactions are gently pulled into the shell; anomalies are not penalized for being outside, but scored by the learned flow model

The restriction loss acts like an elastic wall that keeps normal interactions tightly grouped, making the boundary between normal and anomalous sharp and stable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the shell radii wrong. If r_max is too small, even normal interactions get penalized, and the model overshoots and flags normal data as anomalous. If r_max is too large, the restriction does nothing and the method collapses to semi-supervised overfitting.
Weak residual encoding. If the residual representation (deviation from history) does not capture the anomalies in your specific domain, the entire pipeline fails. For example, in a network where anomalies are silent (interactions that should have happened but didn't), measuring only edge differences will miss them.
Normalizing flow too flexible. If the flow model is overparameterized (too many degrees of freedom), it can still memorize the few labeled anomalies, negating the benefit of the restriction loss. The flow and restriction must be balanced.
Done right. With careful tuning of radii, a residual encoding that matches your domain's anomalies, and a well-regularized flow model, the method finds a boundary that is sharp, generalizes to new anomalies you have never seen, and does not memorize the few labeled examples.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo on a synthetic dynamic graph where you generate normal interactions (e.g., edges between randomly chosen nodes with typical frequencies) and inject a few labeled anomalies (e.g., sudden bursts of edges or edges between normally-disconnected nodes). Train the model on the normal data plus your labeled anomalies. Then, inject new, different anomalies (new patterns the model never saw) and verify that the method catches them at higher rates than baseline unsupervised or semi-supervised approaches, because the restricted normal region plus the learned flow model together generalize beyond the training anomalies.

generalization gap = accuracy_on_labeled_anomalies - accuracy_on_new_anomalies

The demo must show that the generalization gap is small—the model's accuracy on new anomalies drops less than baseline methods. This proves the model learned a boundary that transfers to new patterns instead of memorizing the labeled training anomalies.
