Functional MRI Time Series Generation via Wavelet-Based Image Transform and Spectral Flow Matching for Brain Disorder Identification

Generate realistic brain scan time series by learning to build up frequency patterns layer-by-layer, starting from randomness.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Brain scanners measure blood oxygen over time to see which parts of the brain are active. But real scans are expensive and rare, so scientists need synthetic copies to train diagnosis models. Most generation methods fail because they cannot capture the brain's changing rhythms and the way different frequencies matter differently across brain regions. This paper proposes a dual-frequency pipeline: break the signal into two frequency languages (wavelets for global rhythm changes, frequency bins for local energy), teach a flow model to generate in that combined space, then reassemble into realistic brain time series.

Zero assumption version

Why synthetic brain scans are hard to make

Brain scanners (fMRI machines) measure how much blood flows to active neurons, recorded as a voltage number over time. A real scan takes minutes inside a loud machine and costs thousands of dollars. Scientists want artificial scans to train diagnosis models, but existing synthetic methods miss something critical: the brain's signal changes its character over time — sometimes it is smooth and slow, sometimes choppy and fast — and these changes matter for spotting disease.

Naive generative models (like diffusion models) treat all frequencies equally at first, then gradually erase high frequencies (the choppiness), leaving only low frequencies (the slow drift). But brain signals need high and low frequencies woven together from the start. The paper shows that existing methods struggle because they ignore how the brain's energy lives in different frequency bands and how this energy changes across brain regions.

In one sentence. Transform the brain signal into two frequency languages, learn to generate in that space, and reassemble into synthetic signals that sound like real brain rhythms.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the frequency representation the generative model learns from. Instead of generating directly in the time domain (the raw signal), it works in a dual-frequency space: wavelet decomposition coefficients (which capture rhythm patterns and transients across scales) combined with discrete cosine transform coefficients (which encode energy localization). This dual space forces the model to respect both global rhythm dynamics and local energy packing.

The hidden quantity

The frequency energy profile

measured quantity  =  Frequency energy localization

The paper measures an invisible quantity: how much energy the signal packs into low versus high frequencies, and how that energy concentrates across different brain regions. This is not directly visible in the raw time series — you must transform the signal to see it.

The paper estimates this by applying the discrete cosine transform to the wavelet-decomposed signal. The DCT reveals which frequencies matter most: brain signals are dominated by low frequencies (slow drifts), with energy sharply falling off at high frequencies. The paper teaches the generative model to learn this energy profile — to prefer low frequencies naturally and to preserve spatiotemporal coherence.

Why it works

Why the method works

The core insight: brain signals have structure in frequency space that naive diffusion models destroy. Diffusion starts with noise (equal power at all frequencies) and progressively erases high frequencies, leaving low frequencies. This is backwards for the brain, because the brain already privileges low frequencies naturally.

The dual-spectral approach builds in constraints before learning begins. Wavelets capture multi-scale rhythm changes (how the signal's character shifts over time across different time-scales). Frequency bins map those rhythms into energy pools (revealing the lumpy, uneven way energy clumps in certain bands). By learning directly in this frequency space, the flow model inherits these constraints. When it reverses the transforms, the reconstructed signal automatically has the right frequency balance. The model no longer wastes learning capacity fighting against a uniform noise starting point — it can focus on capturing realistic brain dynamics and disease-specific patterns.

Math after the picture

The frequency-domain flow equation

First in words:

Mode-wise diffusion coefficient depends on the time derivative of the mean and standard deviation schedules, weighted by frequency-specific Laplacian eigenvalues.

Then the compact form:

g(t, k)^2 = 2 * sigma(t, k) * (d_sigma(t, k) / dt - f(t, k) * sigma(t, k))

Every symbol:

g(t, k)Noise level for frequency mode k at time t
sigma(t, k)Noise schedule standard deviation for mode k
d_sigma(t, k) / dtHow fast the noise level is changing for mode k
f(t, k)Drift coefficient incorporating Laplacian eigenvalue lambda_k
lambda_kEnergy compaction factor for frequency mode k (lower for high frequencies)
A tiny worked example
At time t=0.5, frequency mode k=1 (low frequency): sigma(1, 0.5) = 1.0, d_sigma/dt = -2.0, lambda_1 = 0.01
Compute f(t, k) = -2.0/1.0 - 0.01 = -2.01
Then g(0.5, 1)^2 = 2 * 1.0 * (-2.0 - (-2.01)*1.0) = 2 * 1.0 * 0.01 = 0.02, so g(0.5, 1) ≈ 0.14
For high frequency k=10: lambda_10 = 10, f(t, k) = -2.0/1.0 - 10 = -12, g(0.5, 10)^2 = 2 * (-2.0 - (-12)*1.0) = 2 * 10 = 20, g(0.5, 10) ≈ 4.47
High frequencies get large noise gains, allowing diffusion to erase them. Low frequencies get tiny noise gains, preserving their structure. This frequency-selective schedule is the key innovation.

The flow model steers each frequency mode with mode-specific noise, so low frequencies (where energy lives) are protected while high frequencies (where noise dominates) are easily erased.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wavelet and cosine setting mismatch. If the wavelet scales or DCT block sizes do not align with the actual rhythm structure of the disease in question, the frequency decomposition becomes noise-aligned rather than signal-aligned. A wavelet chosen for artifact-heavy data may over-smooth subtle biomarkers.
Flow model underfitting in high-frequency modes. If the velocity network (U-ViT) is too small or training steps too few, the model may learn only low-frequency features and fail to capture realistic high-frequency choppiness, producing overly smooth synthetic signals.
Reconstruction artifacts from inverse transforms. Errors in the learned frequency coefficients can amplify during the inverse wavelet and cosine transforms, producing transient spikes or discontinuities that are not physiological.
Done right. Validate wavelet choice on pilot data, ensure the velocity network has adequate capacity, and post-process synthetic signals to clip unphysiological outliers (e.g., extreme blood-oxygen values).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal end-to-end pipeline on a single-subject fMRI scan (e.g., one person, one 10-minute resting-state scan, one brain region). Apply DWT to extract two wavelet scales. Apply 2D DCT to a few time-frequency blocks. Train a small flow model (e.g., 2-layer neural network that looks at all frequency dimensions) on the real frequency coefficients for 100 steps. Generate synthetic coefficients for 10 new samples. Reverse the transforms. Measure: (1) visual inspection — does the synthetic signal look like the original (same rhythm, no spikes)? (2) frequency spectrum match — does the power spectrum of synthetic data match the real data? (3) downstream task — if you train a simple brain-region classifier (healthy vs. disease) on real + synthetic data, does accuracy go up compared to real-only, without overfitting?

Downstream classification accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN); spectrum match = mean squared error between power spectra of real and synthetic signals

The demo must make impossible to miss: when you overlay the power spectra of synthetic and real brain signals, they should be nearly identical in shape (heavy at low frequencies, sparse at high), not uniform or inverted.
