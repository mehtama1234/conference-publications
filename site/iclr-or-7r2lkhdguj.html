Scaling Laws for Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Language Models

Predicting how much faster a Mixture-of-Experts model runs by introducing a speedup measurement—a number that tells you the speedup ratio for any given architectural choice.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Training huge language models is expensive because computing power costs money. One trick, called Mixture-of-Experts, lets you grow the total number of weights without growing the actual computation. But which expert configuration should you choose? This paper gives you a formula to predict the speedup for any choice, by measuring a speedup ratio between what a standard model needs versus what an MoE model needs to reach the same performance.

How do you choose the right Mixture-of-Experts design?

Scaling up language models is expensive. A naive approach is to make every layer bigger and train on more tokens—but compute budgets are fixed. Mixture-of-Experts sidesteps this by splitting each layer into many smaller experts (think: parallel routes through the network), and training tells the model which expert to use for each word. The total parameters grow, but the actual work (FLOPs) stays roughly constant. The problem is that there are many ways to design an MoE system: how many experts? Should they be large or small? How many experts should each word activate? A simple grid search of 300 experiments costs millions of dollars. What you need is a predictor—a formula that tells you the speedup for any choice, before you train.

Before this paper, nobody had found the underlying power laws. Researchers tried different designs and measured what they got. But they could not tell you: given your compute budget, what is the optimal expert size, expert count, and how many experts each word should use? That meant teams built expensive prototypes blindly.

The solution in one sentence. Measure the speedup ratio (how many times faster than a dense model), and derive a unified formula to predict it from expert activation ratio (how many experts each word uses), expert size, and compute budget. These factors form predictable power laws because the performance-versus-cost tradeoff follows smooth mathematical curves, not random noise.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper measures and predicts the speedup ratio—a single number capturing whether an MoE architecture earns its complexity. Normally, you design an MoE model by guessing or by running dozens of expensive training runs. This paper instead builds a physics-like formula: given how many experts each word activates (activation ratio), expert size, and compute budget, it predicts the speedup. That shifts the design process from blind trial-and-error to calculation.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: the speedup ratio

measured quantity  =  Speedup Ratio

The speedup ratio is how many times more compute a dense model needs than an MoE model to reach the same quality, because dense models must use all experts on every word while MoE uses only a subset, saving compute. Take a dense model that achieves a certain final training loss under a certain compute budget. Now take an MoE model that reaches the same final loss. The speedup ratio is how many times more compute the dense model needed. For example, if a dense model requires 7 times more FLOPs to match the MoE's performance, the speedup ratio is 7. It quantifies the efficiency advantage as a single number.

The paper estimates the speedup ratio by training hundreds of MoE models at different architectural settings, measuring final loss for each, then finding which dense model size reaches the same loss. The speedup ratio is the compute amount needed by the dense model divided by the compute amount needed by the MoE model. Then the paper plots the speedup ratio against architectural choices (expert count, expert size, how many experts each word uses, and total compute budget) and discovers that the speedup ratio follows smooth power-law curves because the cost-versus-performance tradeoff is governed by underlying mathematical structures, not random variation.

Why it works

Why these power laws exist

The core insight: MoE trades off total parameters against activation cost. When you activate only a fraction of experts on each word, you lower the FLOPs per word because you skip computing unused branches. But sparse routing also hurts learning—models learn slower when they do not see all paths. These two forces create a curve. The activation ratio (what fraction of experts turn on per word) is the main lever: lower ratio means lower cost, but only up to a point because below a threshold the model cannot route words to the right specialist experts anymore and performance collapses. The paper discovers that this tradeoff follows a power law: loss decreases predictably as you lower activation ratio, following a smooth mathematical curve, because the learning dynamics follow smooth physics-like equations. Over 300 experiments, that curve shape stays constant. The same pattern holds for expert size (granularity): there is an optimal size because too-large experts waste per-word compute while too-small experts cannot specialize enough to extract patterns. That bowl-shaped curve (loss dips at medium expert size) is also a power law because the capacity-efficiency tradeoff is smooth. Compute budget amplifies everything: larger budgets shift all these curves upward in performance, but preserve the same shape because the underlying cost-benefit equations scale proportionally. So a single unified formula with a few exponents, fitted once to your 300 models, predicts the speedup ratio for any new configuration you plug in.

Math after the picture

The speedup-ratio formula

First in words:

The speedup ratio is the amount of compute a dense model needs divided by the amount of compute an MoE model needs, when both reach the same training loss.

Then the compact form:

Speedup(config) = C_dense(target_loss) ÷ C_MoE(config, target_loss)

Every symbol:

C_dense(L)compute amount in FLOPs for a dense model to reach loss L
C_MoE(config, L)compute amount in FLOPs for an MoE with given configuration to reach loss L
configthe architectural choices: activation ratio, expert size, expert count, layer arrangement
target_lossthe final training loss value, typically what MoE achieves
Speedupspeedup ratio: how many times faster the MoE design is (higher is better)
A tiny worked example
Suppose an MoE model with 10 percent activation ratio, 256-token expert size, trained on 1 trillion tokens reaches training loss of 2.5.
A dense model (all experts always active) needs 4 times 10 to the 21st FLOPs to reach loss 2.5.
The same MoE model needs only 6 times 10 to the 20th FLOPs to reach loss 2.5.
Speedup ratio: 4 times 10 to the 21st divided by 6 times 10 to the 20th equals 6.7. The MoE model is 6.7 times more compute-efficient than the dense baseline.

Higher speedup ratio means the MoE design is better because you spend less compute to hit the same quality—the fewer FLOPs the MoE needs relative to dense, the bigger the advantage—which frees budget to train on more tokens or a larger model within the same cost envelope.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Activation ratio too low breaks the model. If you activate only half a percent of experts per word, the model becomes too sparse to learn well. Routing signals drown in randomness. Loss jumps up. The paper shows a hard floor below which the speedup ratio drops—a failure point where the tradeoff stops working.
Expert size outside the sweet spot hurts. Experts that are too large waste per-word compute; experts too small cannot specialize enough. The bowl-shaped curve has a minimum—move away from it and both loss and speedup degrade.
Scaling laws assume factors work independently. The paper assumes activation ratio, expert size, and shared expert ratio operate independently. In reality, they interact (a high-activation high-redundancy setup might behave differently than the formula predicts). The paper acknowledges this as a simplification needed to tame the search space.
Done right: fit power laws to your data. Collect training runs across a range of configurations and compute budgets. Fit power-law curves to each factor. Check that deviations are small. Then use the unified formula to predict unseen configurations. This procedure is proven reliable within the tested range.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a dataset of MoE training runs, varying activation ratio, expert size, and compute budget across a grid. Train about 20 models across two different total compute budgets. Plot each configuration against its final loss. Fit power-law curves to each factor independently (how loss changes with activation, how loss changes with expert size, etc). Now pick a configuration you did not train. Use the power-law formula to predict what loss it will reach. Train that configuration and measure the actual loss. The prediction should be within five percent of the actual value. Next, compute the speedup ratio for three designs: high-activation, medium-activation, and low-activation MoE, all at the same compute budget. Show that the low-activation variant reaches the same loss using fewer FLOPs by matching the formula's speedup prediction, proving that the speedup ranking is correct.

Prediction error is the absolute value of predicted loss minus actual loss, divided by actual loss; speedup agreement is absolute value of predicted speedup ratio minus actual speedup ratio, divided by actual speedup ratio

If prediction error is small and speedup ranking matches experiments, you have validated that power laws hold and can be trusted to guide design choices without further expensive training.
