Expert Heads: Robust Evidence Identification

A small subset of attention heads in LLMs reliably find task-relevant documents regardless of their position in the input, and using them to vote improves evidence retrieval.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When a language model reads multiple documents to answer a question, it often gets confused if the relevant document is buried in the middle instead of at the start. This paper discovers that most attention heads (the reading mechanisms in a neural network) get distracted by where documents sit, but a few special heads stay focused on task-relevant content no matter the order. The paper names these Expert Heads and shows they can dramatically improve document retrieval by giving vote-weight to heads that ignore position and focus only on what the text actually says.

Zero assumption version

Position bias in multi-document reasoning

When a language model (a machine that reads text and predicts the next word) reads multiple documents to answer a question, its answer depends heavily on where the relevant document sits in the list. If the right document is first, the model answers correctly. If it is buried in position five, the model often fails or makes up facts.

A beginner might think the model just reads all documents equally. But it does not. Inside the model, attention heads (small circuits that decide which parts of the input to focus on) tend to overweight the boundaries—the very start and very end of the input—and ignore the middle documents where the real clues are. The model pays more attention to where a document sits in the list than to what the document actually says, because during pretraining it learned that boundary positions often carry structural information.

The fix: Find the few attention heads that ignore position and trust only the content itself, then use them to vote on which document answers the question.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Permutation test

Shuffle document order and watch which heads always focus on the same documents.

Measure stability

Score each head by how consistently it attends to task-relevant content across shuffles.

Identify Expert Heads

Select heads that fire often on content with low variance across orderings.

Collect votes

For each document, count how many Expert Heads point to it as relevant.

Rank by votes

Return documents ranked by Expert Head votes; more votes means higher chance the document has the answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the model picks which document to trust: instead of letting all attention heads vote equally, it gives more weight to the small set of heads that reliably ignore position and focus on content alone.

the object it moves  =  attention head weighting
The hidden quantity

Permutation stability: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  stability of attention across document shuffles

Permutation stability is how much an attention head sticks to the same documents when you shuffle the input order. A head with high stability points to the same relevant documents no matter where they sit. A head with low stability chases the position and gets distracted.

The paper measures this by running the model on the same question-document set in many different random orders and checking whether each head's attention pattern stays consistent. Heads that maintain their focus are Expert Heads. The paper uses activation frequency (how often a head fires) and variance (spread) across permutations to identify them.

Why it works

Why Expert Heads work

Most attention heads learn a shortcut: they attend to position markers (the start or end of the input) because those markers reliably tell them something about the overall structure. This is fast but brittle—move the structure, and the head fails.

Expert Heads emerge by chance in a few layers (mid-layers in LLaMA and Mistral, deeper layers in Qwen) where the network must do semantic work—understanding what each document actually says, not just where it sits. These heads learn to look at content signals (keywords, argument structure, relevance indicators) and ignore position. Because they rely on content, they work regardless of input order.

When multiple Expert Heads vote, their agreement becomes a confidence signal: if many stable heads point to the same document, that document is very likely to contain the answer. This voting aggregates the opinions of several independent content-focused readers, which is more reliable than trusting any single head or any single traditional retrieval method, because agreement among many independent judges is stronger evidence than one judge's opinion.

Math after the picture

The stability score equation

First in words:

stability score equals the average attention to task-relevant documents minus the standard deviation of that attention across permutations, scaled by activation frequency

Then the compact form:

S_h = freq_h * (mean_perm(attn_h) - std_perm(attn_h))

Every symbol:

S_hstability score for head h (how much to trust this head)
freq_hhow often head h activates on task-relevant content
mean_perm(attn_h)average attention to relevant documents across all shuffled orderings
std_perm(attn_h)how much that attention bounces around across shuffles
A tiny worked example
Head A on 3 shuffles: attends to doc2 at 0.9, 0.88, 0.89. Mean = 0.89, std = 0.01, freq = 0.9.
S_A = 0.9 * (0.89 - 0.01) = 0.9 * 0.88 = 0.792. (High stability.)
Head B on 3 shuffles: attends to doc2 at 0.1, 0.7, 0.2. Mean = 0.33, std = 0.3, freq = 0.2.
S_B = 0.2 * (0.33 - 0.3) = 0.2 * 0.03 = 0.006. (Low stability, chases position.)
Expert Heads have scores > 0.5; normal heads have scores < 0.1. Vote only with Expert Heads.

The equation rewards heads that attend consistently to the same content (low std) with high frequency, penalizing heads whose attention bounces around based on position.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Not enough Expert Heads. If a model has only one or two stable heads, their votes lack diversity and can still be wrong. Large models with more Expert Heads are more reliable because many independent judges voting together make it harder for errors to pass through unchecked.
Adversarial document insertion. If you add a document that is syntactically similar to the relevant one but contains misleading information, a head might get confused and vote for the fake document instead. Expert Heads are content-focused, but not immune to semantic traps.
Misalignment between Expert Heads and ground truth. The paper assumes that the heads learned from pretraining genuinely track semantic relevance. In some domains (highly specialized jargon, cross-lingual reasoning), the heads might not have learned the right signals.
Done right. Expert Heads work when the model has seen diverse multi-document reasoning in pretraining, when documents differ enough to have clear content signals, and when you aggregate votes from a diverse set of stable heads rather than trusting just one.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small retrieval pipeline: take a multi-hop question (e.g., 'Which film directed by the person born in [city]?'), prepare two or three candidate documents (one containing the answer, one a decoy), and shuffle their order in multiple ways. Run the language model on each shuffled version and collect which documents the Expert Heads and ordinary heads vote for. Measure how often the correct document wins under Expert Head voting versus ordinary voting.

robustness = (times_expert_picks_correct / total_shuffles) - (times_ordinary_picks_correct / total_shuffles)

The demo must show that Expert Head voting stays stable across document shuffles while ordinary head voting fails when the correct document is not at the start.
