Solving General-Utility MDPs in the Single-Trial Regime

Plan one step at a time for a single real trial by measuring which state-action pairs you visit, not just rewards.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Imagine a robot that must succeed on its first real deployment. It cannot practice 100 times; it gets one shot. But the success condition is not simply high reward—it might be visiting every location evenly, or imitating a human, or balancing multiple conflicting goals. This paper is the first to solve this: planning for a single try when your objective is based on the pattern of states and actions you visit, not just rewards. The trick is to build a planning tree that explores future paths ahead of time, then pick the action that best steers the occupancy (which states you visit) toward your goal.

Zero assumption version

Single-trial planning with non-standard goals

Most planning algorithms assume you get to try many times and learn a policy that works on average. But in real robotics or one-shot scenarios, you get one trajectory—one chance to behave from start to finish. Your success is measured on that one trajectory alone.

Worse, your success metric is often not simply high reward. You might want to visit each location equally (exploration), copy a human's behavior (imitation), or balance safety and progress (risk-averse). Standard MDPs encode success as a cost or reward signal per state-action pair. These non-standard objectives do not fit that mold—they care about which states you visited and how often (the pattern of your path).

Naive approaches plan step-by-step greedily (maximizing the immediate reward, ignoring the future) or learn an average policy from offline data. Both fail: greedy misses the future impact on your objective, and an offline policy is not tuned to your single run.

In one sentence. Use online planning to search over which state-action pairs to visit (the occupancy) such that the pattern of where you land optimizes your arbitrary goal, all in one real trial.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Reframe the goal

Convert any objective into a function of the occupancy: score how well the visited states match your goal.

Convert to occupancy MDP

Replace the original problem with a new one where state is (original state, history of visits), reward is zero, and you solve for a path that optimizes occupancy.

Use MCTS online planning

At each step in the real world, run a tree search over future occupancies without actually visiting them yet.

Read off the best action

From the search tree, select the action whose branch led to occupancies closest to your goal, because that action steers you toward the best visited pattern.

Execute and repeat

Take one step in the real world, observe the new state, rebuild the tree, repeat until done.

What the paper changes

What changes: the occupancy

The paper does not change weights or rewards. It changes what the agent optimizes for: instead of maximizing a reward signal, the agent optimizes the pattern of states and actions it visits (the occupancy). In a 3-state world, occupancy might be (state A visited 3 times, state B visited 5 times, state C visited 2 times). The goal is to find a single trajectory whose occupancy matches your target pattern as closely as possible.

the object it moves  =  the occupancy (visited states-actions)
The hidden quantity

The occupancy: which states does one trajectory visit?

measured quantity  =  occupancy (visited states and actions)

The occupancy is the fraction of your one trajectory spent in each state-action pair. If your trajectory is 10 steps long and you visit state A in steps 1, 3, and 7, then occupancy of state A is three-tenths (0.3).

To measure it, the paper counts: for each state-action pair, how many times does the actual single trajectory visit it? Then divide by the total trajectory length to get a probability distribution (fractions that sum to 1). This occupancy is then plugged into your objective function to score it. A lower score means your objective is better met, because the occupancy is closer to what you wanted.

Why it works

Why it works: planning by occupancy

Standard planning picks actions greedily to maximize immediate reward. That breaks for non-standard objectives because the impact on occupancy is not immediate. For example, imitation learning cares about matching a human's state-action distribution—missing a state early on cannot be fixed later.

The paper's insight: instead of planning over actions, plan over occupancies. At each step, build a mental tree of all possible futures (using Monte-Carlo tree search). Imagine executing each possible path; for each, compute the occupancy it would produce. Then pick the action whose subtree contains the occupancy closest to your goal. This way, you account for the global structure of what you visit, not just the next reward.

The algorithm runs MCTS: root at your current (state, history), expand one child per iteration by simulating a random step forward, backtrack and update statistics on which subtrees lead to occupancies closer to the goal, repeat for thousands of iterations, then commit to the action with the best simulated occupancy.

Math after the picture

The objective and the single-trial version

First in words:

minimize f(occupancy of trajectory) where f is any function. occupancy is the fraction of time steps spent in each state-action pair.

Then the compact form:

π* = arg min_{π in D_NM} F_{1,H}(π) = E_π[ f( d_{π,H} ) ]

Every symbol:

π*the optimal policy (a list of decisions, one per timestep, allowed to depend on history)
D_NMpolicies that are non-Markovian: decisions depend on the full history, not just the current state
F_{1,H}(π)the objective for a single-trial truncated plan: the goal applied to occupancy over H steps
d_{π,H}the occupancy: a vector counting visits to each state-action pair over the trajectory
fyour arbitrary goal function: takes occupancy, returns a score to minimize
A tiny worked example: imitation learning with 3 states
Suppose you want to imitate a human who visits: state A one-third of the time, state B one-third, state C one-third.
Your single 10-step trajectory visits: A six times, B three times, C once. Occupancy is (0.6, 0.3, 0.1).
Score via sum of squared differences: (0.6 minus 0.333)^2 plus (0.3 minus 0.333)^2 plus (0.1 minus 0.333)^2 equals 0.0665 plus 0.0011 plus 0.0546 equals 0.1222.
Another policy yields occupancy (0.45, 0.35, 0.2): score is (0.45 minus 0.333)^2 plus (0.35 minus 0.333)^2 plus (0.2 minus 0.333)^2 equals 0.0137 plus 0.0003 plus 0.0178 equals 0.0318. This policy is better because its occupancy is closer to the human's.

The occupancy captures what was visited; the objective function f(occupancy) scores it. Planning searches over policies to find one whose occupancy minimizes f.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Huge search trees. MCTS explores a tree of all possible futures. In a world with 1000 states and long horizons, this explodes. The algorithm may run out of time before exploring enough iterations to find the good occupancies.
Occupancy that is impossible to reach. You might set a goal occupancy that the environment cannot achieve (e.g., visiting a blocked state). The algorithm will search forever without finding a path. You need a feasible goal.
Deterministic loops. If the environment forces the agent to loop back to the same state every few steps, the occupancy becomes fixed and rigid. The algorithm cannot steer away from it.
Convergence is slow but guaranteed. With enough MCTS iterations (thousands), the algorithm finds the optimal action. But real-time planning on a robot may time out before then. You must trade off planning time vs. decision speed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a small gridworld: a 5-by-5 grid with 3 target states and a 10-step horizon. Set a goal occupancy: visit the three targets evenly (one-third each). Run three planners: (1) greedy (always step toward the nearest target now), (2) random policy, (3) the paper's MCTS with one thousand iterations per step. Measure the final occupancy of each run and compute how close each is to the goal by summing the squared differences between actual occupancy and target occupancy for each state.

occupancy_error, the sum over all states of the squared difference between final occupancy and target occupancy

The MCTS planner should produce an occupancy much closer to the goal than greedy or random, because it can see one step ahead and route toward a balanced set of states.
