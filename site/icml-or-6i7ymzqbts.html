BioFormer: Aligning Spectral Structure Across Subjects

Biomedical signals share the same oscillation patterns across people, but scaled and shifted differently per person — BioFormer aligns these frequency shifts to learn what matters for the task.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

In biomedical time-series (heart rhythms, brain waves, muscle activity), the same medical signal—say, a heartbeat pattern—looks nearly identical across healthy people, but each person's version is scaled up or down and shifted in phase (timing). A naive model learns the person's quirks instead of the shared medical signal, so it fails when tested on new people. BioFormer solves this by explicitly detecting and removing these subject-specific magnitude and phase shifts in the frequency domain (the oscillation patterns), then using conditional normalization to stabilize what remains.

Zero assumption version

Why cross-subject biomedical models fail

In biomedical time-series, a heartbeat or brain-wave pattern is largely the same across all healthy people—the oscillation structure is shared. But each person's body scales and shifts these oscillations differently. Person A's heart might beat 5% faster or with 10% larger voltage swings than Person B, even though the shape is identical, because body size, fitness, and physiology vary naturally across humans.

A naive model trained on People A, B, C learns the average of their quirks instead of the true medical pattern because it has no way to tell which parts of the signal are the shared medical fact versus which parts are just Person A's or B's body. When tested on a new Person D with different scaling, the model's learned weights no longer match, and accuracy plummets because Person D's body has scaled things in a way the training set never showed. The signal structure is there, but hidden under person-specific noise.

In one sentence. BioFormer explicitly measures and removes person-specific frequency magnitude and phase shifts, then stabilizes what is left.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Convert signal to frequency domain

Take the raw time-series (time-domain waveform) and compute its frequency spectrum (Fourier transform), showing how much power each oscillation frequency contains.

Partition frequencies into bands

Group nearby frequencies into contiguous bands (e.g., 0–10 Hz, 10–20 Hz, 20–50 Hz), because subject-specific variability typically affects entire bands, not single frequencies.

Compute band statistics

For each band, calculate the average magnitude (loudness) and phase (timing), treating these as the band's fingerprint.

Generate modulation factors

Use a small neural network to predict correction factors for each band's magnitude and phase, designed to align all subjects toward a canonical spectrum.

Apply conditional normalization

Rescale the encoder's internal representations using statistics computed from each sample itself rather than subject identity, further stabilizing cross-subject variability.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

BioFormer changes the intermediate frequency-domain representation: it applies band-wise modulation factors (scale and phase correction) to align each person's spectrum toward a shared canonical form, so that all subjects' frequency structures converge.

the object it moves  =  frequency-domain amplitudes and phases
The hidden quantity

Spectral drift: the hidden subject-specific variability

measured quantity  =  spectral drift per frequency band

Spectral drift is the subject-dependent magnitude and phase shift that each person's oscillations undergo within a specific frequency band. For example, Person A's heart-rate band (around 1 Hz) might be 20% louder and 15 milliseconds earlier than Person B's, even though both have the same rhythm.

BioFormer estimates spectral drift by computing band-wise power spectra and comparing mean magnitudes and phase angles across subjects within the same label (task class). It does not model drift per individual frequency bin; instead, it works band-wise, treating a band as a coherent unit that drifts together. This is the paper's insight: subject variability is not random noise but structured spectral shifts that can be undone by adaptive modulation.

Why it works

Why BioFormer works: the causal chain

BioFormer works because it makes subject-specific variability explicit and undoes it systematically. Here is the mechanism: Biomedical signals are oscillations, and oscillations live in the frequency domain. When subject A's heart is slightly faster or amplified compared to subject B's, that difference is NOT scattered randomly through the signal—it appears as coherent shifts in specific frequency bands (the bands where that physiological component dominates), because a faster heartbeat changes the frequency content at the heart-rate band, not across all frequencies equally.

By explicitly measuring these band-level magnitudes and phases, the Frequency-Band Alignment Module isolates what is person-specific from what is task-relevant. It then applies learned modulation factors (multiplicative corrections) to each band's amplitude and phase, rotating all subjects' spectra toward a shared reference. Because oscillations in the same frequency band are coupled (they reflect the same physiological event), treating them as a unit is more data-efficient than per-frequency corrections, so the network learns faster and uses fewer weights (the numbers the network adjusts during training).

After alignment in the frequency domain, residual subject-specific structure remains in the encoder's learned representations. Sample Conditional Layer Normalization removes this by rescaling each sample's feature statistics independently (not grouped by subject or label). This breaks the link between a sample's statistics and its source subject, which forces the network to rely on true task-relevant patterns rather than subject quirks because each sample is normalized using only its own statistics, erasing any fingerprint that would reveal who the subject is. Together, FBAM and SCLN eliminate subject variability without erasing the medical signal.

Math after the picture

The equation: band-wise modulation

First in words:

For each frequency band b, compute a modulation factor (amplitude scale and phase rotation) from learned descriptors (magnitude mean and variance, phase mean and variance, energy), then multiply the band's spectrum by this factor to align it.

Then the compact form:

S_aligned[b] = m_amp[b] * S_original[b] + m_phase[b]

Every symbol:

S_original[b]the original spectral magnitude and phase for frequency band b
m_amp[b]learned amplitude modulation factor for band b (scalar, typically 0.8 to 1.2)
m_phase[b]learned phase rotation for band b (in radians)
S_aligned[b]the corrected spectrum for band b after modulation
descriptorsfive per-band statistics: mean magnitude, variance, phase, energy, and peak response
A tiny worked example
Person A: Heart-rate band (1 Hz) has magnitude 100, phase 0 degrees.
Person B: Same band has magnitude 85, phase -20 degrees (both healthy, same task).
Learned modulation for Person B's alignment: m_amp = 100/85 ≈ 1.176, m_phase = +20 degrees.
After modulation: Person B's band becomes 85 * 1.176 ≈ 100 (magnitude matched), phase -20 + 20 = 0 (phase matched).
Both now have the same frequency signature; the task classifier sees the shared medical signal, not the person's quirk.

Modulation factors are not hand-tuned; they come from an MLP that reads the band descriptors (learned during training on subjects A, B, C), so they generalize to new subjects by predicting their unique modulation from their own spectrum.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Aligning away the signal. If all subjects happen to share subject-specific modulation (e.g., all subjects in one demographic have similar cardiac properties), the modulation factors may over-correct and erase a task-relevant pattern. Mitigation: validate that inter-class discrepancy (task separation) remains strong after alignment.
Training data too homogeneous. If the training subjects are very similar (same age, gender, health status), spectral drift is tiny and alignment offers little benefit. BioFormer's advantage grows with subject diversity.
Overfitting the modulation factors. The MLP that predicts modulation factors could learn spurious subject-specific patterns if not kept simple. The paper mitigates this by deriving modulation from only five band-wise statistics (compact representation, which limits what the MLP can memorize) and using dropout (randomly disabling neurons during training to prevent over-fitting).
Done right: test on truly unseen subjects. Ensure validation and test sets split at the subject level (all samples from one subject go together), not at the sample level. This forces generalization to new subject-specific spectral patterns.

What a runnable demo should prove

Scale and phase-shift each component per subject (e.g., Person A's heartbeat is 20% louder; Person B's is 10% slower). Train a baseline CNN and BioFormer on subjects 1–5, both predicting a binary label (healthy vs. stressed). Measure accuracy on training subjects and on the held-out test subjects (Persons 6 and 7).
