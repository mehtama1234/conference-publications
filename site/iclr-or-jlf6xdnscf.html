STORM: Synergistic Cross-Scale Spatio-Temporal Modeling for Weather Forecasting

Split weather patterns into separate models for each spatial and temporal scale, then combine them to forecast how the atmosphere evolves at all scales together.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Weather happens at many scales at once: huge planetary wind patterns, regional storms, and tiny local gusts. Most weather models train one neural network on everything mixed together, which means they struggle to learn how each scale actually works. STORM separates the atmosphere into scale-specific patterns, learns what each one does independently, then ties them back together to make predictions.

Zero assumption version

Weather patterns hide inside mixed scales

Weather happens because air moves and temperature shifts, but the reason it moves is different at each scale. A hurricane (scale: 1,000 km, timescale: days) forms because of sea-surface temperature gradients and planetary rotation. A thunderstorm (scale: 10 km, timescale: hours) forms because warm air rises fast enough to overcome Earth's gravity. A breeze (scale: 100 m, timescale: minutes) forms because the ground heats unevenly. All three happen at the same time, and a naive model — one neural network trained on raw gridded atmosphere data — must learn all three mechanisms at once, tangled together. Each scale's weights (the internal numbers the network uses to process data) end up capturing a blurry mixture.

What happens next is a beginner might hope that throwing more data or bigger networks at the raw mixture would help. It does not, because the network has no way to cleanly separate a hurricane pattern from a storm pattern when they arrive in the same input tensor (a table of numbers). The network's gradients (the directions to nudge the weights to fit the data) get pulled in conflicting directions — nudges that help the network recognize hurricanes hurt its ability to recognize storms, and vice versa, because a single set of weights cannot simultaneously capture both fast hurricane-scale and slow storm-scale dynamics cleanly. The result: the model learns poorly at all scales.

In one sentence. STORM disentangles the atmosphere into separate scale-specific models, then combines their predictions so each scale learns cleanly.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Decompose

Break the atmospheric data into separate frequency bands: coarse grids capture planetary-scale patterns, fine grids capture local turbulence.

Step 2: Per-scale modeling

Train one neural network branch on each scale; each learns its own temporal dynamics without interference.

Step 3: Scale-specific predictions

Each branch generates the next state of the atmosphere at its own scale and timescale independently.

Step 4: Coherence constraint

Enforce consistency: coarse predictions must align with the average of fine predictions; no contradictions.

Step 5: Multi-resolution output

Combine predictions across scales into a single, coherent forecast at all resolutions from global to local.

What the paper changes

The thing being changed

STORM changes how the input data is prepared before the neural network sees it. Instead of feeding raw atmospheric grids directly, it first separates them into multiple scale-specific decompositions — coarser grids for planetary patterns, finer grids for local details. Each decomposition is a different view of the same atmosphere, but at a different level of zoom.

The hidden quantity

Scale-specific gradient coherence

measured quantity  =  per-scale learning consistency

The hidden quantity STORM measures is the coherence — or mutual consistency — between predictions at different scales. When you predict the next atmospheric state, you are making estimates at coarse resolution (e.g., air pressure on a 200-km grid) and fine resolution (e.g., air pressure on a 25-km grid). If you naively average the coarse prediction, it should match the coarse version of the fine prediction, otherwise the scales contradict each other. Without enforcing this constraint, each scale-specific network learns independently and produces conflicting forecasts.

STORM estimates this quantity during training by comparing the predicted coarse field with the spatially averaged fine field. When they diverge, the loss (a penalty applied to the network) grows. The paper does not spell out a single formula but uses a multi-resolution consistency term to keep forecasts coherent.

Why it works

Why breaking scales apart makes forecasts better

The mechanism is simple: when a neural network learns from mixed-scale data, its weights end up capturing a blurred average of all scales, because the gradients (directions to improve) point in conflicting directions. A weight adjustment that helps it recognize a hurricane hurts its ability to recognize a breeze. By splitting the data into separate scales first, each scale-specific network receives cleaner input, so its gradients all point in coherent directions. A weight that recognizes fast-changing local convection never needs to unlearn hurricane-scale dynamics.

Then, when making predictions, STORM ties the scales back together with a coherence constraint. After each scale-specific branch generates its forecast, STORM checks: does the spatially-averaged fine-scale forecast match the coarse-scale forecast? If not, the loss penalizes the mismatch, steering both branches toward a unified answer. This constraint is the glue: it forces the scales to cooperate even though they learned separately. The result is that each scale captures its own true dynamics (hurricanes, storms, breezes) cleanly and independently, and then the coherence term ensures they combine into a single, self-consistent weather forecast.

Math after the picture

The coherence constraint equation

First in words:

total loss equals scale-specific prediction errors plus a coherence penalty that grows when coarse predictions contradict spatially-averaged fine predictions.

Then the compact form:

L_total = sum_i L_i(pred_i) + lambda * ||avg_spatial(pred_fine) - pred_coarse||^2

Every symbol:

L_i(pred_i)prediction error for scale i (how far off the forecast is from true data)
pred_ithe forecast at scale i
avg_spatial(pred_fine)the fine-scale forecast spatially averaged to coarse resolution
pred_coarsethe forecast made at coarse resolution
lambdahow strongly to penalize scale mismatches
||...||^2squared difference (a measure of distance between two numbers)
A tiny worked example
Suppose coarse grid predicts temperature 15C at a 200-km region.
Fine grid (25-km resolution) predicts 14C, 16C, 14C, 16C in the four 25-km cells.
Average of fine = (14+16+14+16)/4 = 15C.
Coherence mismatch = (15 - 15)^2 = 0 (no penalty, scales agree).
If fine had predicted 12C, 18C, 14C, 16C instead, average = 15C still, so still 0 penalty.
If fine predicted 10C, 12C, 14C, 10C instead, average = 11.5C, then mismatch = (15-11.5)^2 = 12.25 (strong penalty, scales contradict).

The equation enforces that when you zoom out from the fine grid, you should see exactly what the coarse grid predicts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Decomposition too fine. If you break the atmosphere into too many scales, each branch gets too little data to learn from, and overfits (memorizes noise instead of learning patterns). There is a trade-off: more scales means more detail but also more numbers the network must tune.
Coherence penalty too weak. If lambda (the strength of the coherence term) is too small, the scale-specific branches ignore the constraint and learn independently, producing contradictory forecasts. The forecast might predict a hurricane at coarse resolution but fine details that average to no hurricane.
Coherence penalty too strong. If lambda is too large, the coherence term dominates, and the scale-specific branches sacrifice prediction accuracy to keep scales perfectly aligned. The result is a coherent but less accurate forecast.
Decomposition chosen wisely. Pick scale breakpoints that match real atmospheric boundaries (e.g., Rossby radius, boundary-layer height) and set lambda by cross-validation. The method works when decomposition captures real physics.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny weather forecast experiment on a synthetic or small real dataset. Generate random multi-scale noise (e.g., large planetary sinusoids plus small local noise) as the training data. Train two models: (1) a baseline that takes all scales as input and predicts the next state, and (2) STORM that disentangles into two scales, trains separate branches, and enforces coherence. Measure prediction error at each scale separately and the overall forecast accuracy. Also measure how often the two scales contradict (e.g., coarse prediction versus averaged fine prediction). The demo should make it impossible to miss that STORM trades off between per-scale accuracy (depending on lambda) but keeps scale contradictions low.
