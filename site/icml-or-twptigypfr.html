DISSOLVR: Interpretable Solubility Prediction

DISSOLVR predicts how well a molecule dissolves in liquid by grounding predictions in real chemistry properties instead of learned black-box features, making the model's reasoning transparent.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Deep learning excels at predicting molecular properties, but the models are black boxes — you cannot ask why the model thinks a molecule will dissolve. DISSOLVR breaks that pattern: it predicts solubility (how much of a drug or pollutant will dissolve in water) by building a model that reasons with chemistry knowhow, not just pattern-matching. The trick: encode molecules into real physical properties first (like molecular weight or surface charge), then let a simple model learn the rules.

Zero assumption version

Why deep learning models hide their reasoning

Predicting solubility is hard because molecules are intricate: a tiny change in structure (adding one atom) can change how much dissolves dramatically. Deep neural networks (layered models that learn patterns from data) do predict well, but they work like a sealed box. The model sees a molecule, thinks through dozens of hidden layers, and spits out an answer — but there is no way to understand or check its logic.

For pharmaceutical scientists and environmental regulators, this is a huge problem. If the model says a drug will dissolve, they want to know why: is it because of the molecular weight? The charge? Something else? A hidden-layer model cannot tell them. So even if it is accurate, they distrust it. And when it fails on a new type of molecule (one from a completely different chemical family, far from the training data), nobody knows if the failure is a fluke or a real blind spot.

The problem in one sentence. Deep models predict solubility accurately but hide their reasoning, making them hard to trust or debug.

What it actually does

Encode the molecule

Convert each molecule into a list of known chemistry properties (weight, surface area, charge).

Feed to a simple model

A small, transparent model (often just a formula with a few multipliers) learns from those properties.

Measure solubility

The model outputs how many grams dissolve per liter of liquid (or a classification: yes or no).

Explain each prediction

Because the model sees real properties, you can say: this dissolves well because weight is low.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of letting a neural network learn arbitrary features from scratch, DISSOLVR constrains the model: inputs must be physical descriptors (real chemistry properties that chemists already know and trust), and the model itself must stay transparent.

the object it moves  =  molecular features
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how chemistry knowledge gets baked in

measured quantity  =  Structural invariance through descriptors

The hidden quantity is how much of the model's smarts comes from real chemistry versus pure pattern-matching. In a typical deep network, the model invents its own features (learned patterns), which are often uninterpretable and fragile. DISSOLVR measures success differently: it checks whether the model's predictions depend on stable, real physical properties (weight, charge, polarity) that carry across different liquids and conditions. A descriptor like molecular weight does not change whether you dissolve in water or oil; because the descriptor itself is the same, any rule the model learns with it (higher weight tends toward lower solubility) carries over to new contexts, so predictions built on invariant descriptors generalize better than predictions built on learned patterns that were optimized for one specific training set.

The paper estimates this by testing on molecules from completely new chemical families (ones not seen during training) and seeing if the model still predicts well. If it does, structural invariance is strong — the model learned rules that reflect real chemistry, not just memorized patterns.

Why it works

Why grounding in physics beats pure learning

The core idea works like this. A deep neural network has millions of tiny adjustable knobs (weights); after training on solubility data, each knob is set to some value. But those values are entangled — you cannot ask what any one knob does, or why the model chose its setting. Moreover, when the network sees a type of molecule it has never met, the trained knobs often fail because they overfit to training examples.

DISSOLVR side-steps this by reducing the number of knobs and anchoring them to things chemists already understand. Instead of raw pixel-like numbers from the molecule, the model sees chemistry properties (like: the sum of all atomic weights, the number of hydrogen bonds, the spatial spread). These properties are curated to match what chemists know matters for solubility. Now the model has only a few knobs to turn, and each one is tied to a piece of real science. When you adjust a knob, you are turning up or down the weight of a physical property, not tinkering with a hidden layer. The transparency is immediate. And when a new molecule arrives, its properties are computed the same way, so the knobs still point in the right direction because they are rooted in chemistry.

Math after the picture

The equation: a weighted sum of chemistry properties

First in words:

Solubility is the sum of each chemistry property times a learned weight for that property, plus a baseline offset.

Then the compact form:

solubility = baseline + w1*prop1 + w2*prop2 + w3*prop3 + ... + wn*propn

Every symbol:

solubilitygrams dissolved per liter (or a yes or no classification)
prop_ithe i-th chemistry property (e.g., molecular weight)
w_ithe learned weight: how much prop_i influences solubility
baselinethe prediction when all properties are zero; captures average solubility
A tiny worked example with three properties
Suppose we predict solubility using: molecular weight, number of hydrogen bonds, and surface polarity.
Example molecule: weight 180 g per mole, 3 hydrogen bonds, polarity 0.8.
Model learned: w_weight = -0.01, w_hbonds = 0.5, w_polarity = 2.0, baseline = 3.0.
Prediction = 3.0 + (-0.01) times 180 + 0.5 times 3 + 2.0 times 0.8.
= 3.0 minus 1.8 plus 1.5 plus 1.6 = 4.3 grams per liter.
Interpretation: baseline is 3 grams, but higher weight hurts (heavier means less soluble, so -0.01 steers down), more H-bonds help (plus 0.5), and higher polarity helps (plus 2.0).

The weights encode chemistry rules; each term is a lever that twists an aspect of the molecule's structure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad descriptor choices. If the chemistry properties fed to the model are poor (e.g., they do not actually correlate with solubility), the model has nothing to learn. A chemist must choose descriptors wisely.
Descriptor computation errors. If the code that calculates molecular weight or charge has a bug, every prediction is garbage. The model's correctness depends entirely on accurate property extraction.
Overfitting to descriptor space. The model can still memorize if there are too many properties and too few examples. More descriptors mean more knobs to turn, so you need enough training molecules.
Done right. A curator gathers properties that chemistry theory says matter for solubility. Code is tested. Training set is large enough. Validation on molecules from far-away chemical families confirms the rules generalize.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a solubility predictor on a reference dataset (e.g., a subset of molecules with measured solubility in water and octanol, common in pharmaceutical data). Train DISSOLVR and a standard deep network on 80 percent of the data. Test both on 20 percent held-out molecules, then on a disjoint set of completely new chemical families (different functional groups, different ring systems). Measure accuracy on all three.

Out-of-distribution accuracy = (correct predictions on new chemical families) divided by (total new family predictions). Goal: OOD accuracy within 5 points of in-distribution accuracy, versus deep network OOD accuracy 20 or more points lower.

The demo must show that DISSOLVR's accuracy does not collapse when it sees unfamiliar chemistry, whereas black-box models do.
