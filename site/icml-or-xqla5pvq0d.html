Dynamics Are Learned, Not Told: Latent Geometry for Zero-Shot Control

Instead of asking a robot to name physical properties to adapt its skills, the paper learns a smooth map of control spaces that automatically handles unseen dynamics shifts.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a robot learns to move, it works well for one specific environment. If that environment changes—a joint becomes looser, the wind picks up, a tool gets heavier—the learned skills fail. Usually, engineers fix this by identifying and encoding the new parameters (like mass, friction) into the model. This paper instead learns a geometric space of all possible dynamics, using smoothness as the key principle, so robots adapt to new worlds without being told what changed.

Zero assumption version

Why naming parameters breaks adaptation

A robot trained to walk works great in its original setting. But when the ground changes (wet, sandy, steep), or the robot itself shifts (battery dies, joint rusts), the walking skill dies too.

The standard fix: an engineer lists the physical things that changed (weight, friction, motor torque) and encodes them into the model before retraining. This rigid list breaks: what if the change is something you didn't anticipate, such as a structural crack in the leg? What if multiple things shift at once in a way the list never imagined?

The problem: a closed list of parameters cannot capture all possible world changes. The paper learns a continuous smooth space of all possible dynamics instead, so adaptation happens automatically.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the trajectory encoder—the neural network that watches the robot move and outputs a representation of the current world state. Instead of outputting a list of named numbers (parameters like mass and friction), it outputs a location on a smooth, continuous space where nearby worlds map to nearby points.

the object it moves  =  trajectory encoder
The hidden quantity

Lipschitz smoothness: the hidden quantity that controls adaptation

measured quantity  =  Lipschitz smoothness of the encoder

Lipschitz smoothness is a formal measure of how abruptly a function changes. A smooth function produces only gradual output shifts when the input changes slightly; a jagged function produces wild jumps. The paper proves mathematically that the robot's ability to adapt to new dynamics depends on how smooth the encoder is.

The paper estimates and controls this smoothness by using a specific learning signal—a multi-positive contrastive objective—that encourages the encoder to map similar trajectories close together and dissimilar ones far apart. This creates smoothness automatically without needing to label what properties differ.

Why it works

Why smoothness solves the adaptation problem

If two worlds are physically similar, a smooth encoder places them close together on the geometric space. The robot's policy, trained on many points in this space, naturally knows how to behave in the region between them. When the robot encounters a new world for the first time, the encoder places it somewhere on the space; the policy then uses nearby trained examples to guide its action, blending behaviors smoothly.

This is mathematically provable: the error the robot makes in a new world is bounded by how smooth the encoder is. A jagged encoder creates dead zones where no training data exists nearby—the policy must guess. A smooth encoder ensures that every plausible world lives in a well-trained neighborhood, so the policy can interpolate from nearby trained examples rather than extrapolating blind.

The learning signal that builds smoothness

First in words:

For each trajectory sample, pull all similar trajectories close on the map and push dissimilar ones far. The penalty is zero if similar pairs sit within a small distance and dissimilar pairs sit outside it.

Then the compact form:

L = mean over positive pairs of max(0, distance - margin) + mean over negative pairs of max(0, margin - distance)

Every symbol:

positive pairstwo trajectories from similar or same dynamics
negative pairstrajectories from clearly different dynamics
distancehow far apart the two encoded outputs sit
marginthe target separation (e.g., 0.1 in encoding space)
Lthe total learning penalty
A tiny worked example
Positive pair (similar worlds): distance = 0.05, margin = 0.1. Penalty = max(0, 0.05 - 0.1) = 0. Good.
Positive pair (similar worlds): distance = 0.15, margin = 0.1. Penalty = max(0, 0.15 - 0.1) = 0.05. Bad, pull closer.
Negative pair (different worlds): distance = 0.08, margin = 0.1. Penalty = max(0, 0.1 - 0.08) = 0.02. Bad, push apart.
Negative pair (different worlds): distance = 0.3, margin = 0.1. Penalty = max(0, 0.1 - 0.3) = 0. Good.

This loss creates a space where similar trajectories cluster close and dissimilar ones stay far apart, giving the encoder its smoothness.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Covariate shift: if test worlds are far outside the training set. If all training dynamics are mild (small changes in mass) but a test world has a severe structural failure, the encoder has never seen that region of the space. Even with smoothness, blending nearby examples fails when nothing in training is actually nearby.
Insufficient trajectory diversity during training. If trajectories are short or repetitive, the encoder cannot distinguish between subtly different dynamics. The learned space becomes crumpled and loses smoothness where it matters most.
Margin tuning. The margin parameter controls how tightly similar worlds cluster versus how spread out different ones are. If too tight, the space becomes crowded and new worlds have no room to interpolate cleanly. If too loose, the space becomes sparse and smoothness is lost.
Done right: train on diverse dynamics, use trajectories long enough to reveal dynamics clearly, and tune the margin by testing. Validate smoothness on a held-out subset before deploying to new worlds.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple demo where a robot learns to reach a target in multiple physics engines (e.g., different friction, mass, damping in MuJoCo). Train the encoder on 20 random dynamics and freeze it. Then, on 5 new held-out dynamics the encoder never trained on, show that the policy adapts instantly and successfully. Measure success rate as the fraction of new dynamics where the robot reaches the target within 50 timesteps.

adaptation success = (# new dynamics where target reached) / (# new dynamics tested)

The demo must show that zero-shot adaptation to unseen worlds works because the smooth space lets the policy interpolate from nearby training examples rather than guess blind.
