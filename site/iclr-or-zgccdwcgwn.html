AgentGym-RL: An Open-Source Framework to Train LLM Agents for Long-Horizon Decision Making via Multi-Turn RL

Train agents on short interaction chains first, then gradually lengthen the horizon so long multi-turn behavior becomes stable enough to learn.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Training an agent for long multi-turn tasks is hard because the model must explore, act in the environment, and connect a final outcome back to many earlier choices. AgentGym-RL provides a common framework for this setting and adds staged horizon growth, called ScalingInter-RL, so an agent first masters short interactions and then extends them into longer decision chains.

Zero assumption version

Why long-horizon agent training is unstable

A long-horizon agent may need dozens of interactions before success or failure becomes clear. Early actions matter, but the reward arrives much later. Exploration is also difficult because the environment may require external actions such as tool calls, browsing, or multi-step planning before any useful signal appears.

If training starts directly on the full task horizon, the agent spends many episodes wandering through long action chains with weak learning signal. That makes policy updates noisy and unstable, so progress is slow or stops altogether.

In one sentence. AgentGym-RL makes long-horizon training practical by first teaching the agent on shorter interaction chains and only then expanding to deeper multi-turn tasks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training curriculum for long-horizon agents. Rather than training at full depth from the first episode, it grows the interaction horizon in stages.

the object it moves  =  the allowed interaction horizon during training
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: stable credit across many turns

measured quantity  =  how well the training signal still points to the actions that mattered

Long-horizon training fails when the learning signal no longer points clearly to the actions that caused success. The hidden quantity is how much useful credit still reaches those earlier actions.

ScalingInter-RL raises this quantity by beginning with short interaction chains. In short tasks, the reward is close enough to the action that the update points to the actions that caused success. After the agent has learned these short behaviors, the horizon is expanded, so longer tasks inherit a stronger starting policy instead of random exploration because the agent already knows useful early moves.

Why it works

Why staged horizon growth helps

The training curriculum works because a short successful interaction is easier to discover and reinforce than a long one. Once the agent has learned how to gather evidence, call tools, or navigate the environment over a few turns, those behaviors can be reused when the horizon expands.

The framework itself also matters because it standardizes environments, algorithms, and evaluation. That helps researchers compare methods under the same setup. But the central causal point is the staged horizon schedule: it lowers early instability by keeping the reward close to the actions that caused it, then increases depth only after a workable policy has formed.

A tiny worked example
Start with horizon 2, meaning the agent is judged after at most two actions.
After it reaches a target success rate, expand to horizon 4, then 8, then 16.
At horizon 16, the agent is no longer learning from scratch because the shorter successful behaviors already exist.
That staged growth keeps updates more stable than jumping straight from 0 to 16.

The paper may use a more detailed schedule, but the main idea is gradual horizon expansion after earlier stages become reliable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Early stages can teach the wrong habit. If short tasks reward shortcuts that fail on longer tasks, the curriculum may lock in behavior that later stages must undo.
Horizon growth can be too fast. If the schedule expands before the current policy is reliable, the training returns to the same instability that the curriculum was meant to avoid.
Framework breadth can hide environment mismatch. A unified framework is useful, but some environments may need custom reward shaping or action spaces. A one-size setup can under-serve those tasks.
Done right. The schedule helps most when each stage preserves behaviors that remain useful at the next horizon, because then longer tasks inherit real skills instead of noise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build an interactive task where an agent must gather information over several turns before answering. Train one policy directly on the full horizon and another with horizon stages such as 2, 4, 8, and 16 turns.

Track training stability, success rate, and how many episodes reach productive interaction rather than wandering.

stable_success = successful episodes after convergence / total evaluation episodes

The demo should show that staged horizon growth improves long-turn performance because the agent first learns short useful behaviors and then extends them, so later training starts from a stronger policy rather than random exploration.
