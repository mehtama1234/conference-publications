Accelerating Benchmarking via Structure-aware Core-set Selection

Pick a tiny representative slice of brain imaging data that lets you rank models accurately without testing them all.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Scientists need to test many brain imaging analysis methods on huge datasets, but that takes too long. This paper does something smarter: it picks a small, carefully chosen subset of the data that ranks the methods the same way as using all the data. The key is to find samples where brain structure is stable during learning, and to spread those samples across different types of brain patterns.

Zero assumption version

Why testing hundreds of methods takes forever

Brain scientists use brain imaging scans (fMRI) to map how brain regions talk to each other. To understand which mapping method works best, they need to test dozens or hundreds of methods on huge datasets. Testing one method on one dataset takes an hour. Testing 200 methods takes 200 hours.

A naive first try: test every method on the whole dataset. That works, but it is so slow that scientists never do it in practice — so they never really know which methods are best. The bottleneck is that there are so many method-dataset combinations (the paper calls this the combinatorial explosion).

The paper solves this by: Pick 10% of the data (a core-set) that ranks methods the same way as 100% of the data, so you get accurate rankings in one tenth the time.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn each scan's brain pattern

A transformer (a machine learning model) reads each scan's brain structure and learns how each region connects to others.

Score structural stability

Measure how much the learned brain pattern shifts during training. Stable scans get high scores; noisy ones get low scores.

Rank by stability

Sort all scans by their stability score and keep only the top 10%, which represent foundational brain patterns.

Spread samples across brain types

Within the stable samples, add a spreading step (kernel density estimation) so the core-set covers different brain pattern clusters.

Test methods on the core-set

Rank all methods using only this tiny core-set. The ranking matches the ranking from the full dataset.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which scans you pick. Instead of randomly selecting scans or picking based on how a single method trains on them, it selects based on structural stability — how reliably the learned brain patterns hold up during training.

the object it moves  =  core-set membership selection
The hidden quantity

Structural Perturbation Score: the stability measure

measured quantity  =  Structural Perturbation Score

The hidden quantity is the Structural Perturbation Score (SPS). It measures how much a brain scan's learned structure (the connectivity pattern) shifts from one training epoch (round) to the next. Think of it like watching a photograph: if the same scene photographed twice looks almost identical, the stability is high. If small details jump around a lot, stability is low.

The paper estimates SPS by taking the learned brain pattern at each training epoch, comparing it to the pattern from the previous epoch, and measuring the difference. Scans with low differences (stable patterns) are the ones selected for the core-set.

Plain reading The Structural Perturbation Score asks: when the model learns from this scan, how much does its understanding of the brain structure jump around? Stable scans are trustworthy representatives of brain patterns.
Technical reading SPS measures the Frobenius norm (the length of a multidimensional difference, a list of numbers) between the learned structure matrix at epoch e and epoch e-1, summed over all training epochs. The formula shows: take each epoch's learned pattern, subtract the previous epoch's pattern, measure how long that difference is, square it, and average across all epochs.
Why it works

Why ranking is preserved when you pick stable scans

The core insight is that noisy, unstable scans throw off method rankings. When a method trains on a scan where the learned brain structure bounces around, the method's final performance is partly luck (which random noise hit during training) instead of whether the method is fundamentally good. Stable scans filter out that noise.

Here is the causal chain: (1) Each brain imaging method learns by comparing its guess of brain structure to the real data. (2) If the data is noisy, a weak method can accidentally guess right once, or get fooled by random patterns. (3) If you pick scans where the learned structure is rock-solid during training, you get a fair test — the method either actually understands the brain pattern or it does not. (4) This fairness is preserved: the methods' relative ranking on stable scans matches their ranking on all scans. Adding the diversity step ensures you are not just picking a single type of stable brain pattern — you cover all the archetypes.

The stability score equation

First in words:

The Structural Perturbation Score (SPS) is the average difference between learned brain structures at consecutive training rounds.

Then the compact form:

SPS(X) = (1/L) sum_e=1^L || A(e)(X) - A(e-1)(X) ||_F^2

Every symbol:

Xa single brain scan
A(e)(X)the learned brain connectivity pattern at training epoch e
A(e-1)(X)the learned pattern at the previous epoch
||...||_F^2the squared length of the difference (Frobenius norm)
Ltotal number of training epochs
A tiny worked example
Scan A: learned structures at epochs differ by 0.2, 0.1, 0.3, 0.1 per epoch. Sum = 0.7. Average = 0.7 / 4 = 0.175.
Scan B: learned structures at epochs differ by 2.0, 1.8, 2.1, 1.9 per epoch. Sum = 7.8. Average = 7.8 / 4 = 1.95.
Scan A (SPS=0.175) is stable, selected. Scan B (SPS=1.95) is noisy, skipped. Methods tested on Scan A ranking are more reliable.

Lower SPS means the learned brain patterns are stable, so the core-set selects low-SPS scans as representatives.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Core-set is too small to span brain pattern diversity. If you only keep the top 5% most stable scans, you might miss rare but important brain patterns. Solution: use the density-aware sampling step to force spreading.
Stability during training does not mean good for ranking. A scan might be stable but not representative of the real variety in brains. Solution: the contrastive learning step (training the model with a loss that rewards learning similar patterns from similar scans) ensures stable scans are truly representative.
Method designed for single-model benchmarking fails here. Old core-set methods optimize for helping one model train faster by minimizing the single model's error. Ranking many methods fairly is different — you need samples where all methods shine or struggle together, so their differences are clear. A sample that speeds up method A might hide method B's true strength, because optimizing for one model's loss steers toward different samples than optimizing for multi-method ranking consistency.
Done right: stability plus diversity. Combining structural stability (SPS) with diversity sampling ensures the core-set is both rock-solid (methods trained on it learn clearly) and representative (it covers all major brain pattern types).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment on publicly available brain imaging data: (1) Take a real fMRI dataset with at least 50 samples and 5 different brain connectivity methods. (2) Compute method rankings on the full dataset (ground truth). (3) Apply SCLCS to select a 10% core-set. (4) Compute method rankings on only the core-set. (5) Measure how well the core-set ranking matches the full ranking using a rank correlation metric (the paper uses nDCG@k, which scores how many of the top-k methods are ranked the same).

nDCG@k = (gain from correctly ranked methods at position k) / (ideal ranking gain)

The demo is successful if the core-set (10% of data) ranks the methods with at least 90 percent consistency with the full-data ranking. The paper shows 23 percent improvement over baselines in this metric.
