Uncovering and steering racial bias in healthcare LLMs with sparse autoencoders

By finding the hidden circuits in a language model that fire when it sees a patient's race, you can turn them on and off to see how much they wrongly influence medical predictions.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Language models used in hospitals learn to spot a patient's race from medical notes, even when doctors do not mention it directly. This happens because the training data contains examples where race got tangled up with medical outcomes. The problem: you cannot see where in the model this tangling happens, so you cannot remove it. This paper finds the exact places where the model secretly stores race information and shows how to flip those switches on and off to measure (and reduce) bias.

Zero assumption version

Bias hiding inside a black box

When a language model reads a hospital note, it picks up many clues: symptoms, lab results, medication history. But it also picks up something dangerous: the patient's race, even if no one wrote it down. Hospitals know race affects outcomes—some conditions are diagnosed differently in Black versus white patients. The model learned this pattern from training data, so it now bakes racial bias into its medical advice.

A naive doctor might try to remove race mentions from notes before feeding them to the model. But the model reads between the lines: certain symptoms, diseases, and social situations are statistically linked to race in the training data. The model learns these invisible connections. Deleting the word 'African American' does not help, because the model still infers race from context. You need to find where the model stores this race knowledge inside its brain.

In one sentence. Find the exact hidden neurons (latents) in the model that fire when it sees race signals; then steer them up and down to measure and remove bias.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Find race neurons

Run hospital notes through the model, collect all its internal activations (thoughts), feed them into a simple linear classifier that tries to predict patient race. The neurons it relies on most are the race neurons.

Verify they really encode race

Look at example notes that make each race neuron fire. Does a neuron fire on 'African American' and 'incarceration' but not 'aortic valve'? Good sign.

Turn the dial up and down

Modify the activation levels of a specific race neuron by a fixed amount, reconstruct the model's internal state, and re-run the model. Watch predictions change.

Measure the bias

Compare predictions when the race neuron is at normal, high, and zero levels. The difference between high and zero is how much race alone influences the risk score.

Try to reduce bias by removing the race signal

Zero out the race neuron in real predictions and see if bias goes down. In simple artificial tasks it works because the race neuron is separate from medical concepts; in real clinical decisions it often fails because the race neuron is tangled with actual disease patterns that differ between racial groups.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes the firing levels of specific neurons that encode race information. These are not the output predictions (those stay the same); these are the hidden internal thoughts (called latent variables) that the model uses before making its final decision. By turning these hidden race neurons up and down, the paper measures exactly how much they influence medical judgments.

the object it moves  =  hidden race neurons (latents)
The hidden quantity

The race-encoder latents

measured quantity  =  neurons that fire for race

A latent (short for latent variable) is one neuron out of thousands in the model's hidden layers. This paper identifies latents that activate strongly when the model sees race-linked signals in patient notes. They are hidden because the model never mentions them in its output; they are pure internal machinery. The paper finds them by training a simple logistic regression classifier (just a weighted sum and a threshold) on top of the model's middle-layer neurons, tuned to predict patient race. The neurons the classifier weights most heavily are the race-encoding latents.

The paper measures these latents by running hospital notes through the model and recording the firing strength (a single number) for each latent. It also uses a technique called Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), which work like a careful filing system: they take the raw mess of internal signals and sort them into a smaller set of interpretable files (the latents) where each specialist (different latent) is best at noticing one specific pattern.

Why it works

Why turning race neurons up and down reveals bias

The model's prediction for risk depends on all its internal neurons firing together. Most neurons are irrelevant to race: they care about symptoms, test results, medical history. But a few neurons (the race-encoding latents) fire when they detect race. These latents do not directly decide the risk score; they feed into later layers that aggregate all the signals. When you artificially boost a race neuron's activation, you trick those later layers into receiving stronger race signals than they normally would. The later layers respond by adjusting their predictions in favor of patterns they learned to associate with that race.

The magic is that this artificial boost proves causality. If lowering a race neuron reduces bias, then raising it must be what normally causes the bias, because you changed only one neuron while holding everything else the same; nothing else could have changed the prediction. Without this causal intervention, you only have correlation (race neurons and biased predictions go together), and correlation could be backwards: maybe the bias causes the race neurons to fire, not the other way around. By dialing the race neurons up and down, you isolate their causal effect on predictions. The resulting difference in risk scores is pure bias, with everything else held constant.

Math after the picture

The steering formula

First in words:

For each race latent at each position in the note, add alpha times the original activation strength. Alpha controls how much you amplify the race signal.

Then the compact form:

z_prime_i = z_i + (1 if i == r else 0) * alpha * z_max

Every symbol:

z_iactivation strength of latent i in the SAE
z_prime_iadjusted activation strength after steering
rthe index of the race latent you are boosting
alphasteering factor: how much to amplify (0 = no steering, 1 = double the signal)
z_maxthe maximum activation of the race latent across all tokens in the note
A tiny worked example
Say the race latent fires with a max strength of 10.0 across the patient note.
Normal steering: z_prime = z + 1.0 * 10.0 = z + 10.0. You add back a full copy of the original signal.
High steering with alpha = 2.0: z_prime = z + 2.0 * 10.0 = z + 20.0. You double the race signal.
Low steering with alpha = 0.5: z_prime = z + 0.5 * 10.0 = z + 5.0. You add half the race signal.
Zero steering with alpha = 0.0: z_prime = z + 0.0 * 10.0 = z. You remove the race signal entirely.

The formula does not change the prediction directly; it changes the internal activation level, which the model then processes to produce a new prediction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Latents entangle race with medical concepts Some latents fire both on race signals AND on real medical concepts that differ between racial groups (e.g., certain diseases are diagnosed more often in one group). When you zero out the race latent to remove bias, you also remove useful medical information, hurting prediction quality.
Works on toy tasks, fails on real medicine In artificial single-condition vignettes (Does this patient have gestational hypertension?), steering away the race latent improves fairness because the model only needs a few isolated signals to answer and race is not truly entangled with medical concepts. But on realistic multi-condition discharge summaries with complex histories, race-linked words describe actual disease patterns that differ between racial groups due to diagnostic disparities, environmental factors, and social determinants in the training data, so the race latent carries real medical information and removing it hurts prediction accuracy.
Chain-of-thought explanations hide the steering Even when you boost the race latent and the model's risk prediction changes dramatically, the model's written explanation (its chain-of-thought reasoning) does not mention that race influenced it because the model's text-generation process does not have direct access to internal latent firing patterns; the model only sees its own raw output scores, so it constructs plausible-sounding but false explanations that rationalize the biased prediction without acknowledging the actual causal driver. The reasoning text is unfaithful to what the internal race neurons are actually doing.
Used honestly in limited scope The method works best as a diagnostic tool: identify where bias lives and measure it causally. As a bias-mitigation tool in real clinical settings with entangled concepts, it has limits, which the paper acknowledges.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model (or take a pretrained one) and train an SAE on its hidden layer activations. Then train a logistic regression classifier on top of the SAE latents to predict race from clinical text. Identify the top latent with the highest weight. Take a set of test patient notes, run them through the model, apply the steering formula with different alpha values, and measure how the model's risk predictions change. Plot a graph showing risk score (y-axis) versus alpha steering factor (x-axis). The slope of the line is pure causal bias: how much race alone moves the prediction.

bias_slope = (risk_at_alpha_2 - risk_at_alpha_0) / 2

A steep positive slope proves the race latent causally increases the risk predictions for Black patients. A flat or negative slope would mean race latents are not actually driving the bias.
