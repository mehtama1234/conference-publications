Proving how neural networks learn

Theoretical analysis of deep learning through mechanisms like implicit bias, gradient descent convergence, and the internal structure of learned representations.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Neural networks are the dominant model in machine learning, yet their theoretical foundations are surprisingly shaky. When you train a neural network by adjusting its weights using gradient descent — a simple algorithm that repeatedly moves in the direction that reduces error — it somehow finds weights that generalize. But why? The network has millions or billions of weights, thousands of possible local optima, and an enormous space of possible solutions. Gradient descent should get stuck, yet it often finds good solutions. This subtheme asks: what actually happens inside a neural network during training, and why does this process lead to good predictions?

The challenge is that neural networks are nonconvex — they have many hills and valleys in the space of possible solutions, not one single optimal point. Standard optimization theory cannot guarantee gradient descent will find a global optimum. Yet empirically, networks trained on random data often find terrible solutions (high error), while networks trained on real data find good solutions (low error). This suggests something about the structure of real data guides learning. Additionally, the sheer overparameterization — networks often have more weights than training examples — should lead to overfitting, yet somehow regularization (techniques that penalize complexity) and the structure of gradient descent itself prevent it.

Researchers prove statements about how gradient descent updates the network's internal representations, showing that it implicitly biases toward simple or smooth solutions. They study implicit regularization — the way an algorithm's mechanics (not explicit penalties) naturally avoid overfitting. They analyze how the network's early layers learn low-level features (edges, textures) and deeper layers combine them into high-level concepts (objects, animals). They examine specific architectures like convolutional neural networks (which exploit spatial structure in images) to understand why these designs work. The work also includes characterizing which functions a network can represent (expressivity) and which functions a given training algorithm can find (learnability), closing the gap between what is theoretically possible and what is practically learnable.

How the work breaks down

The theoretical understanding of neural networks splits into five core directions: analyzing what happens during training when weight-adjusting algorithms (like gradient descent, which repeatedly moves in the direction that reduces error) update weights, understanding why networks generalize instead of memorizing data, explaining the learned internal structure of networks and what features emerge, characterizing the space of possible solutions and convergence guarantees, and understanding how specific architectures like sequence models with attention (mechanisms that focus on relevant parts of the input) and state-space models learn.

How weight updates shape what networks learn

A central puzzle in deep learning is understanding the implicit bias: when you train a network with gradient descent, the algorithm naturally finds simpler, smoother solutions even though many equally good solutions exist. This happens without any explicit penalty for complexity. Three papers illuminate different facets of this phenomenon.

The paper on saddle-to-saddle dynamics explains how networks learn increasingly complex solutions by evolving near saddle points (points where some directions go up and others go down, like a mountain saddle). Rather than taking a direct path to the solution, networks traverse a sequence of saddle points with increasing structure. This explains why networks tend toward simplicity early in training and gradually add complexity as needed. The paper on grokking and neural collapse reveals the mechanics of late-phase learning phenomena: sudden jumps in generalization happen through emergence of neural collapse, a structure where the network's output layer organizes into a highly ordered form. The paper on grokking and preconditioned gradient descent shows that the transition from a computationally lazy regime (where the network barely changes) to a feature-building regime can be understood as a bridge between two different modes of learning, and that certain variants of the algorithm accelerate this shift.

Together these papers show that weight updates do not simply move toward any good solution—instead, they follow a structured path, progressively building up learned representations in a predictable way.

Benign overfitting and generalization without explicit penalties

One of the biggest surprises in modern deep learning is that networks trained to zero error on random noise—a sign of memorization—can still generalize well on real data. This seems to violate classical learning theory, which says overfitting is inevitable when a model has more capacity than data. The challenge is to explain when and why networks avoid catastrophic memorization.

The paper on directional convergence in leaky ReLU networks proves that benign overfitting occurs on mixture data: even though the network perfectly fits the training set, it learns patterns that generalize. The work identifies phase transitions where benign overfitting abruptly fails, showing the phenomenon is not universal but depends on data structure. The paper on privacy and implicit bias shows that networks trained with stronger implicit bias are paradoxically harder to extract private information from because better generalization means the network focuses on public patterns rather than memorizing individual examples. The paper on data shape and stability shows that generalization depends critically on how data is distributed: low-dimensional data enables learning despite overfitting because the intrinsic structure constrains what the network can memorize, while high-dimensional isotropic data leads to memorization because there is no such constraint. The paper on flat minima provides counterintuitive evidence that flatness alone does not guarantee good generalization; instead, the relationship is more subtle and depends on what kind of flatness the algorithm finds.

These papers converge on a key insight: generalization is not about avoiding overfitting in the classical sense, but about what structure the learning algorithm finds within the space of fitting solutions.

Learning dynamics and feature emergence

Neural networks solve tasks by learning internal representations—features at each layer that transform raw input into useful patterns. Understanding how these features emerge during training is core to demystifying deep learning.

The paper on multi-index models proves that networks achieve information-theoretic sample complexity, meaning they learn as efficiently as any possible algorithm. The key finding is that the first layer must train for more than a trivial number of steps; networks cannot reach optimal performance by just tuning the top layer, showing deep learning requires genuine feature learning at all levels. The paper on feature emergence and delayed generalization provides a theoretical framework for grokking: features emerge suddenly, then generalization follows later. The framework predicts how hyperparameters influence this timing and validates predictions on small networks. The paper on dichotomy of feature learning reveals that the strength of nonlinear patterns in data determines whether networks learn generalizing features or 'unlearn' spurious patterns. Initial scaling of the second layer affects which regime dominates. The paper on Mamba's training dynamics proves that state-space models learn to selectively gate features: the gating mechanism aligns with class-relevant information, formalizing how recurrent gating acts as feature selection.

Collectively these show that feature learning is not just useful—it is essential for generalization, and the dynamics of how features crystallize can be theoretically characterized.

In-context learning in sequence models

Attention-based sequence models have an unusual ability: when given a few examples at test time, they can adapt to new tasks without retraining. This in-context learning is not fully understood, and recent work connects it to classical learning algorithms.

The continuum sequence model paper proves that in the infinite-depth limit, the learned function converges to the optimal predictor—the best solution given the data. This grounds in-context learning in statistical optimality. The paper on learning linear dynamical systems (sequences that evolve according to fixed rules) shows sequence models can compete with Kalman filters on sequence prediction, offering explanations for how attention mechanisms implement the computational steps classical filters perform. The paper on efficient in-context nonparametric regression shows sequence models can learn local polynomial estimators with far fewer learned values than what earlier theory suggested was possible, explaining why they scale well to real tasks. The paper on sequence models learning predictive outcomes proves that these models can approximate learned probability patterns (what outcomes are likely given the data) in-context, with error bounds that depend on attention depth and normalization.

These results suggest sequence models implement a form of implicit learning algorithm, combining multiple computational steps into a single forward pass.

Convergence analysis and solution spaces

Proving that training algorithms converge to good solutions is one of the hardest problems in learning theory because neural networks are nonconvex. Recent work tackles specific architectures and initializations, proving convergence rates and characterizing the paths gradients follow.

The paper on norm scaling with data characterizes how different norms of learned weights grow as data size increases, providing a unified solution for diagonal linear networks. The paper on Tsetlin machines proves almost sure convergence under noise-free training and shows mislabeled data does not prevent learning, establishing strong guarantees. The paper on diffusion model convergence derives tight scaling for convergence bounds, improving the dependence on accuracy while maintaining dimension scaling. The paper on rank-one denoising proves gradient descent converges almost surely and shows the asymptotic behavior aligns with phase transitions in random matrix theory (sudden shifts in the spectrum of eigenvalues, which control the matrix's behavior). The paper on gradient descent as a perceptron shows that nonlinearity in two-layer models yields faster convergence than linearity because the nonlinearity enables the network to learn features that reduce the effective problem size, which helps explain why deep networks optimize faster despite having many complicated valleys. The paper on scaling laws from implicit bias connects the way training algorithms naturally avoid overfitting to the observed patterns showing how performance improves as networks get bigger or trained on more data because implicit avoidance of overfitting biases the algorithm toward solutions that capture real patterns rather than noise, validating these patterns across diverse architectures.

Together these papers show that convergence is achievable under precise conditions, and the paths weight updates follow reveal deep connections to classical learning theory and phase transitions from random matrix theory.
