SLASH: Sharpening Structural Attention in LLMs

LLMs secretly reconstruct graph structure using their attention heads, but this understanding gets buried under noise from the attention sink—SLASH recovers it by redistributing attention.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models do understand the structure of graphs when you feed them in text form. But that understanding gets drowned out by a weird quirk: the model's attention heads (the parts that decide which input pieces to focus on) have a built-in bias toward paying attention to the first piece, called the attention sink. A piece is a chunk of the input, like a word or symbol. SLASH is a simple trick (no retraining needed) that amplifies the model's hidden structural understanding by taking attention back from the sink and redistributing it to pieces that actually encode graph structure.

Zero assumption version

The structural understanding puzzle

Large language models like Claude or Llama are brilliant at understanding language—the meaning of words, the flow of ideas. But they struggle with pure structure. Show a model a graph (a network of connected nodes) written out as text like '(0,1), (0,3), (2,3)', and it should reconstruct what the graph looks like. Naively, you might think the model is just pattern-matching text; it has no reason to build an internal picture of the graph's topology.

But here is the surprising part: the model's attention heads (the internal mechanisms that decide which parts of the input to focus on) spontaneously build a sawtooth pattern that matches the graph structure. The model is secretly doing the structural work. Yet it fails at graph tasks anyway. Why? The answer is that this fragile internal structure gets buried under noise.

The problem in one sentence. LLMs contain a hidden sawtooth signal that encodes graph structure, but a part of their attention mechanism called the attention sink floods it with noise, because the softmax normalization forces a zero-sum competition for attention—the sink grabs 95%, leaving only scraps for the sawtooth—so the model cannot use its own structural knowledge.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Identify sink pieces

Find which head or layer has strong attention concentration on a few early pieces (the sink).

Measure structural signal

Check which attention heads show a sawtooth pattern that aligns with the graph's actual connections.

Calculate redistribution factor

Choose how much attention to recover from the sink (parameter gamma, a number from 0 to 1).

What the paper changes

The single thing the paper changes

SLASH changes how attention weights are distributed across input pieces. Specifically, it recovers a fraction of the attention mass assigned to the first few pieces (the sink) and redistributes that mass to all other pieces. This is done purely at inference time, after the model is already trained—no retraining, no learning step.

the object it moves  =  how attention weights are divided up
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: structural alignment

measured quantity  =  structural alignment score

The paper measures a quantity it calls structural alignment: how well does the sawtooth pattern in a particular attention head match the true edges of the graph? A high alignment score means this head is genuinely tracking graph structure; a low score means it is noise. The model's intermediate layers contain multiple heads with moderate-to-high alignment, but their signals are diluted by the sink's dominance.

The paper estimates this by comparing the attention head's pattern to the ground-truth adjacency matrix of the graph. They also use an entropy-based criterion: heads that focus attention into specific, low-entropy patterns (like a sawtooth) score higher than heads with flat, high-entropy attention.

Why it works

Why the attention sink kills structural understanding

LLMs are trained on language, where paying attention to early pieces (like punctuation or sentence markers) is useful. This creates an anisotropic bias: a built-in preference for certain positions. Combined with the softmax normalization that forces all attention weights in each row to sum to one (a zero-sum game), this creates a competition. Early pieces grab a large share of the attention budget, and everything else fights for scraps.

Graphs require local structure: the model needs to distinguish which node is connected to which. This requires high-frequency signals (sharp, local variations), which are encoded in the sawtooth pattern. But the sink acts like a low-pass filter: it erases high-frequency details and leaves only the low-frequency blur (all nodes looking the same). When you redistribute attention away from the sink, you recover the high-frequency signals. The sawtooth becomes loud, and the model can finally use its hidden structural knowledge.

Mathematically, the paper shows that the structural difference between two pieces' representations shrinks under the sink's dominance. SLASH counteracts this by recovering attention mass from position 0 and redistributing it proportionally to all other positions. The formula involves a control factor gamma that determines what fraction to recover.

The redistribution formula

First in words:

The sharpened attention weight for token i is: weight_i equals the original weight, plus a recovery fraction times the sink loss divided by the number of non-sink positions. The recovery fraction is controlled by gamma, which ranges from zero (no change) to just under one (very aggressive redistribution).

Then the compact form:

alpha'_i,j = alpha_i,j + (1 - gamma) * lambda_i / (N - 1) for j != 0; alpha'_i,0 = gamma * lambda_i

Every symbol:

alpha'_i,jsharpened attention weight from position i to position j
alpha_i,joriginal attention weight before redistribution
gammacontrol factor in range [0, 1), how much sink dominance to allow
lambda_ithe total attention mass assigned to the sink (position 0)
Ntotal number of token positions
A concrete example
Graph with 5 pieces, original sink attention lambda=0.95 (sink takes 95%), gamma=0.3.
Token 0 (sink): alpha'_0 = 0.3 * 0.95 = 0.285 (reduced from 0.95 to 0.285).
Token 1 (non-sink): alpha'_1 = 0.05 + (1 - 0.3) * 0.95 / 4 = 0.05 + 0.168 = 0.218 (raised from 0.05).
Tokens 2,3,4 (non-sink): each also gain 0.168 (same calculation).
After redistribution: sink dropped from 95% to 29%, non-sink token share rose from 5% each to 22% each. Sawtooth signal is now audible.

The formula recovers attention from the sink proportionally and distributes it fairly to all other pieces, amplifying the structural signal while keeping the distribution a valid probability (sums to one).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gamma set too high. If you recover too much attention from the sink (gamma close to 1), you eliminate the low-frequency smoothing entirely. The model loses its semantic coherence and attention becomes scattered noise. There is a sweet spot (typically gamma=0.3 to 0.5) where structural signal peaks without destroying language understanding.
Identifying the wrong heads. Not all heads have structural signals. If you redistribute attention on heads that are purely semantic (e.g., heads that track syntax or argument roles), you dilute useful signals without gaining structure. The method requires scanning heads to find the structural subset first.
Graphs with no clear structure. If the input graph is a clique (every node connected to every other node) or a random graph with no local patterns, the sawtooth signal never emerges. SLASH amplifies what is there; it cannot create structure from noise.
Done right. Choose gamma by validation on a held-out task. Identify structural heads using the entropy-based concentration score. Apply SLASH only at inference; no retraining needed. Gains hold across different LLM families (Llama, Qwen, etc.) and diverse graph tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy graph (e.g., a 5-node graph with a star topology: one center connected to four leaves). Serialize it as a token sequence: '(0,1), (0,2), (0,3), (0,4)'. Run an LLM (Llama-2 or equivalent) with the standard attention pattern (vanilla), then run it again with SLASH redistribution (gamma=0.4). Compare the two attention maps: the vanilla should show a strong sink and buried sawtooth; the sharpened should show a clean, amplified sawtooth aligned with the edges. Then measure task accuracy: ask the model to predict the degree (number of neighbors) of each node. Vanilla should score near 50%; SLASH should score 90%+.

improvement = (SLASH_accuracy - vanilla_accuracy) / vanilla_accuracy * 100

The demo must make it impossible to miss: sharpening the attention reveals that the model already knew the graph structure; it was just buried under noise.
