Making Learner Weakness Actionable with Behavioral Region Maps

When a novice human shows a robot how to do a task, the paper finds which parts of the task are failing and tells the human exactly which demonstrations to improve.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot by showing it examples (called learning from demonstration) works well when someone provides the right examples. But a novice teacher does not know which parts of the robot's behavior are broken or how the robot's learning works internally. CLASP solves this by building a map of the task that connects robot failures directly to specific demonstrations the teacher can improve.

Zero assumption version

Novice teachers cannot fix robot learning errors

A common way to teach a robot a new task is to show it video or trajectory examples of the task being done correctly. The robot learns by studying these examples. But when the robot still makes mistakes after learning, the teacher needs to give it better examples to fix those mistakes, because the robot's errors come from gaps in its training data—regions or behaviors the initial examples did not cover enough.

A human expert might know: the robot fails at grasping small objects because it does not slow down enough during the grasp phase. So they provide new demonstrations focused on slow, careful grasping. But a novice teacher has no idea why the robot is failing. They might give demonstrations of other parts of the task the robot already does well, wasting time and budget. This is the core problem: under limited demonstration budgets, finding the right new examples is almost impossible without understanding the robot's internal learning.

The paper in one sentence. CLASP builds a readable map anchored in the teacher's own demonstrations that shows which task regions are failing and what to change in future demonstrations to fix them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect initial examples

Teacher provides a handful of task demonstrations (robot trajectories showing the task in action).

Train the robot

Robot learns a policy (a decision rule that says what action to take at each point in the task) from these examples.

Run the robot and measure failures

Execute the learned policy and log where and how it fails (reaches wrong position, grasps too hard, etc.).

Partition the task state-space

CLASP divides the full task space into regions anchored by the teacher's original demonstrations, assigning each region a success or failure label based on the measurements.

Retrain with difficulty weighting

Train the robot again, but give more weight to difficult regions where learning is failing, and less weight to easy regions already working.

What the paper changes

The training examples and their weights

CLASP does not change the robot's structure or learning algorithm. It changes two things during retraining: (1) which demonstrations the robot studies, and (2) how much each training example is weighted. Regions where the robot fails get higher weight during retraining, forcing the robot to focus its learning effort there.

the object it moves  =  demonstration examples and their training weights
The hidden quantity

The difficulty of each task region

measured quantity  =  task difficulty: success rate per region

Before CLASP, there is no clear measure of which parts of a task are hard for the robot. CLASP computes this by partitioning the task state-space (all possible positions and configurations the robot might encounter) into regions anchored by the teacher's own demonstrations. For each region, it measures what fraction of robot attempts succeed or fail. A region with a 20 percent success rate is marked as difficult; a region with 95 percent is marked as easy.

The paper estimates difficulty by running the learned policy across many rollouts and recording the success or failure outcome in each region. This gives a concrete, measurable quantity anchored in real task performance, not in abstract learning metrics.

Why it works

Why showing hard regions works

The core mechanism is simple: when a robot trains on examples with unequal importance weights, it allocates its learning budget according to those weights. A region marked as difficult (low success rate) gets a higher weight during training. This nudges the robot to spend more of its learning capacity fixing mistakes in that region, rather than re-learning the parts it already does well.

Because the regions are anchored in the teacher's own demonstrations, the map is intuitive: the teacher sees which spatial or behavioral zones are failing and can target new examples to those zones. An expert teacher might have known this instinctively because they understand learning mechanisms internally; CLASP makes the same insight explicit and measurable for novice teachers who lack that domain knowledge.

Math after the picture

The training weight formula

First in words:

The loss (error) for each training example is multiplied by a difficulty weight. Difficult regions get weight close to 1, easy regions get weight close to 0.

Then the compact form:

weighted_loss = sum over examples [ difficulty_weight_i * loss_i ]

Every symbol:

weighted_lossthe total training error after weighting
difficulty_weight_ihow hard region i is (0 to 1, based on success rate)
loss_ithe error for this single example
A tiny worked example
Example 1 is in a hard region (25% success rate): difficulty_weight = 0.75, loss = 2.0, contribution = 0.75 * 2.0 = 1.5
Example 2 is in an easy region (95% success rate): difficulty_weight = 0.05, loss = 2.0, contribution = 0.05 * 2.0 = 0.1
Total weighted loss = 1.5 + 0.1 = 1.6. The robot focuses 1.5 / 1.6 = 94% of learning effort on the hard region, even though both examples had the same raw loss.

The weight transforms raw examples into a difficulty-prioritized curriculum: hard regions get the robot's learning focus, easy regions fade into the background.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor initial demonstrations. If the teacher's original examples do not cover the full range of the task (e.g., they miss corner cases), the regions will be defined poorly and the difficulty estimates will be misleading.
Overfitting to one definition of success. If success is defined too narrowly (e.g., only endpoint accuracy, ignoring smoothness), the method will optimize for that narrow goal and the robot may behave unnaturally.
Difficulty weights can flip if the policy changes. If the retraining produces a very different policy, the region partitioning anchored in the original demonstrations may no longer align with how the new policy behaves. The map becomes stale.
Done right. CLASP works well when: the initial examples are reasonably representative, the task success metric is well-defined, and the teacher provides follow-up demonstrations that focus on the flagged difficult regions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple pick-and-place task where a 2D robot arm must reach to five fixed target locations, grasp lightly enough not to break an object, and place it in a bin. Provide a novice teacher with just three initial demonstrations (covering only two of the five reaching zones, to create an imbalanced curriculum). Train the robot. Then run CLASP: partition the reachable space into a 3-by-3 grid of regions, measure success in each, weight them by difficulty, and retrain on the same three demonstrations with difficulty weighting. Compare the two policies' success maps before and after.

improvement = (success_after - success_before) / success_before * 100 percent

The demo should show that difficult regions (those the initial examples missed) improve the most after CLASP retraining because those regions received higher weight, and that overall success goes up even on the same demonstration budget because the retraining redistributed learning effort toward the gaps.
