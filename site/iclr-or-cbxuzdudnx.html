TerraFM: A Scalable Foundation Model for Unified Multisensor Earth Observation

Train one Earth-observation model on radar and optical satellite images together, so it learns land patterns that transfer across regions, sensors, and tasks.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Satellite images come from different sensors. Optical sensors record reflected light in several bands, while radar sensors send radio waves and measure the return signal. Each sensor misses something the other can see: clouds block optical images, while radar keeps working through clouds but describes surfaces differently. TerraFM builds one model that learns from both at once. The paper's core move is to treat radar and optical views of the same place as two observations of one underlying patch of land, then force the model to keep the shared content and ignore sensor-specific noise. That matters because a model trained this way can carry useful land information into classification and segmentation tasks across many regions instead of overfitting to one sensor or one country.

Zero assumption version

Why one-sensor pretraining falls short for Earth observation

Earth observation models are often trained on narrow datasets: one sensor, one region, or one limited set of land types. That creates brittle representations, meaning internal summaries the model uses for prediction, because the model learns shortcuts tied to local weather, crop cycles, or sensor artifacts instead of broad land structure.

The difficulty grows when radar and optical imagery must be combined. Their pixel values come from different physical measurements, so the same field or river looks very different across sensors. A model that simply stacks the channels together can miss the common content because it has no clear way to separate what is shared across sensors from what is unique to each sensor.

The paper's move. Use paired radar and optical tiles from the same location during self-supervised training, because shared land structure should stay consistent even when the sensor physics differ.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect paired Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 tiles.

Use radar and optical images from the same regions, because the model needs two sensor views of the same land cover in order to separate shared ground structure from sensor-specific effects.

Turn each sensor into patch features.

Apply modality-specific patch embeddings, meaning separate patch encoders for radar and optical inputs, because the two sensors have different channel statistics.

Fuse the sensor streams.

Use adaptive cross-attention, which is a learned rule for deciding which patches in one stream should read information from the other stream, so shared content can be aligned patch by patch.

Train with local and global contrastive objectives.

Force nearby views of the same place to stay close both for small patches and for large tiles, because land identity appears at multiple spatial scales.

Re-center representations for long-tailed land classes.

Apply dual-centering with class-frequency-aware regularization, meaning the training targets are shifted to reduce domination by very common land types, so rare classes are not washed out.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  shared land identity across sensors and scales

The hidden quantity is the part of the representation that should stay the same when the location is fixed but the sensor changes. Forest, water, urban blocks, and cropland have stable spatial structure even though radar and optical channels record them differently.

TerraFM tries to isolate that shared land identity by combining two pressures. Cross-sensor fusion lets one sensor read evidence from the other at matching patches, and contrastive training pushes paired views of the same place together while pushing unrelated places apart. The centering term then stops frequent classes from setting the default representation for everything, because otherwise rare land types would collapse toward the common ones.

Why it works

Why the multisensor training recipe works

Separate patch encoders matter first because radar and optical values live on different scales and reflect different physics. If both sensors were forced through one raw-input stem, early layers would spend capacity disentangling sensor format instead of learning land content.

Cross-attention matters next because the two sensors should not contribute equally at every patch. Some areas are clear in optical bands and ambiguous in radar, while others are obscured by clouds or have strong radar structure. The adaptive rule lets each patch pull information from the other stream when that transfer reduces uncertainty.

The local-global contrastive design matters because land recognition is not only a patch problem. A single patch can be ambiguous, but a larger tile can show field arrangement, road layout, or shoreline context. Training agreement at both scales forces the model to keep detail and context together.

The dual-centering step matters because Earth-observation data are long tailed, meaning a few land classes appear far more often than the rest. Without a correction, the average representation drifts toward those frequent classes. Re-centering against class frequency keeps the training targets from being set mostly by easy, common terrain.

Two-scale agreement score

First in words:

The training objective combines patch-level agreement, tile-level agreement, and a centering penalty that keeps frequent classes from dominating the target representation.

Then the compact form:

total_loss = local_alignment + global_alignment + centering_penalty

Every symbol:

local_alignmentpenalty when matching radar and optical patches from the same place fail to stay close
global_alignmentpenalty when whole tiles from the same place fail to stay close
centering_penaltypenalty that shifts the representation targets so frequent land classes do not define the default
total_lossthe full training score minimized during pretraining
A tiny worked example
Suppose one radar patch and one optical patch come from the same rice field.
If their patch representations differ too much, the local alignment term increases.
Suppose the full tiles also disagree about the larger area, such as whether the field sits inside a wet agricultural zone.
Then the global alignment term increases as well.
If cropland appears very often in the dataset, the centering penalty stops the model from treating every uncertain patch as generic cropland.

The paper's exact formula is more detailed, but this split captures the training logic: match the same place across sensors at two scales, then correct the class-frequency bias.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad pairing corrupts the shared target. If the radar and optical tiles are misaligned in time or location, the model is asked to match different ground conditions, so the shared representation target becomes noisy.
Fusion can over-trust one sensor. If the cross-attention rule learns to copy mostly from one modality, the second sensor stops contributing much and the model slides back toward one-sensor pretraining.
Class-frequency correction can be mis-set. If the centering term is too weak, common classes still dominate. If it is too strong, the model can over-correct and reduce stability on frequent land types.
Done right. The method works best when paired tiles are well aligned, both sensors contribute patch evidence, and the centering term is strong enough to protect rare land classes without distorting the common ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy benchmark with paired radar and optical crops for a few land classes such as water, forest, urban area, and cropland. Train one small model with separate patch encoders, cross-sensor fusion, and a two-scale agreement loss. Then remove one piece at a time. The demo should show three checks: whether same-location radar and optical crops end up near each other in representation space, whether rare classes remain separable after training, and whether a classifier trained on one region still works on a held-out region.

demo_score = (cross_sensor_match + rare_class_separation + heldout_region_accuracy) / 3
