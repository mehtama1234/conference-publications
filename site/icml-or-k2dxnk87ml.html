Branched Neural Rough Differential Equations

Use special tree structures to let rough differential equations handle random motion and constraint surfaces that normal calculus cannot model.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Neural rough differential equations are fast at handling irregularly-timed data by compressing lots of measurements into a fingerprint-like summary. But they are built on Stratonovich calculus, which cannot capture randomness the way random equations require (Ito calculus) or handle curved surfaces where normal straight-line math breaks down. This paper introduces branched neural rough differential equations, which swap out the tree structures to match the right calculus for the job — unlocking motion on curved surfaces and true random behavior.

Zero assumption version

Rough differential equations miss two key things about real motion

Neural rough differential equations are smart at irregular sampling: instead of computing step-by-step through every messy real-world measurement, they summarize the whole sequence into a fingerprint called a log-signature, then jump forward in big coarse steps. This is fast and accurate when data arrives at unpredictable times.

But they rest on Stratonovich calculus, a flavor of calculus for smooth systems. Two real-world things break it: first, when motion is truly random, you need Ito calculus instead, which measures how wildly the motion jitters. Stratonovich cannot see that jitter. Second, when you are tracking motion on a curved surface (like 3D rotations or covariance matrices), the normal flat-space math for derivatives does not work. On a sphere, moving left from the north pole gets you somewhere; moving left from the equator gets you somewhere else. Rough equations cannot handle the curved-space derivatives that constrained surfaces require.

In one sentence. The paper builds rough differential equations that work on random processes and curved surfaces by swapping in tree structures that match the calculus these systems demand.

What the paper changes

The tree structures that define update steps

Each step of a neural differential equation uses algebraic building blocks called rooted trees to define which combinations of measurements get mixed together. The old method uses Shuffle algebra trees, tied to Stratonovich calculus. The new method swaps these trees: Grossman-Larson trees for random (Ito) motion in flat space, Munthe-Kaas-Wright trees for motion on curved surfaces (a curved surface is one where normal flat-space math does not apply, such as a sphere or the space of rotation matrices).

The hidden quantity

Quadratic-variation terms: the hidden jitter

measured quantity  =  quadratic variation (squared velocity noise)

In random motion, the system does not move smoothly; it jitters randomly. The amount of jitter is called quadratic variation — it is how much the motion wiggles around its average trend. Stratonovich calculus ignores it, but Ito calculus measures it: the jitter is as important as the trend itself.

The paper makes this jitter visible during training by adding a branched signature-kernel objective (a loss function built from branched tree structures and the signature fingerprint). Instead of only watching the average path, the loss function now penalizes errors in how much the trajectory jiggles, so the model learns to capture both smooth motion and random noise. The reason this works: if the loss ignores jitter, the network has no training signal to learn the Ito terms; once the loss sees jitter, the tree structures can be updated to match it.

Why it works

Why swapping tree structures fixes the problem

The old rough equations use Shuffle algebra trees because they work perfectly with Stratonovich calculus — they compress irregular data efficiently. But Stratonovich is designed for smooth motion; it treats randomness as invisible noise, and it assumes flat space.

Ito calculus, which random systems actually use, splits motion into two parts: a smooth drift (the average direction) and a random jitter (the squared velocity of the noise). The jitter is not noise to ignore; it is part of the motion law itself. To capture it, the tree structures must be different. Grossman-Larson rooted trees have the right algebra for Ito dynamics — they expose the quadratic-variation terms that Stratonovich hid.

On curved surfaces (like rotations in 3D or covariance matrices), you need derivatives that bend with the surface, not flat space directions. Munthe-Kaas-Wright planar rooted trees do that: they encode the curved-surface rules into the tree structure, so update steps automatically stay on the surface without needing to project or correct.

By matching the tree to the calculus, every coarse-step update is exact — no approximation, no surface violations, no hidden jitter.

Math after the picture

The update equation

First in words:

The state advances from the log-signature L of the driver, using tree-weighted sums of derivatives, integrating along the appropriate calculus.

Then the compact form:

x_{n+1} = Exp_x_n( sum over trees T: c_T * d_T(L) )

Every symbol:

x_ncurrent state (position on the manifold or in flat space)
x_{n+1}next state after the coarse step
Exp_xexponential map (how to move on the curved surface from a direction)
Llog-signature (compressed summary of all the irregular measurements)
c_Tcoefficient learned by the neural network for tree T
d_T(L)derivative direction built from tree T acting on the log-signature
sum over trees Tdifferent tree structures for Ito (Grossman-Larson) or manifold (MK-Wright) calculus
A tiny worked example
Suppose the state is a unit quaternion (a 3D rotation), x_n = (0.9, 0.2, 0.1, 0.3) on the rotation surface.
The log-signature of 20 irregular measurements is L = (0.05, -0.03, 0.02).
Tree T1 (Grossman-Larson) produces d_T1(L) = 0.05 (trend term), learned weight c_T1 = 0.1.
Tree T2 (MK-Wright, for curved-surface math) produces d_T2(L) = 0.002 (quadratic-variation term), learned weight c_T2 = 0.04.
Tangent direction at x_n is 0.1 * 0.05 + 0.04 * 0.002 = 0.00508.
Exp_x_n(0.00508) applies this tiny rotation direction on the manifold, yielding x_{n+1} ≈ (0.899, 0.203, 0.105, 0.303) (still a valid unit quaternion).
Constraints preserved: exponential map keeps the output on the rotation surface.

The exponential map and tree-weighted sum ensure the update respects both the randomness (squared velocity of jitter) and the surface rules.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the wrong tree type. If you use Munthe-Kaas-Wright trees on flat data (not a curved surface), you lose efficiency without gaining anything. Similarly, Grossman-Larson trees on a curved surface violate surface constraints. The method requires knowing which calculus (Ito vs Stratonovich) and which space (flat vs curved) applies.
Log-signature over-compression. For very long irregular sequences, the log-signature fingerprint may not capture rare high-order interactions. The method compresses aggressively; some fine details can be lost if the averaging is too coarse.
Training without the branched signature kernel. If you use old loss functions, the model never sees the quadratic-variation terms, so it reverts to Stratonovich behavior even though the trees are Ito. The branched signature-kernel objective is essential; skipping it defeats the random modeling.
Done right. Use Grossman-Larson trees for Ito processes in flat space, Munthe-Kaas-Wright trees for curved surfaces, keep the branched signature kernel in your loss, and validate that coarse steps match fine-grained numerical solvers on a test set.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic data generator that produces either random motion (e.g., random walk with known jitter) or motion on a known curved surface (e.g., uniform rotation around a fixed axis). Sample it at irregular timestamps. Train a standard neural RDE and a B-NRDE on the same data, then forecast ahead on unseen time intervals. Measure both average error and whether the model captures the true spread (variance) of the random forecast or whether rotations stay on the rotation surface.
