From Five Dimensions to Many: Large Language Models as Precise and Interpretable Psychological Profilers

Given only Big Five questionnaire scores, large language models can infer a broader trait profile by first writing a compact personality summary and then answering new scales from that summary.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Psychology uses many questionnaires, and the scales are not independent. People who score high on one trait often show predictable patterns on other traits as well. This paper asks whether large language models can recover that broader structure from a very small numerical input: a person's Big Five scores. The answer is surprisingly strong. When prompted with Big Five responses, the models generate answers on nine other scales whose correlation structure matches human data closely. The interesting part is not only the accuracy. The reasoning traces suggest a two-stage process: first compress the raw questionnaire pattern into a natural-language personality summary, then answer other scales from that summary. That matters because it points to an interpretable internal procedure rather than a black-box score lookup.

Zero assumption version

Why predicting many psychological scales from a few inputs is hard

Psychological questionnaires measure overlapping but distinct traits. Knowing a person's Big Five pattern gives some information about other scales, but the mapping is not obvious because several traits interact.

A direct regression can predict target scores numerically, but it often says little about how the information was combined. The paper asks whether a language model can do both jobs at once: recover the cross-scale structure and expose a readable intermediate summary that explains why certain target answers follow from the original Big Five responses.

The paper's move. Turn the Big Five numbers into a compact personality description first, then answer the target scales from that description, because the summary can capture cross-trait interactions that are hard to see in the raw numbers alone.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  compressed trait summary that preserves cross-scale structure

The hidden quantity is the compact personality summary the model forms before producing target answers. It is not just a restatement of the five input scores. It is a compressed description of how those traits interact in one person.

That summary matters because several target scales depend on joint patterns rather than on one trait alone. If the summary keeps those interactions, then downstream answers can match the human correlation structure. If it loses them, the model may predict each scale separately but fail to reproduce how the scales co-vary.

Why it works

Why the two-stage process works

The Big Five scores are compact but not self-explanatory. A language model can turn them into a descriptive summary that states who the person seems to be in ordinary language. That summary can carry joint information such as whether high conscientiousness combines with low neuroticism in a way that implies stable planning under stress.

Once that summary exists, answering another scale becomes easier because the model reasons from a coherent portrait instead of from five isolated numbers. The paper's traces support this view: the model first selects the key factors and then uses the resulting description to answer new items.

The result is interpretable because the intermediate summary can be read directly. The paper also finds limits: the models identify important factors at a broad level, but they do not distinguish item importance within a factor very well. That is why the method captures much of the cross-scale pattern while still leaving room for more fine-grained weighting.

Measure structural match

First in words:

Compare the correlation pattern among generated scales with the correlation pattern among human scales. High agreement means the model reproduced the structure of trait relationships, not just isolated answers.

Then the compact form:

structure_match = squared_correlation(model_scale_links, human_scale_links)

Every symbol:

model_scale_linksthe set of correlations among the scales generated by the model
human_scale_linksthe set of correlations among the same scales in human data
squared_correlation( , )the share of variation in one correlation pattern explained by the other
structure_matchhow closely the model reproduces the human cross-scale structure
A tiny worked example
Suppose the human data show that optimism and resilience move together strongly, while stress sensitivity moves in the opposite direction.
The model generates the same directional pattern across participants.
If the model's list of scale-to-scale links aligns closely with the human list, the squared correlation is high.
If the model gets each scale roughly right but misses how the scales move together, the squared correlation falls.
The paper reports very high alignment because the generated scale network tracks the human one closely.

This score checks structure across scales, not just accuracy on one questionnaire at a time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

A readable summary can still miss fine detail. The model may identify the right broad factors but still flatten item-level importance, because broad compression does not guarantee precise weighting of each questionnaire item.
Cultural or sample differences can shift trait links. If the human population changes, the learned trait relationships may change as well, so a summary tuned to one sample may not transfer cleanly to another.
Good structural match is not clinical validation. A model can reproduce correlation structure without being suitable for diagnosis, because matching survey patterns is not the same as establishing clinical reliability or fairness.
Done right. With careful use as a research tool, the method can reveal cross-trait structure clearly because the two-stage summary makes the model's reasoning easier to inspect than a pure score-to-score predictor.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a held-out set of participants with Big Five responses and several other questionnaire scales. Prompt a language model with only the Big Five data and ask it to produce target-scale responses plus a short personality summary.

The demo should compare the generated cross-scale correlation pattern with the human one and should also test whether adding the summary to the raw Big Five inputs improves prediction alignment. A convincing result shows strong structural match and a useful intermediate summary because the summary captures trait interactions that are not obvious in the raw five scores alone.

summary_gain = structural_match_with_summary - structural_match_from_raw_scores_only

The interesting win is not just prediction quality. It is that the model exposes a readable summary that helps explain why certain target traits follow from the original profile because the summary makes the trait interactions visible.
