Covariance estimation from MCMC chains

When you sample from a hard distribution using a Markov chain, the measurements can estimate covariance as well as random independent samples, but with far fewer queries to the distribution.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Measuring spread and correlation is one of the most basic statistical tasks. Normally you grab many independent random samples and compute the spread yourself. But for hard problems—like estimating how particles move in a confined space—you can only sample by running a long slow chain (Markov chain Monte Carlo). This paper asks: can those slow chain samples measure spread and correlation as well as independent samples? The answer is yes, and you might need fewer queries.

Zero assumption version

The measurement trap

To describe a cloud of scattered data, you need two numbers: how spread out it is (variance) and how the spread correlates across dimensions (covariance matrix). The standard way: grab many independent samples and calculate. But some problems forbid independence. Imagine sampling from the shape of a confined space. The only way to explore it is to walk randomly from point to point—each new point is correlated with the last, not independent. This is a Markov chain: each step depends on where you just were.

A naive thought: use the correlated samples anyway, just like independent ones. This breaks because the measurements are biased—you see phantom spread and phantom correlations that come from the chain's memory, not from the actual shape you are trying to learn.

The method in one sentence. If the chain mixes well (stops depending on where it started) and the shape of the data is smooth enough, dependent chain samples can estimate covariance as accurately as independent ones.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the distribution or the chain algorithm. It changes the estimator—the formula you apply to a chain's output to measure covariance. Instead of naively averaging products of deviations (which is biased when samples are correlated), it uses a weighted average that cancels out the chain's memory.

The hidden quantity

The mixing time: how fast the chain forgets

measured quantity  =  spectral gap

The paper measures a hidden property of the Markov chain: how fast it forgets where it started. This is called the spectral gap—a number between 0 and 1. If the gap is large, the chain loses memory quickly (good for independent estimates). If the gap is tiny, the chain remembers a long time (samples are highly correlated).

The paper bounds this gap using another mathematical property called a Poincaré inequality—a guarantee that the distribution is smooth locally, not lumpy or disconnected. From these two properties, the paper derives how many chain steps you actually need to collect.

Why it works

Why the adjusted formula works

A dependent sample carries correlation with the samples around it. When you compute covariance naively—averaging products of deviations—you double-count the effect of each correlation, because every pair of nearby samples reinforces the signal. The paper's key insight: weight each lag (time distance between samples) by how much correlation it actually contributes. Far-away samples are nearly independent, so their weight is small. Recent samples are highly correlated, but you can measure exactly how much from the chain's transition probabilities.

The Poincaré inequality says: if the shape is smooth everywhere, the chain mixes uniformly. This means you can derive a universal bound—no matter which dimension you are measuring, the same number of samples suffices to cancel the bias. With that guarantee, you can build a covariance estimator that works for any Gibbs distribution (a huge class including neural networks, physics simulations, and constrained optimization).

Math after the picture

The corrected covariance formula

First in words:

bias-corrected covariance = sample covariance minus weighted sum of autocorrelation terms at each lag, where weights decay by the spectral gap

Then the compact form:

Sigma = Sigma_raw - 2 * sum_k rho^(2k) * C_k

Every symbol:

Sigmathe true covariance matrix
Sigma_rawcovariance computed naively from samples
rhothe spectral gap (how fast chain forgets; between 0 and 1)
kthe time lag (distance between samples in the chain)
C_kthe autocorrelation at lag k (how much a sample correlates with itself k steps later)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a one-dimensional sample has raw covariance 10.0, spectral gap rho = 0.9 (chain forgets slowly).
Lag 1 autocorrelation C_1 = 2.0; lag 2 autocorrelation C_2 = 0.5; higher lags negligible.
Weight at lag 1: rho^(2*1) = 0.81; contribution: 0.81 * 2.0 = 1.62.
Weight at lag 2: rho^(2*2) = 0.656; contribution: 0.656 * 0.5 = 0.328.
Total bias: 2 * (1.62 + 0.328) = 3.896.
Corrected covariance: 10.0 - 3.896 = 6.104. This is closer to the truth than the raw 10.0.

The weight rho^(2k) decays exponentially with lag—nearby samples' memory is subtracted heavily, distant samples barely matter.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If the distribution has disconnected regions. The chain will get stuck in one region and never visit others. The Poincaré inequality fails, and the corrected formula breaks. The fix: verify that the distribution is connected and smoothly mixed.
If you start with a bad initialization. Early chain samples reflect the starting point, not the true distribution. You need a burn-in period. The paper's bounds account for this but require discarding the first many steps.
If the spectral gap is tiny. The chain forgets very slowly, so samples remain highly dependent. You need exponentially more samples to cancel the bias. The fix: use a faster-mixing chain algorithm or better initialization, because a larger spectral gap means samples decorrelate faster, reducing the bias term and cutting the number of samples you need.
Done right: start after burn-in, verify smooth distribution, collect enough samples relative to the spectral gap. Then the corrected estimator provably recovers the true covariance with high probability, using far fewer queries than independent sampling.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a simple Gibbs distribution (e.g., a 2D Gaussian or a Gaussian truncated to a simplex). Run a random walk chain (Metropolis or Langevin dynamics). Collect samples after burn-in. Compute the spectral gap numerically (trace a few power-iteration steps or use known formulas for the test distribution). Apply both the naive covariance and the corrected formula. Measure error as the Frobenius norm (the length of the difference matrix). Plot: error versus chain length for both estimators. Show that the corrected error decays with the same rate as independent sampling, while naive stays biased.

error = ||Sigma_estimated - Sigma_true||_F (Frobenius norm: square root of sum of squared differences)

The corrected estimator should converge to zero error; the naive estimator should plateau at a nonzero bias. Switching to the corrected formula is a free win for any MCMC application.
