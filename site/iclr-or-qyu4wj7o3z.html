Data Provenance for Image Auto-Regressive Generation

Detect whether an image came from a generative model by tracing the fingerprints its generation process leaves behind.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Image-generating AI models now produce pictures that look indistinguishable from real photos. This creates a problem: if a fake image spreads online, we need a way to prove it came from a generative model, not a real camera. This paper discovers that every time an image model generates a picture by predicting pixel blocks one at a time, it leaves behind invisible fingerprints in how the image encodes to numbers and back. By measuring those fingerprints, we can reliably identify which images are AI-made without having to modify the model or add watermarks.

Zero assumption version

Fake images without fingerprints

A modern image generator works like this: it writes a picture one small piece at a time, predicting each next piece from all the pieces it has drawn so far. When it finishes, the image looks like a real photograph. But if that fake image spreads on the internet—used to hoax voters, impersonate someone, or spread misinformation—how do we prove it was AI-made and not shot with a camera?

A beginner might try adding a visible watermark, but that marks up the image and is easy to remove. Others have tried adding hidden watermarks baked into the model's training, but if a model was built in secret or shared widely, we have no way to add one after the fact. The paper asks a different question: does the generation process itself leave traces? Are there fingerprints that we can read after the fact, from any image, without needing to own the model or have modified it?

The problem in one sentence. Fake images look real, but their creation process leaves invisible marks we can learn to detect.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Invert the encoder

Train a reverse function that turns the compact number codes back into the feature space.

Measure QuantLoss

Pass an image through encoder, then inverse decoder. Measure how much the recovered features differ from the quantized (rounded) version.

Measure EncLoss

Pass an image through encoder, then original decoder, then encode again. Measure how much the second image differs from the first.

Combine signals

Blend QuantLoss and EncLoss into one fingerprint score.

Detect provenance

Compare the fingerprint score to a threshold set on real and generated examples to classify the image.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Every image generator stores a pair of functions: an encoder (image to codes) and a decoder (codes back to image). This paper trains one new thing: an inverse encoder (codes back to features). By building that inverse function, we unlock the fingerprints buried in how the model quantizes (rounds) values and reconstructs images.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Reconstruction error signature

measured quantity  =  QuantLoss and EncLoss fingerprint

When an image model generates a picture by building it one pixel block at a time, it never runs its output through the encoder-decoder loop that trained it. It makes codes one by one and then decodes them to pixels at the very end. This means the codes it creates are not tuned to be easy for the encoder to recognize if fed back in. Real images, by contrast, were photographed directly and naturally fit the encoder-decoder loop. When you run a generated image through encoder and back, it does not reconstruct perfectly—it leaves a fingerprint. QuantLoss measures the mismatch in the quantized feature space (how much do the rounded numbers disagree with the inverse decoder's guess?). EncLoss measures the mismatch in image space (does encode-decode-encode-decode stay stable?).

The paper estimates both signals by training an inverse decoder on the model's internal representations, then feeding test images through the loop and computing reconstruction errors. Generated images show large consistent errors; real images show small errors. These errors form a signal strong enough to reliably separate real from fake.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

An autoregressive image model breaks down into three pieces: encoder (image to codes), quantizer (rounds the codes), decoder (codes to image). During training, the model sees: (1) real images encoded to codes, (2) the quantizer rounds them, (3) the decoder decodes them back. So the decoder learns to turn rounded codes into images that look like real photos. But during generation, the model does something different: (1) it predicts new codes one by one (never consulting the encoder), (2) the quantizer rounds them, (3) the decoder turns them into an image. The problem: the generated codes are not the output of the real encoder. They are from a different source (the generator's prediction). So they land in a different part of the quantizer's codebook space. When you try to reverse-engineer them, you get larger reconstruction errors than for real images.

The inverse decoder amplifies this signal. By training it on a corpus of real image encodings, the inverse decoder learns what valid feature patterns look like. When it sees features from generated codes, they do not match—the mismatch (QuantLoss) is the fingerprint. EncLoss provides a second angle: if you encode-decode-encode an image, real images stay stable (because all three functions are in sync), but generated images drift (because the initial encoding never happened). Together, the two signals work together reliably to detect generations even after the image is compressed, cropped, or filtered.

Math after the picture

The math in plain words and symbols

First in words:

The combined fingerprint score blends two losses: QuantLoss, which measures how much the inverse-decoder-recovered features differ from their quantized versions, and EncLoss, which is a calibrated ratio of how much a generated image drifts when re-encoded. The combined loss is a weighted sum of these two signals, chosen to maximize separation between real and generated images.

Then the compact form:

fingerprint = QuantLoss + weight * EncLoss where QuantLoss = norm(D_inv(Q(z)) - Q(Q^-1(D_inv(x)))) and EncLoss = L_rec1 / L_rec2, with L_rec1 = norm(x - D(Q(E(x)))) and L_rec2 = norm(x1 - D(Q(E(x1))))

Every symbol:

Qthe quantizer (rounds feature values to codebook entries)

A tiny worked example
Real image flows through: QuantLoss = 0.15 (features align well after decode), EncLoss calibration ratio = 1.02 (stable, close to 1.0). Combined score = 0.15 + 1.0 * 0.02 = 0.17.
Generated image flows through: QuantLoss = 1.8 (features mismatch badly, because tokens never came from encoder), EncLoss calibration = 3.5 (drifts on second encode). Combined score = 1.8 + 1.0 * 3.5 = 5.3.
Decision boundary at 2.0. Generated score 5.3 is well above, real score 0.17 is well below. Clear separation.

The two losses measure different aspects of the generation artifact: QuantLoss catches the token mismatch in feature space, and EncLoss catches the drift that happens when a generated image re-enters the encoder.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Image post-processing disrupts fingerprints. If a generated image is heavily compressed (JPEG), cropped, or blurred before you test it, the reconstruction errors change. The paper handles this by training the inverse decoder with augmentations and choosing QuantLoss over EncLoss in adversarial settings, but adversarial video compression or adversarial blur could potentially fool the detector.
Different model families need different thresholds. A VAR model leaves different fingerprints than a LlamaGen model because they use different quantizers and decoders. The paper tunes the threshold separately for each model, but if an attacker uses a model type you have not trained on, detection becomes harder.
Inverse decoder training requires the original model. To set up detection, you need access to the target model's encoder, quantizer, and decoder. If the model is closed-source, you cannot build the inverse decoder.
Done right. The framework handles JPEG compression, aspect-ratio changes, and mild noise by training the inverse decoder with many augmented examples. It works post-hoc on any image, even if the model is not available at detection time (only during inverse-decoder training).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small classifier that takes 100 real images (from a standard benchmark like CIFAR or ImageNet) and 100 images generated by a lightweight autoregressive model (such as a tiny VAR or LlamaGen trained on low-resolution patches). Train the inverse decoder on the model's encoder-quantizer-decoder. Then compute QuantLoss and EncLoss for each test image and plot a histogram. The demo must show two clearly separated peaks: one for real images (low loss scores clustering near 0.2) and one for generated images (high loss scores clustering near 3.0 or higher). The goal is to make the fingerprint difference visible and unmissable.

detection_accuracy = (true_positives + true_negatives) / (total_images). A demo reaching >= 90% accuracy on held-out real and generated images proves the principle.

When you run the histogram, the two populations should be so far apart that a simple threshold at their midpoint catches nearly every image correctly.
