Latent Denoising Makes Good Tokenizers

Better image tokenizers result from training them to reconstruct images even when their internal compressed codes are intentionally corrupted.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Modern image generators work by first shrinking images into compact codes (tokens), then training to reconstruct those images by reversing corruption like noise or masking. This paper asks: what if we train the tokenizer itself using the same corruption-reversal goal? Doing so makes tokenizers that survive damage better and produces higher-quality final images, because the generator spends less training effort recovering from token decay and more learning the true image patterns.

Zero assumption version

Tokenizers are not built for what comes next

Image generation works in two stages: first, a tokenizer (a learned compressor) shrinks a big image into a small list of numbers called a latent code (just a compact summary). Second, a generative model learns to recover the image by reversing corruptions — it starts with noise or missing pixels and gradually fills them in. Modern generators like diffusion models spend most of their training on this second stage, learning to denoise (fix corrupted inputs) in the compressed space.

The problem: tokenizers are almost always trained separately to just compress well (reconstruct the original image from the compact code). They are not trained to handle what comes next — the corruption and reconstruction that the generator will do. A beginner might try adding noise to inputs while training the tokenizer, but that breaks the core job: the tokenizer no longer compresses reliably because it was told to handle damage it should never see during normal use.

In one sentence. This paper trains tokenizers to compress and survive corruption, aligning them with what the downstream generator will actually do.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: tokenizer training goal

Standard tokenizers are trained with one job: compress an image into codes and decompress them back cleanly. The Latent Denoising Tokenizer is trained with two jobs: compress images and decompress from corrupted codes. The second job forces the tokenizer to learn representations (internal codes) that remain informative even when scrambled by noise or masking.

the object it moves  =  Tokenizer training objective
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: reconstructability under corruption

measured quantity  =  Code-survival margin

The paper measures how well the tokenizer's compressed codes survive corruption. Imagine each code as a point in a space; normal compression puts points anywhere that reconstructs the image cleanly. Good compression clusters points densely around 'safe zones' — regions where noise does not scatter them so far that reconstruction fails. The tokenizer learns to avoid putting codes on cliffs, where small noise nudges mean large reconstruction errors.

The paper estimates this survival margin by adding noise or masks to the codes during training and measuring how well the tokenizer still reconstructs. High survival margin means the generator later will see fewer broken intermediate images while learning to denoise.

Why it works

Why it works: aligned training reduces downstream burden

Generative models operate in two conceptual stages, but training them asks for something awkward: the generator must learn both 'how to denoise corrupted codes' and 'how to work with this tokenizer's quirks.' If the tokenizer's codes fall apart under noise, the generator wastes learning capacity recovering from that damage instead of learning the actual pattern of real images.

By training the tokenizer to survive corruption, this paper removes that second burden. The tokenizer learns to place codes in regions where noise is forgiving — small perturbations do not cause large reconstruction errors. Now the generator receives codes that stay mostly valid under corruption, so it can focus purely on learning to denoise in the way the actual image distribution requires. The tokenizer and generator now share the same objective (denoising), so training either one helps both because progress on one (e.g., learning to denoise cleaner signals) does not conflict with the other.

Think of it like this: imagine teaching a person to walk on a floor that is sometimes slippery. It is easier if you first teach their legs to stay balanced on slippery surfaces, so they spend less cognitive effort fighting instability and more learning the path. That is what this paper does for codes.

The training equation

First in words:

Total error equals reconstruction error plus a penalty for codes that do not survive corruption.

Then the compact form:

Loss = ReconstructionError(original, decompress(compress(original))) + lambda * ReconstructionError(original, decompress(corrupt(compress(original))))

Every symbol:

originalthe image being compressed
compressthe tokenizer encoder (shrinks image to codes)
decompressthe tokenizer decoder (expands codes back to image)
corruptadd Gaussian noise or mask to the codes
ReconstructionErrorpixel-level difference between target and output
lambdahow much to penalize poor robustness (balance between compression and noise-survival)
A tiny worked example
Code from image: [2.1, 0.8, -1.3, 0.5]
Normal reconstruction error: 0.05 (codes are clean, decompress works well)
Add noise: [2.1 + random, 0.8 + random, ...] becomes [2.3, 0.5, -1.5, 0.9]
Reconstruction from noisy code: 0.42 (much worse; decoder struggles)
Total loss (lambda=1.0): 0.05 + 1.0 * 0.42 = 0.47
Error signal nudges tokenizer to place codes in zones where noise causes less damage
After training, same noise might yield reconstruction error 0.08 instead of 0.42

The two error terms push the tokenizer to compress tightly (first term) and to place codes where noise does not break them (second term).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too much noise during tokenizer training breaks basic compression. If corruption is too aggressive while training the tokenizer, it learns to ignore fine details to survive noise — losing information the generator needs. The balance (lambda) must be tuned carefully.
A noise-trained tokenizer does not guarantee the generator learns fast. A tokenizer that survives noise removes one friction point but does not automatically make the generator better because the generator still must learn its denoising objective — the tokenizer only stops being a liability, it does not teach the generator new patterns.
Corruption type mismatch wastes effort. If the tokenizer trains with Gaussian noise but the generator applies masking, the codes may not survive that specific corruption pattern. Types of corruption must align.
Done right: corruption type matches generator. Train tokenizer with the same corruption (noise, masking) that the generator will apply downstream. Tune lambda to preserve compression quality while gaining survival margin.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy image generator in two stages: (1) train a standard tokenizer on 1000 images, then (2) train another tokenizer with the denoising objective (add corruption during training). Generate new images using a simple generative model with both tokenizers. Measure the quality of reconstructed images by comparing pixel-by-pixel error and visual clarity. The demo should make it impossible to miss that the noise-trained tokenizer allows cleaner intermediate reconstructions during the generation process because the generator's denoising steps do not have to fight token decay.

Quality gain = (Avg_reconstruction_error_old - Avg_reconstruction_error_new) / Avg_reconstruction_error_old

The noise-trained tokenizer should show 15-30% lower reconstruction error on corrupted codes, letting the generator focus on learning distribution instead of fighting code breakdown.
