M4PQA: Benchmarking and Training Paper Question Answering

M4PQA is a large dataset of real questions on academic papers that reveals which AI agents can read papers well, and ExTrActor is a method to automatically generate practice conversations to train smaller models to match larger ones.

Reading a scientific paper and answering questions about it is a good way to test whether an AI model can understand text deeply. But there was no realistic test set to measure this. M4PQA gives researchers nearly 14,000 papers with real questions, and a new recipe to automatically create practice conversations so smaller models learn to find answers as well as the big ones.

Zero assumption version

Why we need a honest test for paper reading

Large language models (AI systems trained on huge amounts of text) are now good enough to read papers and answer questions about them. But there was no realistic benchmark to actually test this ability. Without a real test, nobody knew which models are strong and which are weak.

The naive approach: hire humans to read papers and ask lots of questions. That works but is slow and expensive. The alternative: collect real papers and have humans write real questions on them, then test AI models on those same questions. This reveals the true gap between what humans ask about papers and what the models can actually answer, because you are testing on the exact task humans care about instead of a proxy.

The problem in one sentence. We lack a large realistic benchmark of real papers with real human-written questions to test whether AI agents can read and understand scientific papers.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect the benchmark

Hire humans to read 13948 AI papers and write realistic questions asking about findings, methods, or conclusions.

Test existing models

Run several AI models on M4PQA questions. Record where they fail to find the answer or give wrong answers.

Build an automated trainer

ExTrActor uses big AI models to generate synthetic training conversations based on papers and questions.

Train small models

Feed the synthetic conversations to smaller models, teaching them to answer questions step-by-step.

Measure the gain

Re-test the trained small models on M4PQA. They should now match or nearly match large model performance.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes what training data small models see. Instead of random text, they get realistic question-answering conversations generated from actual papers and questions in M4PQA.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: agent performance gap

measured quantity  =  model performance gap on realistic questions

The hidden quantity is the performance difference between small models and large models when both try to answer real paper questions. A small model might score 30 percent correct while a large model scores 80 percent. The gap is 50 percentage points.

The paper estimates this gap by running many models on the M4PQA benchmark. It counts how many questions each model answers correctly, then subtracts small from large. ExTrActor's job is to shrink that gap by giving small models better training data.

Why it works

Why it works: realistic practice teaches real skills

The core mechanism is simple: small models improve when trained on realistic examples of the task they will face, because each training example teaches a direct skill they need. M4PQA provides real papers with real questions. ExTrActor uses big models to write out step-by-step reasoning for how to answer those questions. When small models practice on those conversations, they learn the same reasoning patterns because they are copying what works.

Small models have limited memory—like a bucket that fills up. Training on random text fills that bucket with facts unrelated to paper reading, leaving no room for paper-reading skills. Training on realistic paper questions uses the same bucket but fills it exclusively with paper-reading patterns. Since the bucket is the same size either way, filling it with relevant skills means the model spends no time on useless information and learns faster.

How to measure whether the method works

First in words:

accuracy_gap_before minus accuracy_gap_after, where gap is the percentage-point difference between large and small model scores on M4PQA questions

Then the compact form:

improvement = (large_acc - small_acc_before) - (large_acc - small_acc_after)

Every symbol:

large_accthe accuracy of the largest model on M4PQA questions
small_acc_beforeaccuracy of the small model before training on synthetic data
small_acc_afteraccuracy of the small model after ExTrActor training
improvementhow many percentage points the gap shrinks
A tiny worked example
Before training: large model scores 85%, small model scores 35%. Gap = 85 - 35 = 50 percentage points.
After ExTrActor training on synthetic conversations: large still 85%, small now scores 78%. New gap = 85 - 78 = 7 percentage points.
Improvement = (85 - 35) - (85 - 78) = 50 - 7 = 43 percentage points. The gap shrank by 43 points.

Improvement measures how much we closed the gap between small and large models. A bigger improvement means the training worked better, because the small model learned more of the skills the large model has.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The synthetic examples do not match reality. If the big model that generates ExTrActor examples is poor at reasoning or makes mistakes, small models will learn the wrong patterns. The quality of synthetic data is only as good as the big model creating it.
Not all papers are the same. A small model trained on AI papers might not generalize to biology papers or chemistry papers. Domain differences matter. M4PQA is limited to AI papers, so trained models may not work on other fields.
The model memorizes instead of learns. If ExTrActor creates too many examples for the same paper, a small model might memorize specific answers rather than learning the reasoning process to answer new questions on new papers.
Done right: balance and diversity. ExTrActor works best when it generates varied examples across many papers, using a strong big model, and when the synthetic conversations match the kinds of questions humans actually ask.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small model (under 1 billion parameters). Take a subset of M4PQA: say 100 papers with 10 questions each. Split them into training (80 papers) and test (20 papers). Run ExTrActor to generate synthetic conversations for the 80 training papers. Fine-tune the small model on those conversations. Then test it on the 20 held-out papers and compare the score to a small model trained on random text. The demo wins if fine-tuned small model beats random-trained small model by at least 20 percentage points on the test questions.

accuracy_gain = small_model_fine_tuned_accuracy - small_model_random_accuracy

The demo makes it impossible to miss: when a small model practices on realistic paper-reading examples from M4PQA, it learns dramatically faster than training on unrelated text, because every example directly teaches the skill we measured.
