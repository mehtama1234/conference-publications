Robotics & Control

How can a robot see its surroundings, understand what is happening, and pick actions that achieve its goals in a real, changing world?

What this whole area is about

Robots need to do useful work in the real world. A robot arm needs to pick up a cup without crushing it. A delivery robot needs to navigate a city street without hitting people. A surgical robot needs to make precise cuts. None of these tasks come pre-programmed in—the robot has to perceive what is happening around it and decide what to do next based on what it sees. This is the core challenge of robotics and control: how do you teach a machine to see its environment, understand what is happening, and pick actions that actually work?

The hard part is that the real world is messy and unpredictable. A cup can be placed at any angle, lighting changes, hands and objects occlude things, and there are countless edge cases a designer never thought of. The robot cannot memorize every scenario. It has to generalize—take what it learned from some situations and make reasonable guesses about new ones. It also has to translate from sensing to action, which means connecting high-level goals (like 'pick up the cup') to low-level motor commands (like 'rotate shoulder joint by X degrees, grip by Y amount'). And it has to do this in real time, because the world does not wait. Making learning stable enough to work in the real world, rather than just in simulation, is one of the core unsolved problems in robotics.

Researchers are tackling this from multiple angles. Some teach robots by showing them images or videos and letting them learn what action to take from visual input. Others use reinforcement learning—trial and error with rewards—to shape better behavior, because rewards give the robot feedback on whether it is improving without requiring human-written demonstrations. Many focus on specific hard problems: controlling complex multi-jointed hands, navigating cluttered spaces, predicting the physics of the world to plan ahead. Some use generative models (models that learn to produce new examples by studying patterns in existing data), like diffusion, to produce smooth, natural-looking movements. Others try to make these methods fast enough to run on real robots instead of just in simulation. The throughline across all this work is the same: how do we close the gap between what a robot can sense and the actions it needs to take?

Robotics sits at the intersection of many fields—computer vision, control theory, physics simulation, machine learning—and success in robotics often means those fields have to work together. Getting robotics right also means solving problems that impact real life: autonomous vehicles, surgical systems, warehouse automation, care for the elderly and disabled. The research here is not abstract; it is about making machines that can actually be useful.

Subtheme

Teaching robots to see and act from images

The most direct way to teach a robot is to show it images or videos and let it learn the pattern: 'when you see this scene, do this action.' This is vision-language-action learning. A robot sees an image of a table with blocks. It learns to recognize the scene and decide: 'I should grasp the red block' or 'I should move left.' The appeal is obvious—we teach humans the same way, by showing and demonstrating. But this turns out to be extremely hard in practice because the robot has to compress everything about a scene into one decision. The scene contains infinite visual details, but only a few of them matter for the task. Lighting changes, the camera angle shifts, the size of the object varies, yet the action should stay roughly the same. How does the robot learn which details to ignore and which to attend to?

The core difficulty is that predicting an action from a single image requires the robot to hallucinate what will happen next. If the robot grasps a soft object, it will deform differently than if it grasps a rigid one. If it places an object on a slanted surface, it might roll. The robot cannot 'see' this without reaching into the future. Traditional computer vision alone is not enough—the model has to develop an intuition for physics, the consequences of motion, and how the world will respond. Building this intuition only from images is like learning to drive a car from photographs.

Papers in this area use several approaches. Some train large neural networks (inspired by large language models) on many robot videos, teaching them to predict not just what the robot should do, but also to understand what is happening in the scene. Others combine visual learning with language—so a human can say 'pick up the small red cube' and the robot learns to translate language to action. Some add reasoning steps in between: the robot first understands the scene, then reasons about what to do, then predicts the action. The best results come from training on diverse, large-scale data—watching thousands of hours of robot demonstrations and learning the patterns.

Subtheme

Learning from rewards and feedback

Another way to teach robots is to let them learn by trial and error, the way a child learns to walk or throw a ball. You define a reward—a number that tells the robot how well it did. If the goal is to pick up a cup, the robot gets a high reward when it touches the cup, and a higher reward when it grasps it. The robot tries many different actions, and over time, it learns which actions lead to higher rewards. This is called reinforcement learning. The appeal is that you do not have to provide demonstrations; you just have to define what success looks like. The robot will figure out its own way to get there.

The challenge is defining a useful reward signal and making the learning stable. If the reward is too simple ('just grab the object'), the robot learns a brittle policy that works in one exact situation but fails if the object is slightly different or in a different place. If the reward is complex, you have to specify dozens of details, and robots optimize the reward you gave them, not the goal you intended. Worse, reinforcement learning is slow. A robot in the real world cannot try 10,000 failed actions to learn one good skill—each failure is costly, and each trial takes time. So researchers try to speed it up using simulation, where they can run thousands of trials per second. But then the robot learns in simulation, and when you put it in the real world, the physics is slightly different and the policy fails. This 'reality gap' is enormous in robotics.

Papers here tackle the problem from multiple angles. Some use reward shaping—giving the robot intermediate rewards for progress, so it learns faster. Some use hierarchical learning—teaching the robot to learn simple skills first (grasp), then build on them (grasp and lift). Others use feedback from humans—as the robot learns, a person occasionally corrects it or gives it hints, speeding up learning massively. Some work to bridge the reality gap by training in simulation in a way that the learned policy transfers better to real robots. A few use prediction as a shortcut: instead of learning what reward a real action gives, the robot learns to predict what will happen, and uses that prediction to pick actions without actually trying them as many times.

Subtheme

Controlling complex hands and manipulation

Picking up a coffee cup is one of the hardest manipulation tasks in robotics. A human hand has 27 degrees of freedom—27 different joints that can move. Even a simplified robotic hand has 10 to 20. Controlling all of those joints to grasp and manipulate objects requires solving a puzzle: what should each finger do, in what order, with what force? If one finger is too stiff, the cup breaks. If it is too loose, the cup drops. The fingers have to move in coordination—thumb and pinky cannot work against each other. And the robot has to feel what is happening; it cannot just execute a pre-planned motion because the object is never in exactly the same place or orientation.

The difficulty has two parts. First, the control problem itself is hard—many degrees of freedom, many constraints, and the need for real-time feedback. Second, collecting training data is expensive. You can simulate a hand in a computer, but the simulation is not perfect—friction works differently, object properties are guessed, sensor noise is not realistic. When you train a policy in simulation and move it to a real hand, it often fails. Getting enough real-world data to train on requires either hand-crafting thousands of demonstrations or running the real robot for weeks, trying different approaches. This is slow and costly.

Papers in this area work to bridge simulation and reality. Some use domain randomization—they train the policy in simulation on many slightly different versions of the task (different materials, object shapes, friction values) so that when it sees the real world, it feels like just another random simulation. Others use real demonstrations as a seed, teaching the robot from human examples first, then refining through practice. Some use tactile feedback—the robot's fingers have force and touch sensors, and the learning algorithm uses these signals to adjust grip strength in real time. A few try to learn a model of the real world dynamics from a small amount of real robot data, then use that model to train the policy more safely.

Subtheme

Generating smooth robot movements with diffusion

In recent years, generative models called diffusion models have shown that they can produce high-quality images and videos by starting with noise and gradually refining it into something structured. The same idea can be applied to robot motion. Instead of asking 'what is the one best action,' you can ask 'what does a smooth, natural sequence of actions look like?' and let a generative model produce a sequence of good motions sampled from patterns in the training data. The advantage is that the model learns from the data what good movement looks like, without needing to optimize a complicated reward function. If the robot has seen a thousand hours of successful manipulation, the model learns to generate movements that look similar to those, because the model copies successful patterns from the training data—and movements that look natural to humans often work better in practice because humans have evolved to move in efficient, energy-conserving ways.

The challenge is that robot actions are constrained by physics and the shape and size of the robot body. A robot cannot move its arm backward through its own torso. It cannot apply forces larger than its motors can produce. Generating motions that are smooth, natural-looking, and physically feasible is much harder than generating images, where any pixel value is valid. Also, a diffusion model generates sequences of actions from scratch; it does not naturally connect to the current state of the robot. How do you tell the model 'start from this current position and generate a sequence that reaches this goal'? Conditioning the generation process to respect current state and goals is not straightforward.

Papers here use diffusion models as a policy—a way to generate action sequences in real time. Some condition the model on the current observation and the goal, so it generates an appropriate action. Some use diffusion for planning: the model generates a sequence of future states, the robot picks the first action in that sequence, and repeats. Some train the diffusion model on human demonstrations so it learns to imitate good behavior. The insight across these approaches is the same: if you have access to a good model of what successful motion looks like, you can generate new motions that are likely to succeed, without needing to optimize a complicated reward or planning problem.

Subtheme

Autonomous driving and navigation

Autonomous vehicles have to make split-second decisions in complex environments. A car is driving down a street and sees a pedestrian stepping between parked cars. It has to predict whether the person will continue into the street, estimate how much time it has to react, and decide to brake or swerve. A delivery robot navigating a warehouse has to move around people, obstacles, and other robots while carrying fragile packages. These are all navigation problems: how do you sense the world, predict how it will change, and decide where to move next?

The core difficulty is uncertainty and partial information. The robot's sensors do not see everything—some things are blocked (occluded). People and other objects behave unpredictably. The robot has to make a decision now, but the consequences play out over the next few seconds, and those consequences depend on the behavior of other agents in the world. Predicting whether a person will step into traffic requires modeling human intent, which is not visible. Planning a path for a car is easy in empty space; the complexity comes from other agents, uncertain obstacles, and the need to make decisions in real time. Also, any mistake can be costly—hitting a person or another vehicle is not an acceptable failure mode.

Papers tackle this by combining perception (seeing the world), prediction (guessing what others will do), and planning (choosing a safe trajectory). Some use neural networks to learn end-to-end: camera images map directly to steering and acceleration commands, trained on human driving data. Some build explicit models of the world—tracking where other cars and people are, predicting their future positions—and then use planning algorithms to find a safe path. Others combine the two: use neural networks to extract features from the scene, feed those to a planning algorithm that is aware of the robot's constraints (can only turn so fast, takes time to brake). Recent work emphasizes multi-agent reasoning—modeling not just one other agent, but the interaction between many agents—because traffic is a game where everyone is making decisions based on what they expect others to do.

Subtheme

Learning from few examples and demonstrations

Ideally, a robot learns a new task from just one or two examples shown by a human. A person shows the robot how to stack blocks, and the robot can immediately stack blocks in any configuration it has not seen before. This kind of learning—from few examples, sometimes called few-shot learning—is common in humans. A child sees one person tie a shoe and can tie their own shoes. But for robots, this is extremely hard because the robot has to extract the essential structure of the task from very limited data, without overfitting to irrelevant details.

The challenge is generalization. If you show a robot one video of stacking a red block on a blue block, the robot might learn 'put red things on blue things.' But the underlying task is 'stack blocks on top of each other'—the colors do not matter. Or the task is 'place object A on top of object B' and you should generalize to any objects. How does the robot figure out what matters? With a thousand examples, the robot can see a pattern. With one example, the robot has to guess. The robot also has to transfer what it learned from one morphology (body shape) to another—if the robot saw a human hand demonstrating a task, can it do the same task with a robot hand? The human hand and robot hand are different, so direct imitation does not work.

Papers here use several strategies. Some use meta-learning—train the robot on many different tasks, so it learns how to learn. When shown one new task, it recognizes patterns from previous tasks and adapts quickly, because it has practiced the skill of adapting many times before. Some use language to help—a human says 'put the red block on the blue block,' and language gives the robot a hint about which features matter, because words pick out what is important from the infinite details in the scene. Some use video as raw data—a human demonstrates with their hand, and the robot learns to translate human hand motion into its own motion. Others use implicit representations: the robot does not learn 'do this action,' but rather learns to embed observations in a space where similar tasks cluster together. In that space, one or two examples are enough to find nearby tasks the robot has seen before, because the representation has already organized similar tasks close to each other.

Subtheme

Real-time efficiency and deployment

Modern robot policies are often built from large neural networks trained on internet-scale data. A model might have hundreds of millions of adjustable internal weights—the values that the network uses to make predictions. Running this model on a real robot is a problem. The robot has onboard compute—a small GPU or CPU, not a data center. A large model might take five seconds to produce an action, but the robot needs to react in 100 milliseconds. Also, the model might not fit in memory. And if it takes too much power to run, the robot's battery runs out in an hour instead of a day. So the challenge is: how do you keep the intelligence and capability of large models while making them fast and small enough to run on a robot?

The difficulty is that scaling down a model usually means losing capability. If you shrink the network, remove layers, or quantize weights (represent them with fewer bits), the model becomes faster because it does less computation, but less accurate because it has fewer internal adjustable values to store the patterns it learned. Where is the sweet spot? Also, different robots have different compute budgets. A research robot in a lab might have a high-end GPU. A delivery robot has a small embedded processor. A surgical robot needs absolute reliability and cannot retrain in the field. So efficiency is not a one-size-fits-all problem. It depends on the robot, the task, and the environment.

Papers address this through model compression—techniques to make models smaller and faster without losing too much accuracy, because smaller models have fewer parameters to compute and fewer values to move through memory. Some prune tokens: instead of processing every detail in the image, the model learns which parts of the image are important for the task and skips processing the others, saving computation. Some use distillation: train a small model to imitate a large one. Some quantize weights or use mixed precision—use fewer bits for some weights and normal precision for others. Others optimize the model for specific hardware, rewriting the model to use the instruction set and memory layout of a specific GPU, which makes it faster on that particular hardware because the memory access patterns match the hardware design. The central insight is that real deployment requires thinking about compute from the start, not as an afterthought.

Subtheme

Understanding 3D scenes for robot tasks

A robot that sees only a 2D image is blind to depth. It does not know how far away the object is. Is it one meter away or ten meters away? If the robot reaches for a cup that is three meters away, it will fail. Humans see in 3D because we have two eyes and can compare the slight differences between them. Robots can use stereo cameras, or a single camera plus depth sensors, or learn depth from motion over time. Once the robot has a 3D understanding of the scene, it can do much more: reach for the cup at the right distance, avoid obstacles in 3D space, build a map of the room as it moves around, and reason about how objects interact in 3D space.

The challenge is converting 2D sensor data into a useful 3D representation. A 3D scene can be represented many ways: a point cloud (a list of 3D coordinates), a mesh (connected surfaces), a volumetric grid (voxels), or a neural scene representation (a neural network that can render the scene from any viewpoint). Each representation has trade-offs. Point clouds are fast but sparse—you do not know about empty space or surfaces facing away from the camera. Meshes are clean but hard to extract automatically. Volumetric representations are dense but use lots of memory. Neural representations are flexible but expensive to compute. Also, the robot often cannot see the entire scene from one viewpoint. It sees part of the scene, and other parts are hidden. The robot has to build up a mental model as it moves and looks from different angles.

Papers develop scene understanding methods for robotics. Some use 3D vision—stereo depth, techniques that measure depth by comparing two camera views—to build explicit 3D representations. Some use neural radiance fields (NeRFs), which are neural networks that learn to render 3D scenes, to build implicit models that the robot can query. Some focus on object-centric representations: instead of understanding the whole scene, the robot focuses on objects, their 3D poses (position and orientation), and their properties. This is useful for manipulation—you do not need to understand the entire background, just where the objects are and how big they are. Others use occupancy grids: a 3D grid where each cell is marked as occupied or empty, useful for navigation and planning.

Subtheme

Building world models for prediction and planning

A robot that can predict the future is much more capable than one that can only react to the present. If a robot pushes a block and can predict that it will slide across the table and fall off the edge, it can decide not to push so hard. If a robot predicts that a person will step into a crosswalk, it can brake preemptively. These predictions require a model of the world—an internal simulation of physics. The robot learns from experience: it watches what happens when it takes actions, and it builds a model that says 'if I push with force F at angle Θ, the object will move in direction D.' With such a model, the robot can reason about future outcomes without actually trying them.

The difficulty is that the world is complex and physics is hard. Real friction varies with materials and temperature. Objects deform in complicated ways. Multiple objects interact—one block hits another and they both move. Liquids splash and flow. Hair tangles. Cloth wrinkles. Simulating all of this requires detailed knowledge of material properties and numerical stability. Also, the robot's model is always incomplete and approximate. It sees the world through limited sensors. It does not know the exact mass of an object or the coefficient of friction. So the model is inherently uncertain, and that uncertainty grows as you predict further into the future, because small mistakes compound when you apply them repeatedly step by step. Errors compound—a small mistake about where an object is now becomes a large mistake about where it will be in five seconds.

Papers build world models using learned physics simulators—neural networks that predict the next state given the current state and an action. Some train these models on real robot data and in simulation, then use them for planning: the robot imagines taking different actions and uses the model to predict which one will succeed. Some use world models for control: instead of learning a direct mapping from observation to action, the robot learns a model and a separate 'imagination' module that uses the model to think about future actions. Some combine world models with other components: perception (estimate current state), world model (predict future states), reward model (evaluate which state is best), and planning (search over actions to find one that leads to good future states), so that all parts work together to make decisions. The goal across all these approaches is to give the robot the ability to think ahead.

Subtheme

Scaling policies across different robot types

A robot policy trained on one robot often does not work on another robot, even if they do the same task. A policy trained on one robot arm might not work on a different arm with different proportions or motor speeds. This is a huge practical problem because training a new policy for each new robot is expensive. Ideally, a policy trained on one robot, or on many robots collectively, would transfer to a new robot with minimal additional training. This would let researchers and companies scale: design a robot once, train a policy on a fleet of robots, and deploy to a new variant without retraining from scratch.

The challenge is that robots have different embodiments—different shapes, sizes, sensor suites, and motor capabilities. A robot with a long arm reaches differently than one with a short arm. A robot with high-precision motors behaves differently than one with imprecise actuators. The challenge is to learn policies that are abstract enough to work across embodiments but concrete enough to be actually useful. Some researchers focus on learning what matters for the task—a good gripper pose for grasping does not depend on arm length, just on object position and orientation. Others try to learn representations that are invariant to embodiment: the robot learns to extract features from images that do not depend on its own morphology. If the visual understanding is good enough, different bodies can all benefit from it.

Papers tackle embodiment transfer through several approaches. Some use visual-based policies exclusively—the robot sees the scene and learns to predict actions, and the visual understanding transfers across robots. Some learn a shared representation: train on multiple robots, extract features that all of them find useful, and build policies on top of that. Some use simulation: train on a diverse set of simulated robots, each with different properties, so the policy learns to work across different embodiments and shapes. Others use adaptation: train a base policy on a diverse robot fleet, then quickly fine-tune it to a specific new robot with a small amount of real data. The throughline is the same: visual and physical understanding can be separated and reused, so you do not have to retrain from scratch for each new body.
