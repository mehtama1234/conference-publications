MathFimer: Step Expansion for Better Math Reasoning

A model learns to fill in missing steps in math solutions so you can expand weak training data into stronger training data without needing a smarter model.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

When you teach a language model to solve math problems, the quality of the solution steps in your training data directly limits how well it learns. MathFimer solves this by training a specialized model to read a math problem and its final answer, then write out all the detailed intermediate steps in between. You use this to expand your existing training datasets, making them richer without needing an expensive external model.

Zero assumption version

Weak training data bottlenecks learning

When a language model learns to solve math problems from examples, it reads each problem and its solution. The more detail in the solution steps, the clearer the learning signal. But often training data has skimpy steps like 'add them up' with no breakdown. The model sees less structure, so it learns less well.

A naive attempt: use a smarter external model (like GPT-4) to expand the steps. It can write elaborate reasoning, but this is expensive—you pay per query every time, and you cannot afford to re-expand your whole dataset when you collect new data. So your dataset stays with sparse steps, and your model hits a ceiling.

The core move. Train a lightweight model to reconstruct missing steps by seeing only the problem start and the answer end.

What the paper changes

What changes: the intermediate steps

The paper modifies the reasoning steps in your training examples. It does not change the model architecture or the final answer. It takes sketchy, bare-bones steps and fills them with detail.

the object it moves  =  intermediate reasoning steps
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: step detail and completeness

measured quantity  =  step coverage (how much of the solution path is spelled out)

Most training data has a hidden measure: how much of the reasoning work is actually written down versus skipped. A solution that says 'compute 2 plus 3, add them, get 5' covers maybe 20% of what a student actually thinks through. A solution that says '2 plus 3: the first number is two, the second is three, adding means combining both groups, two combined with three gives five' covers 80%.

MathFimer estimates this by training a model to predict what should be there. When the model successfully reconstructs the missing middle, that tells you that the steps were predictable and well-connected. When it struggles, the steps might be incoherent or the answer might be wrong.

Why it works

Why it works: teaching through reconstruction

The core idea is borrowed from code completion: when you hide part of a program and ask someone to write it, they learn the hidden syntax deeply because they have to reason backwards from clues. The paper applies this to math reasoning. When you hide intermediate steps and train a model to reconstruct them, the model learns what good, coherent steps look like. It learns the patterns of mathematical thinking: 'when you see this problem, the next step should be this kind of reasoning.'

Once trained, you run this step-filler over all your existing solutions. It inserts new intermediate steps everywhere. Your training data now has much more detail. When you train your final reasoning model on this expanded data, it sees more examples of good reasoning patterns, so it learns to reason better because the learning signal is clearer—each step shows the next idea explicitly. You do not need GPT-4 to expand the data. A smaller, specialized model trained on your own data can do it cheaply and repeatably.

Math after the picture

The fill-in-the-middle loss

First in words:

loss equals negative log probability of predicting the true middle steps given the prefix and suffix

Then the compact form:

L_fim = -log P(middle_steps | prefix, suffix)

Every symbol:

L_fimthe training loss for the fill-in-the-middle task
middle_stepsthe true intermediate steps hidden from the model
prefixthe problem statement and any initial work shown
suffixthe final answer
P(middle_steps | prefix, suffix)the probability the model assigns to the correct steps
A tiny worked example
Problem: What is 15 + 27? Final answer: 42.
Prefix: 'What is 15 + 27?' Suffix: '42.'
True middle steps: 'First, add the tens: 10 + 20 = 30. Then add the ones: 5 + 7 = 12. Finally, 30 + 12 = 42.'
Model predicts: P(true_steps | prefix, suffix) = 0.8 (high confidence because the steps follow naturally).
Loss = -log(0.8) = 0.22 (low loss, model is learning well).
If model predicts bad steps: P(bad_steps | prefix, suffix) = 0.05, then loss = -log(0.05) = 3.0 (high loss, model gets corrected).

The model learns by seeing problems and answers, then guessing the reasoning in between. Correct guesses lower the loss; wrong guesses raise it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong answer or incoherent path. If the suffix (final answer) is incorrect or disconnected from the prefix, the model cannot learn what good steps look like. It invents nonsense to bridge the gap.
Repeating the prefix or suffix. The model might just copy parts of what it was given instead of generating new reasoning. This makes the expanded steps useless because they add no new information and the training model learns nothing new.
Domain mismatch. If you train the step-filler on one type of math problem (e.g., solving equations) but apply it to another (e.g., proofs), it may fill in steps that do not fit the new domain because the reasoning patterns are different.
Done right. Use high-quality training data to teach the step-filler, validate the expanded steps before using them, and stay in the domain you trained on.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a dataset of 100 simple arithmetic problems with sparse steps (e.g., 'What is 23 + 18?' with steps 'add the numbers' and answer '41'). Train MathFimer on 50 of them to learn step-filling. Apply it to expand the other 50. Take two identical language models, train one on the original sparse data and one on the expanded data. Measure their accuracy on unseen arithmetic problems.

improvement = (expanded_accuracy - original_accuracy) / original_accuracy

The expanded-data model should solve noticeably more problems correctly because its training steps revealed the hidden reasoning structure.
