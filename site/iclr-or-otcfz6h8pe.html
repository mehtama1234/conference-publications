DiscoX: Discourse-Level Translation Benchmark

To evaluate whether a translation model understands an entire expert-domain text as a connected whole, not just sentence-by-sentence, measure accuracy, fluency, and appropriateness together and penalize errors by severity.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Current ways of grading machine translation are broken: they look at each sentence in isolation, check if it is grammatically correct and sounds natural, and miss the bigger picture. When a document is about medicine or law, a single mistranslated term can make the whole thing wrong, and a sentence that reads fine might betray the author's tone or mess up the story across pages. DiscoX fixes this by providing 200 real expert-domain translations and a new way to grade them that looks at the whole document at once and counts different kinds of mistakes by how bad they are.

Why sentence-by-sentence grading fails

Imagine you hire a translator to convert a medical textbook from Chinese to English. A naive grader checks: is each sentence grammatically correct? Does it flow naturally? But it misses the real job of translation. The translator might use the wrong technical term consistently, making sentences 2 through 50 all refer to the same thing by different names. The grader only looks at sentence 5 in isolation: it reads fine. Or the translator might nail the meaning but use a formal register in a casual section, breaking the author's voice. Again, sentence-level grading says it is good.

A beginner might try: just show human experts the translation and ask, is it good? But human experts are expensive and slow. And they disagree on borderline cases. What the field needs is a benchmark—a set of real translations from real expert domains with clear rubrics (what counts as an error?) and a fast automatic grader that aligns with expert judgment when the experts agree.

The gap. Existing graders measure local accuracy and grammar, but miss discourse coherence, terminology consistency, and cultural appropriateness across a whole document.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect real texts

Domain experts gather 200 actual documents from 7 specialized fields (law, medicine, etc.) in Chinese.

Write expert rubrics

For each text, domain experts write specific checklists: which terms must stay consistent? Where does tone matter? What sounds natural in English?

Filter for clarity

Run the benchmark texts through model translations, filter out low-quality examples, refine the rubrics based on actual errors.

Score translations in three stages

(1) Check if the output is a valid translation at all. (2) Count three kinds of errors: accuracy (mistranslations), fluency (grammar, phrasing), appropriateness (tone, terminology, culture). (3) De-duplicate errors to find the root cause.

Apply severity weights

A major error (wrong medical term in a cardiology paper) costs more points than a minor one (single awkward phrase). The final score subtracts all weighted error penalties from the maximum possible score.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Before DiscoX, when you wanted to evaluate translation, you grabbed a sentence-level metric and ran it on each sentence. DiscoX swaps out the unit being graded: instead of sentences, you now grade the entire document as a coherent unit. The benchmark is the dataset (200 expert-domain texts with rubrics). The metric is the grading system (Metric-S, which looks at accuracy, fluency, and appropriateness all together and assigns different penalties for different error severities).

The hidden quantity

The severity-weighted discourse score

measured quantity  =  hierarchical error severity penalty

When you grade a translation, not all errors are equal. A mistranslated medical term that cascades through a whole paragraph is worse than a single clumsy phrase. The hidden quantity here is the severity level assigned to each error detected. Errors are classified as major (wrong term, missing whole sentence) or minor (single grammatical hiccup, awkward word choice). Metric-S counts both, but a major error deducts more points from the final score.

The paper estimates this by having domain experts write rubrics that tag exactly which kinds of mistakes are major (e.g., in a legal document, mistranslating a key phrase to mean the opposite is major). Then, when the automatic system detects an error, it cross-references the error type against the rubric to assign severity, de-duplicates overlapping errors (if a mistranslation cascades into three sentence-level problems, count it as one root error), and sums up the severity-weighted deduction.

Why it works

Why measuring severity across the whole document works

The old metrics miss big errors because they chop text into sentences. Metric-S works by reversing that: it reads the whole output, checks it is actually a valid translation (not gibberish), then looks for errors in three orthogonal dimensions: does every meaning from the original come through accurately? Does the result read fluently in English? Is the tone and terminology appropriate for the domain?

For each error found, the system does NOT count it once per sentence. Instead, it traces back: what is the root cause? If mistranslating term X causes awkwardness in three sentences, that is one root error with severity 4, not three separate mistakes. The final score deducts points based on the severity and count of unique root errors. A single major error (wrong term) costs more than three minor errors (grammar quirks) because the major error breaks the document's core content, while the minor errors are like surface scratches.

inputA translated document (100-2000 words or phrases)
stepInstruction-follow judge: is this even a valid translation?
stepThree error judges in parallel: find Accuracy errors, Fluency errors, Appropriateness errors
stepDe-duplicate: trace each error to its root cause (e.g. three sentence-level problems from one mistranslation count as one)
stepApply severity weights: sum deductions based on error severity
outputFinal score: max possible minus total deduction

The score calculation

First in words:

For each dimension (Accuracy, Fluency, Appropriateness), the score equals the maximum possible score minus the sum of all weighted error deductions. An error of severity level multiplied by that level's weight gives the deduction. The total score adds the three dimension scores together.

Then the compact form:

Score = S_Accuracy + S_Fluency + S_Appropriateness, where S_x = MAX_x - sum_i (w_i * e_i) for dimension x, w_i is the severity weight (1-4) and e_i is the count of unique errors of severity i

Every symbol:

S_xthe score for dimension x (Accuracy, Fluency, or Appropriateness)
MAX_xthe highest possible score for that dimension (e.g. 100 points)
w_ithe penalty weight for error severity level i (1=minor, 4=major)
e_ithe count of unique root-cause errors at severity level i
Scorethe final combined score (0-300 if each dimension maxes at 100)
A tiny worked example
Suppose Accuracy maxes at 100, Fluency at 100, Appropriateness at 100.
Translation has: 1 mistranslation (Accuracy severity 4), 2 grammar errors (Fluency severity 1 each), 1 tone mismatch (Appropriateness severity 2).
Weights: severity 1 = 5 points, severity 2 = 15 points, severity 3 = 25 points, severity 4 = 40 points.
S_Accuracy = 100 - (1 * 40) = 60 (big mistake costs a lot)
S_Fluency = 100 - (2 * 5) = 90 (minor grammar stuff is cheap)
S_Appropriateness = 100 - (1 * 15) = 85 (one tone issue hurts more than grammar)
Total Score = 60 + 90 + 85 = 235 out of 300

The weights ensure that mistranslated technical terms (which break discourse coherence) hurt the score far more than a single awkward phrase.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Rubrics are too strict or vague. If domain experts write rubrics that are inconsistent across texts, the grading becomes unreliable. Example: one expert marks informal phrasing as a major error, another as minor. Fix: blind experts review rubrics before locking them in.
Error de-duplication overshoots. If the system incorrectly groups unrelated errors as one root cause, it undercounts. Example: two separate mistranslations that happen to affect the same sentence might be de-duplicated into one. Fix: domain experts audit the de-duplication on borderline cases.
Severity weights are not calibrated. If a minor grammar error is weighted too high, the metric over-penalizes fluency. Example: a single missing comma should not cost as much as a mistranslated term. Fix: check that weights correlate with human expert preference on multiple texts.
Done right. When rubrics are precise, de-duplication is conservative, and weights are calibrated to match expert judgment, Metric-S achieves strong correlation with human scoring and reveals real gaps in model performance.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Implement a simplified version of Metric-S on two texts (one medical, one legal). For each, collect 5-10 error annotations from a domain expert (mark accuracy errors, fluency errors, appropriateness errors, assign severity). Run Metric-S with a fixed weights table: severity 1 gets 5 points, severity 2 gets 15 points, severity 3 gets 25 points, severity 4 gets 40 points. Test on pairs of translations: have a domain expert rank which translation in each pair is better by reading both and deciding which is more accurate, more fluent, and more appropriate for the domain, because an expert's judgment depends on whether the document as a whole makes sense and uses terminology correctly. Then check whether Metric-S gives the higher score to the translation the expert preferred. Count how many pairs Metric-S ranks correctly. This reveals whether the metric aligns with expert thinking or misses the things that matter.

agreement = (number of pairs where Metric-S rank matches expert rank) / (total pairs) * 100

If Metric-S correlation with expert judgment exceeds 85 percent on domain-expert pairs, the demo proves that severity-weighted multi-dimension scoring correctly ranks translations the way humans do, something sentence-level metrics fail at because they ignore discourse coherence.
