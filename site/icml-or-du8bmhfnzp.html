PATRA: Pattern-Aware Alignment for Time Series Reasoning

Extract trends and seasons from time series, teach them to a language model, and use reward-balancing to stop simple tasks from drowning out hard ones.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Language models are trained to turn words into predictions, but they often see time series (sequences of measurements over time) as just text or pictures. They miss the patterns that humans instantly recognize: a stock price rising, a monthly repeating cycle, a sudden spike. PATRA teaches a language model to first find these patterns (trends and repeating seasons) from the raw data, then learns to reason through them in a balanced way, so that the model gets just as good at hard questions as it does at easy ones.

Zero assumption version

Language models struggle with time series because they skip the patterns

A language model is software trained to predict the next word in a sentence. When you feed it a time series (just numbers, one per day or hour), it struggles in two ways.

First, it treats the series like text or a picture instead of spotting the key patterns humans see: does it trend upward? Does it repeat every month? Does it spike unpredictably? These patterns are the language of time series. A naive model that converts the numbers into text loses that language.

Second, in real applications, you have both easy questions (like 'what is the last value?') and hard ones (like 'will next week be higher or lower, and why?'). When training on mixed difficulty, the easy questions dominate because they produce larger rewards quickly (they are solved in 1 or 2 steps, while hard ones need many steps to learn), so the average reward signal pulls the training toward easy-task solutions and barely nudges the model toward hard reasoning. This leaves deep reasoning underdeveloped.

The beginner's fix: convert time series to words and hope the model fills in the patterns. This fails because the patterns (trend, seasonality) are baked into the numbers, not into language.

In one sentence. PATRA teaches a model to extract trends and seasons first, then balances training so hard reasoning questions get as much learning weight as easy ones.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Detect patterns in the time series

Separate trend (long-term direction) and seasonality (repeating cycles) from noise.

Step 2: Describe the patterns in language

Turn 'trend: rising 0.5 units per step' and 'season: repeats every 7 days' into words the model can learn.

Step 3: Feed patterns + time series to the language model

Model reads: 'Values show an upward trend and spike on Mondays. Next value is likely higher.'

Step 4: Score questions by difficulty

Mark easy questions (e.g., read the last value) vs. hard (e.g., explain why it spiked).

Step 5: Reweight rewards to balance training

Easy-question rewards are scaled down; hard-question rewards scaled up so both get equal learning signal.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the input to the model

Instead of feeding raw numbers or text, PATRA adds extracted patterns (trend + seasonality) alongside the time series. The model now sees both the data and its patterns labeled in language.

the object it moves  =  input: patterns added
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: learning weight per task

measured quantity  =  balanced task weight

When you train on many tasks at once, each task produces a reward signal (a number saying 'good job' or 'try harder'). All rewards get averaged, but if easy tasks produce huge rewards (because they are solved in 1 step) and hard tasks produce small ones (because they take many steps to solve), the average is dominated by easy tasks. The model's training nudges mostly toward easy-task solutions and neglects hard reasoning.

PATRA estimates this imbalance by measuring the reward each difficulty level produces during training, then adjusts the weight of each task's reward so all difficulty levels contribute equally to the learning signal. This is the hidden quantity: how much each task actually pulls the model's training, after balancing.

Why it works

Why it works: the two reinforcements

PATRA works by making two things explicit. First, the model sees the patterns (trend and seasonality) directly, not hidden in the numbers. This is like reading 'the graph is rising steeply' instead of trying to infer slope from coordinates. The model can then link those pattern words to the answer more directly because the language it already knows about change and cycles now directly applies, building stronger, faster reasoning.

Second, the balanced reward ensures both easy and hard questions train the model equally. Without balancing, training is like learning to play soccer while your coach only gives feedback when you pass sideways (easy, high reward) and stays silent when you take a difficult shot (hard, low reward). You become a side-passer and never learn to shoot. With balancing, the coach gives equal feedback on both, so you develop both skills.

Math after the picture

The balancing formula

First in words:

balanced_loss = sum over tasks of (raw_loss divided by estimated_reward_scale for that difficulty level)

Then the compact form:

L_balanced = sum_d (1 / R_scale_d) * L_d

Every symbol:

L_balancedthe total training loss after balancing
L_dtraining loss from tasks at difficulty level d
R_scale_dthe expected reward size for difficulty level d, estimated from recent training
1 / R_scale_dinverse weight: small rewards get boosted, large rewards scaled down
A tiny worked example
Easy tasks averaged reward 100. Hard tasks averaged reward 10.
R_scale_easy = 100, R_scale_hard = 10.
Inverse weights: 1/100 = 0.01 for easy, 1/10 = 0.1 for hard.
A loss of 50 from easy tasks becomes 50 * 0.01 = 0.5 in L_balanced.
A loss of 50 from hard tasks becomes 50 * 0.1 = 5.0 in L_balanced.
Same loss magnitude, but hard-task loss contributes 10x more to training. Model learns hard reasoning equally.

Dividing by reward scale un-does the imbalance that large rewards introduce, so every difficulty tier pulls the training equally.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pattern extraction is brittle if data is noisy. If measurements are very random (like stock prices in a crisis), the trend and seasonality extraction can fail to find real signals, sending the model bad input. Preprocessing to smooth the data or using more resistant pattern-extraction helps because cleaner input and fewer false patterns let the model focus on actual structure.
Balancing breaks if reward scale estimates are wrong. If you misjudge which tasks are really easy or hard (e.g., a 'hard' task turns out solvable in 1 step), the balance weights will be backwards, and the model trains toward the wrong goals.
Some domains have no clear patterns. If the time series is genuinely random (like lottery tickets each draw), extracting trends or seasons adds false structure the model believes, hurting generalization.
Done right. Use unsupervised pattern detection (e.g., statistical tests for trend and periodicity) on a held-out validation set to confirm the extracted patterns match human intuition. Estimate task difficulty in advance, not from live training data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time series question-answering dataset: 10 synthetic time series (some with trend, some seasonal, some noisy), each with 3 easy questions (e.g., 'What is the last value?') and 3 hard questions (e.g., 'Will the next 5 days be higher on average? Why?'). Train a small language model twice: once with raw numbers and balanced reward (baseline), once with pattern extraction and balanced reward (PATRA). Measure accuracy and reasoning depth on a held-out test set.

reasoning_improvement = (hard_acc_PATRA - hard_acc_baseline) / hard_acc_baseline

The demo must show that hard-question accuracy improves visibly when patterns are pre-extracted (because the model gets clearer input), and that balanced reward prevents easy questions from dominating training (because equal reward weights mean both learn at once).
