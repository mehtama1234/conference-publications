TsLLM: Teaching a Language Model to Read Time Series Numbers

Augment a large language model with a specialized time series encoder-decoder so it can analyze and predict numerical patterns while explaining them in plain English.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Large language models are great at reading text and reasoning about it, but they stumble when faced with rows of numbers that change over time (like stock prices or sensor readings). TsLLM solves this by giving the language model a specialized "sense" for time series: a trained encoder that converts numerical sequences into compact meaning-carrying shapes, then injecting those shapes directly into the language model's thinking so it can reason about time and numbers together.

Zero assumption version

Why language models can't read numbers well

A language model (a system trained to predict the next word) learns by reading enormous amounts of text. When you ask it to handle time series — sequences of numbers like daily temperatures, stock prices, or power consumption — it faces two problems. First, language models break words into small chunks that can be processed one at a time; numbers split poorly this way. The model has to convert 3.14159 into character chunks and process them one by one, losing precision and wasting computation. Second, the model has almost no training data with time series in it, because most text on the internet is words, not numerical measurements. The parallel: the model can speak English fluently (predict the next word in text) but stumbles on time series because it has never studied numerical patterns during training.

A naive attempt: just feed the numbers as text strings and hope the language model handles them. This breaks because the model wastes computation parsing digits instead of learning what the numbers mean, and it can't distinguish between 10 and 100,000 when text representation obscures how big the numbers are.

The move: Train a specialized time series encoder to compress numerical sequences into meaning-carrying shapes, then inject those shapes directly into the language model's internal representation space (the list of numbers it uses to think) alongside text, so both modalities speak the same language.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Pre-train the time series encoder

A specialized neural network (16-layer encoder-decoder) is trained on 50 million sequences using a loss called beta-VAE, which forces it to create compact numerical summaries without throwing away the shape of the signal.

Step 2: Add a scale-aware layer

Before encoding a sequence, normalize it to remove magnitude (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation), but separately remember the original mean and scale. This works because the encoder can now focus on shape (the pattern of ups and downs) without being confused by huge or tiny numbers, while the saved mean and scale let the model reconstruct absolute sizes afterward.

Step 3: Plug an adapter between encoder and LLM

A lightweight adapter learns to translate time series shapes into the language model's internal language by comparing patterns (learned weights that ask: which time series shape matches which language meaning?).

Step 4: Stage 1 alignment (train on 1 billion examples)

Train only the adapter and scale layer, freeze the encoder and LLM. This ensures the time series shapes translate cleanly into the LLM's internal representation space without destabilizing either.

Stage 2-3: Unfreeze and fine-tune (29 billion more examples)

Unfreeze everything and train on mixed time series and text tasks (forecasting with context, question-answering, anomaly detection, report generation) all as next-word prediction.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper injects a time series representation into a language model. Instead of converting numbers into text chunks (which wastes computation), it encodes sequences of 32 time steps (patches) into compact numerical shapes (128-number lists), then inserts those shapes directly into the sequence the language model processes. This works because a single 128-number shape carries the information of 32 time steps in the same internal format the language model uses for text, so the model can reason about both.

the object it moves  =  time series input representation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: patch compression quality

measured quantity  =  Patch compression balance

The core quantity the paper measures is how well the encoder squeezes a patch of 32 time steps into a dense list of 128 numbers without losing the information needed to reconstruct the signal or understand its shape. This is the trade-off between reconstruction (how close the decoder can rebuild the original sequence) and compactness (how small and tidy the list is).

The paper estimates this using a beta-VAE objective, a training loss that penalizes both reconstruction error (if the decoder can't rebuild the patch) and divergence from a standard shape (to keep the 128-number lists compact and comparable). Higher beta forces compactness because the penalty for divergence grows, squeezing the encoder to use fewer effective numbers; lower beta allows richer detail because the divergence penalty is weaker, so the encoder keeps more nuance. The paper uses beta at 1.5, a tuned balance point.

Why it works

Why injecting time series shapes into the language model works

A language model works by building a representation (an internal list of numbers) for every chunk (a word or punctuation mark or number), then using those representations to predict the next chunk. Normally, if you give it a number like 3.14, it breaks it into character chunks ('3', '.', '1', '4') and processes each separately, wasting computation and losing magnitude information. TsLLM replaces this with a single, information-dense shape produced by the time series encoder. The encoder has learned (on 50 million sequences) what patterns matter in time series: whether values are rising or falling, how fast they change, whether they repeat, whether they spike. It compresses all that into 128 numbers. When the language model receives those 128 numbers in its representation space (courtesy of the adapter), it can reason over them exactly as it does text: it sees the patterns the encoder extracted, it knows how to combine them with text shapes, and it can predict both the next time series patch and the next text word.

Why the adapter matters: the encoder was trained on pure time series data (fitting shapes), while the language model was trained on text (fitting words). They speak different languages. The cross-attention adapter learns a translation: it watches how time series embeddings should align with the language model's learned meanings so that when the model sees a time series input alongside text, it connects them correctly. By freezing the encoder and LLM during alignment (Stage 1), the adapter can learn this mapping without breaking the two pre-trained pieces. Then, unfreezing everything in Stages 2-3 lets the model refine both the encoding and the language understanding for the specific task mix.

The training objective

First in words:

Reconstruction loss plus (beta times divergence from standard shape), summed over all training patches

Then the compact form:

loss = ||x_reconstructed - x_original|| + beta * KL(q(z|x) || p(z))

Every symbol:

x_originalthe 32-step time series patch you want to encode
x_reconstructedwhat the decoder produces from the encoder's output
zthe 128-dimensional embedding the encoder creates
q(z|x)the encoder's belief about what z should be given x
p(z)the standard Gaussian shape we want all embeddings to follow
KL(...)divergence: a number measuring how different two distributions are
betaa strength dial; 1.5 in this paper; higher beta = more compact embeddings
Tiny worked example
Suppose a patch is [1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, ...] (rising values).
Encoder produces z = [0.5, -0.3, 0.2, ...] (128 numbers).
Decoder tries to rebuild: [1.01, 1.09, 1.21, 1.29, ...] (close).
Reconstruction loss: sum of squared differences = (1.0 minus 1.01) squared plus (1.1 minus 1.09) squared plus ... is about 0.005.
KL divergence (how far z is from a standard Gaussian): about 0.8.
Total loss = 0.005 plus 1.5 times 0.8 = 0.005 plus 1.2 = 1.205.
During training, loss shrinks. Reconstruction improves, KL improves, embeddings become compact and interpretable.

The beta-VAE loss forces the encoder to find a sweet spot: reconstruct the time series faithfully while keeping embeddings compact and comparable across different patches.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor encoder pretraining ruins everything. If the encoder is trained on too little data or with a bad beta, the patch embeddings will be noisy or lossy, and no amount of fine-tuning can recover the lost information. The 50 million sequences in this paper are essential.
Scale information gets lost. If you normalize sequences without saving the original mean and scale, the model learns only shape and forgets absolute magnitude. The paper's separate encoding of mu and sigma is essential.
Adapter misalignment during Stage 1. If you unfreeze the encoder or LLM too early, training updates corrupt the pre-trained representations before the adapter has learned to translate between them. Stage 1 (freezing both) prevents this.
Done right: balanced training. Pre-train the encoder well on large data, learn the adapter in isolation, then co-train everything on diverse tasks with careful learning rates and loss weighting.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small version: (1) Pretrain a tiny encoder (4-layer MLP) on 100,000 synthetic time series (sine waves with varying amplitude and frequency). (2) Plug it into a small language model (124M parameters). (3) Fine-tune on a mixed dataset: 500 forecasting examples paired with a sentence describing the signal (e.g., 'Sales are rising steadily'), 200 anomaly-detection examples ('Label the spike'), and 200 question-answering examples ('When does it drop below 5?'). (4) Measure: Can the model forecast accurately when given contextual text? Can it detect anomalies when described? Can it generate explanations?

Task accuracy = (forecasts within 10% of true + anomalies correctly labeled + QA answers on topic) / total_examples

The demo makes impossible to miss: when encoder embeddings are injected, the model solves mixed time series + text tasks that pure time series models cannot even attempt.
