Forecasting weather and natural phenomena

Building AI weather forecasters that blend physics knowledge with data to predict storms, climate, and environmental changes more accurately.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Weather is driven by equations — the laws of fluid dynamics and thermodynamics. Current weather models solve those equations step by step on supercomputers. A five-day forecast takes hours to compute. Beyond ten days, the tiny uncertainties in the current state blow up and predictions become useless. Climate models try to project centuries into the future, but the compute and data requirements are staggering, and the models still have significant errors.

The bottleneck is that solving the equations is expensive. What if we trained a neural network on decades of past weather data and current observations to learn to predict the next day, or the next week, directly? A learned model could generate a forecast in seconds. The challenge is that weather obeys physics — storms do not just teleport — and a model trained only on data might learn fake patterns that work on test data but fail in the real world. It might predict a storm that violates conservation of energy, or forecast rain in dry air.

This cluster trains neural networks to forecast weather and climate, and deliberately infuses them with physics. Some networks are trained on historical weather data at global scale, learning to predict pressure, temperature, and precipitation days ahead without solving the equations. Others blend learned components with traditional physics solvers: use a fast learned model to do the bulk of the computation, then correct it with a physics-based step to ensure conservation laws hold. Still others use the learned model to improve initial conditions or to handle sub-grid phenomena (small-scale features too fine to resolve, like how clouds form in a single grid cell). The payoff is faster, more accurate forecasts and climate projections that respect physics because adding physics constraints prevents the model from predicting impossible storms or violating energy conservation.

How the work breaks down

The work in weather forecasting splits into five main directions because different bottlenecks limit accuracy: building models that respect physics laws prevents predictions of impossible weather, designing network layers that capture weather at multiple scales preserves the details that drive storm formation and wind patterns, predicting extreme events like storms and high winds needs special focus since they happen rarely but matter most, correcting predictions after the fact to enforce physics guards against drift in coupled systems like oceans and atmosphere, and trying alternative network designs to replace standard grid-based approaches can deliver the same accuracy faster and cheaper.

Physics baked into the model

The core problem is that neural networks trained purely on data often learn patterns that violate the laws of physics. A model might predict rain in impossible places or violate conservation of energy. Two papers address this by deliberately encoding physics into the network structure itself.

DeepPrim learns weather by solving the primitive equations—the fundamental equations of fluid dynamics that govern how air moves vertically and horizontally—rather than treating weather as a black-box pattern in the data. This keeps the model physically plausible because it is constrained to follow the actual rules of atmosphere. It captures the full 3D dynamics without resorting to simplifications that traditional models use.

GeoFAR handles a different physics problem: when you zoom in from a coarse global forecast to a fine local forecast, high-frequency details become harder to predict. The method uses geography-aware learning to correct these fine-scale errors in both deterministic and generative forecasts. Both papers show that encoding the physics constraint directly into how the network operates prevents the model from predicting physically impossible weather.

Preserving weather frequencies and scales

Real weather has structure at many scales: tiny turbulence, mesoscale storms, and planetary-scale jet streams all matter. Most neural networks compress this information, losing important frequencies. These two papers fix that by designing architectures that preserve the spectral structure—the frequencies—that encode weather dynamics.

The spectral fidelity paper shows that a model trained on coarser 1.5-degree resolution can match models trained on fine 0.25-degree data, but only if the network preserves spectral alignment across all frequencies. This is counterintuitive: fewer data can work as well as more data if you keep the frequency content intact. EMFormer takes a different approach using multi-scale transformers: it processes weather at many spatial scales simultaneously, then combines them. This keeps both coarse features (like high-pressure systems) and fine features (like local wind shear) in play because both scales carry information—coarse scales drive the jet streams that steer weather, and fine scales capture where storms form. As a result, forecasts beyond ten days improve dramatically, and the model catches extreme events much better.

Predicting extreme weather events

Normal weather forecasting optimizes for average accuracy: getting the middle 80% of cases right. Extreme events—hurricanes, tornadoes, floods—are rare but catastrophic. Standard neural networks are biased against predicting these because they represent such a small fraction of training data. These papers specifically target extreme prediction.

Improving extreme wind prediction uses frequency-informed learning to focus the model's weight on the rare high-velocity cases in the data, which means the network learns what distinguishes a 100-mile-per-hour wind from normal wind. Rather than treating all wind speeds equally, it trains the network to emphasize extreme cases more heavily because extreme events need special attention—a standard model trained on mostly calm data will be mediocre at predicting rare dangerous winds. StormInsight tackles this differently for convective storms: it models the vertical structure (how a storm is layered top-to-bottom) and the environmental forcing (the larger-scale weather patterns that spawn the storm) because a storm is not an isolated event but a response to its environment. By capturing both the local storm dynamics and the surrounding environment, it produces coherent 3D reconstructions of how storms evolve—showing how updrafts intensify layer by layer. Both approaches reject the false choice between average accuracy and extreme accuracy.

Correcting forecasts after prediction

Even after training a neural network forecaster, the predictions can drift away from physics. Ocean and atmosphere are coupled—they influence each other—and a pure learning approach might predict a scenario where ocean heat and atmosphere temperature disconnect in an impossible way. The PnP-Corrector framework runs after the neural network prediction to inject physics constraints as a correction step.

On coupled 300-day ocean-atmosphere forecasts, this post-processing step cuts error by 28% because it fixes a specific problem: neural networks can predict ocean temperature and air temperature that together violate energy conservation—they might cool the ocean without warming the air enough, which is impossible. The way it works is that correction is separate from initial prediction: the network does the expensive heavy lifting of computing the next state, then a physics-aware corrector re-distributes energy and heat to ensure conservation laws hold. This is computationally cheap because the corrector is a small additional step, not a full re-training, so you get the benefit without re-training the entire network.

Alternative architectures for speed and reach

Some papers sidestep the question of how to enforce physics and instead ask: what architecture is fastest and most general? These papers explore different neural designs to handle the core forecasting problem across different domains and time horizons.

OmniField uses conditioned neural fields—a flexible representation—to learn patterns in multimodal sensor data (different types of measurements, potentially with gaps) because a single network architecture can adapt to different sensor types and missing data. It outperforms eight other spatiotemporal models on reconstruction, interpolation, forecasting, and even cross-modal prediction, and it keeps working even when sensors have noise because the flexible representation can ignore noisy sensor readings and learn from the clean ones. U-Cast is a simpler design that matches the accuracy of leading methods like GenCast but trains 10x faster and runs 10x quicker (the computation time to produce a forecast), which means you can retrain frequently and deploy faster. This speed is critical for operational forecasting where you need results in minutes—a 10x speedup can mean hours of lead time for warnings. UrbanGraph applies a graph-based approach to urban microclimate prediction: it models a city as a graph where each grid cell is connected to its neighbors, weighted by physics (wind-carrying heat between cells) because wind and heat flow between adjacent locations in predictable ways. This achieves top accuracy while cutting computation by 73% compared to earlier graph methods because the physics-aware weighting is more efficient, making fine-grained local forecasting practical.
