The Geometry of Representational Failures in Vision Language Models

VLM errors come from how overlapping concept directions tangle and block each other in the model's internal space.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Vision-language models (models trained to understand both images and text) fail in puzzling ways: they hallucinate objects that are not there, or confuse which of several objects looks most like what you asked for. This paper shows these failures are not random bugs but stem from how the model stores concepts: the directions representing different visual ideas get tangled up inside the model, like crossed threads in a loom.

Zero assumption version

What the model sees and gets wrong

A vision-language model is trained on millions of images paired with text descriptions. When you show it a picture and ask, 'What color is the flower?', the model computes an answer by sliding that image through many layers of neural nets (millions of tiny mathematical operations stacked together). The output should be right more often than not.

But sometimes it fails in very specific ways. Show it a red flower next to a blue vase and ask which is red, and it picks the vase. Or it sees a photo of three dogs and reports four. Or it says it 'sees' an elephant in a photo where there is none. A beginner might assume these are training bugs or label mistakes, but they repeat with a pattern: the errors cluster around situations where multiple objects share visual properties (two red things in the same image) or where similar-looking things are nearby (two dogs that look alike).

These failures look like a human cognitive problem called the binding problem — when your eye sees two red objects, your brain has to bind the red with its correct owner (this red is attached to the left object, that red to the right). If binding fails, you see a mismatch. But we did not know what inside the model causes these same kinds of mix-ups.

In one sentence. Extract directions (concept vectors, which are just lists of numbers encoding single visual ideas) inside the model, then measure how much they overlap; overlap predicts where the model fails.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Extract concept vectors

Run the model on images with and without each property (red vs not-red, flower vs not-flower); measure the difference in the model's hidden state.

2. Measure pairwise overlap

For every pair of concept vectors, compute how much they point in the same direction (cosine similarity, which is how aligned two arrows are).

3. Apply steering

Artificially nudge the model's internal activations (the intermediate numbers flowing through the nets) along a concept direction and observe how output changes.

4. Correlate overlap with errors

Check whether high overlap between two concepts predicts the model gets confused in images where both appear.

5. Validate in naturalistic scenes

Test on real photos with multiple objects and confirm the overlap pattern explains the failure locations.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper does not retrain the model or change its weights. Instead, it reaches inside the running model and measures the directions (in the hidden numerical space) that encode each visual concept. By identifying and measuring these directions, the paper makes hidden structure visible.

the object it moves  =  Concept vector directions
The hidden quantity

Geometric overlap between concept vectors

measured quantity  =  Concept vector overlap (cosine similarity)

Imagine the model stores information as arrows (lists of numbers) pointing in different directions in a space with many dimensions (like having hundreds of degrees of freedom instead of the three directions up/left/forward). Two concept arrows — say, one for red and one for flower — are nearly parallel if the model has tangled their meanings. The overlap is measured as cosine similarity: how much they point the same way (1.0 means identical direction, 0.0 means perpendicular, at right angles).

The paper estimates these vectors by showing the model many images, recording how its hidden numbers change when an attribute is present versus absent, and taking the average difference. Then it checks overlap using a simple measurement: multiply corresponding elements of the two arrows and add them up, then divide by how long each arrow is.

Why it works

Why overlapping concepts break the model's ability to separate them

When the model outputs a token (a word like 'red' or 'flower'), it computes a score for each possible word based on its internal hidden state. If the hidden state mixes red-ness and flower-ness into nearly the same direction, then both red and flower tokens get boosted together. The model cannot score red high and flower low, or vice versa — it bumps them as a package.

In a multi-object task, this creates conflicts. If the model is trying to decide which object is red, its internal representation loads up both red-ness and flower-ness at high strength for the red flower. But it also loads red-ness and (slightly) flower-ness for any other red object. When red and flower point the same way, the output score for flower rises even when no flower is there. The model hallucinates the flower because its internal space does not have a separate pathway for 'red minus flower.' The way the directions are laid out forbids the combination.

Steering experiments confirm this: if you artificially push the model's hidden state along the red direction only (without pushing on flower), the model reports red more often. This proves the direction is causal and real. The stronger the overlap, the harder steering must push to separate the concepts, because they are already pointing nearly the same way — you need a larger nudge to split them apart.

Math after the picture

Measuring overlap between concept vectors

First in words:

similarity = dot_product(red, flower) divided_by product(length(red), length(flower))

Then the compact form:

cos_sim(c1, c2) = (c1 · c2) / (||c1|| * ||c2||)

Every symbol:

c1first concept vector (e.g., red-ness direction)
c2second concept vector (e.g., flower-ness direction)
c1 · c2dot product: sum of element-wise products
||c1||length of c1: square root of sum of squares of all elements
||c2||length of c2
cos_simcosine similarity, ranges from -1 to 1; close to 1 means nearly parallel
A tiny worked example
Say red vector in a tiny space is [8, 2, 1] and flower vector is [7, 3, 2].
Dot product: 8*7 + 2*3 + 1*2 = 56 + 6 + 2 = 64.
Length of red: sqrt(8² + 2² + 1²) = sqrt(64 + 4 + 1) = sqrt(69) ≈ 8.3.
Length of flower: sqrt(7² + 3² + 2²) = sqrt(49 + 9 + 4) = sqrt(62) ≈ 7.9.
Cosine similarity: 64 / (8.3 * 7.9) ≈ 64 / 65.6 ≈ 0.98.
High overlap (0.98): red and flower nearly parallel, model cannot separate them cleanly.

High cosine similarity means the two concept vectors point nearly the same direction, making it hard for the model to activate one without the other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Extraction noise. If the concept vectors are estimated from too few images or noisy data, the overlap measurements become unreliable. This failure is mitigated by averaging across many examples.
Layer-dependent effects. Different layers of the model store information differently. A concept that overlaps high in layer 5 might be separate in layer 3. The paper must test at the right layer or report results per layer.
Steering does not force the model. Nudging the hidden state along a direction changes probabilities but does not guarantee the output. Very strong steering can also trigger saturation (numbers hit a wall and stop responding). Moderate intervention is key.
Done right. Concept vectors are stable across image sets; overlap measurements are consistent; steering reproduces the predicted failures reliably; and the geometric story explains previously mysterious error clusters.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small vision-language model (or use Qwen-VL open-weight checkpoint) and extract concept vectors for five visual properties: red, blue, flower, car, round. Compute pairwise overlaps. Generate a test set of images: single objects, pairs of objects (red flower with blue car), triplets. For each image, run the model normally and ask, 'Is the main object red? A flower?' Measure accuracy. Then, correlate concept vector overlap with error rates across all question-property pairs. Plot: x-axis is cosine similarity between the tested property concept and the distractor concept; y-axis is error rate. A strong positive correlation confirms the paper's claim.

correlation = how_much_higher_overlap_predicts_error_rate

If the plot shows a clear upward trend (high overlap means high error), the paper's story is real: the model's failures are not mysteries but consequences of how its internal concept directions tangle.
