Imitation Learning as Return Distribution Matching

Teach a robot to copy the expert's entire risk attitude, not just average score, by matching the shape of its rewards over time.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

When we learn from an expert by copying, we usually only care that our copy gets the same average score. But experts have personality—some are cautious and get steady, reliable results; others are bold and chase big payoffs with big risks. This paper asks: what if we taught our robot to copy that personality too? The trick is to match not just the average return, but the entire distribution—the pattern of what rewards the expert actually gets, spread out from worst to best.

Zero assumption version

Copying the average but missing the personality

When we teach a robot to imitate an expert, we only check: does the robot get the same average score? But this misses a core fact about how humans actually work. A surgeon who plays it safe gets 100 points every time. A surgeon who experiments gets 200 points half the time and 0 points half the time. Both have the same average (100 points), but they have completely different personalities.

Standard copying methods ignore this. They only match the average. A naive student of the safe surgeon would think: great, let me try wild experiments too. But the student ends up copying the wild experimenter by accident.

Why does this matter? Because the expert's personality is baked into their reward pattern. The cautious expert succeeded *because* they avoided risk. Copy the average and you copy the worst instincts of both styles at once.

The fix in one sentence. Instead of copying just the average score, copy the entire pattern of what the expert actually got—from worst outcomes to best.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect expert trajectories

Record many full episodes from the expert, keeping the sequence of states, actions, and rewards.

Measure expert reward distribution

For each trajectory, sum up all the rewards to get a single return. Plot all returns—this is the expert's distribution.

Augment state with cumulative reward

Teach the student policy: given current state *and* the total reward earned so far, decide next action.

Train policy to match distribution

Adjust student policy so its returns match the expert's pattern, measured by distance between two distributions.

Test on new trajectories

Run student on new tasks and verify it gets rewards with the same shape: same highs, same lows, same typical patterns.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the student's decision rules from Markovian (depending only on current state) to non-Markovian (also remembering cumulative reward so far). This memory of past reward acts like internal emotional state—a running tab of how well things are going. The student's next move now depends on both what it sees and how it feels (how much reward it has earned). This small change in what information is available turns out to be exactly what is needed to copy an expert's personality.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: distribution distance

measured quantity  =  Wasserstein distance between return distributions

This is the measure the paper actually cares about. Imagine lining up all the expert's returns on one axis and all the student's returns on another. Wasserstein distance measures: how much work would it take to slide and rearrange the student's returns to match the expert's exactly? If expert always gets 100 and student always gets 100, distance is 0. If expert gets 0 or 200 (half and half) and student gets 100 always, distance is big, because you would have to spread out the student's boring middle value to create the expert's dramatic extremes.

The paper uses this distance as the target: make algorithms that shrink this number. The algorithm computes the expert's actual distribution from past trajectories, then adjusts the student's policy to pull its own distribution closer, step by step, until the shapes match.

Why it works

Why matching the full distribution works

Here is the key: if you only match the average, a risky strategy and a safe strategy look identical, and the student can learn either one and still pass. But if you match the entire distribution—the exact spread of outcomes—the risky strategy and safe strategy become distinguishable. A safe strategy has a narrow, tall peak. A risky strategy has a wide, flat spread with rare extremes. These shapes force the student to learn *which* personality the expert actually has.

The paper's insight is that a Markovian policy (one that only looks at current state) is too rigid to match arbitrary distribution shapes. But if you give the policy access to its cumulative reward history—even just a rough count of how much reward it has earned so far—suddenly it becomes flexible enough. When the reward is high, it can adjust behavior cautiously; when reward is low, it can recover aggressively. This internal counter lets the policy paint any reward distribution shape. The algorithm then just tunes the policy to paint the expert's shape.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

Wasserstein distance between two return distributions: the average 'movement cost' to rearrange one distribution into the other.

Then the compact form:

W(η_π r, η_π_E r_E) ≤ ε

Every symbol:

WWasserstein distance (movement cost between two distributions)
η_π rthe return distribution of our student policy π under reward r
η_π_E r_Ethe return distribution of the expert policy π_E under expert reward r_E
εthe target accuracy: how close is close enough
A tiny worked example
Expert's return distribution: 100 points (100% of trajectories). Expert returns histogram has a single spike at 100.
Student's return distribution (before training): 50 points (30%), 100 points (40%), 150 points (30%). Student returns histogram is spread out.
Wasserstein distance: to move the student's histogram onto the expert's, we would need to shift 30% from 50→100 (cost 50×0.3=15), and shift 30% from 150→100 (cost 50×0.3=15). Total cost: 30 units per sample. This is the Wasserstein distance.
After training with the paper's algorithm: student returns histogram also shows 100 points (100%). Now Wasserstein distance is 0. Student has learned the expert's personality.

The equation says: we want our student's return distribution to be so close to the expert's that their Wasserstein distance shrinks below some tolerance ε.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unknown expert reward. If you do not know what reward the expert is actually optimizing for, you cannot measure their true distribution. The paper addresses this in a separate case with an oracle, but standard use needs the reward function known.
Non-Markovian policies have a cost. Making the policy remember cumulative reward means the state space is bigger (every combination of state plus cumulative reward count). This can make learning slower in complex environments.
Discretizing the reward counter. The cumulative reward is discretized into buckets to keep computation tractable. If the buckets are too coarse, you lose information; if too fine, you blow up the state space. Choosing the right granularity is a tuning problem.
Done right: tabular setting. The method is proven efficient in the tabular (lookup-table) setting where the number of states and actions is small and manageable. This limitation is honest; extending to large continuous spaces would need different techniques.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple gridworld where an expert policy has a clear personality: for example, a cautious expert that stays on the safe path and gets reward 100 every time, and a risky expert that gambles and gets 200 or 0 randomly. Collect trajectories from both. Then train two student policies: one with standard behavior cloning (average only) and one with this paper's distribution-matching method. Run both on fresh test episodes. The standard student should often crash (if it learned the risky strategy) or seem confused (mixing both). The distribution-matching student should consistently stay on the expert's chosen path.

Wasserstein distance = average cost to move student returns histogram to match expert returns histogram
