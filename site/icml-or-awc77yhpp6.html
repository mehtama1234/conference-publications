A Very Big Video Reasoning Suite

VBVR is a million-video dataset with 200 reasoning tasks and rule-based evaluation that shows how well video models understand spatiotemporal causality and interaction.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Video models today can make pretty pictures, but can they actually reason? This paper asks: do video models understand what causes what in the world—like why a cup falls when knocked over, or how objects interact in space? To answer this, researchers built the Very Big Video Reasoning Dataset (VBVR): over one million videos labeled with 200 different reasoning questions, making it roughly a thousand times bigger than past video reasoning datasets. They also built a new way to score answers—not by having another model guess if you are right, but by checking your reasoning against hand-written rules that match how humans think. Using VBVR, they tested if bigger datasets and bigger models learn to reason about new kinds of problems they have never seen before.

Zero assumption version

Video models skip reasoning, and we cannot measure it

Video models today focus on one thing: making videos look good. They can generate clear frames, remove blur, or upscale images. But a deeper question remains invisible: do these models actually understand what is happening in a video? A beginner might assume that if a model can predict what the next frame looks like, it must understand why things happen—causality, object interaction, how the physical world works. That assumption breaks immediately. A model can memorize that 'brown thing often moves after hand-shaped thing approaches' without grasping that the hand is pushing the brown thing. It copies patterns in pixels without reasoning about spatiotemporal cause and effect.

Worse, we have no good way to measure reasoning. Past video datasets asked simple questions (usually counting or color-matching) on tiny toy videos. To study whether models truly reason about why and how, we need a massive, systematic collection of videos labeled with diverse reasoning questions—and a way to score answers that does not rely on having some other model guess if you are right. Without that, scaling video reasoning is like driving in fog.

The core idea. VBVR is a dataset of one million videos with 200 diverse reasoning tasks and a rule-based scorer that does not depend on other models.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Organize spatiotemporal reasoning

Design 200 reasoning tasks grouped by type: causality (why did this happen), interaction (how do objects move together), continuity (does this make physical sense), prediction (what comes next).

Label one million videos

Collect and annotate video clips with diverse ground-truth answers to each reasoning question—curated, not randomly generated.

Build a rule-based scorer

Write down the logic for correctness: if the model answered 'ball rolled left because it was pushed', match this against rules that check object tracking + collision logic.

Train models of different sizes

Feed VBVR to video models ranging from small to large, measure reasoning accuracy at scale.

Test generalization

Measure whether models trained only on subset of reasoning types can answer unseen types correctly.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

VBVR changes the measurement instrument: it replaces model-based judging (asking another neural net 'is this right?') with rule-based judging (checking answers against explicit hand-written rules written by humans). This shift makes two things possible: reproducible evaluation (you can run the same test tomorrow and get the same answer) and interpretable diagnosis (you can see exactly why the model failed instead of a black-box confidence score).

The hidden quantity

Emergent generalization: does the model reason about new task types?

measured quantity  =  generalization to unseen reasoning tasks

The paper measures a hidden quantity: whether a model trained on a subset of the 200 reasoning task types can answer questions about the remaining unseen types. This is generalization—the model is not just memorizing 'causality questions' but actually learning what causality is, then applying it to new scenarios. A model that merely copies patterns within seen task types will score near-random on unseen types. A model that learns the underlying reasoning principle will transfer.

The paper estimates this by splitting the 200 reasoning tasks: train on 150, hold out 50, then measure accuracy on those 50. If accuracy on unseen tasks stays high, emergent generalization is happening. The scaling studies (testing models of different sizes) show early signs of this—bigger models do better on held-out reasoning types because they have more knobs to turn (more weights, or the adjustable numbers inside the model) and thus can store abstract rules rather than memorizing specific examples, not just reproducing what they have seen.

Why it works

Why scaling with VBVR reveals reasoning

The mechanism works in two parts. First, curating 200 diverse reasoning task types forces the dataset to span the full range of spatiotemporal understanding—causality (when does A cause B?), interaction (how do objects move together?), continuity (is this motion physically possible?), prediction (what comes next?). If a dataset only asks 'count the red balls', a model can solve every question by pattern-matching without reasoning. Covering all these types means no single trick solves everything; the model must learn underlying principles.

Second, rule-based scoring matters because it removes the confound of a second model's biases. When you train a model to score answers, that model has its own blindspots—maybe it accepts any answer that uses certain keywords, or fails on answers phrased unexpectedly. Rule-based scoring says 'if the model tracked object A, detected collision B, and predicted motion C correctly, mark it right'—concrete facts, not a neural guess. This means every data point is trustworthy. When you train on a million trustworthy labels across 200 task types, scale effects become visible: bigger models show emergent reasoning on unseen tasks. That emergent jump is the sign that genuine reasoning is emerging, not just pattern memorization.

Math after the picture

The scaling formula

First in words:

generalization_accuracy = base_performance plus (model_size factor times task_diversity_bonus) minus (held_out_task_difficulty_penalty)

Then the compact form:

accuracy_unseen = a + b * log(model_params) + c * num_train_tasks - d * (1 - overlap_with_unseen)

Every symbol:

accuracy_unseenthe model's score on reasoning tasks it never trained on
model_paramstotal number of weights in the video model
num_train_taskshow many of the 200 reasoning task types were in the training set
overlap_with_unseenhow similar the training tasks are to the held-out tasks (0 = completely different, 1 = identical)
a, b, c, dfitted constants from the scaling studies
A tiny worked example
Small model (100M params): trained on 100 task types, tested on 50 held-out types. Base performance a=10, log(100M)=8, num_train_tasks contribution = 100*0.2=20, overlap penalty = 30. Accuracy = 10 + 1.5*8 + 20 - 30 = 22 out of 100.
Medium model (500M params): same setup. log(500M)=8.7, num_train_tasks = 20, overlap=30. Accuracy = 10 + 1.5*8.7 + 20 - 30 = 30 out of 100.
Large model (2B params): same setup. log(2B)=10.3, num_train_tasks = 20, overlap=30. Accuracy = 10 + 1.5*10.3 + 20 - 30 = 40 out of 100.
Larger model improves on unseen tasks (10 point jump from medium to large), suggesting it learned generalizable reasoning principles, not just memorization.

This formula captures the intuition: bigger models learn more because their extra parameters can store more reasoning patterns, more training task diversity helps because the model sees more examples of 'how to think', and if held-out tasks are very different from training tasks they hurt because there is less to transfer. The log(model_params) term reflects how learning typically scales with model size.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reasoning tasks too similar to training tasks. If the 'unseen' reasoning types are only slight variations on training types, the model appears to generalize when it is really just extrapolating. The paper guards against this by ensuring task types are orthogonal (e.g., causality reasoning is distinct from interaction reasoning).
Rule-based scorer misses legitimate reasoning. Hand-written rules are brittle. If a model reasons correctly but expresses the answer in unexpected words or logic, the rule-based scorer may mark it wrong. The paper must validate rules against human judgment to avoid false negatives.
Video quality bias hides reasoning failures. If some videos are clearer or more obvious than others, a model might solve those through visual shortcut-taking rather than reasoning (e.g., bright colors encode the answer). Curation must balance video diversity with clarity.
Done right: careful task taxonomy. The paper succeeds by defining the 200 task types orthogonally (no overlap), validating rule-based scores against human raters, and using diverse high-quality videos. This keeps the signal clean.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show three things. First, train a small video model (hundreds of millions of parameters) on VBVR for a few task types (e.g., causality + interaction). Second, freeze the model and test it on a held-out reasoning type (e.g., continuity) using the rule-based scorer. Third, repeat with a larger model and show that the larger model scores higher on the held-out type. This makes emergent generalization concrete: the bigger model learned abstract reasoning principles and can apply them to new situations because it has more representational capacity to capture deep patterns, rather than just memorizing by shortcut. Ideally, vary the held-out task type (try causality as held-out, then interaction, then prediction) and show that generalization stays strong across reasoning types.

generalization_gap = accuracy_on_held_out_unseen_tasks - accuracy_on_random_guessing

If the generalization gap stays above zero and grows with model size, the model is learning reasoning principles, not memorizing patterns.
