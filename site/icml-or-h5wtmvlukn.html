Predicting Large Model Training Loss with Batch Size

A loss prediction formula that forecasts what the training error will be given the model size, batch size, and number of training steps—working even when batch size changes.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

When you train a giant language model, you want to know in advance: how big should the model be, how many examples should I train on per step, and how many steps should I train for? Today's scaling laws can only guess. This paper introduces a formula (the Noisy Quadratic System) that predicts the final training error for any combination of these choices, and it works at much bigger scales than previous methods.

Zero assumption version

Predicting training loss is blind without batch size

Today's scaling laws (like Chinchilla) predict training error using only model size and total dataset size. But they ignore a third knob: batch size—how many examples the model learns from in one step.

Why does batch size matter? Smaller batches introduce more noise in each training step, and that noise changes how the model converges. The old formulas ignore this, making them inaccurate when you vary batch size or try to predict loss at much larger scales (like 1000 times bigger).

A beginner's first try: fit a curve to past training runs and extrapolate. But this fails because the pattern changes when batch size shifts, and the fit collapses at distant scales.

The paper predicts training loss using three variables: model size, batch size, and training steps—instead of just two. Instead of a black-box curve fit, the paper builds a mechanistic formula grounded in how gradient noise (randomness in the direction to update weights) drives training dynamics.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect training runs

Train large models with different sizes, batch sizes, and durations. Record the final training error.

Build a mechanistic model

Express loss as the error on a quadratic (curved) loss surface plus noise from random mini-batch gradient steps.

Account for LayerNorm

A technique called LayerNorm (which normalizes activations) changes how fast training actually moves—add that correction.

Fit to your data

Adjust the formula's constants to match your collected runs, even when batch size is different.

Predict at new scales

Plug in any model size, batch size, and step count—the formula outputs predicted loss, even at 1000 times larger scales.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces a formula that includes batch size as an explicit input—the old formula did not. Because batch size affects training noise, and training noise affects final loss, the new formula is mechanistically grounded: it models how random (random) gradient steps converge to a minimum on a curved loss surface (the bowl-shaped region where training stops).

the object it moves  =  batch-size-dependence in loss prediction
The hidden quantity

Gradient noise—the invisible force shaping convergence

measured quantity  =  training noise and effective learning rate

Gradient noise is the randomness in each training step—it comes from seeing only a small random batch instead of the entire dataset. Smaller batches create larger random fluctuations in the gradient direction.

The paper measures this noise implicitly by including batch size in the loss formula. Smaller batches add noise; the model 'sees' this because small-batch training slows convergence, and that slowdown is captured as higher final loss for the same number of steps.

The paper also accounts for LayerNorm, a technique in all modern language models that divides gradients by the norm (magnitude) of the weight vector—this acts like an automatic learning rate adjustment. The effective learning rate is proportional to 1 divided by the weight magnitude, which means the formula must track this effect to predict loss correctly at different batch sizes.

Why it works

Why small batch size raises final training loss

The paper grounds its formula in a key insight: training is like rolling a ball downhill on a curved surface (the loss surface) while the slope keeps changing randomly (due to different mini-batches). A large batch gives you a steady estimate of the true slope—so your nudge is well-aimed and the ball rolls smoothly to the bottom. A small batch gives a noisy estimate of the slope—so you sometimes nudge sideways or even uphill, wasting steps and taking longer to reach the minimum.

Modern language models use LayerNorm, a normalization step that automatically scales the learning rate inversely with weight magnitude. This means the effective learning rate depends on the current state of the weights. The paper includes this feedback loop: small batch training is noisier, so it explores more of the loss surface before settling, and during this exploration the weight magnitudes change, which changes the effective learning rate, which changes convergence speed.

The formula captures this whole dynamic: as you increase batch size (less noise), or increase the model size (more parameters means a larger region to optimize), or increase training steps (more time to converge), the final loss follows a specific mathematical pattern. The pattern is a noisy quadratic—meaning the underlying loss surface is smooth and bowl-shaped, but random fluctuations in the gradient estimates add a variance term that depends on batch size.

Math after the picture

The formula: loss from model size, batch size, and training steps

First in words:

Training loss equals a baseline error plus a penalty that grows with noise (inverse batch size) and model size, tempered by training time (more steps reduce the penalty).

Then the compact form:

Loss(N, B, K) = E_irr + alpha * N / sqrt(K) + beta * (1/B) / sqrt(K)

Every symbol:

Loss(N, B, K)predicted training loss at end
E_irrirreducible error—lowest possible loss on this task
Nmodel size (number of parameters)
Bbatch size (examples per training step)
Ktotal number of weight updates (training steps)
alphaconstant controlling how much model size affects loss
betaconstant controlling how much batch size affects loss
sqrt(K)square root of training steps; more steps reduce the penalty
A tiny worked example
Suppose E_irr = 2.5, alpha = 0.8, beta = 1.5.
Model A: N=100M, B=256, K=10,000 -> Loss = 2.5 + 0.8*100/sqrt(10000) + 1.5*(1/256)/sqrt(10000)
= 2.5 + 0.8*100/100 + 1.5*0.00391/100
= 2.5 + 0.8 + 0.000059 = 3.30
Model B (larger, same budget): N=200M, B=128, K=5,000 -> Loss = 2.5 + 0.8*200/sqrt(5000) + 1.5*(1/128)/sqrt(5000)
= 2.5 + 0.8*200/70.7 + 1.5*0.00781/70.7
= 2.5 + 2.26 + 0.000165 = 4.76
Model A (smaller model, larger batch, more steps) converges better. Model B's larger size and noisier training (smaller batch relative to steps) hurt more than its extra steps can fix.

The square-root-of-steps term comes from averaging noise over random training steps; larger models and smaller batches add noise, but more training time lets that noise average out.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Different architectures have different noise curves. The formula fits to a specific model family (e.g., all models with the same block structure). If you move to a different architecture, you must refit the constants.
Extreme batch sizes break the assumptions. The formula assumes batch size is large enough that the central-limit theorem holds (gradients are approximately Gaussian noise). Batch size of 1 or 2 violates this.
LayerNorm tuning matters. The effective learning rate depends on LayerNorm scaling; if you turn off LayerNorm or use a different normalization scheme, the formula must be refit.
Done right: collect training runs that cover your target region (different N, B, K combinations), refit once per architecture family, validate on a held-out set before using. The formula is fast to compute—just plug in numbers—so you can evaluate it on a grid of (N, B, K) to find the best configuration for your compute budget.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train three language models: one small with large batch, one large with small batch, one medium with medium batch—all with the same total compute budget (total number of gradient operations). Use the formula to predict each model's final training loss before training. Then train them and measure the actual loss. The demo succeeds if the formula's predictions rank the three models in the same order as the actual results, and the gap between prediction and reality is small.

prediction_error = |predicted_loss - actual_loss| for each model; smaller is better. Also: did prediction correctly rank all three in compute-constrained comparison?

The key moment: the formula should correctly predict that the medium-batch model converges faster than the tiny-batch model, even though both spent the same total compute budget. This is impossible for the old formula (which does not know batch size), but the new formula nails it because it includes the noise-convergence relationship.
