VenusX: Unlocking Fine-Grained Functional Understanding of Proteins

VenusX is a benchmark that tests whether protein-learning models understand the detailed jobs of individual amino acid building blocks, not just the whole protein.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Proteins are like factories with many parts, and each part has its own job. Current benchmarks test whether models can predict the overall job of a protein, but they ignore what each tiny piece does. VenusX is the first benchmark that asks: does your model really understand the fine details inside a protein?

Zero assumption version

Models see the whole protein, but miss the detailed pieces

Imagine a car engine test that only checks if the car moves forward, but ignores whether the spark plugs, valves, and pistons work. That is what protein benchmarks do today. They test if a model can predict what a whole protein does, but they do not check if the model understands what each amino acid (the building block) does inside the protein.

A beginner would say: my model predicts protein function well, so it must understand how proteins work. But that is like saying a calculator that gives correct answers must understand why the math is true. The model might be pattern-matching surface signals instead of really grasping the internal structure.

The gap: Existing benchmarks only test whole-protein predictions; they never check whether models understand which exact amino acids handle the binding, which ones are conserved over evolutionary time, or which ones are the active sites where the chemical reaction happens.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect annotations

Gather 878 thousand labels from three databases, each marking which amino acids do what.

Define six task types

Active sites (where chemistry happens), binding sites (where other molecules attach), conserved (unchanged over evolution), motifs (repeated patterns), domains (functional chunks), epitopes (antibody targets).

Create realistic splits

Separate proteins by evolutionary distance using three similarity thresholds: same family, distant family, unknown family.

Test diverse models

Run protein language models, sequence-plus-structure hybrids, pure structure methods, and alignment-based techniques.

Measure which models fail

Show accuracy drops for each model type, revealing which gaps exist and which tasks are hardest.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Functional resolution

measured quantity  =  Functional resolution

Functional resolution means how well a model understands the internal structure of protein function. A model with high resolution knows not just what the protein does, but exactly which building blocks do each part of the job. A model with low resolution might guess the overall function correctly but fail when asked about specific amino acids.

VenusX measures functional resolution by testing predictions on six types of amino acid labels: which ones are active sites, which bind other molecules, which are evolutionarily unchanged, which form repeating patterns, which form functional chunks, and which are antibody targets. The paper tracks how much accuracy drops when the protein comes from a different evolutionary family than the training set, which is the clearest signal that a model lacks true internal understanding.

Why it works

Why this benchmark reveals the real gaps

Whole-protein prediction is too coarse. A model can predict that a protein is an enzyme even if it misses half the amino acids that make the reaction happen. It is like identifying a car as a sedan by looking at the shape, without checking if the engine runs.

VenusX forces models to predict the exact role of each amino acid (residue-level and fragment-level tasks), which requires genuine internal understanding. When a model sees a protein from a different evolutionary family, it cannot lean on surface similarity anymore. The model has to transfer its understanding of how active sites work, how binding sites look, how motifs are organized, and how domains fold. The accuracy drop on out-of-distribution families is the truth test: it shows whether the model learned real mechanisms or just memorized training patterns.

The six task types are not arbitrary. Each one probes a different kind of knowledge: active sites test chemical understanding, binding sites test how other molecules attach, conserved sites test evolutionary knowledge, motifs test pattern recognition, domains test structural chunks, and epitopes test antibody targeting. Together they form a complete picture of whether a model has internalized how proteins actually function at the amino acid level.

Math after the picture

The measurement equation

First in words:

For each amino acid, compute the classification accuracy separately for in-distribution (same evolutionary family) and out-of-distribution (new family). Calculate the drop as the difference. Average across all amino acids and all six task types.

Then the compact form:

GeneralizationGap = Accuracy_InDist - Accuracy_OutOfDist

Every symbol:

Accuracy_InDistthe model's success rate predicting amino acid roles in proteins from the training family
Accuracy_OutOfDistthe model's success rate predicting amino acid roles in proteins from a new, unseen family
GeneralizationGaphow much accuracy the model loses when facing new protein families
A tiny worked example
Protein language model on in-distribution (same family): identifies 89 out of 100 amino acids correctly = 89% accuracy.
Same model on out-of-distribution (new family): identifies 71 out of 100 amino acids correctly = 71% accuracy.
Generalization gap: 89% - 71% = 18 percentage points. The model loses nearly a fifth of its accuracy on truly new proteins.
If the gap is small (5%), the model learned general principles; if large (20%+), it memorized the training family.

A small gap means the model understood the deep structure of proteins; a large gap means it learned surface patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Models memorize one family. A model can achieve 90% accuracy on proteins from the same evolutionary family simply by learning surface patterns specific to that family. It will crash on new families.
Annotation errors hide real performance. If the 878 thousand labels from the databases contain mistakes, no model can score perfectly. VenusX depends on data quality from InterPro, BioLiP, and SAbDab.
Six tasks are not exhaustive. Real proteins may have other functional features VenusX does not test, such as allosteric sites (places where binding elsewhere changes behavior) or cross-protein interaction surfaces.
Used correctly. Compare models on the hardest splits (cross-family generalization) and weight the six task types equally if you want a fair overall score. Report both in-distribution and out-of-distribution numbers always.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small protein classifier using one of the baseline models (e.g., a pretrained protein language model). Train it on 5 thousand samples from one protein family (e.g., kinases). Then evaluate it on 1 thousand new samples from the same family and 1 thousand samples from a completely different family (e.g., antibodies). Plot the accuracy separately for each group. The demo makes it impossible to miss: the model's performance collapses on the new family, because the model learned to recognize patterns in kinase sequences that do not appear in antibody sequences, so when asked to label antibody amino acids, it has no learned mapping to fall back on.

Out-of-distribution drop = (accuracy on same family) - (accuracy on new family)

If the drop is large (15% or more), the model learned only family-specific patterns and does not truly understand protein mechanics at the amino acid level.
