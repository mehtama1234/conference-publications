Instilling an Active Mind in Avatars via Cognitive Simulation

To make avatars truly expressive, combine an AI reasoner that understands emotion and intent with a diffusion model that fuses audio, images, and text into natural motion.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Video avatars today can move smoothly but often feel robotic because they just sync motion to audio waves without understanding what the character is actually trying to express. This paper proposes two ideas working together: first, an AI reasoner (like a brain) that reads the audio and images to figure out the character's emotion and intent, and second, a specialized diffusion model (a method for gradually building images by removing noise) that accurately blends audio, images, and text guidance to produce motion that feels authentic and emotionally coherent.

Zero assumption version

Why avatars feel robotic today

Current video avatar generators work by taking audio and images of a person and then generating new video frames showing smooth motion. The problem is they treat audio like a simple mechanical signal—they learn to sync lips to the loudness and pitch of sound waves, nothing more. They never actually understand what emotion the person is expressing (sadness, excitement, confusion) or what intent drives the motion (asking a question, making a point, showing surprise).

A beginner might try to fix this by just adding more training examples or making the model bigger. But that does not work because the audio signal alone does not carry emotional meaning—it carries only raw frequency patterns. Without something to interpret those patterns and figure out intent, no amount of data helps, because the model has no way to learn the connection between an ambiguous audio signal and a specific emotion. It is like learning to recognize whether someone is excited or nervous just from their voice—without seeing their face or knowing the context, these sound nearly identical, so pure audio-based learning is stuck. The motion stays robotically tied to the audio waveform instead of reflecting the character's inner mental state.

The fix in one sentence. Before generating motion, use an AI reasoner to read the audio and images and figure out what emotion and intent are happening, then feed that understanding to a specialized motion generator that fuses all the information together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Agentic reasoning

A language model reads the audio transcript and images to infer emotion, intent, and context.

Step 2: Semantic text guidance

The language model outputs a structured text description (for example: 'character is confused, tilts head, asks a clarifying question').

Step 3: Multimodal fusion model setup

A specialized diffusion model receives three input streams: audio (encoded as a list of numbers), image features, and text (encoded as a list of numbers), all mixed together in a neural network layer.

Step 4: Pseudo last frame conditioning

During training, the model learns from both the first and last frame of the video clip, preventing the static reference image from blocking motion learning.

Step 5: Noise removal generates motion

The diffusion process gradually removes noise, using the semantic guidance and audio to sculpt motion that matches emotion and intent.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of feeding audio directly into the motion generator, the paper inserts a language model in front that reads the audio and images, figures out the character's emotion and intent, and outputs a text summary. This summary then flows into the motion generator alongside the audio and images. Because the text summary directly names the emotion and intent, the motion generator can learn to connect motion patterns to emotional meaning, rather than just to audio waveforms. This works because semantic text is unambiguous (the word 'excited' always means one thing) while audio is ambiguous (excitement and anxiety sound similar).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: semantic alignment

measured quantity  =  semantic alignment

Semantic alignment is how well the generated motion matches the actual emotion and intent of the character, rather than just matching audio waveforms. For example, if someone says 'I am confused' in a sad tone, pure audio-driven motion might sync lips perfectly but move the head calmly—missing the confusion. Semantic alignment means the head tilts, the gaze shifts, and the hands gesture in ways that express confusion.

The paper measures semantic alignment through user studies where humans watch the video and rate whether the motion feels emotionally consistent with what the character is expressing. They also compute objective metrics like motion naturalness and gesture dynamics (how expressive the head and hand movements are). The agentic reasoning module directly improves this hidden quantity because it explicitly names the emotion and intent, which the diffusion model then uses as guidance.

Why it works

Why this two-step approach works

Emotion and intent live in a different layer of meaning than raw audio. Audio carries frequency patterns; emotion carries information about mental state. A diffusion model (a neural network that learns by watching videos) looking only at audio must guess mental state indirectly, which is hard—like trying to guess someone's mood just by listening to their voice without seeing their face or knowing what they are saying. Is a high pitch excitement or anger? Is fast speech enthusiasm or nervousness? These are ambiguous.

By adding a language model that can read both the audio transcript and the visual reference image, the system can answer these questions directly. The reasoner sees the person says 'This is amazing' and sees them smiling, so it infers excitement, not anxiety. This explicit semantic information then flows into the diffusion model as text (encoded as numbers), giving it a clear goal for what kind of emotion and intent to produce in the motion. The diffusion model can now focus on the harder problem: generating motion that looks natural and physically plausible while expressing that specific emotion. This works because the diffusion model no longer has to guess what the character is feeling—the semantic text tells it directly.

The paper also introduces a technical trick called pseudo last frame conditioning. During training, the model learns from both the first and last frame of the video sequence. This prevents the static reference image from blocking the learning of motion—when the model sees the ground truth last frame is different from the first, it learns that large motion is allowed and should be learned from the semantic guidance.

The diffusion equation with semantic guidance

First in words:

At each denoising step, the model predicts which noise to remove from the motion. The prediction is guided by a weighted combination of audio, image, and text information. The final loss measures how well the predicted motion matches the ground truth video.

Then the compact form:

L = E[ ||x_0 - x_pred||^2 + lambda_sem * sem_guidance_loss + lambda_audio * audio_consistency_loss ]

Every symbol:

x_0the original clean motion frames
x_predthe model's predicted clean motion after noise removal
lambda_semweight for how strongly semantic guidance influences the result
lambda_audioweight for audio consistency
sem_guidance_losspenalty if the generated motion contradicts the semantic intent
audio_consistency_losspenalty if lip sync is poor
A tiny worked example
Scenario: video clip with character saying 'I am excited' and the LLM inferred 'excited + forward lean'.
At a denoising step, the diffusion model has two choices for the head: stay vertical or tilt forward.
Baseline loss (prediction error): tilt forward is 0.5 units off ground truth; stay vertical is 0.4 units off ground truth.
Semantic guidance adds a penalty: staying vertical contradicts 'excited' intent, so add 0.3 units to the vertical choice.
New effective loss: tilt forward is 0.5, stay vertical is 0.4 + 0.3 = 0.7.
The model chooses tilt forward because it now has lower loss, bringing the motion into alignment with the semantic intent.

The diffusion model is nudged by semantic guidance to prefer motion that matches emotion and intent, not just motion that is close to some baseline prediction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The LLM reason is wrong. If the agentic reasoner misunderstands the audio (for example, mistakes sarcasm for sincerity), the semantic guidance will be wrong and the generated motion will express the wrong emotion. The motion will look natural but mean the opposite of what was intended.
Semantic guidance conflicts with audio. If the semantic text says 'excited' but the audio is quiet and measured, the diffusion model must resolve the conflict. Poor weighting of lambda_sem versus lambda_audio can lead to motion that is jarring or incoherent.
Identity drift in multi-person scenes. With multiple people in one video, audio and semantic guidance can become ambiguous (who is speaking? whose emotion?). The method requires speaker-specific masking to keep audio features tied to the correct person.
Done right. When the LLM correctly infers intent, and the multimodal diffusion model weights semantic guidance appropriately relative to audio and image, the result is motion that is both lip-synced and emotionally coherent.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a video avatar generator that takes an audio clip, a reference image of a person, and text describing the emotion (for example, 'excited', 'confused', 'angry'). Generate a short video clip of the character performing an action in that emotional state. Measure three things: (1) how well lips sync to audio, (2) how much the motion changes when you change the emotion text (if emotion text is ignored, the output will be identical regardless of input), and (3) have humans rate whether the motion feels emotionally consistent.

semantic_consistency = (user_ratings_with_LLM_guidance - user_ratings_without_LLM_guidance) / user_ratings_without_LLM_guidance

The demo succeeds if motion naturalness and emotional consistency improve when semantic guidance is added, even if lip sync stays equally good. This works because semantic text is unambiguous (the word 'excited' clearly means one emotion) while audio is ambiguous (excitement and anxiety sound similar), so the semantic text helps the model learn what motion matches what emotion more clearly than audio alone can.
