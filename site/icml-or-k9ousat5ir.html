Learning Decentralized LLM Collaboration with Multi-Agent Actor Critic

When training multiple language models to work together in parallel, a centralized referee that watches all of them learns faster and works better on hard problems than letting each agent learn from its own limited view.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Suppose you need multiple language models to solve tasks together—one writes code, another reviews it, a third runs tests. The hard part: how do you train them to work well as a team without slowing down by waiting for responses? The paper compares two ways to do this. One way has a single referee watching all the agents and grading their moves (centralized). The other way has each agent grade itself independently (decentralized). The centralized approach learns faster because it sees the full picture and sends clean, reliable feedback, and it handles really hard problems better (especially when rewards are rare) because that clean signal is essential when errors compound over many steps.

Zero assumption version

Why decentralized LLM teams are hard to train

When you have one language model, you train it like this: show it a task, let it try, compare its answer to what's right, then nudge its weights based on the error. But with multiple models working together, it gets messy. You could have them take turns talking to a central coordinator, but then they're waiting around and you lose the speed of parallel processing. Or you could have each agent just learn from its own experience—but each one sees only a tiny slice of what's happening, so their feedback is noisy and unreliable.

The naive way to train teams is called Monte Carlo: roll out full dialogues, see how well the whole team did at the end, then push every agent in the direction that led to that outcome. This works, but it's incredibly wasteful. If one agent made a tiny mistake early on that cascaded, you still have to run the whole dialogue again to learn anything. That noise adds up, and you need thousands of extra runs to make progress.

The real solution. Keep a referee (a critic model) that learns to predict how well each agent will do, then use that prediction to guide training instantly, without rolling out the whole thing.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes where the critic (the referee that predicts value) lives and what it can see. In the centralized version, one critic watches the entire game state and all agents' moves. In the decentralized version, each agent has a separate critic that only sees its own actions and rewards. This single change ripples through learning speed and final performance.

the object it moves  =  the critic's visibility
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: value prediction error

measured quantity  =  how far off the critic's prediction is

A critic is useful only if its predictions are accurate. If it guesses the final score will be 8 out of 10 but actually gets 3, then the agents learn the wrong lesson. The paper measures something you cannot directly see: how much error the critic makes when predicting the value (how good the outcome will be) from the current state. A centralized critic that sees all agents has a richer, less-noisy signal, so its predictions are more reliable. A decentralized critic is blind to other agents' choices, so it guesses from incomplete information and makes bigger mistakes in how it estimates value.

The paper estimates value prediction error by having each critic predict a score, then comparing that prediction to the actual reward received at the end of the dialogue. A smaller gap means the critic learned which states lead to good outcomes; a large gap means it is steering the agents wrong.

Plain reading. The critic is a separate model that watches the game and learns to guess the final score before it happens. If it gets good at guessing, the agents can learn faster because they get instant, reliable feedback instead of waiting.
Paper reading. The critic network outputs a value function V(s), which estimates the expected cumulative reward from state s onward. In actor-critic methods, the temporal difference error—the difference between predicted value and observed return—tells both actor and critic which direction to adjust their weights. A centralized critic reduces the noise in this error signal because it conditions on the full state; a decentralized critic only observes local state features.
Why it works

Why centralized critique works better

Here's the core insight: training a team requires fast, accurate feedback. When agent A makes a move, what should it learn? That depends on what agents B and C are going to do next. If each agent only sees its own moves, its critic can only guess what the others will do—and that guess is usually wrong, especially early in training. A centralized critic that watches all three agents at once has the real picture and sends a clean signal: your move was smart because it set up your teammates for success, or it was dumb because it cornered them.

This clean signal makes a huge difference in hard problems. When rewards are rare (you only get a score at the very end of a 100-step conversation), or when you need dozens of steps to succeed (long-horizon), noise in the value prediction catastrophically misleads the agents. They update their weights based on bad guesses, learn useless patterns, and spiral. A centralized critic cuts through that noise because it has the full context. Even a small improvement in prediction accuracy compounds over thousands of training steps.

The learning rule

First in words:

Loss equals the agent's loss (how far the agent's action is from ideal) plus a term that penalizes the critic for guessing wrong about the value. The value penalty grows with how far the prediction missed reality.

Then the compact form:

L_actor = -advantage * log_prob_action L_critic = (V_predicted - V_actual)^2 advantage = reward + gamma * V_next - V_current

Every symbol:

V_predictedthe critic's guess about how good this state is
V_actualthe real return that actually happened
advantagehow much better this move was than the average move from this state
log_prob_actionhow likely the agent's chosen action was under its current policy
gammahow much we care about future rewards vs immediate rewards
A tiny worked example
Scenario: agent is at a state where critic predicts value 7.0.
Agent picks action A. Real outcome: end with score 8.0.
advantage = 8.0 - 7.0 = 1.0 (action was better than predicted).
Actor update: push this action slightly more likely, scaled by advantage (1.0).
Critic update: penalty = (7.0 - 8.0)^2 = 1.0; adjust critic to predict higher next time.
Now scenario 2: agent at same state, picks action B, real outcome score 6.0.
advantage = 6.0 - 7.0 = -1.0 (worse than predicted).
Actor update: push this action less likely, scaled by -1.0.
Decentralized: if this agent's private critic did not see other agents' state, it would miscalculate advantage and push the wrong direction.

The math shows that smaller critic error (V_predicted closer to V_actual) makes the advantage signal cleaner and agents learn faster, because the training nudge steps now point the right way instead of misleading agents half the time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Decentralized critic trains slower. If you insist on running each agent independently with its own critic, you lose the speed boost because each agent learns from a blindfolded referee that cannot see what other agents are doing, so convergence is slow and sample-inefficient. This fails catastrophically on sparse-reward or long-horizon tasks because the noise in the feedback signal compounds and misdirects the agents.
Centralized critic needs infrastructure. A single critic that sees all agents must receive and process all their state information. If agents are running on different machines or with high latency, the centralized critic becomes a bottleneck. This can actually slow down how fast the agents can generate output if communication is expensive.
Value prediction error can propagate. If the centralized critic makes a systematic error (always overestimates or underestimates), agents learn biased updates. The critic and actors must train together carefully so the critic stays accurate.
Done right: use centralized critic for hard tasks. When rewards are sparse or the task needs many steps, the centralized critic's superior signal is worth the infrastructure cost. When tasks are simple and short, save the overhead and let agents train independently.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy team: two language models (or two smaller neural nets trained to act on tokens) that must collaborate on a task—for example, translating a phrase where one model suggests words and another picks the order. Run three scenarios: (1) train with Monte Carlo (no critic), (2) train with decentralized critics (each model gets its own value estimator), (3) train with one shared centralized critic. Measure how many training samples each needs to reach 80 percent accuracy. Plot the sample efficiency (steps to target performance) for each method across tasks of increasing length and sparsity of rewards.

samples_to_80_pct_accuracy = steps needed for decentralized / steps needed for centralized

The ratio should be >2 on long-horizon or sparse-reward tasks, proving that centralized critique cuts the sample budget in half.
