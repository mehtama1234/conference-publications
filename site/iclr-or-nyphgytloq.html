Fair in Mind, Fair in Action: The IRIS Benchmark

A benchmark that measures fairness across both understanding and generation in multimodal models by aggregating 60 metrics into three unified dimensions.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Multimodal models (models that read both images and text) often treat different groups of people unfairly — some groups get worse answers than others. But fairness itself is a slippery idea: people disagree about what fair means. The IRIS Benchmark solves this by collecting 60 different fairness measurements into three core dimensions, letting you see exactly where and how a model fails to treat everyone equally.

Zero assumption version

Fairness is a tangled knot

Multimodal models — systems that understand both images and text together — are being used everywhere: hiring, medicine, housing, hiring. When a model sees a person's face or name, it should give the same quality answer no matter who they are. But it does not.

The trouble: philosophers and researchers have dozens of different ideas about what fairness means. Some think it means equal accuracy across all groups. Some think it means the model should make the same kinds of mistakes. Some think it means the model should ignore demographic information entirely. These ideas contradict each other — you cannot satisfy all of them at once.

Worse: multimodal models have multiple failure points. A model might be fair when reading an image but unfair when writing text. Or it might treat a group fairly on one task but unfairly on another. There is no unified way to measure and fix these problems.

In one sentence. The IRIS Benchmark collects 60 fairness measurements into three unified dimensions, so you can see exactly where a multimodal model fails to treat different groups equally.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take a multimodal model

Start with any model that reads images and text.

Run it on diverse demographic groups

Test the same tasks across people of different ages, genders, ethnicities, etc.

Collect 60 fairness measurements

Measure accuracy, consistency, and bias using 60 different definitions of fairness.

Map into three dimensions

Project all 60 metrics into three core fairness dimensions: ideal, real-world, steerability.

Diagnose failure modes

Identify specific patterns like generation gaps and personality splits that reveal systemic biases.

What the paper changes

What changes: unified fairness measurement

Instead of picking one fairness metric and hoping it captures what you care about, IRIS measures 60 metrics simultaneously and aggregates them into three unified dimensions. This lets you see fairness trade-offs instead of pretending they do not exist.

the object it moves  =  fairness measurement space
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: fairness in three dimensions

measured quantity  =  IRIS fairness score (high-dimensional)

IRIS does not use a single fairness number. Instead, it measures fairness in three directions at once: Ideal Fairness (how far from perfect), Real-world Fidelity (how fairness works in actual use), and Bias Inertia & Steerability (how hard it is to fix). Each dimension summarizes roughly 20 of the 60 underlying metrics.

The paper computes this by running the model on diverse demographic groups, collecting ground-truth labels (what the correct answer is for each person), and then comparing what the model outputs to the ground truth for each group and dimension. Why it works

Why unified dimensions help

The core insight: most fairness metrics measure the same deep idea but in different ways. If you measure accuracy on group A versus group B, or if you measure whether the model makes the same type of error for both groups, or whether the model gives consistent confidence — you are circling around the same core problem, just from different angles. IRIS combines these 60 angles into three directions that matter most: How far from perfect? How fair in real use? How fixable?

This works because fairness trade-offs are real. You cannot optimize for all 60 metrics at once without contradicting yourself. But you CAN optimize for the three dimensions and tell a coherent story: this model is close to ideal on some groups but fails badly on intersections (people in multiple marginalized groups), and the failure is because of a systematic bias in generation (writing descriptions) rather than understanding (reading images).

How to score fairness in three dimensions

First in words:

Aggregate 60 metrics into 3 dimensions using weighted averaging; then compute disparity (difference) between groups.

Then the compact form:

IRIS_d(g) = mean(M_g ∩ dimension_d) for group g and dimension d; Disparity(g1, g2) = abs(IRIS_d(g1) - IRIS_d(g2))

Every symbol:

IRIS_d(g)fairness score for group g on dimension d
M_gall 60 fairness metrics applied to group g
dimension_done of three fairness directions (ideal, real-world, or fixable)
Disparitydifference in fairness between two groups
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model is tested on two groups: people with light skin and people with dark skin.
20 metrics measure accuracy fairness. For light-skin group: scores are 85, 88, 82, 90... average = 86. For dark-skin group: 72, 68, 71, 69... average = 70.
Disparity in accuracy fairness = abs(86 - 70) = 16 points.
20 other metrics measure generation fairness. Light: average 84. Dark: average 62. Disparity = 22 points.
The generation disparity is bigger, so the model is less fair at writing text than reading images.

The three IRIS dimensions let you see which kinds of fairness failures matter most instead of averaging over all 60 metrics and losing the diagnosis.

Once you identify which groups and tasks have the biggest disparities, you can apply targeted interventions like reweighting training data for under-served groups or modifying generation rules for specific demographics. This works because you concentrate your fixes exactly where they help most, instead of spreading fixes thinly across all groups.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Conflicting fairness definitions. The 60 metrics contradict each other by design. Optimizing for Ideal Fairness might hurt Real-world Fidelity. The IRIS approach acknowledges this trade-off instead of pretending it does not exist.
Intersectionality is hard to measure. Fairness for women is not the same as fairness for women from a specific ethnicity. IRIS detects intersectional gaps (personality splits) but needs many data samples to measure them reliably.
Dataset bias leaks into metrics. If the training data for the model was biased, IRIS will measure that bias faithfully — but it cannot fix it. The benchmark diagnoses; you have to fix the training.
Done right. Use IRIS to identify which dimensions and demographic groups have the largest gaps, then run targeted debiasing on those groups and dimensions rather than trying to improve everything at once, because a blanket fix spreads your limited retraining budget thinly but a focused fix concentrates effort on the worst disparities.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Download a pretrained multimodal model (e.g., CLIP, LLaVA). Run it on a small dataset with images labeled by demographic group (skin tone, gender, age). Compute 10 fairness metrics manually: accuracy per group, confidence per group, and consistency across repeated captions. Visualize these 10 metrics in a 3D scatter plot, with one axis per IRIS dimension. Show how different models (and different training techniques) cluster in different regions of this fairness space.

Disparity = max_fairness_score - min_fairness_score across groups; smaller is more fair.

The demo makes it impossible to miss that fairness is not one number but a trade-off space (because the 60 metrics contradict each other), and that different models fail in systematically different ways (because their training data and architecture choices push them toward different demographic groups and tasks).
