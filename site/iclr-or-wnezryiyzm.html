FORMED: Repurposing a Pre-trained Time Series Model for Medical Data

Share a single classifier across many medical time series datasets during training, so it learns patterns that work on completely new datasets without needing to retrain.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Medical time series (EKG, blood pressure, temperature) look different in every hospital: different machines record different signals, patients have different conditions, and tasks vary (diagnose disease, predict decline, classify rhythms). A model trained on one hospital's data fails on the next. FORMED fixes this by using a shared attention-based classifier trained across many medical datasets at once, which learns to recognize the core patterns that matter, then adapts to new data by training only a tiny label-query layer.

Zero assumption version

Medical time series break when the data changes

A hospital's EKG classifier is trained on their machines, their patient types, and their specific problem (diagnose arrhythmia). When you move to a different hospital with different equipment, different patients, and maybe a different task (predict heart attack risk instead), the model's accuracy drops from 92% to 61%. The problem is that the model memorized details that were specific to the first hospital.

Naive fix: retrain from scratch on the new hospital's data. But this requires hundreds of labeled examples and months of ML work for every new deployment. Alternatively, fine-tune the model on new data, but this causes catastrophic forgetting: the model unlearns its knowledge of the old pattern while absorbing noise from the new dataset.

The core failure: A model trained to classify EKG on one dataset uses its entire learned capacity to pick up dataset-specific details, not general EKG patterns. When you deploy it elsewhere, those details do not apply.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take a pre-trained time series model

Use a model that was already trained to forecast time series on generic data (like weather or stocks). Its backbone (the layers that extract patterns) is frozen and never changes.

Build a shared attention-based classifier

Create a new layer on top that uses learnable queries (one set per class) and attention (a mechanism that picks out which signals are important). Train this layer on a cohort of medical datasets at the same time.

The layer learns what matters across hospitals

By seeing EKG data from Hospital A, B, and C during training, the attention layer learns which patterns predict disease regardless of the equipment or patient demographics.

Freeze the shared layer

Once training is done, lock the classifier in place. Its parameters will not change anymore.

Adapt to a new hospital with 0.1% retraining

For Hospital D (never seen before), only train tiny label queries that bind the general patterns to D's class labels. Everything else stays fixed.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of giving each dataset its own classifier head (which absorbs noise), FORMED uses one shared classifier head trained across all datasets in the training cohort. This single head contains all the learnable parameters (except for the tiny per-dataset label queries). The backbone of the pre-trained model is always frozen.

the object it moves  =  The classification layer: shared across datasets instead of task-specific
The hidden quantity

Medical domain knowledge in the attention patterns

measured quantity  =  Task-agnostic feature-query alignment

This is the quantity that tells you the method is working: how well do the learned queries (one per class) align with the actual features that distinguish one medical condition from another, independent of the specific hospital, equipment, or patient population? If the queries are generic (learned on A, B, C), they should light up the right regions of new data D.

The paper measures this by seeing if the queries trained on the cohort transfer to unseen datasets. If a query trained on Hospital A's heart-failure EKGs fires on Hospital D's heart-failure EKGs without any retraining, that means the query learned something genuine about heart failure, not noise.

Why it works

Why sharing a classifier makes it transfer

When each dataset gets its own classifier (the traditional approach), that classifier can tune itself to fit every quirk of that dataset: the specific shape of the EKG machine's noise, the age of patients, which conditions are common. It overfits to the details. When you move to a new hospital, those details do not apply, so the classifier fails.

When a single classifier is trained on many datasets at once, it cannot overfit to any one dataset's quirks (because the next dataset contradicts them). Instead, it must learn the shared pattern: the core shape of a normal rhythm, the diagnostic markers of atrial fibrillation, the waveform signatures that work no matter the equipment. This is called the bottleneck: the classifier has to compress its knowledge down to what works everywhere. A new dataset can then use those patterns with minimal retuning.

The paper freezes the shared classifier and only trains tiny label queries per new dataset. A label query is just a small list of numbers that says which of the shared patterns matter for this dataset's specific class labels. This is why only 0.1% of parameters need to be retrained: the 99.9% of hard work (learning the patterns that work across hospitals) is already done.

Math after the picture

The shared attention classifier equation

First in words:

Final logit for class j is the average of k learned queries, each scoring how well the flattened features match that query through multi-head attention and a feed-forward network.

Then the compact form:

y_hat_j = (1/k) * sum_{i=1}^{k} FFN(MultiHeadAttn(Q_i, H_flat, H_flat))

Every symbol:

Q_ithe i-th learnable query (a list of numbers) for this class (shared across datasets)
H_flatall extracted features from the backbone, stacked into one long list of numbers
MultiHeadAttnattention mechanism (asks: which features are important for this query)
FFNa small feed-forward network that converts attention output to a prediction score
knumber of queries per class (hyperparameter, e.g. k=5 means five different perspectives per class)
y_hat_jfinal prediction score for class j (before converting to a probability)
A tiny worked example
Suppose Hospital A sends an EKG with 2 channels, and Hospital B sends the same EKG with 3 channels.
Backbone outputs features [f1, f2, f3, f4, f5] and [f1, f2, f3, f4, f5, f6, f7] respectively (different lengths).
Query 1 for class 'atrial fibrillation' attends over these features: attention weights = [0.1, 0.7, 0.15, 0.04, 0.01, 0.0, 0.0] for B = [0.1, 0.7, 0.15, 0.04, 0.01].
Attended features (weighted sum) pick out the same diagnostic markers (f2, f3 have high weights).
FFN converts this to a prediction score 3.2 in both cases.
Averaging k queries (say k=3 queries all fire similarly) gives final prediction score 3.1 for A and 3.0 for B.
Both correctly predict atrial fibrillation despite different input shapes, because the shared query learned to look for the same diagnostic pattern.

The shared queries learn to pick out the same diagnostic features across hospitals with different channel counts and signal shapes, because training updates them to fit all datasets at once, which forces them to ignore hospital-specific noise.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Cohort does not represent diversity. If hospitals A, B, C in the training cohort are all similar (same equipment, same patient population), the shared queries will overfit to that narrow range. Deploying to a truly different hospital (different equipment vendor, pediatric patients instead of adults) will fail. The training cohort must span the real-world variation you expect to see.
Backbone is mismatched for the task. FORMED reuses a pre-trained backbone designed for time series forecasting (predicting the next value). Medical classification cares about different features (diagnostic markers, not just temporal trends). If the backbone does not extract medically relevant features, the shared queries cannot fix it.
New dataset is radically different. If Hospital D's data is so different (e.g. pediatric patients instead of adults, or a completely different disease, or equipment that produces inverted signals) that the training cohort has no examples of it, the shared queries will not align, and 0.1% label-query tuning is not enough. Some domain-specific adaptation is still needed.
Done right. Choose a training cohort that spans realistic variation (different equipment, patient demographics, disease mixes). Validate that the backbone is extracting features relevant to medical conditions, not just fitting temporal patterns. When deploying to new data, verify that label-query tuning is enough; if not, you need a richer cohort or a different backbone.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Collect medical time series from three hospitals (or simulate varying channel counts and signal lengths). Train the shared attention classifier on data from hospitals A and B together. Freeze the classifier. On hospital C (held out, never seen during training), retrain only the 0.1% label queries and measure accuracy. Compare against a model that was trained only on hospital A (which should fail on C). FORMED should beat it, proving that the shared training across A and B taught something that generalizes to C.

Improvement = accuracy_FORMED_on_C_unseen - accuracy_A_only_on_C_unseen

A single shared classifier trained on diverse medical datasets transfers to new hospitals without full retraining, because it learned the patterns that matter across hospitals, not the noise specific to one place.
