Scaling Entropy Estimation for Unnormalized Densities

Fix the hidden bugs in a scaling method so it can reliably measure disorder in weirdly-shaped probability distributions.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

When you have a probability distribution that you know up to a scaling constant (the true probability but without the scaling multiplier), measuring its disorder (how unpredictable or spread-out the outcomes are) is hard. The paper fixes a recent method called P-SVGD that tried but failed at high dimensions due to broken assumptions and missing calculations. The fix, MET-SVGD, corrects the calculation, checks invertibility, and uses a principled formula to tune the settings, and this keeps the estimate accurate even at high dimensions.

The challenge: disorder in incomplete probability descriptions

Probability distributions appear in many places: image generation, robotics decisions, climate forecasts. A complete description would give you the probability of every outcome. But often you have only a partial recipe: the relative odds (which outcome is twice as likely as another), not the absolute scaling constant that makes all probabilities add to one.

A beginner might think: measure how spread-out the outcomes are, ignoring the scaling constant. But this breaks. The scaling constant is not just a number; it changes what spread-out actually means. The paper's predecessors tried approximating methods, but they made three silent mistakes: they assumed the approximation could always be inverted (swapped in both directions), they left out a correction term in their calculation of curvature, and their adjustment mechanisms drifted unstable in high-dimensional spaces.

In one sentence. The paper corrects three bugs in the scaling method to estimate disorder reliably.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes three things in the calculation and tuning of the Stein Variational Gradient Descent method. First, it corrects the invertibility check (a swap-ability guarantee that the old method assumed but did not verify). Second, it adds the forgotten trace-of-Hessian term (a curvature correction that the old math skipped). Third, it replaces unstable tuning heuristics with a principled formula for setting the method's strength.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: entropy under incompleteness

measured quantity  =  Differential entropy of an unnormalized density

Entropy is a number that measures disorder: how spread-out or unpredictable an outcome is. For a complete probability distribution, calculating entropy is standard. But here, you do not have the global scaling constant. That missing piece makes entropy calculation ambiguous unless you pick a reference frame. The paper measures differential entropy, which asks: how much more disordered is this distribution compared to a base case (say, a Gaussian)? This removes the ambiguity.

The paper estimates this by running particles (sample points) through an iterative algorithm that pushes them to match the distribution. Once they settle, the spread of the final particle positions lets you read off the entropy. The old method tried this but made calculation mistakes.

Why it works

Why it works: fixing three silent failures

The old method, P-SVGD, tried to move particles (sample points) so they track the true distribution. To do this, it needed to reverse a mathematical operation (invert it). The code assumed the operation was invertible, but in high dimensions this assumption breaks silently—the operation can fail to invert even though the code does not catch it. This causes particles to move in wrong directions.

Second, the method updates particles using a gradient (a direction to move). But gradients alone miss curvature—how the surface bends. The old code forgot to add the trace-of-Hessian term, a correction that measures this bending. Without it, the particle motion is biased.

Third, the algorithm has a tuning knob (how hard to steer particles). The old code used ad-hoc heuristics to set this knob. In high dimensions, these heuristics let the setting drift unstable, causing particles to overshoot or undershoot.

MET-SVGD fixes all three. It checks invertibility explicitly. It includes the curvature correction. It uses a principled formula to set the knob so particles converge reliably. These fixes stack, and together they let the method scale to high dimensions where the old method crashed.

The update rule with corrections

First in words:

Updated particle position = old position + step size times (gradient from density plus curvature correction), scaled by a stable tuning parameter.

Then the compact form:

x_i^new = x_i + (gamma / n) * sum_j [ grad log q(x_j) + (1/2) trace(H log q(x_j)) ] + O(1) stable tuning

Every symbol:

gammatuning parameter for step strength
nnumber of particles

trace(H log q(x))correction term for how curved the space is
O(1) stable tuningprincipled formula that keeps gamma from drifting
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 4 particles at positions 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0.
Density gradient at position 2.0 is 0.3 (move in positive direction).
Curvature correction at position 2.0 is 0.05 (additional nudge).
Tuning parameter gamma is set to 0.2 (stable, proven formula).
New position = 2.0 + (0.2/4) * (0.3 + 0.05) = 2.0 + 0.05 * 0.35 = 2.0 + 0.0175 = 2.0175.
Without curvature correction: 2.0 + 0.0150 = 2.0150 (biased lower).
Without invertibility check: particle might have moved 2.0 + 0.1 = 2.1 (unstable).
The three fixes together keep the particle motion honest and bounded.

Each particle moves a small amount toward the region of higher probability, boosted by curvature awareness and tuned for stability.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Non-invertible operation. If the swap operation fails, particles move in wrong directions. The fix checks explicitly, not just assuming.
Missing curvature biases the estimate. Without the Hessian trace correction, particles move as if on a flat surface when the surface actually bends. The fix adds this term.
Tuning knob drifts unstable. Ad-hoc heuristics for step size let particles overshoot or undershoot in high dimensions. The fix uses a principled formula.
Done right. With all three fixes, particles converge reliably and entropy estimates stay accurate even in 50 or 100 dimensions.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 2D example: a non-standard probability distribution (say, the product of two strongly skewed normal curves). Run both the old P-SVGD method and MET-SVGD on it. Plot the particle positions as they iterate. Plot the entropy estimate as a function of iteration count. The demo should show that particles with MET-SVGD converge to the true shape and produce a steady entropy estimate, while the old method's particles diverge or overshoot.
