Limiting privacy risks while learning

Building learning algorithms that extract insights from data while mathematically guaranteeing that sensitive information about individuals remains protected.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A hospital wants to train a model to diagnose disease using patient records. But patient data is sensitive: names, addresses, medical histories, genetic information could be misused if leaked or stolen. The hospital faces a dilemma: the more data they use, the better the model, but more data means more privacy risk and more potential harm if the model is compromised. Additionally, even if the data is properly secured, a clever adversary might extract sensitive information about specific patients from the model's predictions — just by seeing how the model responds. A technique called membership testing can determine whether a specific patient's data was in the training set by observing how the model behaves, which reveals private facts without accessing the database. This subtheme tackles: how can you learn useful models while mathematically guaranteeing that sensitive information remains private?

The challenge is that perfect learning and perfect privacy seem to conflict. A model trained to fit data well might memorize specific individuals' sensitive attributes. In distributed settings where data stays on individual devices and only model updates are shared, an adversary might infer private information from the updates. Additionally, privacy has multiple faces: differential privacy protects individual records, but other concerns include inference privacy (preventing attackers from deducing properties of the training set), and fairness privacy (preventing models from using sensitive attributes like race). Standard privacy techniques like data anonymization are brittle — they fail when multiple datasets are combined or linked.

Researchers develop differential privacy, a mathematical framework that adds controlled noise to computations or data, ensuring that an adversary cannot confidently infer whether any single individual's data was in the training set, even with infinite computing power. They analyze privacy-utility tradeoffs: how much accuracy you lose to gain privacy protection, so practitioners can choose the right balance for their setting. They study federated learning, where models are trained on data that never leaves individual devices, combining local models with privacy guarantees, which keeps sensitive data on each person's phone or device. They investigate privacy-preserving inference: once a model is trained, how should you deploy it to prevent extraction of sensitive training data from model queries? They also examine when privacy protection must come early (adding noise during training) versus late (limiting what queries to the model are allowed), because timing affects how much noise is needed. The work aims to enable beneficial learning from sensitive data without exposing individuals, which protects vulnerable populations from harm and from being re-identified or targeted — a risk that exists even with strong data security because the model itself can leak — while allowing the data to be useful for improving care or diagnosis so society benefits from the data without sacrificing privacy.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five directions: detecting what models accidentally memorize and why memorization harms privacy; protecting data that arrives in a stream; making specific learning algorithms (like game-playing algorithms or regression) private; understanding how attackers can extract private information even when data is hidden; and creating synthetic data and measuring privacy guarantees precisely.

Detecting what models memorize

When a model learns from sensitive data, one privacy risk is that it memorizes specific training examples. Later, an attacker can test whether a particular person's data was used to train the model just by watching how the model responds to queries about them. This means understanding memorization is the first step to defending against it. The problem is computationally expensive: measuring true memorization requires expensive tests, and researchers need fast ways to spot it.

The Memorization Through Sample Gradients paper shows a clever shortcut: instead of the expensive exact tests, use the gradient (the direction of improvement) of the model on each training example as a proxy. This achieves a speedup of five orders of magnitude compared to exact memorization scores. The method also lets you stop training early when the weight norm (a measure of the model's magnitude) peaks, without needing a separate validation set. The Understanding Private Learning From Feature Perspective paper tackles a different angle: it measures learning through the statistical signal-to-noise ratio. The paper shows that learning private signals requires higher signal quality than learning public signals because the privacy noise makes it harder for the model to distinguish true patterns from noise. Critically, the paper proves that memorizing noise in non-private learning also transfers to private learning, which means the privacy mechanism doesn't eliminate that memorization problem but relocates it. The Dataset Distillation for Memorized Data paper exposes a practical attack: when a teacher model is distilled into a student (a smaller, faster model trained to copy the teacher's predictions), soft labels (confidence scores) can leak information about training data the teacher was supposed to forget, because the student must approximate the teacher's confidence scores exactly, which requires copying even the sensitive patterns the teacher memorized. This shows that even knowledge distillation, a technique meant to make models smaller, creates a backdoor that sensitive information can escape through. Together, these three papers form a coherent story: memorization is a concrete, measurable phenomenon; it persists even when privacy protections are added; and it can leak through surprising channels like model distillation.

Private algorithms for streaming and continuous learning

In many real-world settings, data doesn't arrive all at once. Hospitals receive patient records over time. Websites log user behavior continuously. A bank processes transactions stream. In these streaming scenarios, you cannot wait to see all data before learning because decisions must be made now. Yet adding privacy protection to streaming is harder than batch (all-at-once) settings because you must commit to privacy decisions before seeing future data, and you cannot revise them later. The challenge is controlling error while respecting privacy budgets that shrink as you make more public statements.

The Differentially Private Continual Release with Relative Error paper studies this directly: when you must continuously release statistics about a stream. The paper shows that if the stream is non-adaptive (an adversary doesn't change what data arrives based on your releases), you can reduce error by using relative error (error scaled by the true answer) instead of absolute error. However, if an adversary adaptively sends data that depends on your previous answers, the error becomes unavoidably large for selection tasks (choosing the top-K items). The Adaptively Resettable Streaming paper extends this to other statistics like power-p moments (measures of how spread out the data is) and Bernstein statistics. Despite the privacy constraints, this paper achieves space complexity that grows logarithmically, meaning it uses very little memory. The Keeping a Secret Requires a Good Memory paper proves a fundamental lower bound: any algorithm that privately counts distinct elements (a basic streaming task) requires space that grows with the cube root of the time T. This result shows that privacy and space cannot both be cheap simultaneously—privacy simply requires more memory than non-private algorithms. These three papers together establish a principle: privacy in streaming is achievable but comes at measurable costs, and the costs depend on whether the adversary adapts to your releases.

Making specific learning algorithms private

Not all learning algorithms respond the same way to privacy protection. Some algorithms might need massive noise injection to guarantee privacy, while others can remain accurate with modest noise. This group studies privacy in three specific algorithmic settings with different challenges: game theory, causal inference (understanding which variables cause changes), and neural networks with special structure.

The Differentially Private Equilibrium Finding in Polymatrix Games paper tackles a game-theoretic setting where multiple players each want to optimize their own goals. The paper presents the first algorithm that achieves both vanishing Nash gap (players are nearly optimizing their strategy given others' strategies) and privacy simultaneously, while also maintaining both guarantees as the number of players grows. Differentially Private Two-Stage Gradient Descent for Instrumental Variable Regression handles causal inference under privacy: when you want to learn cause-and-effect relationships from data but the data is sensitive. This is the first work to provide both privacy guarantees and proven convergence rates for this specific task in linear models, with exact bounds on how much accuracy you trade for privacy. The Optimization, Generalization and Differential Privacy Bounds for Gradient Descent on Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks studies a newer neural network architecture (KANs) that has special structure. The paper shows that polylogarithmic width (making the network only logarithmically wider than necessary) is enough for fast optimization and good generalization, and reveals a qualitative gap between private and non-private training—meaning the network must be structured differently when privacy is required. These three examples demonstrate a pattern: the same privacy techniques work across different algorithms, but the specific costs (accuracy loss, network width, convergence speed) differ sharply depending on the problem structure.

Understanding privacy leakage and attacks

Even when data is encrypted and access is controlled, a trained model itself can leak private information. An adversary doesn't need to steal the database—they just need to query the model and observe its outputs. This group studies three types of leakage: through interpretability methods, through model specifications, and through unlearning.

The Barriers to Counterfactual Credit Attribution for Autoregressive Models examines a specific interpretability technique (counterfactual credit attribution, or CCA) that explains which inputs caused which outputs. The paper proves that CCA faces unavoidable theoretical barriers when applied to autoregressive models (like language models that generate text token by token), unlike differential privacy which has no such barriers. This means CCA might leak private information in ways that cannot be fixed by the algorithm design. The Statistical Framework for Analyzing Specification Resistance to Learnware-Inversion Risks studies learnware: machine-learning models packaged as reusable components. The paper analyzes whether an attacker can invert the specification (recover training data properties) from the model specification itself. It proves that properly-sized specifications introduce negligible privacy risk while retaining enough information for the model to function correctly. The Unlearning Evaluation through Subset Statistical Independence paper addresses a different scenario: after training, you might want to delete someone's data from the model (machine unlearning). The paper provides an evaluation method to check whether unlearning actually worked or whether the model still retains information about that person. These three papers address a unified concern: privacy leakage doesn't happen only during training, but also through interpretation, model packaging, and what remains after deletion. Defenses must address all three.

Creating private synthetic data and measuring privacy precisely

One practical way to allow data sharing without privacy risk is synthetic data: artificial data generated from real data that has the same statistical patterns but does not contain real individuals' sensitive information, which means you can share it with third parties without risking re-identification. Yet creating synthetic data and measuring privacy guarantees are both hard. You must ensure the synthetic data is useful (resembles real patterns) and private (doesn't reveal real individuals). Additionally, privacy can be measured in different ways, and connecting different measurements is non-trivial.

The Optimal Domain-Aware Privacy Mechanisms for Synthetic Data Generation paper (PubMix) tackles the utility problem: standard privacy mechanisms are domain-agnostic, treating all data the same way. PubMix incorporates knowledge about the specific domain (healthcare, finance, etc.) to improve synthetic data quality compared to domain-agnostic methods, because each domain has different patterns that respond differently to privacy noise. This means that privacy and utility are not inherently in conflict—if you design the privacy mechanism with the domain in mind, you can protect privacy while keeping synthetic data useful. The Optimal conversion from Rényi Differential Privacy to f-Differential Privacy tackles measurement: there are multiple mathematical definitions of privacy (Rényi, f-differential, and others). The paper establishes that the intersection rule gives optimal bounds for converting between them, marking the fundamental limit of what can be inferred from one definition when converting to another. This matters because different applications use different privacy definitions, and practitioners need to know when converting definitions is reliable and when it loses information. The Privacy-Aware Data Integration for Enhanced Quantile Inference under Heterogeneity paper addresses another practical scenario: combining data from multiple sources (hospitals, regions, demographic groups) while maintaining privacy and handling differences between sources. The paper establishes theoretical guarantees that inference (statistical reasoning) remains valid and effective even when integrating heterogeneous sources under privacy constraints. Together, these papers show that synthetic data creation is achievable with domain knowledge, privacy definitions can be translated consistently, and heterogeneous data can be integrated safely—enabling real organizations to collaborate and share insights without exposing individuals.
