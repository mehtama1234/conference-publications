ParaS2S: Speech-to-Speech with Emotion and Tone

Train a speech model to match not just what a person says, but how they sound by using AI feedback to score emotion and tone.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When you speak with emotion or sarcasm, a good listener responds not just with correct words, but with matching tone. Speech-to-speech models (which convert spoken input directly to spoken output, like a translator robot that hears you and speaks back) have learned to generate fluent responses, but they ignore the emotion, tone, and speaker style in the original voice. This paper shows how to teach them to notice and mirror emotional style using an automatic scoring system and reward-based learning.

The Tone-Deaf Speech Model

Today's speech-to-speech models hear a question and respond with the right factual answer. If you ask in a calm, confident voice, they answer confidently. But if you ask sarcastically or with frustration, they still give the same robotic answer, missing the emotional intent entirely.

A beginner might try simply recording sad/happy/angry versions of each response during training. The problem: making labeled emotional speech data is expensive. You need actors reading thousands of scripted lines in different styles. Most models use only text or limited speech data, so they never learn the mapping between input emotion and output emotion.

In one sentence. Train a speech model to learn which responses match which emotional tones by automatically judging both the content accuracy and the emotional fit of each candidate response.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build a dataset of emotional queries

Generate test questions in text, specify two contrasting emotional styles (e.g., angry vs. calm), then convert to speech using text-to-speech.

Train PolyTone judge

Fine-tune audio analysis models to detect emotion, sarcasm, age, and gender by learning from speech with identical words spoken in different emotional tones.

Judge distillation for speed

Compress the multi-stage judge into a single fast reward model that can score responses during training without slowing it down.

Generate training responses

For each emotional query, sample diverse response speech from the model and score each using the judge.

RL training with GRPO

Use reward-based learning to nudge model weights so that high-scored responses become more likely and low-scored responses become less likely.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the model's output speech waveforms themselves. Instead of training the model only to match text responses (which ignores emotion), it directly optimizes the acoustic patterns (the actual sound waves) to balance both what is said and how it sounds.

the object it moves  =  Response waveforms
The hidden quantity

Paralinguistic alignment: How well the tone matches the intent

measured quantity  =  Paralinguistic alignment score

Paralinguistic alignment is a measure of how well the emotional tone and speaker attributes in a response fit the emotional tone of the input question. If the input is sarcastic and the response is also sarcastic, alignment is high. If the input is angry and the model gives a cheerful response, alignment is low.

The paper estimates this using PolyTone: separate neural networks trained to extract emotional labels from speech while ignoring the actual words. By training them on utterances where the words stay the same but the emotion changes, the analyzers learn to detect genuine tone, not infer emotion from word choice. The judge then scores how well the response's detected emotion matches the input's detected intent.

Plain reading. PolyTone trains acoustic detectors on speech where the words are the same but emotion varies. This forces the detectors to listen to voice features (pitch, tempo, intensity) instead of guessing emotion from the text itself.
Paper reading. The method uses grounded captioners — acoustic analysis models fine-tuned on emotion and sarcasm datasets (IEMOCAP, MELD) that are trained with content-invariant, style-augmented data (same script, different emotional delivery).
Why it works

Why it works: The feedback loop

The core insight is that you cannot teach a model to handle emotion if you only show it labeled examples of correct responses. Instead, you build an automatic judge that rates any response on both factual correctness and emotional fit, then use that judge to guide learning through trial and error.

Here is the causal chain: The model generates candidate responses to an emotional query. The PolyTone judge analyzes each response, extracting its detected emotion and checking it against the input's detected emotion and the factual content. Responses that are both factually correct and emotionally aligned get high scores. The RL algorithm (GRPO) sees these scores and adjusts the model's weights to make high-scoring responses more probable and low-scoring responses less probable. Over time, the model learns to produce responses that balance both what to say and how to say it. This works without needing thousands of hand-labeled emotional recordings, only unlabeled speech and the automatic judge.

The reward-based learning equation

First in words:

For each pair of sampled responses, the reward difference is scored, and the model learns by pushing up the probability of better responses and down the probability of worse responses, weighted by how strong the reward gap is.

Then the compact form:

loss_GRPO = -log(P(response_chosen)) * max(0, r_chosen - r_rejected - margin)

Every symbol:

P(response_chosen)the probability the model assigns to the chosen (better-scoring) response
r_chosenthe reward score from the judge for the better response
r_rejectedthe reward score from the judge for the worse response
margina minimum gap to avoid learning from close calls (typically 0.5 or 1.0)
A tiny worked example
Response A (emotionally matched): judge score = 8.0, model probability = 0.3
Response B (emotionally mismatched): judge score = 3.0, model probability = 0.2
Reward gap: 8.0 - 3.0 = 5.0 (strong signal), margin = 1.0, so signal is 5.0 - 1.0 = 4.0
Loss = -log(0.3) * 4.0 = 1.20 * 4.0 = 4.8 (a strong push to increase P(A))
If responses were close in score (gap = 1.2): 1.2 - 1.0 = 0.2 (tiny push, ignore noise)

The loss grows larger when the judge gives a clear gap between responses and the model's probability is low; training drives up the chosen response's probability.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Judge overfits to synthetic speech. If the judge is trained on artificially generated speech with synthetic emotions, it may not generalize to real human speech with subtle, messy emotions. The paper addresses this by training on real emotion datasets (IEMOCAP, MELD) and using the PolyTone method to detect acoustic cues, not text-inferred emotion.
RL pushes toward reward instead of naturalness. RL optimizes the reward signal directly. If the judge scores exaggerated emotion higher than natural emotion, the model will produce over-the-top responses. This is why the paper uses a multi-stage judge grounded in real emotional speech and validates with human listening tests.
Cold start: no unlabeled speech for learning. RL requires diverse candidate responses to learn from. If the model is poorly initialized, it generates gibberish responses that the judge cannot usefully score. The paper trains a warm-up model with supervised learning first, then applies RL refinement.
Done right. The paper combines automatic scoring (PolyTone judge), real emotional speech data (to ground the judge), and structured RL (GRPO to avoid reward hacking) to learn emotional tone without massive labeled datasets.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small speech-to-speech model trained on a handful of scripts. Create five test queries: angry, sarcastic, calm, sad, and confident. Generate responses using the model trained only with supervised learning, then retrain it with RL using a basic automatic judge. Have a listener (human rater or a pretrained speech emotion classifier) judge whether the output tone matches the input tone.

tone_alignment = (number of queries where output emotion matches input emotion) / (total number of queries)

The demo must show that the RL-trained model produces emotionally matched responses while the supervised-only baseline produces flat-toned responses to all emotions.
