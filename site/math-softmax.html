Softmax

Convert raw scores into a set of probabilities that sum to one.

In plain terms

Imagine you are at a bakery and the manager has calculated a raw satisfaction score for each type of pastry—based on ingredient quality, customer demand, and profit margin. One pastry scores 2, another scores 5, and another scores 3. But these raw numbers do not tell you anything concrete. The manager wants to know: what fraction of my customers should I expect to want each pastry? What if the first pastry is actually the most wanted? The raw scores need to be transformed into percentages, or probabilities, that add up to 100 percent. That transformation is what this operation does.

In machine learning, models produce raw scores constantly. A classifier looks at an image and outputs a raw score for each possible label—dog (score: 2.1), cat (score: 4.8), bird (score: 1.2). But a probability is more useful than a raw score because it tells you confidence: cat has a 70 percent chance, dog has a 20 percent chance, bird has a 10 percent chance. A language model predicts the next word by outputting a score for thousands of possible tokens (small units of text); you need to know the probability of each so you can make a sensible guess or sample fairly. A reinforcement learning agent picks actions by scoring each one; you need probabilities to decide whether to explore a low-scoring action or exploit a high-scoring one. Anywhere a model outputs multiple scores, this operation converts them into a probability distribution—a set of numbers between zero and one that sum to one.

The operation is called softmax because it is a soft version of the selection operation (picking the highest instead of hard-picking just one winner). Rather than assigning 100 percent to a single option, softmax gives higher percentages to higher-scoring options but lets every option receive some probability. This softness is intentional because it allows for uncertainty and exploration. The formula works in two steps: first, it takes the exponential of each raw score, then it divides each exponential by the sum of all exponentials. The exponential step amplifies the difference between high scores and low scores—a big raw score becomes much bigger when you exponentiate it, while a small score stays relatively small. Since the exponential of any number is always positive, no probability goes negative. Then dividing by the sum forces all probabilities to add to one.

The equation
p_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}
p_iThe probability of option i. A number between zero and one that tells you the confidence or likelihood of that option.
z_iThe raw score for option i. Can be any number—positive, negative, or zero—and comes directly from the model.
eEuler's number, approximately 2.718. The exponential function e^{...} grows rapidly and is always positive.
e^{z_i}The exponential of the raw score. This step amplifies large scores and keeps all values positive.
\sum_j e^{z_j}The sum of all exponentials. The denominator that makes all probabilities add up to one.
Worked example
Converting three pastry scores to probabilities
raw scores: z_1=2, z_2=5, z_3=3
exponentials: e^2 ≈ 7.4, e^5 ≈ 148.4, e^3 ≈ 20.1
sum: 7.4 + 148.4 + 20.1 = 176.0 (approximately)
p_1 ≈ 7.4/176 ≈ 0.042 (4.2%); p_2 ≈ 148.4/176 ≈ 0.843 (84.3%); p_3 ≈ 20.1/176 ≈ 0.114 (11.4%)
Why it shows up

This operation appears in 54 papers because it solves a fundamental problem: models produce raw numbers, but the world requires probabilities. Every classification task needs it—to convert logit scores (raw outputs from a neural network) into class probabilities. Every attention layer needs it—to decide how much weight each previous token gets in influencing the current token. Every reinforcement learning policy needs it—to assign probabilities to each possible action so the agent can explore and learn. Every generative model needs it—to create a probability distribution over the next word, the next image patch, or the next action step. Softmax is the bridge between raw model computation and the probability distributions that make sense in the real world.

The reason this primitive is so universal is that it solves a hard constraint: you need numbers between zero and one that sum to one. No other simple operation does this automatically. The exponential step is essential because it ensures all scores are positive (negative scores are not valid probabilities) and makes differences between scores more extreme, so confident predictions stand out sharply while low-scoring options still get some probability mass. This means softmax is interpretable—the output truly represents the model's relative confidence. Unlike raw scores, where you cannot tell whether a score of 2 is weak or strong without context, a softmax probability of 0.843 clearly means the model strongly prefers that option. This interpretability is why softmax appears across computer vision, language models, reinforcement learning, graph neural networks, time series prediction, and robotics—anywhere uncertainty must be quantified and decisions made.
