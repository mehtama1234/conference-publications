NeMo-map: Continuous Motion Maps via Neural Functions

Instead of storing motion patterns in discrete grid squares, the paper builds a neural function that outputs motion predictions for any location and time continuously.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots working near humans need to predict where people will move next. Today's systems chop up space into grid squares and store one motion pattern per square, but this wastes memory in empty regions and breaks smooth prediction at square boundaries because predictions at adjacent squares can differ sharply. This paper replaces the grid with a neural function that directly answers 'what motions happen at location (x, y, time t)' for any coordinates; the function learns a single smooth mapping across the entire space, so predictions flow naturally from one region to another.

Why today's motion maps fail

Robots need to know how humans move in shared spaces so they can avoid collisions and plan smooth paths. In crowded cafes or hospitals, people move in patterns — some walk toward the door, others toward a table. Today's systems store these patterns by dividing the space into a grid (rows and columns of squares) and attaching one motion model to each square.

This grid approach has two big problems. First, grids waste memory: an empty hallway gets a motion model even though nothing moves there. Second, grids break smoothness: the motion prediction at the edge of one square differs sharply from the prediction right next door, even though the real world changes gradually. A robot querying the grid at (3.1, 4.9) and then (3.2, 4.9) gets wildly different predictions, which confuses trajectory planning.

The core idea. Train a neural function that takes coordinates (x, y, time) as input and outputs a set of likely human motions at that exact spot (their speeds and directions), without any grid discretization.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect tracking data

Record where humans were and what velocity they had at location (x, y) and time t.

Train a neural network

Feed all (x, y, t) coordinates to an MLP that outputs settings for a Gaussian mixture model.

Gaussian mixture model captures branching

At some locations, people move in multiple directions (to cafeteria or exit). The mixture has multiple peaks to represent this.

Query at test time

Given a robot at position (x, y) at time t, pass those numbers to the trained network and get the predicted motions (speeds and directions) instantly.

Use predictions for planning

Robot plans its path knowing the likely human motions, avoiding collisions more safely and smoothly.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper replaces the discrete grid storage of motion patterns with a learned neural mapping from coordinates to motion predictions. Instead of 'grid squares store motion models,' the object is now 'a neural function that outputs the likely speeds and directions humans move.'

the object it moves  =  storage representation
The hidden quantity

Spatio-temporal continuity

measured quantity  =  Smoothness of motion patterns across space and time

The hidden quantity is continuity: how smoothly the motion predictions change as you move one step left or forward, or advance one second in time. A grid-based map has sharp jumps at square boundaries; a neural function produces changes that vary smoothly.

The paper measures this indirectly through a metric called negative log likelihood (NLL). When predicting an observed motion at location (x, y, t), the model computes how likely that motion is under its predicted set of speeds and directions. Lower NLL means the model's predicted set tightly covers the actually observed motion; it is a proxy for how well the continuous representation captures real patterns. The paper shows NeMo-map achieves NLL of 0.775 compared to baseline grid methods around 0.8+: the grid methods' higher score means their predictions are more dispersed and less confident, failing especially in sparse regions where the grid cells have few training samples to learn accurate local patterns.

Why it works

Why continuous functions work better

A neural function learns a smooth mapping from coordinates to motion patterns. During training, the network sees many (x, y, t, observed velocity) tuples and adjusts its weights to minimize prediction error. A grid stores motion models separately in each cell, so the predictions at two nearby cells can differ wildly. The neural function, by contrast, must find one smooth mapping that explains all observations across the entire space. This constraint pushes the network to avoid sharp jumps at boundaries: the training loss penalizes inconsistency.

In sparse regions where few people were tracked, the network interpolates: it sees nearby dense regions and extends their patterns smoothly into the gap, rather than leaving the sparse cell blank or with a poorly-trained local model. At test time, querying the network at any real-valued coordinate (not just grid centers) yields smooth motion predictions, so the robot gets honest predictions that vary gradually with location and time. This smoothness matters for motion planning: a planner that sees jerky, discontinuous predicted flows will create jerky, inefficient plans.

The equation

First in words:

The network Phi takes coordinates and outputs the parameters of a Semi-Wrapped Gaussian Mixture, which is a set of bell curves (Gaussians) centered at different motion directions. Each curve has a mean direction, a spread (variance), and a weight indicating how common that direction is. At test time, given location (x, y) and time t, the likelihood of observing velocity v is the sum of each mode's contribution, weighted and scaled by the mode's importance.

Then the compact form:

p(v | x, y, t) = sum_k w_k(x,y,t) * G_k(v; mu_k(x,y,t), sigma_k(x,y,t))

Every symbol:

p(v | x, y, t)probability that velocity v occurs at location x,y and time t
kindex of each motion mode (direction the human typically moves)
w_kweight (how common mode k is at this location and time)
mu_kmean velocity direction of mode k
sigma_kspread (how varied the speeds within mode k)
G_kGaussian (bell curve) centered at mu_k with spread sigma_k
A tiny worked example
At cafe location (3, 5) at 2pm, the network predicts two modes: 70% toward exit, 30% toward kitchen.
Mode 1 (exit): mean direction (1, 0) m/s (moving right), spread 0.2 m/s. Prob of observing velocity (0.9, 0.1): Gaussian centered at (1, 0) assigns 0.85.
Mode 2 (kitchen): mean direction (-0.5, 0.8) m/s, spread 0.3 m/s. Prob of observing (0.9, 0.1): Gaussian assigns 0.10.
Overall: p((0.9, 0.1)) = 0.7 * 0.85 + 0.3 * 0.10 = 0.595 + 0.03 = 0.625. The network confidently predicted this observed velocity.

The neural network learns to set the weight, mean, and spread for each mixture mode at each location and time so that the mixture is most likely to generate the observed human motions in the training data.

What can go wrong

Overfitting in low-data regimes If tracked data is very sparse (only a few observations), the neural network may memorize noise instead of learning true motion patterns. The grid approach, storing one model per cell, can actually perform better with tiny datasets because each cell only needs to model its local observations.
Failure to represent sharp spatial discontinuities If motion patterns genuinely change abruptly (a wall forces people to go around), the neural function may smooth over this boundary instead of respecting it. The grid approach can capture such jumps by assigning very different motion models to adjacent squares.
Query cost scales with network complexity Querying the neural function requires a forward pass through all layers. If the network is large to capture complex patterns, query cost can rival or exceed grid lookup. The paper mitigates this with a shallow MLP and Fourier feature encoding, but dense real-time queries (e.g., motion planning over thousands of candidate locations) may still be expensive.
Done right: sufficient training data With moderate to large datasets (hours of tracking), the neural function learns smooth reliable patterns, dramatically outperforming grids while staying computationally efficient (19 minutes training, microsecond query time per location).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Collect motion observations from a crowded space (a public dataset like ETH or UCY pedestrian tracks works). Split into training (one day) and test (one hour held out). Train a grid-based baseline (CLiFF-map) and NeMo-map on the training set. On the test set, evaluate both methods on: (1) regions where observations were dense during training, and (2) regions where observations were sparse. Measure negative log likelihood (how confidently each method predicted test velocities). Visualize the learned flow field (arrows showing predicted direction and speed) on a map, watching how smoothly the arrows change direction and speed in NeMo-map versus the sharp jumps you see at grid boundaries.

NLL = -mean(log p(v_test | x_test, y_test, t_test)) across all test samples. Lower is better.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: in sparse regions, NeMo-map's predictions form smooth, natural flow patterns, while the grid method shows visible boundaries and gaps. Query time for NeMo-map should be sub-millisecond per location, versus milliseconds or more for grid lookup and local model evaluation.
