Specialized domain understanding

Applying multimodal models to specific fields like medicine, remote sensing, and architecture where they need to understand technical diagrams and specialized data.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A general-purpose multimodal model trained on public internet data can caption a photo of a cat or answer 'what is in this picture.' But it struggles in specialized domains where images carry technical meaning. A doctor looking at a chest X-ray is not just seeing shapes; they are interpreting specialized visual patterns that indicate disease. A satellite imagery analyst is not just looking at colors; they are reading terrain and vegetation patterns to assess crop health or detect construction. An architect is not just seeing a drawing; they are understanding how it specifies a building. These domains have vocabularies, conventions, and rules that a model trained on generic images has not learned. A model that can caption a dog might misidentify anatomical structures in medical imaging or fail to recognize specialized chart types in technical diagrams.

The challenge is that labeled data in specialized domains is expensive to create. Medical imaging requires radiologists to annotate images, which costs time and expertise. Satellite imagery interpretation requires experts with domain knowledge. And even if you have data, the data you have looks very different from internet images. Medical images are often grayscale or in specific frequency ranges. Satellite images have resolutions and color channels that are not standard photography. Technical diagrams have precise geometric meanings. A model that learned to recognize clouds in natural images might mistake a technical artifact in a medical scan for a real pathology. Transfer learning helps — a general model trained on billions of images has learned about shapes and patterns — but it is not enough because the domain has different values about what matters and what should be ignored, so the model needs explicit help to unlearn misleading patterns and focus on what the specialized domain actually cares about. You need domain-specific tuning.

Research in domain-specific multimodal understanding focuses on building models that work in specialized contexts. Some work combines multimodal models with domain knowledge: the vision part learns to interpret images, but the model is also given structured information (like medical ontologies — organized lists of diseases and their relationships) that helps it understand the bigger picture. This hybrid approach works because it anchors the model's predictions to real-world constraints and relationships, so the model can't drift into nonsensical outputs. Some approaches use task-specific training: rather than training on generic image-text pairs, you train on domain-relevant examples, like pairs of medical images and radiologist reports. Some work focuses on helping models understand specialized notation: if the domain uses specific symbols or chart types, the model can learn to recognize them. Others use weak supervision, training on large amounts of noisy domain data (like medical textbooks paired with automatically extracted images) and then refining with smaller amounts of high-quality expert-labeled data. The goal is to take the power of foundation models trained on billions of images and adapt them so they can be trusted in specialized, high-stakes domains where mistakes matter.

How the work breaks down

Work in specialized domain understanding splits into three natural directions: adapting multimodal models to medical imaging where images show anatomy and disease patterns, applying multimodal models to remote sensing and other technical domains where images encode information about terrain or infrastructure, and combining multimodal models with structured domain knowledge like medical ontologies to anchor predictions in real-world constraints.

Medical imaging with visual reasoning

Medical images carry specialized meaning. A chest X-ray is not just a picture; it is a recording of how sound or radiation passed through anatomy, creating patterns that reveal disease. A sleep study records brain electrical activity over time. A model trained on generic images has not learned to read these patterns. The challenge is that medical data is expensive to label (radiologists must annotate) and looks completely different from internet images (often grayscale, narrow resolution, specific frequency ranges). A model needs explicit help to unlearn patterns from natural photos and focus on what medical experts actually care about, because generic training teaches the model to detect features that matter in everyday photos but are misleading or irrelevant in medical imaging.

MedSIGHT trains directly on 72K instruction pairs pairing medical images with detailed descriptions. This teaches a vision-language model to look at an X-ray, a CT scan, or an ultrasound and describe what it sees in medical terms, not generic visual features. By training on medical data specifically, the model learns that certain grayscale patterns mean specific things in this context. It also performs segmentation (drawing boundaries around organs or lesions), which requires understanding spatial anatomy. OpenTSLM tackles a different modality: time-series medical data like ECG (heart electrical activity) and motion capture. It combines raw sensor readings with text descriptions, so a model learns to read a time series the way a cardiologist does. Boosting Medical Visual Understanding From Multi-Granular Language Learning (MGLL) goes deeper into alignment. When you pair a medical image with radiologist text, you need the model to learn the connection at multiple levels: the image connects to specific diseases, specific anatomical structures, and specific findings within those structures. MGLL learns these connections simultaneously at fine-grained and broad levels, which means the model understands that one image region indicates one finding, which belongs to one disease, which has broader implications—this multi-level learning improves accuracy because each level reinforces the others.

Bridging domain knowledge and vision

Domain knowledge exists in structured form outside of images. Medical ontologies list diseases and how they relate to one another. Clinical guidelines describe decision rules. Disease-Centric Vision-Language Pretraining with Hybrid Visual Encoding for 3D Computed Tomography anchors a vision model to this knowledge by making the model disease-aware. Instead of training on generic image-text pairs, it structures training around diseases. A CT scan is not just visual features; it is evidence about whether specific diseases are present. The model learns to recognize visual patterns that indicate each disease by name, turning the disease ontology into the organizing principle for what the model learns to see.

Why Specialist Models Still Matter shows that combining generalist and specialist models works better than either alone, because each type has different strengths: a generalist multimodal model (trained on billions of images) has broad knowledge but cannot focus on the details that matter in medicine, while a specialist model (trained only on medical data) understands medical details but might overfit or miss broader context. By routing different tasks to different models and combining their outputs, the system achieves higher accuracy on clinical decisions. This demonstrates that bridging knowledge is not just about feeding domain structure into one model; it is about designing a system where specialized and general understanding work together and fill each other's gaps.

Achieves 84.4% AUC on CT-RATE (+5.1%), 75.4% on Rad-ChestCT (+5.4%), and +9.8% on 60-disease benchmark with strong transfer to report generation.

Scientific knowledge extraction

Scientists communicate through papers, which contain diagrams, equations, and figures that encode domain knowledge visually. A researcher reading a paper needs to understand both the text and the images: how the diagram illustrates the method, what the figure shows about the results. A model that understands scientific writing needs to read across modalities (text and images together) and extract the actual technical content.

Paper2Code (Automating Code Generation from Scientific Papers in Machine Learning) treats a paper as a complete artifact: text, figures, and equations all encode the algorithm. Given a paper, it extracts the method description and generates Python code that implements it. This requires understanding how the paper's visualizations relate to the code; the diagram of a neural network architecture, for instance, must translate to specific layers and operations. The model learns to read scientific conventions (like how architecture diagrams are drawn) and convert them into executable code. AutoFigure solves the inverse problem: given a text description of a result or method, generate a publication-ready scientific figure. This requires understanding what kind of figure communicates the idea (a line plot for trends, a diagram for structure, a heatmap for relationships) and what visual conventions scientists expect. Both papers treat scientific images not as decorative but as core technical content that must be understood and generated with precision.

Remote sensing and spatial analysis

Satellite imagery and microscopy images encode information about physical systems at a distance. A satellite image does not show a building; it shows patterns of light reflectance that indicate terrain, vegetation, construction, water. A microscopy image does not show a biological structure directly; it shows how that structure interacts with stains or labels. Interpreting these images requires translating visual patterns into knowledge about the real world, and different imaging modalities (different wavelengths, stains, or resolutions) create completely different visual appearance even when showing the same underlying phenomenon.

Unifying Heterogeneous Multi-Modal Remote Sensing Detection Via Language-Pivoted Pretraining handles the multimodality problem: satellite data comes in different frequency bands (visible light, infrared, microwave), and a model needs to detect objects (buildings, roads, changes) using information from all of them simultaneously. Training these different modalities together is unstable because they have different statistics and scales. By using language as a bridge, the model learns to translate each modality into a shared conceptual space (describing what it sees in words), then perform detection in that space. This stabilizes training and lets the model use information from all modalities together. Fusing Pixels and Genes: Spatially-Aware Learning in Computational Pathology faces a similar challenge in microscopy: tissue samples can be imaged visually and also sequenced to show which genes are active at each location. A pathologist looking at a microscopy image is inferring which genes are active; the reverse is also true—knowing which genes are active helps interpret the visual appearance. The model learns to fuse these two modalities by understanding that genes and visual patterns are two views of the same tissue location, and strong performance requires learning their connection.

Unified medical understanding and generation

Medical imaging models often specialize: one for classification (is this disease present?), one for segmentation (where is the lesion?), one for report generation (describe this scan in words). Building a single model that handles all of these requires a unified architecture that can take an image as input and produce both understanding (classification, segmentation) and natural language (reports, captions).

UniMedVL: Unifying Medical Multimodal Understanding and Generation through Observation-Knowledge-Analysis structures the model around three stages: observation (analyze the image), knowledge (apply medical understanding), and analysis (generate output). This framework allows the same model to perform diverse tasks: classification and segmentation (understanding tasks) as well as virtual staining (colorizing grayscale tissue) and super-resolution (sharpening low-detail images) and report generation (generating text). By learning a unified representation that supports both understanding and generation, the model becomes more flexible and learns richer representations of medical images. Each task reinforces the others, which means understanding what you see in an image helps you describe it or enhance it, and generating descriptions forces the model to encode the visual features that matter semantically.

UniMedVL: Unifying Medical Multimodal Understanding and Generation through Observation-Knowledge-Analysis
Achieves competitive understanding performance and natively supports diverse interleaved generation tasks like virtual staining and super-resolution.
