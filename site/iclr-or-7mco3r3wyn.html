TempFlow-GRPO: Knowing When Each Step Matters

When training a model to draw pictures humans prefer, reward early decisions more because they shape the whole image, not equally like today's methods do.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Flow models draw images one noisy step at a time, gradually removing blur until a clear picture emerges. Standard reinforcement learning (learning by reward) treats all steps the same, even though early steps pick the big structure and later steps just add details. TempFlow-GRPO finds which steps matter most and focuses learning effort there, training 10 times faster because every training update improves decisions that actually shape the outcome, not wasted on steps that barely change anything.

Zero assumption version

Every step looks the same to today's methods

An image generator works like an artist revealing a painting under a thick fog. At first, you can only see shadows and rough shapes (big structure). Halfway through, the fog lifts and you see major colors and layouts. By the end, only fine details remain. Each step of this process is different in how much it shapes the final picture.

Today's methods treat all steps equally: they give each step the same learning signal when a human likes the final result. This is like paying an artist the same for deciding the composition as for adding a few brush strokes. The big decisions get lost in the noise. Early steps (which determine whether it looks like a cat or a dog) and late steps (which add whiskers) get the same learning signal, so the model wastes training effort on learning to fine-tune early-stage details instead of on perfecting structure—the learning signal cannot distinguish which decisions actually shaped the outcome because all steps are weighted equally.

A beginner might try: 'Just add a tiny reward at each step so learning spreads out.' This fails because you cannot know in advance what small rewards to use, and even if learning spreads to all steps equally, each step's nudge toward correctness is wasted if that step barely affects the final image—like trying to steer a car by adjusting the mirror angle and the steering wheel equally.

The fix. Track which steps actually affect the outcome most by testing steps in isolation, then weight learning stronger for high-impact steps.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure impact by branching

Run the model once with random (random) noise injected at only timestep k, compare final image to baseline, measure how reward changed.

Repeat for each timestep

Test noise at step 0, then step 1, then step 2, etc. Record how much each step's randomness shifted the final reward.

Assign weights by impact

Steps that moved the reward most get higher learning weights. Steps that moved it barely get low weights.

Train with weighted gradients

During reinforcement learning, multiply each timestep's learning signal by its weight so important steps get stronger training nudges—the model learns to refine structure-shaping steps because those are where learning actually sticks and moves the reward.

Control for noise in seeding

Use the same random initializations across tests to isolate the true effect of each timestep without confounding randomness.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the learning signal (gradient) is distributed across timesteps. Instead of sending the same learning signal to all steps, it sends stronger signals to early steps (where one pixel choice cascades into big changes because the entire rest of the image is built on top of it) and weaker signals to later steps (where choices have already solidified from earlier steps and cannot be undone).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Timestep criticality

measured quantity  =  How much each step shapes the outcome

This is the hidden quantity: for each step in the generation process, how much does changing that step actually change the final image quality score? Early steps that determine shape and color have high criticality. Late steps adding texture have low criticality.

The paper measures it by switching a timestep from smooth denoising to noisy denoising in isolation, then recording how much the final reward shifted. If switching step 1 to noisy changes reward by 5 points but switching step 7 changes it by 0.1 points, step 1 has much higher criticality. The paper uses these measured criticalities as weights during training.

Why it works

Why focusing on critical steps speeds up learning

Here is the core: a flow model draws an image by removing blur step by step. Early steps carve out big shapes (a head, arms, background). Late steps just refine texture. When humans score the final image, they score based on whether it looks right overall, not whether the last texture is perfect. So early decisions influence the score much more than late ones.

Standard learning spreads the reward signal equally to all steps: each step gets nudged the same amount. But most of that nudge at late steps is wasted—those decisions barely affect the score, so the learning cannot stick. It is like trying to teach someone by giving identical feedback for critical choices and trivial ones.

TempFlow-GRPO measures how much each step actually moves the score (by testing in isolation). Then it weights the learning signal: steps that move the score a lot get strong training nudges; steps that barely move it get weak nudges. Early steps now absorb most of the learning effort, where it counts. The model converges faster because every learning step improves something that matters.

Math after the picture

The weighting formula

First in words:

For each timestep, multiply the learning update by a weight proportional to how much noise at that timestep moves the final reward.

Then the compact form:

L = sum_t w_t * loss_t, where w_t is proportional to std(reward | noise at t)

Every symbol:

Ltotal learning loss for this batch
sum_tadd up the loss from all timesteps
w_tlearning weight for timestep t
loss_thow wrong the model is at timestep t
std(reward | noise at t)how much the final reward changes when you add randomness only at timestep t (the measured criticality)
A tiny worked example
Test 1: Add noise at step 0 (early structure decision). Final reward ranges 18-22 across runs. Standard deviation = 2.0.
Test 2: Add noise at step 5 (mid texture). Final reward ranges 19.9-20.1. Standard deviation = 0.1.
Weights: w_0 = proportional to 2.0 = 2.0 relative units. w_5 = proportional to 0.1 = 0.1 relative units. After normalizing: w_0 = 20x, w_5 = 1x.
During training, step 0 gradients are multiplied by 20, step 5 gradients by 1. Step 0 receives 20x stronger training signal because it actually moves the outcome.

The weight is the measured impact: steps that wiggle outcomes a lot when perturbed get higher multipliers, so learning focuses where it matters.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Seed confounding. If you use different random starting noise for each branching test, you cannot tell if reward changed because of the timestep or because of the seed. Fix: hold seeds constant across all impact measurements.
Late-stage weights collapse to zero. If late steps have near-zero impact, their weights become tiny. The model stops learning to refine them at all. Fix: enforce a minimum weight so even low-impact steps get some training signal.
Noisy impact measurements. If the model is random (random), testing a single branching point gives noisy numbers. You measure criticality incorrectly if you only test once. Fix: average over many branching trials per timestep.
Proper execution. Use the same batch of random seeds for all impact tests, run many trials per timestep, compute standard deviation carefully, then normalize weights to have mean 1 so the overall learning rate stays sane.
