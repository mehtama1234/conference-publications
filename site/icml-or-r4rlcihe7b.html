Approximating Drift-Diffusion Models for User Decisions under Nudging and External Information

Approximate the response-time distribution of a user whose evidence accumulation rate changes over time because notifications or feeds arrive while the decision is still unfolding.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

A drift-diffusion model describes a decision as noisy evidence accumulation toward a threshold. The classic version assumes the average accumulation rate, called the drift, stays constant. That assumption fails for many online decisions. A notification, recommendation, or feed update can arrive mid-decision and change how quickly a user moves toward action. Once the drift becomes a function of time, the first-passage time distribution, meaning the distribution of times at which the process first hits the decision threshold, is usually hard to compute exactly. This paper derives a closed-form approximation for a single-threshold model in the high-threshold regime. That matters because it turns a simulation-heavy problem into an analyzable one, so a platform can reason about when an external signal should be sent if it wants to maximize the chance of a response within a time window.

Zero assumption version

External signals make response-time models hard

User response time depends on both internal preference and time-varying outside information. A reminder can raise urgency at one moment, while a distracting feed item can lower it at another. Standard drift-diffusion models handle the case where evidence accumulates at a fixed average rate, but they lose their simple formulas once the drift changes continuously over time.

That mathematical obstacle matters in product settings because timing is often the action variable. If a platform wants to ask when to send a nudge, it needs a response-time model that can be evaluated quickly across many candidate send times. Repeated simulation is possible, but it obscures the dependence on the signal schedule and is hard to optimize directly.

The paper's move in one sentence. Approximate the first time a time-varying drift-diffusion process hits the response threshold with a closed-form expression that is accurate when the threshold is high.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the part of the drift-diffusion model that is assumed constant. It allows the drift to change over time and then approximates the resulting response-time law analytically because the time-varying version no longer has the standard closed-form formulas.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  The response-time density induced by a time-varying drift

The user does not expose a direct clock for when action will happen. What we need is the probability density over response times implied by the hidden accumulation process. With constant drift, that density has standard formulas. With time-varying drift, it becomes the hidden quantity that the paper approximates.

This density encodes the effect of signal timing. If a notification temporarily increases drift before the threshold is reached, the mass of the density shifts earlier. If the same signal arrives after the likely crossing time, it has little effect. The approximation keeps this dependence visible.

Why it works

Why the high-threshold approximation is useful

When the response threshold is high, random short-term fluctuations are less important than the accumulated effect of the drift path over a longer interval. That lets the paper approximate the crossing event by a formula driven mainly by the integrated drift and the threshold height. The approximation is useful because it converts a difficult path-dependent probability problem into an expression that can be evaluated quickly.

This matters for optimization. A platform that wants to schedule a notification cares about how the nudge shifts the response-time density as the send time changes. A closed-form approximation exposes that dependence directly, so send-time optimization becomes a tractable calculation rather than a nested Monte Carlo loop.

Math after the picture

The response probability target

First in words:

Choose the signal time that maximizes the probability that the first threshold crossing happens before the deadline, where that probability is computed from the approximated first-passage time density.

Then the compact form:

t_signal* = arg max_t integral_0^T f_tau(s | t) ds

Every symbol:

t_signal*the chosen time at which the external signal is sent
Tthe response deadline or evaluation horizon
f_tau(s | t)the approximated density of the response time s when the signal is sent at time t
integral_0^T f_tau(s | t) dsthe probability of responding before the deadline under signal time t
A tiny worked example
Suppose sending at minute 2 gives an approximated response probability of 0.31 before minute 10.
Sending at minute 4 gives 0.38.
Sending at minute 7 gives 0.22.
The policy chooses minute 4 because that send time places the positive drift bump where many users are still below threshold but close enough to be moved.

The paper derives the density approximation itself. The optimization step then becomes straightforward because it only compares integrated probabilities across send times.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The threshold regime may be wrong. If the decision threshold is not high, the approximation may lose accuracy because short-term fluctuations matter more than the asymptotic argument assumes.
User behavior may violate the single-threshold model. Real users may have abandonment, repeated reconsideration, or multiple competing actions, none of which fit a one-boundary process cleanly.
The signal effect may be misspecified. If a notification changes more than the drift, such as the noise level or the threshold itself, the model can attribute response shifts to the wrong mechanism.
Done right. This approximation is most useful when the main modeling need is timing analysis under moderate signal perturbations rather than a full behavioral account of every user state transition.
