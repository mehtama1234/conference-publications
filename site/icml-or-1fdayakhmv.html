Fisher-Preserving Guidance

Keep diffusion-model guidance on the safe training data manifold by constraining updates to stay on a Fisher isosurface.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a diffusion model predicts navigation waypoints, adding task guidance (like minimizing path length) often pushes predictions far from the training data into regions the model never learned. This paper proposes a training-free method that steers the model toward the task goal while keeping predictions close to where the training data actually lived, using a constraint based on Fisher information (a measure of how much the model's predictions change when its inputs vary slightly).

Zero assumption version

Guidance pulls predictions away from real training data

A diffusion model learns to predict good actions by training on real navigation data. At test time, guidance adds a task objective—like minimizing path length or collision cost. The naive approach computes which direction moves the current prediction in the good direction, then nudges the prediction that way.

This breaks because the good-direction nudge moves the prediction into regions far from any training data. The model was never trained there, so its predictions become unreliable or drift into dead ends.

A beginner might try adding stronger guidance or more iterations, but stronger nudges and more steps just drive further from training data, making predictions worse because the model was never trained on those regions. The core problem is that nothing stops the prediction from leaving the region where the model learned its behavior.

The fix in one sentence. Constrain each guidance step to move along a surface (a Fisher-information surface) where the model's sensitivity to its inputs stays constant—a geometric rule that keeps predictions near training data.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compute task guidance direction

Calculate which way to nudge to reduce task loss (path cost, collision, etc).

Measure Fisher sensitivity

How much does the model's prediction jitter when inputs change slightly.

Project guidance onto isosurface

Find the closest movement direction that keeps sensitivity constant.

Apply the constrained step

Move in the projected direction, staying within the training data region.

Blend multiple predictions

Use uncertainty (Fisher sensitivity) to weight which samples to average together.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes the guidance direction itself. Instead of moving straight toward the task goal (which leaves the training region), it constrains the guidance to a tangent plane—a geometric surface that forces each step to preserve Fisher information locally. The navigation prediction still heads toward the task objective, but takes a safe path that stays within where the training data actually exists.

the object it moves  =  guidance direction
The hidden quantity

Fisher sensitivity: how much the model jitters

measured quantity  =  Fisher sensitivity per step

Fisher information measures how sharply the model's predictions change when inputs (observations) vary. High Fisher means small input changes cause big prediction swings. Low Fisher means predictions are stable. The paper computes Step-wise Fisher Denoising Sensitivity (Step-FDS): at each denoising step, it calculates how sensitive the predicted noise is to observation perturbations. This quantity lives in a high-dimensional space, but the paper uses a low-rank factorization (decomposing it into a product of smaller parts) to compute it cheaply.

Why measure it? Because predictions living on a constant-Fisher surface stay close to the data manifold. The paper enforces first-order Fisher consistency: each step's update preserves Fisher information (up to small residuals). This is the safety guardrail. The paper also uses Step-FDS directly as an uncertainty signal: noisy or uncertain predictions (high Step-FDS variance) are weighted down when blending multiple samples.

Plain reading. A model learns to navigate by seeing patterns in real training videos. Fisher sensitivity captures: if I show you a slightly different video, how differently would the model act? High sensitivity means it acts wildly different (risky). Keeping sensitivity constant means staying in neighborhoods where the model has consistent, predictable behavior.
Paper reading. Step-wise Fisher Denoising Sensitivity is the squared Frobenius norm of the Jacobian of the noise prediction with respect to observations, restricted to the top ChH dimensions (where C is channels, H is hidden dimension). The paper uses an Outer Product Span projection to compute Fisher-preserving updates via low-rank factorization.
Why it works

Why constraining to a Fisher-information surface works

The training data defines a region in action space where the model has learned good predictive behavior. This region has a geometric property: near the data, Fisher information is relatively uniform (stable). Far away, the model has learned nothing, so Fisher varies wildly or becomes meaningless.

Each guidance step computes a direction to reduce task loss. Constraining the update to a surface of constant Fisher means: move toward the task goal, but choose the movement that keeps Fisher information fixed. The update removes the component of guidance that would change Fisher (which would take you away from training data) and keeps only the component that preserves it (which keeps you safe).

Because the decomposition uses a small subspace, computing this constraint requires only one backward pass (one gradient computation) per step, not a complex inversion. Single passes are fast because they avoid repeated table operations, making real-time navigation feasible. The second ingredient is Truncated Fisher Denoising Sensitivity (TFDS) as an uncertainty signal: predictions with high TFDS are near the uncertain regions of what the training data showed, so the algorithm averages multiple samples, weighting confident ones higher.

Math after the picture

The isosurface projection equation

First in words:

The constrained guidance update is the original task gradient minus its component perpendicular to the isosurface, scaled by step size.

Then the compact form:

Delta_t = u_t - (u_t^T g_t / ||g_t||^2) * g_t

Every symbol:

Delta_tthe constrained guidance update at step t
u_tthe task loss gradient (naive guidance direction)
g_tthe Fisher isosurface normal (computed from low-rank Jacobian)
||g_t||^2the squared length of the normal vector
a_{t-1} = mu_t - gamma * Delta_tthe new denoised action: mean minus scaled update
A tiny worked example
Suppose task loss gradient is u_t = [4, 1], and Fisher normal is g_t = [2, 0].
Dot product: u_t^T g_t = 4*2 + 1*0 = 8.
Squared length: ||g_t||^2 = 2^2 + 0^2 = 4.
Component to remove: (8/4) * [2,0] = 2 * [2,0] = [4, 0].
Constrained update: Delta_t = [4,1] - [4,0] = [0, 1].
Interpretation: the task wanted to move [4,1], but Fisher said no to the [4,0] part (off-manifold). Only the [0,1] part (safe direction) survives.

The constraint ensures that the new direction is orthogonal to the surface normal, so each step stays on the Fisher-information surface up to small residuals from calculus approximations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Rank truncation cuts important modes. The low-rank factorization discards modes of the Jacobian (the gradient map) with small strength. If task guidance lies partly in a discarded mode, the constraint will be approximate. The paper bounds this error via a ratio (called kappa) but does not guarantee perfect constraint.
Fisher assumes smooth manifold. The method assumes the training manifold is smooth and Fisher information is well-defined. If the data has sharp boundaries or discontinuities, Fisher sensitivity becomes unreliable and the isosurface breaks down.
Uncertainty signal can be noisy. Truncated Fisher Denoising Sensitivity is estimated from single samples. If the estimate is high noise, weighting predictions by it may discard good actions and keep bad ones.
Done right. Keeping rank high enough (testing via hyperparameter kappa), validating that Fisher estimates are stable across multiple runs, and blending samples from multiple independent noise seeds all reduce these failure modes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 2D navigation task (e.g., a grid with obstacles). Train a diffusion policy on a dataset of safe paths. Then run inference with three methods: (1) no guidance (random walk), (2) naive task guidance (straight to goal), (3) Fisher-Preserving Guidance. For each, measure success rate (reached goal), path length, and collision count. Plot the average trajectory to show that naive guidance drifts into dead ends, while FPG stays near training-data paths.

drift_distance = mean( ||predicted_action - nearest_training_action|| ), success = 1 if goal reached else 0

The demo must make it impossible to miss: naive guidance produces scattered, out-of-bounds predictions; FPG predictions cluster near the training region and reach the goal reliably.
