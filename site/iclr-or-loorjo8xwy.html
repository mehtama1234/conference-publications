Complementing Self-Consistency with Cross-Model Disagreement for Uncertainty Quantification

Measure uncertainty from two sources at once: variation within one model's repeated answers and disagreement across several similar-size models.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

A language model can be wrong while sounding certain. A common way to detect uncertainty is self-consistency: ask the same model several times and see whether its answers vary. That captures one kind of uncertainty, but it breaks when the model repeats the same wrong answer over and over. In that case the within-model spread is low even though the answer is unreliable. This paper studies that failure mode and adds a second signal: do several different models, each of similar scale, produce semantically different answers for the same prompt? The method calls the first signal aleatoric uncertainty, meaning variation inside one model's own answer samples, and the second signal epistemic uncertainty, meaning disagreement between models beyond what each model shows internally. Adding both gives a total uncertainty score that catches cases where one model is confidently wrong but peer models do not agree with it.

Zero assumption version

Why repeated sampling from one model can miss confident mistakes

If a model answers a prompt five times and gives five different answers, we have direct evidence that the model is unsure. That is the intuition behind self-consistency as an uncertainty score.

The problem is the opposite case. A model can lock onto one wrong reasoning pattern and repeat it across samples. Then self-consistency reports low uncertainty because the samples agree, even though the answer is still wrong. Looking only inside one model misses the possibility that the model's whole belief state is miscalibrated.

The paper's move. Keep the within-model variation score, but add a second score that asks whether other models disagree in meaning with that answer more than each model disagrees with itself.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Sample several answers from one model.

Measure how much that model varies across repeated runs, because low internal variation is the standard self-consistency signal.

Sample answers from a small peer ensemble.

Use several models of similar size, because the method wants disagreement that reflects different learned beliefs rather than only huge scale differences.

Score answer meaning overlap.

Compare how similar the generated answers are in semantic content, because the method cares about whether answers mean the same thing rather than whether they share surface words.

Compute a cross-model gap.

Measure how much more the models disagree with each other than each model disagrees with itself, because that gap isolates uncertainty hidden by one model's repeated confidence.

Add the two uncertainty terms.

Combine within-model spread and cross-model disagreement, because each captures a different reason an answer may be unreliable.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes uncertainty estimation from a single-model self-consistency score into a two-part score that also uses disagreement across similar-size models.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  disagreement that is invisible inside one model

The key hidden quantity is not raw answer diversity. It is the extra disagreement that appears only when we compare several models to each other. If one model is consistently wrong in the same way, its internal samples will look stable. But if peer models answer with different meanings, that exposes uncertainty that self-consistency cannot see.

The paper estimates that hidden quantity by comparing inter-model similarity with intra-model similarity, where similarity means how close two generated answers are in meaning. A large gap says the models disagree with each other more than they disagree with themselves, so the answer should be treated cautiously even if one model sounds certain.

Why it works

Why peer disagreement catches confident failures

Self-consistency measures randomness inside one model's sampling process. That is useful when the model is wavering between several answers. It is not enough when the model has settled on one wrong answer pattern, because repeated samples then all support the same mistake.

Cross-model disagreement adds a second view. Different models trained on different data orders, initialization histories, or tuning decisions often make different errors. If one model is wrong but several peers give a different answer meaning, the disagreement reveals that the apparent certainty was local to that model rather than shared by the broader model family.

Comparing cross-model similarity against within-model similarity is the key step because it filters out cases where all models are naturally diverse on the prompt. The method does not flag every disagreement. It flags disagreement that is larger across models than within each model's own samples, which is the pattern expected when one model's confidence is not well founded.

Math after the picture

Two-part uncertainty score

First in words:

Total uncertainty is the sum of the within-model uncertainty term and the cross-model disagreement term.

Then the compact form:

total_uncertainty = aleatoric_uncertainty + epistemic_uncertainty, where epistemic_uncertainty = between_model_disagreement - within_model_disagreement

Every symbol:

aleatoric_uncertaintyvariation across repeated samples from one model
epistemic_uncertaintyextra disagreement that appears across models beyond their own internal variation
between_model_disagreementhow different the answer meanings are across models
within_model_disagreementhow different the answer meanings are across samples from the same model
A tiny worked example
Suppose one model answers a prompt five times and the answers are nearly identical in meaning.
Its within-model uncertainty is low.
Now compare that answer to two peer models and find that their answers differ strongly in meaning.
The between-model disagreement is high while the within-model disagreement stays low.
The epistemic term becomes large, so the total uncertainty rises even though self-consistency alone would stay small.
That is exactly the pattern the method wants to catch.

The extra term is designed for black-box access, so it only needs generated text from each model rather than internal model states.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The peer models can share the same blind spot. If several models are wrong for the same reason and say nearly the same thing, cross-model disagreement stays low and the extra uncertainty term will not rescue the failure.
Similarity scoring can be noisy. If the answer-meaning comparison says two answers are close when they are not, or far when they are close, the epistemic term will be distorted because it is built directly from those similarity values.
Very different model families may add irrelevant disagreement. If the peer set mixes models with very different capabilities or styles, disagreement may reflect style differences rather than true answer uncertainty.
Done right. The method works best with a small, comparable peer set and a strong meaning-similarity scorer, because then the cross-model gap reflects genuine belief differences rather than formatting noise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Pick a benchmark with long-form answers and known correctness labels. For each prompt, sample several answers from three similar-size models, compute self-consistency, compute the cross-model gap, and sort prompts by each uncertainty score. Then test two behaviors: ranking calibration and selective abstention. The demo should show that prompts flagged by the combined score contain more confident wrong answers than prompts flagged by self-consistency alone.

risk_at_coverage = wrong_answers_kept / answers_kept

The key evidence is not just higher uncertainty on random errors, but higher uncertainty on the cases where one model sounds most certain and is still wrong.
