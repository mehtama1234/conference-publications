Context Learning for Multi-Agent Discussion

Train small context generators for each LLM agent to dynamically refine their instructions per discussion round so they stay aligned instead of drifting into majority noise.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When multiple LLM agents discuss a problem together, they often drift apart because each gets a pre-written instruction that becomes outdated as the discussion evolves. This paper introduces M2CL, which trains small generator models for each agent that automatically refine those instructions after each discussion round based on what the agent has learned so far. This keeps all agents aligned toward the correct answer instead of collectively converging on a wrong "majority noise" solution.

Zero assumption version

Why multi-agent discussion fails

When you have multiple LLMs solve a problem by discussing it together, they each start with a hand-written instruction (like "you are the mathematician" or "you are the engineer"). The problem: as the discussion unfolds, each agent's original instruction becomes stale. It does not know what the other agents have said, what mistakes are being made, or what the group has learned so far.

A naive fix is to just let them keep talking—but they often converge on a wrong answer together. All agents drift toward the same mistake that the loudest or most confident agent pushed for, a phenomenon called majority noise. The real issue is that their instructions are misaligned: they guide each agent in a slightly different direction, and once talking begins, there is no mechanism to steer them back into sync.

The method. Train a small context generator for each agent that rewrites its instruction after each discussion round based on what has been said so far and what direction the group is drifting.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Initialize base instructions

Each agent starts with a high-level instruction defining its role or viewpoint.

Step 2: Round 1 discussion

All agents answer the question based on their base instruction.

Step 3: Generator refines instructions

A small trained generator reads all responses and refines each agent's instruction to steer toward consensus and away from errors.

Step 4: Round N discussion with refined instructions

Agents re-answer using the updated, context-aware instructions.

Step 5: Repeat until convergence

Generators keep refining instructions per round until all agents agree on a final answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces static, hand-written instructions with dynamic ones. Instead of each agent reading the same instruction every round, a trainable generator—a small neural network—reads the most recent responses from all agents and produces a new, slightly refined instruction for its agent before the next round. This new instruction is tailored to the current discussion state and nudges the agent toward the group consensus.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: context coherence

measured quantity  =  Context coherence

Context coherence is a number that measures how synchronized the agents are at any moment in the discussion. Specifically, it is the inverse of how different the activations are across the agents' internal attention mechanisms—a mathematical way of saying how much the agents' internal processing is aligned. When agents read instructions that push them in similar directions, their attention patterns are similar (high coherence). When misaligned, their attention patterns are different (low coherence).

The paper measures this by computing the distance between each pair of agents' attention activations (the numbers that flow through the attention mechanism at each step). During training, the generators are explicitly encouraged to keep this distance small—that is, to maintain high coherence—while also letting each agent contribute unique insights.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

The mechanism has three parts. First, each agent's instruction naturally shapes how it reads and reasons about the problem—different instructions steer the agent's attention in different directions inside its neural networks. Second, the generators are trained to predict which instruction adjustments will increase agreement (high coherence) between agents' internal states. This training happens on small samples of multi-round discussions where we know the right answer. Third, because the generators are trained to recognize when instructions push agents apart and how to fix it, they can apply this pattern to new problems during inference: they read the current responses, detect divergence, and refine the instructions to realign the group.

The generators are tiny compared to the LLMs themselves—they only need to produce short instruction text—so retraining them is cheap. And critically, the generators learn to avoid a key failure mode: when the group confidently converges on a wrong answer (majority noise), the generator recognizes this as a sign of lost diversity, and it will refine instructions to reintroduce healthy disagreement on that specific issue while maintaining alignment on correct pieces.

Math after the picture

The equation: controlling context and coherence

First in words:

The training objective penalizes two things: (1) instructions that change too much from their base version (to avoid wild swings), and (2) any divergence in agent attention patterns. The objective is the original multi-agent loss plus a weighted sum of a regularization term and a coherence alignment term.

Then the compact form:

L = L_base + lambda * ||I_i^t - I_i^base|| + beta * sum_{i,j} ||a(C_i^t) - a(C_j^t)||

Every symbol:

Ltotal training loss (error to minimize)
L_baseloss from the original multi-agent discussion (how wrong the answers are)
I_i^tinstruction for agent i at round t (the text)
I_i^baseoriginal base instruction for agent i (before refinement)
lambdaconstraint weight: penalizes large instruction changes
betacoherence weight: penalizes misalignment in attention patterns
a(C_i^t)attention activation vector for agent i at round t (the numbers flowing through attention)
||...||distance between two vectors (how different they are)
A tiny worked example
Suppose agent 1 has base instruction and generates new instruction that changes 3 words out of 50.
Change penalty: lambda times 3 = 0.01 times 3 = 0.03 (small, because lambda=0.01 and the change is minimal).
Suppose agent 1 and agent 2's attention patterns differ by a distance of 8.0 (moderately misaligned).
Suppose agent 2 and agent 3's patterns differ by 2.0 (well aligned).
Coherence penalty: beta times (8.0 + 2.0) = 0.5 times 10.0 = 5.0 (beta=0.5 means coherence matters a lot).
Total new penalty: 0.03 + 5.0 = 5.03 added to the base loss. During training, the generator learns to reduce this: it tweaks the instruction slightly to align agent 1 with the others.

The two terms work together: the lambda term prevents instructions from thrashing wildly, and the beta term ensures all agents' internal processing stays synchronized around the correct answer.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Generators overfit to training tasks. If the generators are trained on only a few types of problems, they learn to recognize those specific patterns and fail on new, unseen task types. Solution: train on diverse problem types and validate on held-out domains.
Instruction changes become too timid. If lambda (the constraint weight) is set too high, the generators learn not to change instructions at all, defeating the purpose. The coherence reward is drowned out by the penalty for change.
Generators mask genuine disagreement. A generator trained only to maximize coherence might force all agents to agree even when one agent has spotted a real error the others missed. The solution the paper uses: include diversity signals so the generator learns to value disagreement on uncertain topics.
Done right. The paper validates on multiple domains (reasoning, embodied tasks, control) to ensure the learned patterns transfer. The constraint weight is tuned by trial-and-error so instructions evolve noticeably but stay grounded. The training objective explicitly penalizes forced consensus on incorrect answers.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple proof-checking task: three LLMs discuss whether a mathematical statement is true. Write a simple script that (1) initializes three agents with different base instructions ("think like a pure mathematician," "think like an engineer," "think like a skeptic") because different viewpoints naturally lead agents to miss-align, (2) runs one round of discussion, (3) trains a tiny generator on 10 worked examples showing which instruction tweaks improve consensus on correct proofs (the generator learns patterns like "when engineer says X but mathematician says Y, nudge engineer's instruction toward Y"), (4) uses that generator to refine instructions for a fresh proof, (5) runs discussion again and measures how many agents agree on the correct answer. You do not need real LLMs; a small language model will suffice for a proof-of-concept.

agreement_gain = (M2CL_final_agreement - baseline_final_agreement) / baseline_final_agreement

The demo makes it visceral: without the generator, agents drift into conflicting answers because their static instructions never adapt to what the group learns. With even a tiny trained generator refining instructions, they lock into agreement and get the right answer more often because the generator has learned to spot when agents are diverging and nudge them back into sync. That is the entire insight: dynamically refine instructions, not static ones.
