Zero assumption version

Large-scale 3D reconstruction with mismatched resolution

Gaussian Splatting is a way to model a 3D scene as a set of soft blobs (Gaussians), each with a position, shape, and color. Training learns where to place each blob and what color it should be, supervised by comparing rendered images to photographs. For small rooms, one simple strategy works: start low-resolution, gradually raise to high-resolution, densify when the model is stuck. But on city-scale scenes with sparse photos, this breaks: early in training, the model is still guessing the coarse shape and structure; feeding it high-resolution supervision anyway pushes it to learn fine texture detail it does not yet need, forcing wasteful densification of tiny redundant blobs everywhere. Later, when the model needs fine detail, it is bloated and slow.

A beginner would try: always train at highest resolution, add densification every N steps. This fails because the model overfits to noise in over-observed regions while ignoring barely-observed gaps, and the fixed schedule does not adapt to how much the scene has actually converged.

The method in one sentence. SIG watches the frequencies the Gaussians are currently capturing, and raises image resolution and triggers densification only when the model is ready to learn finer detail.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compare to target

Check if the scene frequency has reached the Nyquist limit (the max frequency that current image resolution can provide).

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper does not change the Gaussians themselves or the rendering formula. Instead, it changes the scheduler that decides when to raise image resolution during training and when to add new Gaussians for densification.

the object it moves  =  training schedule (resolution and densification timing)
The hidden quantity

The average Gaussian bandwidth

measured quantity  =  Average recoverable frequency of the scene

The paper introduces a new measurement: the average frequency that all Gaussians in the current model can collectively represent. This is not frequency measured from the rendered output, but derived analytically from the shapes and sizes of the Gaussian blobs themselves. Each blob has a covariance matrix (a matrix describing its shape and size); the paper computes that blob's maximum frequency (called the Nyquist frequency for that blob) via its 3 dB bandwidth from signal processing. The average bandwidth across all blobs is the scene's current recoverable frequency.

To estimate this, the paper first computes the Fourier transform of each Gaussian function, finds its half-power bandwidth, then averages across all Gaussians weighted by their opacity (how much they contribute to the final image). This gives a single number that tells the training loop how much fine detail the current model can possibly represent.

Why it works

Why matching frequencies works

The core insight is borrowed from signal processing: a photograph at a given resolution can only show detail down to a certain fineness (the Nyquist frequency for that resolution). If you feed the model finer images before it can represent fine frequencies, three things happen: (1) the model cannot fit the extra detail, so it overfits by creating redundant tiny blobs everywhere; (2) training steps are wasted on unrecoverable frequencies; (3) memory and compute explode. Conversely, if the model has already learned all the detail that the current resolution can provide, raising resolution will give it new information to fit.

SIG solves this by computing analytically what frequency the Gaussians can currently represent (from their bandwidth), comparing it to the image resolution's Nyquist frequency, and only raising resolution when the model is ready. This keeps training efficient: early iterations stay low-resolution, forcing the model to learn the basic shape and structure without distraction. Once the bandwidth catches up, resolution is raised, and densification can add finer blobs only when they can actually contribute. The Sphere-Constrained Gaussians further reduce redundancy by constraining each blob to stay near the initialized point cloud, preventing wild extrapolation into empty space.

Math after the picture

The average bandwidth equation

First in words:

The average recoverable frequency is the sum over all Gaussians of opacity-squared times the Nyquist frequency for that Gaussian, all divided by the sum of opacity-squared values.

Then the compact form:

avg_freq = sum_i (o_i^2 * omega_3dB_i * det(Sigma_i)) / sum_i (o_i^2 * det(Sigma_i))

Every symbol:

o_iopacity (how visible Gaussian i is)
omega_3dB_ithe 3 dB bandwidth of Gaussian i, derived from its covariance
det(Sigma_i)the volume of Gaussian i (determinant of its shape matrix)
avg_freqthe average frequency the scene can represent
A tiny worked example
Say we have 3 Gaussians: G1 (opacity 0.8, bandwidth 10, volume 1.0), G2 (opacity 0.6, bandwidth 20, volume 1.5), G3 (opacity 0.4, bandwidth 15, volume 0.5).
Numerator: 0.8^2 * 10 * 1.0 + 0.6^2 * 20 * 1.5 + 0.4^2 * 15 * 0.5 = 6.4 + 10.8 + 3.0 = 20.2
Denominator: 0.8^2 * 1.0 + 0.6^2 * 1.5 + 0.4^2 * 0.5 = 0.64 + 0.54 + 0.08 = 1.26
Average frequency = 20.2 / 1.26 = 16.0. If image resolution supports Nyquist of 20, we keep low-res. If Nyquist is 15, we raise resolution.

The numerator counts each Gaussian's bandwidth contribution weighted by how opaque it is and how much volume it occupies.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Initialization bias. If the initial point cloud from Structure-from-Motion is sparse, some regions have too few Gaussians to reach the full Nyquist frequency. The scheduler will wait forever to raise resolution. Fix: initialize with a minimum density, or use a fallback timer.
Camera calibration drift. If camera poses from Structure-from-Motion are incorrect, the model cannot fit detail at any resolution. The bandwidth will plateau, and resolution will not raise properly. Fix: refine camera poses during training.
Occlusion and view gaps. In regions visible in very few photos, the Gaussians cannot learn what is there. The bandwidth may saturate prematurely, stopping resolution increase. Fix: estimate visibility per region and raise resolution region-wise, not globally.
Done right. When initialization is dense, poses are accurate, and the scheduler is region-wise (per block), SIG tracks scene frequency and adapts resolution smoothly without artifacts or bloat.

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo reconstructs a city block from 50 wide-baseline photographs at 1920x1080 pixels. Train one model with a hardcoded schedule (raise resolution every 5000 iterations) and one with SIG's adaptive schedule. Log the scene's average bandwidth every 1000 iterations, and log the rendered PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio, a measure of pixel accuracy) and the count of Gaussians.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: SIG reaches the same quality in less time, with fewer Gaussians, and the bandwidth curve shows a clear step where resolution is raised, whereas the hardcoded baseline raises resolution at arbitrary iterations.
