Quorus: Layerwise federated learning for heterogeneous quantum computers

Quorus trains quantum models across different computers by letting each one use as many layers as it can handle, guided by layer-by-layer loss signals.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Imagine ten offices each have a quantum computer (a machine that uses quantum physics to solve hard problems), but they are all different ages and have different error rates. They want to train a shared model together without sharing data. The problem: the expensive quantum computers have different circuit depths they can reliably run, so one shared model does not fit them all. Quorus solves this by letting each computer use its own depth, then training them in layers from bottom up so they stay synchronized.

Zero assumption version

Federated learning breaks when quantum computers are all different

Quantum computers run circuits (sequences of quantum gates) to train models. But real quantum computers lose information over time—a quantum qubit decays and forgets its state. The deeper the circuit (more layers), the more information is lost. Different offices have different computers: one office has a new, low-error machine that can run 10 layers safely; another has an older machine that only reliably runs 5 layers. If you try to use a single shared model, you are stuck: either make it so shallow that it wastes the new machine's power, or make it deep and the older machine produces garbage.

A beginner would try to train one shared model at full depth and just ignore the noise from weaker machines. This breaks because the weaker machine's training signals are corrupted—the gradient (the direction to nudge the model) points in the wrong direction. The whole group pulls in confused directions and learning stalls.

The move. Instead of one shared circuit depth, each machine trains its own depth locally, and they synchronize by comparing layer-by-layer losses.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Machine A runs its full depth (say, 8 layers), machine B runs shallow (4 layers).

Each computes a prediction at every layer it reaches.

Compare all outputs from the shared layers.

Where both ran, check: do we agree? If not, penalize the disagreement.

Deep machine also trains its extra layers normally (layers 5-8).

Nothing is wasted; extra depth is trained on the classification goal.

Broadcast the layer-wise losses and apply gradients locally.

No data leaves. Each machine nudges its own weights based on how it should behave at each layer.

Repeat across rounds.

Machines stay synchronized on shared-layer knowledge; deep machines gain more over time.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Classical federated learning trains a single shared model. Quorus changes what is being trained: instead of one model, each machine trains its own model of the same depth as it can handle, but those models share the same weights in the layers they both run. The key move is adding intermediate classifiers (predictions) at each layer so that machines of different depths can compare their knowledge at the layers they share.

the object it moves  =  Intermediate-layer classifiers and depths
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: layer-wise agreement

measured quantity  =  Layer-by-layer consensus loss

The hidden quantity is how much the machines agree with each other at each layer. In classical federated learning, you only measure agreement at the top (final output). Quorus measures agreement at every layer. At layer 3, if deep machine says 'yes' with confidence 0.9 and shallow machine says 'yes' with confidence 0.7, that is a disagreement—and Quorus quantifies it as a penalty using a mathematical score called the Kullback-Leibler divergence (a measure of distance between two probability distributions—just think of it as a 'how different are these two predictions' score).

The paper estimates this quantity by running each machine's circuit and measuring the output qubit at each layer. Because of a quantum quirk (measurement collapses the quantum state), they have to be clever: they use ancilla qubits (helper qubits) to measure without destroying the main data. This gives them the layer-wise outputs they need to compute the disagreement penalty.

Paper reading. The loss for machine k sums the classification error at each layer, plus a divergence score (how different the predictions are) between every pair of machines at each layer. This forces alignment at intermediate representations.
Why it works

Why it works: synchronization without shared depth

Standard federated learning assumes all clients run the same model, so they all train the same weights in sync. But quantum computers are different depths. If you force them into the same depth, the weaker machines produce noisy gradients and corrupt the shared knowledge.

Quorus sidesteps this. Each machine trains its own model at its own depth. But they share weights in the layers they both run. The trick is to measure agreement at every shared layer (not just the top) and penalize disagreement. This has a mechanical effect: when deep machine disagrees with shallow machine at layer 3, the penalty flows backward through training (nudging the weights in the direction that would reduce the error). Both machines nudge their layer-3 parameters to reach consensus. Over rounds, shallow machine's knowledge (which comes from a low-noise, reliable circuit) tugs all the shared layers toward safety. Deep machine keeps its extra layers separate, so it can learn richer patterns on the full depth. The gradient signals flow in the right direction because every machine is training on its own data with its own noise profile—no contamination from out-of-spec machines.

The layer-wise loss also solves a quantum-specific problem: measuring a qubit collapses its quantum state, making it impossible to pass the state to the next layer. Quorus uses ancilla qubits (helper qubits) to measure without collapsing (a weak measurement that reveals the answer without fully destroying the quantum state's complex information). This lets machines read off predictions at each layer without blowing up the quantum state, so the training signal from layer 4 can influence the training of layer 5.

Math after the picture

The layer-wise loss equation

First in words:

For machine k with depth d_k, the total loss is the sum of cross-entropy loss at each layer, plus the average Kullback-Leibler divergence between the predictions of every pair of machines at each layer.

Then the compact form:

L_k = sum(i=1 to d_k) of L_i^ce + (1/(d_k - 1)) * sum(i=1 to d_k) of sum(j=1 to d_k, j != i) of D_KL(p_j || p_i)

Every symbol:

L_i^ceCross-entropy classification loss at layer i
d_kDepth (number of layers) of machine k
p_iProbability prediction from machine i at a given layer
p_jProbability prediction from machine j at a given layer
D_KL(p_j || p_i)Kullback-Leibler divergence, a score of how different p_j and p_i are
A tiny worked example with three machines
Machine A (depth 3): predicts at layers 1, 2, 3. Outputs: [0.8, 0.7, 0.9].
Machine B (depth 2): predicts at layers 1, 2. Outputs: [0.75, 0.72].
Machine C (depth 2): predicts at layers 1, 2. Outputs: [0.76, 0.68].
Layer 1 consensus loss: measure disagreement among all three: D_KL(B||A) + D_KL(C||A) + D_KL(A||B) + D_KL(C||B) + D_KL(A||C) + D_KL(B||C). Suppose this sums to 0.3.
Layer 2 consensus loss: same for all three. Suppose 0.25.
Layer 3 classification loss (only A): how wrong A's output 0.9 is compared to the true label (measured by a standard error metric). Suppose 0.15.
Total L_A = 0.15 + 0.3 + 0.25 = 0.70. (The 0.3 and 0.25 are shared penalties; they also appear in L_B and L_C.)
All three machines backpropagate from these same consensus penalties, so their shared weights (layers 1-2) move toward agreement.

By comparing predictions at every layer (not just the top), machines with different depths stay synchronized on what they share, and deep machines learn extra patterns safely because the disagreement penalty only applies to shared layers.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Ancilla qubits are scarce. To measure without collapsing, you need one helper qubit per layer. Shallow quantum computers with only 5 qubits cannot afford this. Quorus has a backup 'Funnel' design that drops the measurement trick and gradually shrinks the model at each layer—worse accuracy, but it works on tight hardware.
Shot budget explodes. Each quantum step requires measuring the circuit many times (shots) to get accurate predictions. Measuring at every layer multiplies shots. If one machine is slow or expensive, it becomes a bottleneck.
Disagreement penalty backfires. If the shallow machine is truly the wrong model (e.g., it cannot represent the task's solution), forcing agreement with it holds back the deep machine. The remedy: tune the weight of the consensus loss so deep machines can break away if needed.
Done right. Use Quorus when you have machines of genuinely different capabilities (real NISQ hardware heterogeneity), shot budgets are reasonable, and you trust that the shallow machines give low-noise signals. Skip it if one machine is the clear winner—use a simpler depth-adaptive scheme.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Simulate two quantum machines: one with 10-layer capacity (low error), one with 5-layer capacity (higher error). Train a binary classifier on MNIST (is the digit 0 or 1) using federated learning. Run three experiments: (a) flatten both to 5 layers and train together, (b) each machine trains solo at its own depth, (c) Quorus with layer-wise loss. Measure test accuracy and gradient magnitude (how strong the training signals are) after each communication round.

accuracy = correct predictions / total predictions; gradient magnitude = average of abs(gradient) across all weights; learning progress = accuracy after round N minus accuracy after round 0

The demo succeeds if Quorus (c) reaches higher accuracy than naive flattening (a) and shows stronger gradient signals (meaning training pulls in a clear direction, not multiple confused directions). Solo training (b) will show weak gradients because the high-error machine's training signals are corrupted by quantum noise.
