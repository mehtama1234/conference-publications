KineFlow: Kinematic Second-Order Flow Matching for Time-Series Forecasting

A time-series forecasting method that treats future states as flowing gradually under forces, with built-in momentum that filters out noise and captures the physics of real systems.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When forecasting weather, stock prices, or engine performance over time, most methods either guess a single point value (which oversimplifies reality) or model how values jump around randomly (which ignores how systems naturally have inertia). KineFlow takes a third path: it models forecasting as a gradual flow where forces (like temperature changes or new orders) push the system to move with momentum, just as a kicked ball rolls rather than teleporting. This built-in momentum acts as a noise filter and captures the real physics of how complex systems evolve.

Zero assumption version

Two broken approaches to forecasting

Standard time-series forecasting guesses one point: what will the value be next? This method assumes the future is always a clean, deterministic step forward. But real systems are messy. Stock prices jump due to news; weather has chaotic swings; engine vibrations vary with tiny changes in load. A single-point guess misses all this uncertainty and produces overly smooth, dull forecasts.

Newer generative methods (flow matching) model randomness by imagining a probability cloud that flows forward in time. But they treat the cloud as if it teleports instantly with each time-step, ignoring momentum. A ball doesn't stop and start with each tick—it rolls smoothly. This mismatch means noisy, jittery predictions and lost information about how systems naturally behave.

The fix in one sentence. Use momentum and forces to make the forecast flow gradually, so noise gets filtered out and the system physics stay intact.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Track both position and momentum

Keep not just the current value but also its velocity (how fast it is changing).

2. Forces nudge momentum, not position

Exogenous inputs (like new weather) change the acceleration (the rate at which velocity changes), creating smooth transitions.

3. Integrate twice

Acceleration becomes velocity; velocity becomes new position. Double integration is a built-in low-pass filter.

4. Use flow matching to learn the acceleration field

Train a neural network to predict which accelerations match observed trajectories, without memorizing noise.

5. Generate by rolling forward

Sample starting positions and momentums; apply learned forces; roll into the future with natural physics.

What the paper changes

The thing being changed

The paper changes how the model represents a forecast: instead of predicting a single future value or a random cloud that teleports, it predicts a probability distribution that flows forward with momentum. Acceleration (the neural field the paper learns) is the object—the actual quantity the model adjusts during training.

the object it moves  =  Acceleration field
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Acceleration

measured quantity  =  Acceleration (force per unit mass)

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity is changing. In everyday terms: if velocity is how fast you are moving, acceleration is how quickly that speed is changing. Pressing the gas pedal is acceleration. Hitting the brakes is negative acceleration. For time-series forecasting, acceleration is what the paper measures—it is the hidden force that makes the system evolve smoothly from one state to the next.

The paper estimates acceleration by training a neural network (a learned function) to find which accelerations, when applied as forces over time, generate trajectories that match the observed data. The network never sees raw noise; it only sees the smooth aggregate motion. This means the learned acceleration field is naturally denoised.

Why it works

Why momentum fixes forecasting

Real systems have inertia: they do not jump between states; they glide. A stock price does not teleport; it drifts. Weather does not reset; it flows. When you model the system as position-plus-velocity-plus-acceleration, you are building in this gliding property. A force (exogenous input) does not move the position directly; it changes the acceleration, which slowly changes velocity, which slowly changes position. This two-step chain (force makes acceleration, which makes velocity, which makes position) is called double integration.

Double integration is also a low-pass filter: high-frequency jitter (noise) gets integrated twice and shrinks to tiny changes in position. Smooth, slow trends survive intact because they accumulate over time. The result is a forecast that is both uncertain (the model generates many possible futures) and physically plausible (each one follows real system dynamics). The neural network learns the acceleration field by comparing predictions to observed data, and it only updates the field when a mismatch is large enough to survive the double-integration filter.

The second-order update rule

First in words:

The change in position is the old velocity plus acceleration times time. The change in velocity is acceleration times time. The neural network learns acceleration as a function of position, velocity, and force.

Then the compact form:

x_new = x_old + v_old * dt + 0.5 * a * dt^2; v_new = v_old + a * dt; a = f_neural(x, v, force_input)

Every symbol:

x_oldcurrent position (the time-series value)
v_oldcurrent velocity (how fast the value is changing)
aacceleration (learned by the neural network)
dttime step size (e.g., one hour, one day)
f_neuralthe neural network that learns acceleration from state and force
force_inputthe exogenous driver (e.g., temperature, market news)
A tiny worked example
Current position x_old = 100 (e.g., stock price is 100). Current velocity v_old = 2 (rising by 2 per day). Acceleration a = 0.5 (slowly speeding up). Time step dt = 1 day.
Position change: 2 * 1 + 0.5 * 0.5^2 = 2 + 0.125 = 2.125. New x = 100 + 2.125 = 102.125.
Velocity change: 0.5 * 1 = 0.5. New v = 2 + 0.5 = 2.5 (rising faster now).
Compare to first-order (no acceleration): x_new = 100 + 2 = 102. The acceleration adds a small extra bump because the trend is accelerating. Over many steps, this captures smooth, realistic curvature.

The two integrations (acceleration to velocity to position) create the low-pass filter that suppresses noise while preserving slow trends.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Momentum lags when the system suddenly changes regime. If a stock market crashes or weather suddenly shifts, the model's learned acceleration may be slow to flip. The inertia that filters noise also resists abrupt transitions. Systems with sharp regime switches need careful training or hybrid approaches.
Acceleration field is harder to learn than position. The neural network must predict not just the next state but the rate of change of the rate of change. If the training data is noisy or short, the model may overfit to spurious accelerations or forget the learned pattern when data distribution shifts.
Tuning the momentum strength is critical. Too much momentum smooths away real volatility; too little and the noise filtering disappears. The strength of the acceleration terms (the coefficients in the equation) must match the actual inertia of the system being forecast.
Done right: use domain knowledge to set system mass. If you know the system's time-scale (e.g., stock prices move over hours, weather over days), encode that into the momentum. If you can estimate noise frequency (e.g., sensor jitter at 100 Hz), tune the filter to suppress it. This turns a liability into a strength.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple time-series generator that creates smooth real trajectories (e.g., a damped pendulum, or synthetic stock prices with momentum) plus additive high-frequency noise. Train KineFlow and a first-order flow baseline on the noisy data. Compare: (1) how close each forecast is to the ground-truth noise-free trajectory, (2) how often each generates physically implausible jumps, and (3) how much computational overhead the second integration adds. The demo should make it obvious that the extra physics pays for itself in stability.

The demo proves that momentum is not just a mathematical trick—it is built-in knowledge about how real systems behave that any forecaster can exploit to turn noise into signal.
