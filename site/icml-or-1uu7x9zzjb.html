Active-o3: Giving MLLMs the Ability to Choose Where to Look

Teach a vision-language model to pick smart regions of an image to zoom in on, using reward-based training, so it wastes less effort on irrelevant areas.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Imagine a person looking at a busy photograph. They don't study every inch equally — they pick out the important bits and focus hard there. Most vision-language models (computer programs that read both images and text) don't do this; they process the whole image at once, wasting time on distracting details. Active-o3 teaches them to learn where to look, by rewarding them for picking regions that actually matter for the task.

Zero assumption version

The problem: models look everywhere equally

Vision-language models (programs that understand both images and text together) process a whole image at once. If a photo has a tiny object buried in a crowd, the model reads millions of pixels of background noise before it ever focuses on the target. That wastes time and fails on crowded scenes. A human would zoom in on the promising region first.

A beginner might try: show the model the whole image once, then ask it to describe what it sees. But it gets lost in detail. Or hand-code which regions matter (write rules saying zoom in on faces, or cars). But that breaks the moment the task changes. What we need: let the model learn by experience which regions to zoom into, the same way humans learned to scan photos efficiently.

The paper's move in one sentence. Use reward-based training (giving points for good choices) to teach a vision-language model to decide which image regions to zoom in on and examine closely, without anyone telling it the answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: region selection decisions

The paper does not change the core vision-language model itself (that stays the same). Instead, it adds a new trainable decision-making layer that picks which regions to zoom into, and fine-tunes that layer to maximize rewards.

the object it moves  =  region selection policy
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: region informativeness

measured quantity  =  how much task-relevant information a region contains

The paper measures something new: which image regions actually carry the information you need to solve the task (find the small object, read the distant sign, etc.). A region with the target object is high-informativeness; a region of blank sky is low-informativeness.

The paper estimates this by asking: after the model zooms into this region, does it get the answer right? High accuracy on a region means that region was informative; low accuracy means it was not. The training process uses this signal (reward for good regions, penalty for wasteful ones) to teach the model's selection logic.

Why it works

Why it works: reward teaches smart region choices

The core mechanism is simple. Start with a model that picks regions randomly (no skill). Every time it picks a good region (one that helps solve the task), give it a big reward, because that region contained information needed to get the answer right. Every time it picks a bad region (wastes compute, finds nothing), give it a small reward or a penalty, because that region wasted resources without helping. The training algorithm nudges the region-selection logic to repeat the high-reward choices and avoid the low-reward ones, because mathematically the learning rule updates in the direction of higher rewards. This mirrors the way a child learns to reach for red apples after tasting sweet ones.

Why does this work? Because the task itself encodes what matters. If the task is to find a tiny insect in a photo, the model quickly learns that zooming into the green plant region pays off (reward comes back high), but zooming into sky does not. The model never sees a rule saying zoom at plants; it discovered that pattern from reward. This works on any task: object detection, reading text from a distance, autonomous driving — the reward signal always points the model toward informative regions because the task outcome is the ground truth.

Math after the picture

Equation: weighted reward for region choices

First in words:

Total training reward is the sum of task-solved-correctly reward plus efficiency reward (time spent), weighted to balance finding the answer fast.

Then the compact form:

R = w_task * reward_task(region) + w_eff * reward_efficiency(region) where reward_task is 1.0 if answer correct, 0.0 otherwise, and reward_efficiency penalizes unused computation.

Every symbol:

Rtotal reward for this region choice
reward_taskdid zooming this region lead to the right answer (1.0 yes, 0.0 no)
reward_efficiencypenalty for wasted computation time (larger penalty for regions that took long but failed)
w_taskhow much we care about getting the right answer
w_effhow much we care about being fast (not wasting tokens on irrelevant regions)
A tiny worked example
Region A: found the target insect, took 4 tokens. reward_task = 1.0, reward_efficiency = 0.9 (fast). R = 0.8 * 1.0 + 0.2 * 0.9 = 0.98 (strong reward).
Region B: found nothing useful, took 2 tokens. reward_task = 0.0, reward_efficiency = 0.95 (very fast but pointless). R = 0.8 * 0.0 + 0.2 * 0.95 = 0.19 (weak reward).
Region C: found nothing, took 6 tokens. reward_task = 0.0, reward_efficiency = 0.3 (slow waste). R = 0.8 * 0.0 + 0.2 * 0.3 = 0.06 (tiny reward).
Training learns to pick regions like A (high reward) and avoid regions like C (waste of time).

The two-part reward (correctness plus speed) mirrors how humans learn to scan: we zoom into promising regions fast, and ignore distracting areas entirely.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward signal is weak or ambiguous. If the task itself is unclear (maybe the target object is borderline visible), the model receives mixed rewards (sometimes 1.0, sometimes 0.0 from similar regions). Training becomes noisy and slow. The model cannot learn a clear strategy.
Regions are too coarse or too fine. If the model can only zoom into huge chunks, it still sees lots of clutter. If regions are tiny pixels, it misses context. The zoom granularity must fit the task; the paper finds this through ablation.
Model overfits to specific benchmark regions. If training only shows objects in the center, the model learns to zoom center. Then on a real photo with an object in the corner, it fails. Reward training requires diverse data.
Done right: use task-derived reward on realistic data. Train on a variety of images and target locations. Let the reward signal (whether the zoomed region helped or not) be the sole teacher. The model generalizes to new images because it learned the underlying principle: informative regions help, uninformative ones do not.
