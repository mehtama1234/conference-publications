Entropy

Measures how spread out probabilities are across possible outcomes.

In plain terms

Imagine you want to know whether a coin is fair or rigged. A fair coin shows heads exactly half the time and tails exactly half the time. A rigged coin might come up heads 99 percent of the time and tails 1 percent. If you ask which one is more predictable, the answer is the rigged one: you can guess heads almost every time and be right. The fair coin keeps surprising you.

This is what entropy measures: how surprised or uncertain you should be before you see each outcome. A distribution (a list of probabilities for each outcome) where one outcome is almost certain has low entropy because you will not be surprised often. A distribution where all outcomes are equally likely has high entropy because you are surprised just as much no matter what happens. Entropy is a number that captures this degree of surprise built into the probability distribution.

The equation
H(p)=-\sum_i p_i \log p_i
H(p)the entropy, a single number that measures the total surprise in the distribution
p_ithe probability of outcome i, a number between zero and one
log p_ithe logarithm of the probability, which grows slowly as probabilities shrink
−the negative sign, which flips the sum to make entropy always positive
Worked example
Fair coin vs rigged coin
Fair: p=[0.5, 0.5]. log(0.5)≈−0.30 for each.
Sum: −0.5(−0.30) − 0.5(−0.30) = 0.30
Rigged: p=[0.99, 0.01]. log(0.99)≈−0.005, log(0.01)≈−4.6
Sum: −0.99(−0.005) − 0.01(−4.6) = 0.05 + 0.046 = 0.096
Fair coin entropy ≈ 0.69 bits. Rigged coin ≈ 0.08 bits.
Fair is much more uncertain.
Why it shows up

Entropy shows up everywhere in machine learning because models must handle distributions over many possibilities. When training a language model, the model produces probabilities over thousands of words. Entropy tells you whether the model is confident in its answer or genuinely uncertain between many words equally. When building generative models, you want to sample from distributions that have the right amount of spread: too concentrated and you get boring repetition, too spread out and you get gibberish. Entropy measures exactly this trade-off. In information theory, entropy measures how many bits you need on average to encode an outcome from that distribution, so models that produce low-entropy outputs are easier to compress. Across diffusion models, alignment methods, representation learning, and any place where the model outputs probabilities, entropy quantifies how much surprise the distribution contains.
