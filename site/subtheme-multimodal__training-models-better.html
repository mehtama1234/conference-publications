Training models better

Using new techniques like reinforcement learning and preference optimization to train models that follow human judgment more closely.

What this is

Why train models better? Because standard training methods break down when there is no single right answer, which is the reality of multimodal tasks. For most of the history of machine learning, training has been straightforward: show the model examples, it makes predictions, compare the prediction to labeled ground truth, and adjust the model to match the right answer. This works fine when there is one objectively right answer. But real-world multimodal tasks do not have a single right answer. A caption can be accurate but vague, or detailed but focusing on the wrong thing. A question can be answered correctly but unhelpfully. Different people would judge quality differently — one person wants brevity, another wants detail, a third wants emotional resonance. A single labeled correct answer cannot capture this variation. Standard training methods fail in this situation because they force a choice that does not exist. So researchers are developing better training methods — methods that work by learning from human preference and judgment, not from a single labeled answer, which means the model can produce outputs that different humans would actually prefer. Reinforcement learning and preference-based learning try to do this by letting humans rank or score different outputs, and then training the model to produce outputs that humans consistently prefer.

In the context of multimodal models, this is especially useful. Suppose your image captioning model outputs 'a dog sitting.' Is that right? Well, maybe — it's accurate, but the ground truth might be 'a tan dog sitting on a green grass lawn.' The first caption is not wrong, but the second is more detailed and specific. Or suppose your visual question answering model answers 'yes' to 'Is there a cat in the image?' when the image shows a cat but it's far in the background and blurry. Technically correct, but maybe not what the user wanted. Preference learning lets you say: I prefer detailed captions to vague ones, or I prefer answers that acknowledge uncertainty when the image is ambiguous. The model learns not just to get the right answer, but to get it in the right way.

The tools for this include reinforcement learning, where the model is rewarded for behaviors you like and discouraged from behaviors you don't. It also includes preference optimization, a newer technique where the model directly learns from pairs of outputs — 'I like this caption better than that one' — without needing an explicit reward function. This works because the model learns to chase the kinds of outputs humans prefer, which is more efficient than guessing at a reward formula. Some research applies these methods specifically to multimodal tasks, like training a model to generate image descriptions that humans consistently pick as better. Humans prefer detailed, natural-sounding descriptions that actually capture what's in the image because those are more useful for understanding the picture, so training the model to match those preferences makes it more practical. Other applications include training to avoid hallucinating details in visual question answering. The result is a model that is not just accurate but aligned with what humans actually want.

How the work breaks down

Training multimodal models better splits into five main directions, because different tasks need different approaches. Building better reward models lets you judge multimodal outputs the way humans would, which is essential because human judgment is the ground truth. Using reinforcement learning on structured tasks helps when there is a clear right form (like converting charts to code), because the model can then learn from feedback about whether the output actually works. Adding reasoning and thinking capabilities to vision models helps them solve complex visual problems because decomposing reasoning into steps makes hard problems tractable. Directly optimizing models to match human preferences avoids needing intermediate reward functions, which saves complexity because you skip one layer of training. Training specialized models for specific modalities like video, audio, and speech works better because each modality has unique properties that require tailored methods.

Building Reward Models for Multimodal Tasks

Reward models are a foundation for modern training: they learn to score outputs the way humans would, so you can use those scores to improve the main model. In multimodal tasks this is especially hard because there are so many ways an output can be good or bad. An image caption can be detailed or brief, accurate or creative, formal or casual. A reward model needs to capture these nuances and agree with human judges. The challenge is that building a reward model requires labeled data where humans rank or score different outputs, and collecting enough diverse examples takes time and money.

BaseReward tackles this by creating a strong foundation model for multimodal reward scoring. It shows that a well-trained reward model can score image-text pairs, video-text pairs, and other multimodal outputs reliably, and when you plug this into a full reinforcement learning pipeline, the main model actually gets better at the tasks humans care about because it now has trustworthy feedback. Importance Sampling for Multi-Negative Multimodal Direct Preference Optimization improves on this by being smarter about which examples to learn from: instead of treating all rejected outputs the same, it samples hard negatives that are close to the right answer, which teaches the model more efficiently because it focuses on the boundary cases where the model is uncertain. Omni-Reward goes further and builds a general reward model that works across all modalities at once—text, images, audio, video—in a single model, which means one reward model can guide training for many different types of tasks because it shares knowledge across them. R1-Reward focuses on training reward models through stable reinforcement learning, showing that the way you optimize the reward model itself matters because if you train it carefully, using multiple independent reward signals, it becomes more reliable and generalizes better to new types of multimodal tasks.

Reinforcement Learning for Structured Outputs

Some multimodal tasks have a clear right form: a chart should be converted to runnable code, a scientific diagram should be parsed into its structured components, and those structures should match what experts would extract. Standard supervised learning often hits a ceiling on these tasks because the training data cannot capture all the ways to do it right, or because small mistakes in form break the downstream code. Reinforcement learning can help because the model is rewarded for producing outputs that actually work or are judged correct by human evaluators, not just for matching one labeled example, so it learns what actually matters.

Breaking the SFT Plateau: Multimodal Structured Reinforcement Learning for Chart-to-Code Generation shows that supervised learning plateaus on chart-to-code tasks, but reinforcement learning breaks through that ceiling. When the reward is 'did the code run without error and produce the right result,' the model learns to be more careful about syntax and structure because mistakes matter. DaVinci tackles scientific diagram parsing by reinforcing models to understand visual-structural syntax—the way diagrams are organized visually, which parts connect, what the notation means—using reinforcement learning to reward correct parses. Chart Deep Research in LVLMs via Parallel Relative Policy Optimization speeds up the reinforcement learning process itself by running multiple reward calculations in parallel, so the model can learn from more examples per unit of time, and also improves how you measure whether the model is actually getting better by focusing on errors it makes uniquely.

Reasoning and Thinking in Vision

Vision models can be limited if they process an image and immediately output an answer. More complex problems—visual reasoning, understanding charts, solving math from diagrams—often need the model to think through the image step by step, ask itself clarifying questions, and piece together an answer. Adding reasoning capability means the model learns to break down the visual problem, explain its thinking, and use that reasoning to guide the final output. This requires training that rewards good reasoning, not just good final answers.

Self-Rewarding Vision-Language Model via Reasoning Decomposition and Multi-Reward Policy Optimization teaches a model to break down visual reasoning into steps and reward each step, so the model learns that taking time to reason pays off because each step gets validated. SophiaVL-R1 does this with 'thinking reward,' which trains the model to internally track and update what it knows as it looks at the image, similar to how humans think about what they see. The model learns that high-quality thinking—where it adjusts its beliefs as it gathers evidence—leads to better answers, so it invests in that thinking during inference because investing in reasoning is explicitly rewarded. Mini-o3: Scaling Up Reasoning Patterns and Interaction Turns for Visual Search shows that if you train a model with a budget of reasoning turns, it learns when to use those turns wisely, and at test time it can use even more reasoning turns if it wants, achieving better results than models that were simply trained on the final answer because the model has learned where reasoning helps.

Direct Preference Optimization and Alignment

Preference optimization is a newer approach to training: instead of having a reward model that scores outputs, you directly learn from pairs where humans say 'I prefer output A over output B.' The model learns to imitate those preferences without needing an explicit reward formula. This is often simpler and more stable than full reinforcement learning, because you avoid the complexity of designing and training a separate reward model. The challenge is that human preferences can be inconsistent or depend on subtle context, especially in multimodal tasks where visual and textual cues interact.

Beyond Fixed Biases: Decoding the Role of Reasoning Uncertainty in MLLM Modality Conflicts addresses a core problem: when vision and language cues disagree, the model may waver between trusting vision or text, or get confused about which to weight more. Preference steering through targeted fine-tuning on boundary cases—images where the answer is genuinely ambiguous—teaches the model to make consistent, reasonable choices because it learns from the hardest examples. CFPO: Counterfactual Policy Optimization for Multimodal Reasoning improves preference learning by using counterfactual reasoning: if you change a detail in the image or question, the model should change its answer in a predictable way. Training on these counterfactual pairs teaches the model to be sensitive to relevant details and not just memorize answers because it must generalize to slight variations. Empowering Small VLMs to Think with Dynamic Memorization and Exploration stabilizes the tradeoff between supervised learning and reinforcement learning because when a small model learns from preferences, it can forget what supervised training taught it, so this work uses dynamic memory to hold onto important learned behaviors while still improving from preferences. ViSurf: Visual Supervised-and-Reinforcement Fine-Tuning for Large Vision-and-Language Models combines supervised learning and preference optimization in a principled way, so the model gets the stability of supervised learning and the improvement from preferences, without forgetting older skills because it integrates both methods carefully.

Specialized Training for Audio and Video

Multimodal training for audio and video has special challenges. Video models need to understand how things change over time and track beliefs about the world. Audio and speech models need to hear not just what is said, but how it is said—emotion, tone, style. These modalities require training methods that work with temporal sequences and acoustic details. Standard methods from vision and text often do not transfer well.

SPIKE-RL: Video-LLMs meet Bayesian Surprise teaches video models to track uncertainty: as the model watches a video, it updates its beliefs about what is happening, and when something surprising occurs—contrary to its prediction—that surprise signal helps it learn because unexpected events are the most informative for updating beliefs. This trains the model to notice important changes and transitions because surprises highlight where the world violates predictions. TTS Can Speak in Any Style with Any Voice tackles the problem that text-to-speech models need to clone voice and control style, which are separate dimensions: a model needs to sound like one speaker but with calm or excited tone. This paper uses training that disentangles these factors so the model can independently control voice identity and speaking style because treating them separately is more efficient than treating them as one bundle. Can Speech LLMs Think while Listening improves speech understanding by having the model reason in text space as it listens—converting what it hears to text internally and reasoning about meaning—which boosts accuracy because reasoning in a more abstract representation helps with understanding, and using reinforcement learning on reasoning quality (via DPO) reduces latency because it learns to reason efficiently. EmotionThinker: Prosody-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Explainable Speech Emotion Reasoning trains a model to ground its emotion predictions in fine-grained acoustic details—prosody—so the model does not just guess but can explain why it thinks a speaker is happy or sad, based on pitch, timing, and energy because being grounded in real acoustic features makes the predictions trustworthy. Echo: Towards Advanced Audio Comprehension via Audio-Interleaved Reasoning interspersesreasoning steps throughout audio processing: as the model listens, it periodically pauses to think about what it has heard so far, rather than waiting until the end, which improves understanding of long or complex audio because pausing lets the model consolidate what it has learned so far rather than overloading memory.
