LAGEA: Language-Guided Embodied Agents

A robot learns to avoid mistakes by reading language descriptions of what went wrong, grounded step-by-step in video, as reward signals shaped adaptively.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots learning manipulation are usually given a single success-or-failure score per attempt. LAGEA asks: what if the robot could read a language explanation of exactly where and why it failed? The paper shows that when a vision-language model (a model trained to read images and generate text) writes a short structured summary of each failed attempt—like "grasped wrong spot, hand collided"—and the robot learns to align that text with the exact moment it happened in the video, the robot starts learning from mistakes instead of random exploration. The result: 9% better success on standard robotic tasks, because credit assignment becomes possible—the robot now knows which action to change.

Zero assumption version

Robots get weak signals about mistakes

A robot learning to manipulate objects—picking, placing, pushing—usually trains by trying something and getting a score: 1 for success, 0 for failure. That binary signal is all it has to learn from. The problem: that score tells you whether you succeeded, but not what you did wrong.

A beginner's fix: just feed the robot every frame of video and hope it learns to spot mistakes visually. It does not work well because video is high-dimensional noise—the robot cannot easily find the critical moment when things went sideways. The signal drowns in irrelevance.

The paper's move: Turn a language description of what went wrong into a dense reward signal that shows up exactly at the steps where the failure happened.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

After each attempt, invoke a vision-language model

Ask it to read the video and write what went wrong, in structured JSON form.

Pick the critical moments

Scan the video for frames where the robot was closest to the goal or moving fast—the moments that mattered.

Encode the text and align it to those frames

Convert the language summary into a list of numbers, teach a neural net to match frames to that summary.

Compute frame-by-frame bonus rewards

Boost reward at frames where language aligned to visual state tightly, because high-saliency frames get high weight and irrelevant frames get low weight, so the signal concentrates where it matters most.

Fade the bonus as the robot improves

Early on, language guidance is loud; as success climbs, shrink the bonus so the robot relies on real task reward.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a new source of reward: adaptive feedback-driven bonuses, shaped step-by-step across each episode and faded over training as the policy learns.

the object it moves  =  step-wise reward shaping
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frame importance

measured quantity  =  temporal saliency of each step

The paper measures which frames in the video actually mattered—the ones where the robot was close to success, moving decisively, or at a critical boundary. This is frame importance, or temporal saliency. High-saliency frames are the moments where the outcome was decided.

The paper estimates it by computing how close the robot was to the goal at each frame (goal-proximity), how fast that proximity was changing (temporal derivative), and how sharp the turn was. Frames that rank high in this saliency signal are the ones the method uses to align the language feedback.

Why it works

Why it works: feedback anchored in time

Here is the causal chain. A robot tries to grasp a block and fails. A vision-language model watches the video and outputs: 'Grasped the wrong part of the object; hand was too high; collision at frame 18.' That text is a diagnosis. But for a reinforcement learning agent, text alone is noise.

The paper bridges the gap by asking: when was that collision? Frame 18. Which frames matter most for learning? The ones where the outcome turned—measured by saliency. Now: align the text to those frames. Teach a neural network to recognize that frames around moment 18 match the diagnosis 'wrong grasp, collision.' When they align, give a reward bump at frame 18. Why does this work? Because now the robot's learning algorithm can pinpoint: at frame 18, the hand position caused the collision. Next attempt, adjust the hand. Without the temporal anchor, the text diagnosis is too abstract—all 50 frames share the blame equally, and the signal is noise. With the anchor, one frame stands out. Credit assignment becomes possible.

As the policy learns and success rate climbs, the reward bonus fades. Early on, the robot needs the loud signal to explore correctly. Later, the real task reward (grasp success) is all it needs. This adaptive fade prevents the language reward from becoming a crutch—it pulls out as the robot grows competent.

Math after the picture

The reward shaping equation

First in words:

Total reward at step t equals the base task reward plus a feedback reward delta, scaled by an adaptive coefficient that gates the signal and a temporal weight that highlights critical frames.

Then the compact form:

r_t = r_task_t + rho_t * w_hat_t * r_tilde_t

Every symbol:

r_tTotal reward given to the robot at step t
r_task_tThe base task reward (usually 1 for success, 0 otherwise)
rho_tAdaptive coefficient (starts high, fades toward 1 as policy improves)
w_hat_tTemporal weight from saliency (high when step t is critical)
r_tilde_tFeedback reward delta (1 if text aligns with step t, 0 otherwise)
A tiny worked example
Suppose an episode has 50 steps. At step 18, the hand collision happens.
Saliency identifies step 18 as critical: w_hat_18 = 0.9 (high).
Vision-language model text aligns with step 18 in the embedding space: r_tilde_18 = 1.0.
Early training: rho_t = 3.0 (signal is loud).
Step 18 receives: r_18 = 0 (failed attempt) + 3.0 * 0.9 * 1.0 = 2.7 bonus reward.
Step 20 (not critical): w_hat_20 = 0.1, r_tilde_20 = 0 → gets 0 bonus, only base task reward.
Late training: rho_t = 1.0 (signal fades).
Step 18 now gets: 0 + 1.0 * 0.9 * 1.0 = 0.9 bonus (much smaller), and the policy relies on improving the base task reward instead.

The equation shows why the method works: the text feedback is only loud at the frames that matter (high w_hat_t) and only when saliency thinks the frame caused the outcome; early noise is damped, but later training is not hijacked by the language reward.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Vision-language model hallucinates or misreads. If the VLM generates incorrect diagnoses (e.g., blames the wrong moment), the alignment step will pair the text to misguided frames, and the policy learns the wrong lesson because the bonus lands on the wrong action. Mitigation: enforce schema-constrained JSON output and audit the model's claims.
Keyframe saliency misses the true critical moment. If saliency peaks at frame 30 but the actual collision happened at frame 18, the reward bonus lands on the wrong frame, because the policy then learns that frame-30 actions avoid collision when in fact frame-18 actions do. The temporal derivative must be precise to correctly locate the crux.
Reward hijacking: language bonus becomes a crutch. If the adaptive coefficient rho_t does not fade fast enough as the policy learns, the policy may learn to satisfy the language reward signal rather than actually complete the task, because the language bonus is louder than the real task signal. The fade schedule must shrink the bonus aggressively so it recedes faster than policy competence grows.
Alignment fails on unexpected failure modes. If the policy learns a wildly different failure mode than those seen during training, the VLM text will not align well to the new frames, because the neural net was trained only on old failure patterns. The alignment model must be broad enough to handle new mistakes.
Done right. The method works when the VLM is accurate, saliency peaks at the true crux, the alignment is tight, and the fade schedule is conservative—the language signal accelerates but never hijacks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robotic pick-and-place task in simulation. The robot sees the scene as a small image, chooses an action (grasp location and lift height), and gets a success score. Run two policies in parallel: one trained on task reward alone, the other augmented with language feedback as LAGEA describes. After the same number of environment steps, measure which one has learned faster and reached higher success, because the language-guided one had timed feedback that pointed to the right action to fix. Log a few failed episodes, show the VLM's language diagnoses, and highlight the frames where saliency peaked and the bonus was applied. A reader should see: language explanation plus the exact moment it pinpointed in the video corresponds exactly to where the bonus appeared in the reward curve.

success_rate = (episodes succeeded / total episodes attempted) measured every 50k environment steps

The demo must make it impossible to miss: LAGEA with language reaches high success with fewer attempted episodes than the baseline, because the feedback-guided bonus steers exploration to the right part of the state space early on.
