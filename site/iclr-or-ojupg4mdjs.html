Wasserstein Geodesic Principal Component Analysis

Instead of squashing probability distributions into points, find the curved paths through distribution space that best describe how your data varies.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) finds the straight lines that capture how a dataset varies. But real datasets often live on curved surfaces, not flat spaces. This paper asks: what if your data points are not single numbers but entire probability distributions? Then the straight lines do not exist—you need curved paths instead. This paper shows how to find those curves using the Wasserstein distance, a way of measuring how much mass must move to transform one distribution into another.

Zero assumption version

Squishing distributions into flat space breaks what makes them different

When you have a collection of probability distributions—say, images of chairs with varying shapes, or digit images that morph from 1 to 2—and you want to find the main patterns, the standard trick is to flatten everything into a list of numbers and run PCA. But a probability distribution is not a list of numbers; it is a shape, a hill over the space of possibilities. Flattening loses the structure.

The naive approach: pick a single list of numbers to represent each distribution (compress it), then run standard PCA on those lists. This works when the distributions sit on a flat surface. But when they curve—when going from one distribution to another involves a bend or a spiral—the flat lines you find miss the real paths the data follows.

In one sentence. The paper finds true curved paths through distribution space without flattening it.

What the paper changes

The thing being changed: geodesic paths through distribution space

Instead of fitting straight lines to compressed representations, the paper directly optimizes curved paths that live in Wasserstein space. Two neural networks encode each path: one transforms the base probability shape, and another encodes a velocity field. Together they trace the curve, trained to minimize the sum of squared distances from each distribution to the nearest point on the curve.

the object it moves  =  Geodesic curves in distribution space
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: projection distance from each distribution to the geodesic

measured quantity  =  Distance from data to geodesic

For each distribution in your dataset, you measure how far it sits from the learned curved path. This is the Wasserstein distance from the distribution to its nearest point on the geodesic. Small distances mean the geodesic captured the variation well.

The paper estimates this by computing the Wasserstein distance from each distribution in your sample to every candidate point on the learned curve, then taking the minimum. During training, the algorithm adjusts the neural network parameters to minimize the sum of these minimal distances, pulling the curve toward the data.

Why it works

Why it works: staying in curved space avoids distortion

The old method—compress each distribution into a vector, then run PCA on vectors—introduces distortion. The compression throws away information about the shape of the distribution, and then PCA finds lines that are optimal for the compressed vectors, not the original distributions. The new method sidesteps this by never leaving Wasserstein space.

For Gaussian distributions, the paper lifts the problem into the space of invertible linear maps, where geodesics have a closed form. For more general distributions, it uses neural networks to learn a parameterization of the geodesic directly. Two neural networks work together: one learns to transform points in a base distribution, and another learns a potential function that defines a velocity field. Together they trace out the true shortest path in Wasserstein space. Because the path is optimized in the original space, it captures real variations without distortion. And because geodesics are true shortest paths, the first component points along the direction the data actually varies most.

Math after the picture

The optimization: minimize projections onto the curve

First in words:

sum over all distributions of the squared minimum Wasserstein distance from each distribution to any point on the first geodesic curve

Then the compact form:

minimize sum_i [min_t W(mu_i, mu(t))^2] where W is Wasserstein distance, mu_i are data distributions, mu(t) is the geodesic path

Every symbol:

mu_ithe i-th distribution in your dataset
mu(t)the geodesic curve at parameter value t, where t ranges from 0 to 1
W(mu_i, mu(t))the Wasserstein distance, which measures how much mass must move to transform mu_i into mu(t)
min_tover all possible positions t on the curve, find the closest point to mu_i
sum_iadd up the squared distances for all distributions in the dataset
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have three distributions: A, B, C.
The learned geodesic passes through them at t = 0, 0.5, 1.
Distance from A to nearest point on curve: W(A, mu(0))^2 = 2.0.
Distance from B to nearest point on curve: W(B, mu(0.48))^2 = 0.1 (B is close to the curve at t=0.48).
Distance from C to nearest point on curve: W(C, mu(1))^2 = 1.5.
Total loss: 2.0 + 0.1 + 1.5 = 3.6.
Training adjusts the neural network parameters to reduce this sum, pulling the curve closer to all distributions.

The sum of squared distances penalizes outliers, pulling the geodesic toward the densest region of the data, just like standard PCA pulls a line toward clustered points.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Ill-conditioned distributions. If your distributions are very spread out or have heavy tails, the Wasserstein distance itself becomes hard to compute accurately. The learned geodesic will be unreliable.
Not enough data. Neural networks need many distributions to learn a good parameterization of the curve. With 10 or 20 distributions, overfitting is likely.
High dimensional base space. When each distribution is defined over a very high dimensional space (e.g. images), estimating Wasserstein distance becomes computationally expensive and numerically unstable.
Done right: curated datasets, sufficient samples. The method works well on well-characterized distributions with at least 50–100 samples per dataset and modest dimensionality.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic dataset where you know the geodesics by construction: sample 100 distributions uniformly along a known curved path in the space of 2D Gaussians. Then run the algorithm and check that it recovers the curve accurately. Plot the recovered geodesic against the ground truth and measure the Hausdorff distance between them. Then do the same on a real dataset—MNIST digits morphing from 1 to 2—and verify that the interpolations along the learned geodesic are smooth, visually coherent, and that the projection distance correctly identifies outliers.

Recovery accuracy = 1 - (Hausdorff distance between recovered and true geodesic) / (max pairwise distance in space)

The demo must make clear that the learned curve passes through the densest regions of the data and that you can sample smoothly anywhere along it, not just at the original datapoints.
