APPLE: General Active Perception via RL

Train a robot to decide what to sense next by jointly learning where to look and what it sees, using rewards instead of hand-written rules.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot touching an object only feels one small patch at a time, so it must choose where to explore next. Most methods tell the robot exactly how to explore (look for the shape, maximize information gain), but this locks them to one task. APPLE learns a decision policy and a perception module together from reward signals, letting the same robot solve grasping, pose estimation, classification, or anything else.

Zero assumption version

Robots see one tiny piece at a time

A robot's gripper has a touch sensor smaller than your fingertip. When it explores an object, each contact reads only that one spot, not the whole shape. The robot must decide: move left? right? press harder? Current methods solve this by hand-coding the goal (find the shape, maximize information gain for grasping), so each robot program is built for one job.

A beginner would try: let the robot randomly poke until it learns. This fails because random poking takes thousands of touches and never finds a strategy—the robot wastes time on useless spots. The real gap is that no method lets one robot solve multiple tasks by learning what to sense from rewards alone.

The core problem. Current active perception methods are locked to one task with hand-coded objectives; they do not learn a general policy for deciding where to sense next.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Observe current state

The perception module reads all touches collected so far and predicts the object's properties (shape, pose, class).

Step 2: Policy chooses next move

The decision policy looks at the current prediction and outputs the best sensor move (direction, force, contact point) using reinforcement learning.

Step 3: Execute and sense

The robot makes that move, the sensor reads a new patch, and the environment gives a reward (progress toward the goal).

Step 4: Update both modules

Both the perception module and policy learn from the new observation and reward via a shared neural network backbone, using one loss function.

Step 5: Loop until task solved

Repeat steps 1-4 until the robot's prediction is accurate enough to grasp, estimate pose, or classify correctly.

What the paper changes

What changes: the joint training objective

APPLE does not hand-code a task-specific objective like maximize information gain. Instead, it treats active perception as a reinforcement learning problem: the policy learns to move the sensor by maximizing a reward signal, and the perception module learns to predict from the sensor history. Both modules share a deep neural network backbone (which reads sequences of sensor touches) and train together on the same reward.

the object it moves  =  joint RL-perception loss
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: expected future reward

measured quantity  =  expected future reward per move

As the robot explores, each sensor move opens different futures. Some moves lead to fast, accurate predictions; others waste time. The policy must learn which move is most valuable right now—that is, which move will bring the highest total reward across all remaining steps. This is the expected future reward, and the paper estimates it by running episodes and measuring how quickly each move sequence reaches the goal.

The policy learns to rank moves by their expected future value using a model based on a transformer architecture (which reads a sequence of sensor touches and outputs predictions). High-value moves (those that narrow down the object's properties fastest) get chosen first. The perception module feeds into this ranking by outputting the current best guess, which helps the policy decide what to sense next.

Why it works

Why it works: shared learning breaks the task lock

Old methods tie the robot to one task by hand-coding the objective (find shape, maximize information gain). APPLE breaks this lock by learning from reward signals instead. Because both the perception module and policy train on the same transformer backbone and use the same reward, they learn to cooperate: the perception module gets better at reading the sensor data, and the policy gets better at choosing moves that maximize the shared reward.

This generality comes from abstraction. The neural network backbone learns to represent sensor history in a flexible way—not optimized for shape or pose or class, but for whatever the reward signals (the task goal) demands. The policy then learns to steer exploration by this flexible representation. Change the reward from grasping to pose estimation to classification, and the same trained modules adapt because they learned a general strategy: gather high-value information.

Math after the picture

The policy gradient equation

First in words:

change to policy weights equals expected value of (log likelihood of chosen move times cumulative discounted future reward)

Then the compact form:

dtheta_policy = E[(dtheta log pi(a|o)) * sum_t gamma^t reward_t]

Every symbol:

dtheta_policywhich direction to nudge the policy weights to increase total reward
pi(a|o)probability the policy chooses move a given current observation o
dtheta log pi(a|o)which way to nudge weights so this move becomes more likely
gammadiscount factor (typically 0.99), weights immediate rewards more than far-future ones
reward_tthe signal at step t (how close we are to solving the task)
sum_t gamma^t reward_ttotal future reward from now on, with older rewards weighted less
A tiny worked example
Suppose the policy chooses move Right at step 0, and the robot then gets rewards: step 0 = +1.0 (small progress), step 1 = +2.0 (bigger progress), step 2 = +1.5 (slight progress). Cumulative (gamma=0.99): 1.0 + 0.99*2.0 + 0.99^2*1.5 = 1.0 + 1.98 + 1.48 = 4.46.
If log probability of Right is +0.5 (the policy was confident), the gradient is 0.5 * 4.46 = 2.23. This nudges the weights to make Right even more likely in similar future situations.
If a different move Up had been tried with the same observation and gotten total reward 2.0, then gradient for Up is 0.5 * 2.0 = 1.0—the weights pull less strongly toward Up, because it yielded lower total reward.

The policy learns to increase the probability of high-reward moves and decrease the probability of low-reward moves, automatically trading off exploration and exploitation without hand-coded heuristics.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Sensor gets stuck in local optima. If the robot learns a lazy exploration strategy early (poke only in one spot), the policy may not explore enough to solve harder tasks. Early random exploration or a curriculum (start with simple tasks) helps because random moves keep the policy from settling into a dead-end strategy, and easy tasks let the policy find a good foundation before facing complexity.
Reward signal too sparse or unclear. If the reward only fires at the very end (binary: solved or not), the policy takes too long to learn which moves help. Shaping the reward (give small +0.1 for progress) accelerates learning because frequent, small rewards act like stepping stones—the policy can see which moves nudge it closer to the goal right away, instead of waiting hundreds of steps for one big signal.
Perception module overfits to training objects. If the robot trains only on wooden blocks, it may fail on rubber or cloth because the touch signal shifts. Training on diverse materials helps because the perception module must learn which features matter (stiffness, texture, shape) across many surfaces, so it builds a more general predictor that works on unseen materials.
Done right. With a well-shaped reward, diverse training data, and enough exploration noise, APPLE learns a policy that works on new tasks and new objects it has not seen before.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Implement APPLE on a simulated tactile robot (or the Tactile MNIST benchmark, a standard test set where the goal is to classify digits 0-9 by touch alone). Train the policy and perception module jointly for 10,000 episodes. Then swap the reward signal from classification (maximize correct digit predictions) to pose estimation (minimize angle error), and fine-tune for 1,000 episodes. The demo should show that the same robot policy solves both tasks, adapting its exploration strategy purely from the new reward, proving that APPLE breaks the task lock.

task_generality = (accuracy_task_A after training on task_A + accuracy_task_B after fine_tuning on task_B) / 2, compared to task-specific baselines on each task separately

The core proof: one robot, one learned policy, two different tasks—achieved by removing hand-coded objectives and letting reward signals teach exploration strategy.
