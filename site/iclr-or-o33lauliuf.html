Score Smoothing Makes Diffusion Models Interpolate

Diffusion models become creative by learning smoothed score functions, which make the denoising process draw samples between training points instead of copying them.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Diffusion models (systems that turn noise into data by repeatedly denoising) are surprisingly creative — they make new data unlike anything in the training set. This paper shows why: when you train a neural network to estimate the direction toward real data at each noise level, the network naturally learns a smoothed version of this direction. This smoothing acts like a gentle blur that spreads out the training data, so the denoising process can fill in the gaps and create entirely new samples.

The mystery of creative generation

Diffusion models work in a simple way: start with pure noise and make it less noisy, step by step, by nudging it toward where real data lives. The puzzle is that they create new data not in the training set. A naive approach would be to just memorize every training sample and output one of them when asked — no true creativity at all. But something in how the neural network learns prevents sharp memorization, instead allowing the model to fill in gaps between training points.

The question the paper addresses is: what allows diffusion models to generate samples that lie between the training data rather than copying them exactly? Understanding this matters because if models could only memorize, they would be useless for creating truly new data. This paper shows the answer lies in how neural networks naturally smooth out the learned map of where data lives.

The mechanism. Neural networks learning score functions naturally smooth out sharp edges, which turns memorization into interpolation.

What the paper changes

What changes: the score function

The paper changes how the score function (the learned map of which direction to move at each noise level) gets smoothed. Instead of a sharp, jagged map that peaks exactly at training data, regularized networks learn a blurred version. This single change — making the map smooth instead of spiky — ripples through the entire denoising process.

the object it moves  =  score function smoothness
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: smoothness penalty

measured quantity  =  non-smoothness of the score function

The paper measures how 'spiky' the learned score function is — a quantity called the non-smoothness penalty. A sharp map (one that jumps between data points) has high non-smoothness. A blurred, gentle map has low non-smoothness.

The paper shows that weight decay (a common technique: making the network's weights small) is mathematically equivalent to penalizing this non-smoothness. So training with weight decay naturally pushes the network toward learning a smooth version of the score function, not a spiky one.

Paper reading. The paper analyzes a variational problem involving score matching loss and a non-smoothness measure tied to the weight norm of neural networks. It proves that regularizing weight norm (weight decay) is equivalent to penalizing the non-smoothness measure.
Why it works

Why it works: the interplay of smoothing and denoising

When you train a neural network to estimate where real data is (the score function), you want it to point toward the training data. But a network trained on a finite dataset would naturally learn a map with sharp peaks exactly at each training point — that would be the most accurate. Weight regularization (penalizing large weights) biases the network against creating these sharp peaks. Instead, the network spreads out its signal into gentle hills centered near the training data.

Now, when denoising starts from random noise and follows this blurred map, something interesting happens. The gentle slope does not point straight at any single training point. Instead, it points toward the middle ground between nearby training points. So as the denoising process unfolds, samples are nudged to land in the valleys between the training data, not directly on it. This is interpolation, not memorization. The paper proves this effect holds analytically for data on a line, and confirms it with real experiments on circles and more complex shapes.

Math after the picture

The equation: score matching with smoothness cost

First in words:

total cost equals how well you predict the score plus a penalty for non-smoothness

Then the compact form:

loss = score_matching_loss + lambda * non_smoothness_penalty

Every symbol:

score_matching_losserror in predicting which direction points toward real data
lambdahow much to weight the smoothness penalty; larger lambda means smoother learned function
non_smoothness_penaltya measure of how spiky the network function is; related to the weight norm of the network
A tiny worked example
Suppose at one training point the raw (unsmoothed) score function would be 100 (a sharp peak).
With regularization (lambda = 0.5), the penalty term discourages this spike.
The network learns a smoother solution: instead of a peak of 100, it spreads the signal into gentler peaks of 30 nearby, and 0 far away.
This small change in how the network predicts direction has a large effect downstream: denoising now reaches the gaps instead of the peaks.

The equation shows that weight decay biases the network toward learning a smooth score function, which makes denoising interpolate instead of memorize.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too much smoothing collapses the data. If you smooth too aggressively (very large lambda), the score function becomes so blurred that it no longer points toward the training data at all. The denoising process then spreads samples too widely and loses the training signal.
Regularization is implicit, not explicit. The paper shows that gradient-based training of neural networks has implicit regularization effects (the network smooths even without weight decay). If you use an optimizer that avoids this implicit effect, the smoothing may disappear and memorization can return.
The effect is strongest in low-dimensional subspaces. The theoretical results assume the training data lie on a low-dimensional subspace (like a line or circle). In very high-dimensional spaces, the interpolation effect weakens because there are many more directions to explore.
Done right: tune the smoothing strength. If you choose lambda (the regularization weight) carefully — not too large, not too small — the network learns to interpolate smoothly between training points while still preserving the signal about where the data lives. This is the sweet spot.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A working demo would train a diffusion model on a small set of training points in 2-D space (say, four points at the corners of a square). Then, run the denoising process from different random starting points and visualize where the samples end up. The demo should show two versions: one trained with weight regularization (smooth score) and one without (sharp score). Plot the generated samples on the same graph as the training data. With regularization, samples should cluster in the gaps between the training points. Without regularization, samples should cluster on or near the training points themselves.

interpolation_quality = (fraction_of_samples_in_gaps) / (fraction_in_training_points)

The demo must make it impossible to miss: the smoothed version creates samples in the open space between training points, while the sharp version memorizes the corners.
