Are Reasoning LLMs Robust to Interventions on their Chain-of-Thought?

Reasoning models can recover from disruptions in their step-by-step thinking, and doubt is how they know something went wrong.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models that reason step-by-step before answering are useful because you can see their work. But when their thinking gets interrupted—a wrong step inserted, a confusing rephrase, nonsense injected—do they notice and fix it? This paper cuts into a chain of thought at different points, adds seven kinds of disruptions, and watches how reasoning models recover. The key finding: they do recover, but the way they do it (by showing doubt) matters more than the disruption itself.

Zero assumption version

Chains of thought can hide problems

When a reasoning model writes out its step-by-step thinking before answering, we assume it is thinking honestly. A human reader can spot a bad step. But the model itself never checks its own work during generation—it just keeps writing the next step, even if an earlier step was nonsense.

A naive fix would be: add a step that says validate each step. But that adds cost and complexity. The real question is: when a reasoning model encounters a broken or weird step in its own chain, does it even realize? And if it does, how?

The question. Can reasoning models detect and recover from disruptions inside their own thinking chains?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take a working reasoning chain.

Start with a correct step-by-step solution from the model.

Cut it at a point.

Choose 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, or 90% through the thinking.

Insert a disruption.

Replace or alter the step at that point with one of: wrong math, nonsense, rephrase, paraphrase, or unrelated chain.

Let the model continue.

Force the model to keep writing from the broken step onward.

Check recovery.

Did the final answer stay correct, and what did the model write?

What the paper changes

What the paper is changing

The paper does not change the model itself. Instead, it changes the input: the chain of thought the model sees and must continue from. By interrupting at five different points in the reasoning and injecting seven kinds of corrupted steps, the paper measures how a fixed, frozen model responds.

the object it moves  =  the reasoning chain
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Doubt signals

measured quantity  =  fraction of doubt words

The paper discovers that when a reasoning model recovers from a disruption, it does not just erase the mistake. Instead, it writes expressions of doubt: words and phrases like hmm, wait, let me reconsider, I think there is an issue. The paper measures this by counting the words the model outputs that signal uncertainty.

Why does this matter? Doubt signals are a window into the model's internal state. When the model writes doubt, it is showing you it detected a problem. When a stylistic change (like a rephrase) reduces doubt, the model is less likely to recover. This lets researchers see the mechanism the model uses to stay on track.

Why it works

Why the model recovers

A reasoning model is trained to predict the next token given all previous tokens. When it encounters a weird step—one that does not match the pattern it learned—the model's internal representations (computed across all layers) register a mismatch. This is not a deliberate check; it is what the model's learned weights naturally do.

The model then has a choice: follow the weird step or flag it. The paper shows that models trained with reasoning-focused objectives learn to raise doubt before pivoting. Doubt is not an error signal; it is a recovery mechanism. By writing hmm or wait, the model is buying time to recompute. The next tokens it writes then steer the chain back into familiar, correct territory.

Stylistic changes (like paraphrasing a correct step into synonyms) break this mechanism because they confuse the pattern-matching. The model does not see a logical error; it sees an unfamiliar phrasing. It stops raising doubt, and recovery fails. This shows that the model is pattern-matching, not doing formal reasoning verification.

Measuring intervention impact

First in words:

accuracy after disruption = (1 - disruption_strength * disruption_type_factor * (1 - recovery_rate)) * baseline_accuracy

Then the compact form:

acc_final = acc_base * (1 - c * factor_t * (1 - recovery_i(t)))

Every symbol:

acc_baseaccuracy on the original problem with no disruption
cconstant capturing overall disruption severity
factor_thow disruptive intervention type t is (0 to 1)
recovery_i(t)probability model recovers by final answer given intervention at timestep t
acc_finalaccuracy on the disrupted version of the problem
A tiny worked example
Baseline problem accuracy: 96% (model gets it right normally).
At 50% through, insert a wrong arithmetic step (factor_t = 0.8, very disruptive).
Model recovers 70% of the time from disruptions at the midpoint (recovery_i = 0.7).
Final accuracy = 96% * (1 - 1.0 * 0.8 * (1 - 0.7)) = 96% * (1 - 0.24) = 96% * 0.76 = 73%.
Now try paraphrasing the same step (factor_t = 0.3, subtle style change).
Recovery drops to 20% (paraphrasing confuses the pattern) (recovery_i = 0.2).
Final accuracy = 96% * (1 - 1.0 * 0.3 * (1 - 0.2)) = 96% * (1 - 0.24) = 73%.
Same final accuracy, but the reason differs: subtle confuses more than obvious errors.

The model's recovery depends on whether it can detect the problem via learned patterns, and stylistic confusion blocks detection more than semantic error.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stylistic confusion breaks recovery. Paraphrasing a correct step (rewording the same math in synonyms) causes the model to stop raising doubt, because it does not see a logical error, just unfamiliar phrasing. Recovery drops below semantic errors.
Early disruptions are more damaging. A disruption at 10% of the chain gives the model few earlier steps to anchor recovery. By 90%, the model has built up context; it can recover even from nonsense.
Recovery inflates output length. When a model recovers, it writes extra steps (hmm, wait, let me reconsider) to recompute. Outputs can balloon 200% longer, raising the computational cost of recovery.
Done right: larger models recover better. Scaling the model to 70 billion weights improves recovery across all intervention types and timings. Larger models have seen more examples during training, so they have learned more precise patterns of what good reasoning looks like. When the disrupted step arrives, the model can recognize it as unusual, because it deviates clearly from the patterns it knows.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a test suite: take five math or logic problems from a standard benchmark (e.g., MATH, Science QA, or a logic puzzle set). For each problem, generate the model's correct step-by-step solution. Then, for each solution, cut it at 50% and inject a deliberately wrong arithmetic step. Force the model to continue from there. Check: (1) does the model write doubt words? (2) does the final answer match the original? (3) how many extra words did recovery cost?

recovery_rate = (problems_with_correct_final_answer_despite_disruption) / (total_disrupted_problems) ; doubt_frequency = (doubt_token_count) / (total_tokens_in_recovery)

If the model writes doubt and still lands on the right answer, you have just seen the mechanism: the model's internal patterns detected a problem and steered toward recovery.
