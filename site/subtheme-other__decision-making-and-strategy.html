Decision making and strategy

Papers on reinforcement learning, game theory, and optimization—training agents to make good sequential decisions and play strategic games.

What this is

Imagine you are teaching a computer to play chess or manage a robot arm. You can't just tell it the right move for each position — there are too many possible situations. Instead, you need it to learn a strategy: a way of thinking that lets it evaluate future moves, understand the consequences of each choice, and pick the action that leads to the best long-term outcome. This is what reinforcement learning does — the model learns by trying things, getting rewards or penalties, and adjusting its strategy to earn more rewards over time.

The hard part is that the consequences of each decision can stretch far into the future, and they depend on what other players (or other parts of the system) do in response. If a chess engine sacrifices its queen now, it might win several moves later — but only if it plays perfectly afterward. If it miscalculates, the sacrifice was a disaster. Training a system to handle this requires balancing exploration (trying new moves to learn) with exploitation (using what you already know works). It also requires coordinating between multiple agents when they are competing or cooperating.

Researchers approach this through game theory and optimization. Some build systems that learn by playing against themselves or other agents, gradually discovering better strategies. Others use mathematical techniques to prove that a strategy will work or to find the best possible move in each situation. Some focus on how to make agents work together in multi-agent settings, where every actor is learning at the same time and the rules are constantly shifting beneath each agent's feet.

How the work breaks down

The papers in this subtheme split into five natural directions: training language models and agents to reason through hard problems using RL feedback, because most problems have too many possible solutions to memorize; understanding how markets and economies reach equilibrium when many agents make decisions, so we can predict whether they stabilize; designing systems where multiple agents coordinate or compete, so that teams solve problems faster than individuals working alone; building better algorithms for planning and sampling, because searching through huge solution spaces requires smarter methods than random search; and applying decision-making techniques to specific domains like chess, finance, and health, where each field has unique structure and goals.

Training agents to reason through RL

Teaching a language model to solve hard math problems is different from teaching it facts. The model has to learn a strategy: how to work through the problem step by step, when to try a different approach, when to give up on a direction. Reinforcement learning gives it feedback on whether the final answer was right, so it can learn which reasoning paths work. The hard part is that a single wrong step early on ruins the whole solution, so the model needs to learn to catch and fix its own mistakes before they compound, because downstream steps build on earlier ones.

Rectifying LLM Thought from Lens of Optimization uses RL to enhance reasoning across multiple benchmarks in math, science, and coding by identifying and fixing suboptimal reasoning behaviors. Scaf-GRPO scaffolds the RL training so that learning happens in stages, which helps the model master easier reasoning first before tackling harder problems; this approach boosted a model with billions of learned weights's performance on AIME by 44% relative to simpler RL training. QuestA expands the model's reasoning capacity by generating additional related questions that help it think through problems more thoroughly, reaching top results on multiple math benchmarks with small models. Together these papers show that even small models can solve hard problems if trained with the right RL signal and the right structure.

Markets and equilibrium in multi-agent systems

When many buyers and sellers interact in a market, they each make decisions that depend on what everyone else is doing. An economy reaches equilibrium when no one can improve their situation by unilaterally changing their strategy. Understanding these equilibria matters because it tells us whether markets are fair, whether they compute prices correctly, and whether computational systems can actually find these stable states. The challenge is that proving equilibrium exists and is computable is often much harder than people assume.

Infinite Horizon Markov Economies proves that recursive equilibria exist in economies where agents make decisions forever into the future, and shows how to compute them using neural networks as part of the solution method. How to Price Data establishes that when companies buy and sell data, equilibrium prices exist under realistic utility functions and can be computed efficiently for some types of markets, though not all. Equilibrium Pricing in Oligopolistic Data Markets proves that if a few big data sellers set prices beyond simple uniform rates, a 2-approximate Nash equilibrium is guaranteed to exist, and real markets converge to it quickly. These three papers build a foundation for understanding when and how multi-agent systems stabilize.

Multi-agent coordination and communication

When multiple agents must work together toward a goal, each one needs to decide what to communicate and how to interpret what the others say. If they all broadcast everything to everyone, the communication becomes noisy and slow. If they stay silent, they can't coordinate. The challenge is learning which signals matter and which can be ignored, so that the team achieves the goal efficiently while keeping communication sparse.

Self-Organization in Multi-Agent Systems introduces a framework where communication adapts as the task unfolds; instead of using a fixed protocol, agents learn to condition their messages on what has happened so far. Graph-of-Agents structures multi-agent collaboration as a graph where only the most relevant agents are selected to work on each problem rather than running all agents together, which is faster and more focused because irrelevant agents add overhead without benefit. Small Agent Group is the Future of Digital Health demonstrates that a team of smaller specialized agents often outperforms a single large model in medical settings, with better reliability and lower deployment cost, because each small agent focuses on its expertise rather than trying to know everything. These approaches show that coordination works best when the team size and communication are tailored to the specific problem.

Optimization algorithms and sampling

Many hard problems require finding the best solution among exponentially many possibilities. Some problems need exact answers; others just need good approximations. Some have structure we can exploit (like convexity); others are totally chaotic. The fundamental challenge is that trying all possibilities is impossible, so we need algorithms that explore smartly, focus on promising regions, and converge to a solution without wasting computation.

Program Synthesis with Neurally Interpreted Languages tackles problems where a program must express multi-step computations; the system learns to write code that generalizes far beyond its training length by using calculus-based search (adjusting weights via derivatives) as the method. Poisson Midpoint Method for Log Concave Sampling proves that sampling from high-dimensional distributions can converge faster (in Wasserstein distance) than previously proven possible when the distribution has certain mathematical structure, which matters because slow convergence wastes computation. Reinforced Sequential Monte Carlo for Amortised Sampling combines two classical sampling methods: it learns to weight proposals from one method using RL, which improves both quality and training stability compared to using either method alone, because the system figures out which method works best in each situation. Together these represent different angles on the core problem of how to search and optimize when the space is vast.

Decision-making in specialized domains

Decision-making looks different in each domain: a chess engine needs to evaluate board positions, a financial trader needs to pick which assets to buy, a social media platform needs to decide which creator incentives work, and a model explaining human choice needs to capture why people take risks. These domains have their own constraints, data types, and success metrics. The shared problem is learning a strategy that works in that specific environment.

Chessformer creates a unified architecture specialized for chess that learns to play, predict human moves, and enable interpretability by using domain knowledge about how the game is structured. Personalized Policy Learning through Discrete Experimentation proposes a method for a social media platform to optimize creator incentives by learning from carefully chosen experiments; it substantially outperforms existing methods on real data. Signature-Informed Asset Allocation applies neural networks to build allocation strategies for equity portfolios by using a feature called signature (a mathematical summary of a time series) to outperform both traditional and modern forecasting methods. Using Reinforcement Learning to Train Large Language Models to Explain Human Decisions combines cognitive modeling with RL so that models can both predict human risky choices accurately and explain why people make those choices. Each paper tailors its approach to its domain's specific structure and goals.
