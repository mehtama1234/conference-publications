Generative Modeling of Irregular Time Series via SDE-Induced Continuous-Discrete Variational Inference

SDEVI learns time series with gaps and uneven timing by inferring the hidden continuous process that generated the messy real data.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Real-world time series are messy: measurements come at irregular intervals, are sparse, and arrive asynchronously. Standard models assume data points sit on a regular grid. SDEVI solves this by treating the observations as samples from a hidden continuous process—a smooth trajectory governed by a random differential equation (an equation describing random motion)—and then learning both the continuous process and the observations together. The method is computationally fast and works across healthcare, physics, climate, and sensor data.

Zero assumption version

Why regular models fail on messy time series

Most time-series models assume your data arrives in neat, evenly-spaced snapshots: one reading per hour, one per day, one per second. But real-world data is messy. A hospital records a patient's heart rate every 3 minutes during one period, then every 2 minutes during a crisis, then nothing for 20 minutes while they sleep. A climate station measures temperature every 6 hours, but sensors miss readings. IoT devices send data whenever they feel like it.

When you force this irregular data into a regular-grid model, you either lose information (by resampling to a fixed grid, throwing away time), or pad gaps with zeros or previous values (which lies to the model), or use complicated workarounds. A beginner might try interpolating—guessing the missing values—but this adds error and hides uncertainty. Existing models that handle gaps use expensive inference methods (path-based variational inference) or assume the hidden process has a special simple form, which doesn't match real data.

The bottleneck. SDEVI shifts inference from the path (which changes shape) to the observations (which are fixed), making it scale and stay accurate even when the hidden process is complex.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Posit a continuous process

Assume there is a hidden smooth trajectory (governed by a random differential equation) that generates all observations.

Step 2: Use inference to guess the trajectory

Instead of guessing every point on the path, SDEVI reasons about the distribution (the range of possibilities) for that trajectory given only the actual observations.

Step 3: Use SDEs as the inference model

The posterior (our best guess for the hidden process, conditioned on the data) is itself an SDE—a simpler one, chosen to be computationally tractable.

Step 4: Extend to harder dynamics

For data with nonlinear behavior, the method generalizes to nonlinear SDEs, and even complex-valued data.

Step 5: Run at inference time

To predict missing values or forecast, sample trajectories from the learned continuous model, then read off values at the times you care about.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper changes where inference happens: not on the entire path (every point in time), but only on the discrete observations you actually have. This one shift—focusing computation on what you know—removes the bottleneck.

the object it moves  =  inference target
The hidden quantity

The posterior distribution: which continuous paths are plausible given the messy data

measured quantity  =  posterior distribution over trajectories

The 'hidden quantity' here is not a single number but a distribution: the range of plausible continuous curves consistent with your observations. Since each observation is a noisy sample from the true curve, many curves could have generated your data. The posterior is a description of all curves that make sense.

SDEVI estimates this posterior by assuming it is itself an SDE—a random process. This is clever because SDEs are flexible (can describe many kinds of smooth behavior) yet computationally fast to work with. The paper measures this posterior by sampling trajectories from it and comparing their likelihood to the data.

Why it works

Why it works: inference on observations, not paths

The core insight is to flip the direction of reasoning. Old methods tried to guess the entire trajectory point-by-point, which is expensive and requires restrictive assumptions. SDEVI instead reasons backwards: given the observations you have, what distribution of continuous paths would most likely produce them? By encoding this distribution as an SDE (a process equation), you get computational efficiency and flexibility.

Here's the causal chain. First, you assume the true data-generating process is continuous and smooth. Second, you don't try to guess every point on that smooth path; instead, you infer the random process itself—what kind of random walk or drift would naturally produce observations at the times and places they appeared. Third, because you model the posterior as an SDE, you inherit all the mathematical tools for working with SDEs: Euler stepping, adjoint methods, fast likelihood calculations. This means the inference cost does not explode as your time series gets longer or messier. Finally, the method extends to nonlinear SDEs and complex-valued data, so it can handle oscillations, sudden shifts, and higher-dimensional irregularity without breaking.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

The likelihood (probability) of the data under the model equals the integral of the probability density along the inferred trajectory, accounting for observation noise.

Then the compact form:

log p(y) = sum_t log p(y_t | x(t)) + log p(x | theta)

Every symbol:

y_tthe observed value at time t
x(t)the hidden continuous trajectory at time t
p(y_t | x(t))probability of seeing y_t given the true value x(t) (observation noise)
p(x | theta)probability of the trajectory under the SDE model with parameters theta
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 3 observations: y1=10.2 at time t=0, y2=11.5 at time t=5, y3=12.8 at time t=10.
Assume observation noise has std dev 0.5, so p(y_t | x(t)) is a bell curve centered at x(t) with width 0.5.
For a candidate trajectory x(t)=[10, 10.5, 11, 11.5, 12, 12.5, 13], compute: log p(y1=10.2|x=10) ≈ -0.1, log p(y2=11.5|x=11.5) ≈ 0.0, log p(y3=12.8|x=13) ≈ -0.5.
Exact likelihood includes the SDE probability term too, but this sketch shows: trajectories that pass near the observed points score higher.

The posterior SDE encodes which trajectories have high probability; sampling from it gives predictions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatch between observation noise and reality. If the true observation noise is much smaller or larger than assumed, the posterior will misweight the observations. For example, if you assume sensors are noisy but they are actually precise, the inferred trajectory may overfit to the data.
Nonlinearity too extreme for the model. The extension to nonlinear SDEs requires solving harder equations. If the true dynamics are chaotic or have sharp discontinuities, the method may struggle even with nonlinear SDEs.
Very sparse data in long intervals. If you have only one observation in a month-long gap, the posterior over trajectories in that gap becomes very wide. Forecasting will be uncertain, which is honest but not always useful.
Done right. When observation noise is well-calibrated, dynamics are smooth or learnable, and data density is reasonable, SDEVI produces accurate forecasts and honest uncertainty estimates.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic time series: generate 100 time points from a known nonlinear SDE (e.g., a damped pendulum or a predator-prey model), but only observe 20 of them at random times. Corrupt the observations with Gaussian noise. Train SDEVI on these 20 observations. Then evaluate: (1) Can it reconstruct the missing 80 points (interpolation)? (2) Can it forecast beyond time 100 (extrapolation)? (3) How does it compare to a simpler baseline, such as linear interpolation or a grid-based LSTM? Measure mean squared error on interpolation, extrapolation, and forecasting tasks. Visualize the posterior mean trajectory plus a shaded confidence band, overlaid with the true trajectory and observed points. The demo should make clear: the posterior captures the shape of the truth even in regions with no data.
