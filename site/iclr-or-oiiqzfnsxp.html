Fine-tuning Behavioral Cloning Policies with Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning

Learn a safe starting policy from expert recordings, then refine it online by asking humans which action pairs they prefer, using a weighted blend that trusts each signal based on how certain it is.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Training robots or AI systems to do useful tasks faces two hard problems: figuring out what counts as good (rewards are messy to hand-write), and exploring safely without breaking things while learning. This paper proposes a two-step path: first, learn from a pile of video recordings where experts do the task correctly and safely; second, refine the policy live by showing humans two action choices and letting them point to the better one. The paper introduces BRIDGE, an algorithm that merges these two signals by weighing each decision — trusting the expert recordings more when the human signal is uncertain, and trusting human feedback more when the algorithm has already learned the basics.

Zero assumption version

Two incompatible paths to safe, practical learning

In the real world, you cannot safely explore by trial-and-error. A robot that tries random arm movements to learn will break things; a medical AI that experiments with treatments harms patients. So people collect videos of experts doing the task right and teach the AI to copy those movements — called behavioral cloning.

Copying is safe but crude: the expert recordings show only a tiny slice of all possible situations, so the learned policy gets stuck. A better path is to ask humans to compare two candidate actions and point to the better one — preference-based learning. Humans are good judges of quality: they can instantly spot when an action is clumsy or will fail, which takes the algorithm hours of trial-and-error to learn by bumping into failures. Their feedback also adapts live as the policy improves. But preference learning from scratch is data-hungry because each human judgment teaches the AI only a little.

The naive approach tries one path or the other: pure behavioral cloning stays safe but never improves beyond the expert data because once the algorithm can mimic every expert example, it has nothing new to learn; pure preference learning learns faster than cloning but wastes all that expert video and spends its first stage exploring blindly instead of starting from a known-good policy.

In one sentence. The algorithm learns a safe initial policy from expert videos, then refines it using human preference judgments, weighting each data source by how confident it is.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Gather expert videos

Collect recordings of humans or existing controllers doing the task well.

Train a copy policy

Use behavioral cloning to learn an initial policy that mimics the expert videos.

Show pairs of actions

During live deployment, generate two candidate actions and ask a human which is better, because humans see mistakes instantly that the algorithm only detects by trial-and-error.

Measure algorithm confidence

Estimate how sure the algorithm is about each data point — expert recordings, human preferences, its own samples.

Blend with uncertainty weights

Nudge the policy using both signals, but scale each by how confident it is: high confidence in one source means low confidence in the other.

What the paper changes

The two signals and how they are blended

The algorithm changes how the policy processes two different kinds of guidance. Instead of picking one (either follow experts or follow human preferences), it takes both and weighs them based on confidence. Where the expert recordings are unclear, human preference gets more say; where human judgments are inconsistent, the expert path gets more say.

the object it moves  =  Confidence weights on data sources
The hidden quantity

Uncertainty: how confident is each signal?

measured quantity  =  Algorithm confidence in each data point

Confidence is a number between 0 and 1 for each decision the algorithm makes: 1 means the signal is clear and reliable; 0 means it is noisy or contradictory. The expert videos have high confidence in situations the experts showed many times, and low confidence where experts rarely went. Human preferences have high confidence when many humans agree, and low confidence when there is disagreement or when the algorithm has not seen that situation before.

The paper estimates confidence by measuring how much the algorithm's prediction changes when you remove one expert video or one human judgment. If removing one piece of data barely changes the answer, that piece has low confidence — the algorithm did not rely on it heavily. If removing it shifts the answer a lot, it has high confidence — the algorithm was counting on it.

Why it works

Why uncertainty weighting connects offline data to online efficiency

The core insight is that expert data and human feedback teach at different rates. Expert recordings are static — once collected, they do not change. Human feedback arrives one judgment at a time during live deployment and can immediately correct mistakes. If you weight them equally, you waste the expert advantage (abundant and already known) and suffer from the human disadvantage (each judgment is noisy and sparse).

By measuring confidence in each signal, the algorithm dynamically rebalances: when human feedback is scarce or noisy (low confidence), the expert policy acts as a guardrail, keeping the learned policy close to the safe, known behavior. When human feedback grows consistent and plentiful (high confidence), the algorithm trusts it more and allows bigger changes, because many humans pointing the same way means that direction is safe. This way, the algorithm starts safe because it inherits the expert policy, and improves fast as humans provide signal because it can refine an already-working policy instead of starting from scratch.

The regret (total cost of mistakes) shrinks with more expert videos, because a bigger, more diverse expert dataset makes the initial policy work well in more situations, which makes the algorithm more confident in it, which allows human feedback to refine it faster without wasting samples. Fewer mistakes early on means fewer human judgments needed overall, because the algorithm can focus on difficult edge cases instead of struggling through basics.

Math after the picture

The weighted loss that balances both signals

First in words:

Total loss = confidence in expert data times expert loss plus confidence in human preferences times preference loss

Then the compact form:

L = w_expert * L_bc + w_human * L_pref, where w_expert + w_human = 1 and w_expert = 1 / (1 + uncertainty_pref), w_human = 1 / (1 + uncertainty_expert)

Every symbol:

LTotal loss to minimize (the error the training process tries to shrink)
w_expertWeight on expert data, between 0 and 1 (how much to trust expert videos)
w_humanWeight on human preferences, between 0 and 1 (how much to trust human feedback)
L_bcBehavioral cloning loss (how far the policy is from expert actions)
L_prefPreference loss (how often the policy picks the action humans prefer)
uncertainty_prefMeasured noise or disagreement in human feedback (0 = clear, 1 = very fuzzy)
uncertainty_expertMeasured noise or disagreement in expert data (0 = clear, 1 = very fuzzy)
A tiny worked example
Suppose expert recordings are very clear: uncertainty_expert = 0.1, so w_expert = 1 / (1 + 0.1) = 0.91
Human feedback is inconsistent early on: uncertainty_pref = 0.9, so w_human = 1 / (1 + 0.9) = 0.53
First iteration: L = 0.91 * L_bc + 0.53 * L_pref ≈ 0.91 * 5.0 + 0.53 * 8.0 = 4.55 + 4.24 = 8.79 (mostly expert-driven)
After 50 human judgments, humans agree more: uncertainty_pref = 0.2, so w_human = 1 / (1 + 0.2) = 0.83
Later iteration: L = 0.91 * L_bc + 0.83 * L_pref ≈ 0.91 * 3.0 + 0.83 * 2.0 = 2.73 + 1.66 = 4.39 (balanced, shifting toward human)
As humans teach more, their weight grows, and the policy adapts beyond the expert baseline while staying safe because expert loss never drops below 0.91 × L_bc.

The weights sum to less than 1 (they are not 0.91 and 0.53, they are 0.63 and 0.37 after normalization), but the idea is the same: one signal rises while the other falls, driven by measured confidence in each.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Expert data is biased or limited. If all expert videos show the same narrow style or miss edge cases, the initial policy is weak, and the algorithm trusts a bad guardrail. The uncertainty estimates then overweight flawed expert data. You must audit the expert dataset for coverage.
Human feedback is inconsistent or adversarial. If humans do not agree or deliberately give misleading preferences, the measured confidence in human feedback stays high but is actually useless. The algorithm confidently learns the wrong thing. Collect human feedback from reliable judges or validate agreement.
Uncertainty estimates are inaccurate. If the method that measures confidence in each data point is biased (e.g., it always overestimates human signal), the weights become imbalanced and one source drowns out the other. Validate that your confidence measure correlates with actual prediction error.
Done right. Audit expert data for quality and diversity; collect human feedback from consistent judges; validate uncertainty estimates on held-out data; start by trusting experts, shift toward humans as feedback accumulates.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple control task (e.g., a cartpole or a simulated gripper reaching for an object). Collect 50 expert demonstrations (trajectories where the expert solves the task). Train a behavioral cloning policy and measure its regret (sum of failures) on a test set of 100 new situations.

Then, generate 200 online human preference judgments by having a human compare pairs of the algorithm's actions at random and pick the better one. Run three learning procedures: (1) behavioral cloning alone (ignore human feedback), (2) preference learning alone (ignore expert data), (3) BRIDGE with uncertainty weighting. Measure regret for each at 10 checkpoints (after 0, 20, 40, ..., 200 human judgments).

The demo should make it unmissable that BRIDGE starts with the lowest regret, because it inherits the expert policy rather than starting from random actions, and shrinks regret faster than preference learning alone because humans can refine a working policy rather than teaching from scratch, while behavioral cloning stays flat because it has no new signal to improve on. Plot the three curves: BRIDGE is the best of both worlds — lowest initial cost, fastest improvement, lowest final cost.

regret = sum of failure costs on test set = mistakes where algorithm picked a worse action than the human-preferred one

BRIDGE's uncertainty weighting is the bridge: it ensures that expert knowledge de-risks the start while human feedback accelerates improvement without wasting safe initial steps.
