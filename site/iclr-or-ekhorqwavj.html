Spatially Guided Training for Vision-Language-Action Robots

A robot learns to act by aligning what it sees with where things are, through two training stages that keep spatial understanding intact while teaching motor control.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Large language models paired with image understanding excel at conversation and answering questions, but robots need more: they must turn words and images into actual arm movements and grasps. The challenge is that teaching a model to control a robot can scramble its spatial understanding—the ability to locate objects and plan where to move. This paper presents a two-stage method that protects spatial reasoning while learning control.

Zero assumption version

Why naive robot training breaks spatial understanding

Modern image-understanding models are trained on billions of pictures and text descriptions, so they learn strong patterns: they recognize objects, understand spatial relationships, and can answer questions about scenes. When a robot uses such a model, it can read an instruction like pick the red cup and see where the cup sits in an image.

A beginner would try: take that pretrained model, glue on an action expert (a separate module that outputs arm movements), and train the whole thing on robot data. The problem: during training, the action expert pulls the shared model in one direction (making actions work), while spatial grounding—the ability to localize objects and reason about space—starts to atrophy because the training signal does not reinforce it. The two objectives collide: training nudges weights one way for action control and another way for spatial understanding. The spatial knowledge gets erased in the name of control.

The method in one sentence: Train the model in two stages: first, teach spatial grounding (point and box prediction) until it is rock-solid, then layer action learning on top with spatial prompting that keeps spatial reasoning active throughout.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: The training recipe and how updates flow

The paper does not change the model architecture much; instead, it changes how the model is trained. Specifically: (i) it sequences training in two stages instead of one, (ii) it uses spatial prompting—the action expert reads spatial features from the VLM without modifying those features directly, and (iii) it applies a gradient decay factor (a small damping coefficient) when training the shared multimodal encoder, so action gradients do not overwrite spatial weights.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Gradient alignment between objectives

measured quantity  =  Gradient alignment (or objective conflict)

As the model trains, each objective—spatial grounding and action control—produces its own training signal that pulls weights in a direction to reduce error. If those signals point in opposite directions, the weights thrash: one step forward for action learning erases spatial understanding, and spatial refinement undoes action progress. The paper measures this collision using Projected Symbiotic Similarity (PSS), a number that compares how similar the two training directions are. Low PSS means the two objectives fight; high PSS means they agree.

Why measure it? The paper shows empirically that without spatial prompting, PSS plummets during co-training: spatial and action objectives conflict, and spatial perception (ability to locate objects on RefCOCO-g, a spatial grounding benchmark) drops while manipulation performance (success on WidowX tasks) rises. With the proposed two-stage training and spatial prompting, PSS stays high because the two training signals point in similar directions. When the directions align, both spatial and action performance improve together instead of trading off, because action training no longer erases spatial weights.

Why it works

Why it works: Three shifts that preserve spatial reasoning

The core insight is that a single training signal can destroy two different learned capabilities if they disagree about which weights matter. Spatial grounding cares most about weights that recognize object outlines and positions. Action control cares most about weights that convert spatial features into motor commands. When both train together with equal urgency, action training updates overwrite spatial weights because action error reduces loss more visibly than spatial error does.

The paper solves this in three steps. First, Stage 1 pre-training etches spatial reasoning into the model so deeply (via point/box/trajectory prediction) that it becomes resistant to later training. Second, Stage 2 uses spatial prompting: instead of letting the action module update the shared spatial encoder directly, the action module reads intermediate spatial features through a cross-attention layer (queries from the action expert, keys and values from the VLM). This layer acts as a firewall—training signals flow into the cross-attention module but not into the core spatial encoder, because the attention queries are not part of the encoder. Third, a small dampening factor ensures that any training signals that do reach the shared model are weakened, so spatial weights drift slowly and stay mostly intact. Together, these create a situation where spatial and action objectives cooperate: action training uses spatial features (high similarity between directions), and spatial grounding remains accurate (high precision scores) even as action capability grows, because the two no longer pull in opposite directions.

The training objective: Weighted spatial and action losses

First in words:

Total loss equals the spatial grounding loss plus a coefficient times the action loss, all reduced by a gradient decay that damps updates to the shared model.

Then the compact form:

Loss = Loss_spatial + lambda * Loss_action; gradient_update = decay_factor * d(Loss) / d(theta)

Every symbol:

Loss_spatialsum of point prediction error plus box prediction error on the spatial dataset
Loss_actionsum of action prediction error on robot demonstration trajectories
lambdaweight controlling how much action training affects the shared model (typically 1.0)
decay_factordampening coefficient (e.g., 0.5) applied to gradients flowing into shared parameters
thetathe weights of the shared VLM encoder
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight theta_1 controls object-edge detection (spatial task). Its gradient from spatial loss is +0.8 (increase the weight).
The same weight receives a gradient from action loss of -0.6 (decrease it, for action control).
Without decay, the weight receives conflicting signals: +0.8 and -0.6. Over epochs, it oscillates.
With decay_factor = 0.5, the effective gradient is 0.5 * (0.8 - 0.6) = 0.5 * 0.2 = 0.1 (small positive step).
The weight drifts slightly (+0.1) instead of thrashing. Spatial reasoning remains intact, and action updates happen gently.

The decay factor acts like shock absorbers: it slows down weight changes so both objectives can work together without one trampling the other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Decay factor too high. If the decay dampens gradients too much, action learning stalls. The robot learns to output safe but useless actions because weight updates are too timid.
Spatial pre-training insufficient. If Stage 1 does not establish strong spatial grounding (e.g., with weak pretraining data), Stage 2 action training still erodes spatial weights because there is no deep anchor.
Spatial prompting not aligned with spatial encoder. If the cross-attention queries do not extract the right spatial features (e.g., they target low-level edges but the task needs high-level object identity), action gradients will pull the encoder toward irrelevant spatial patterns.
Done right. Tune the decay factor (e.g., 0.3–0.7) on a validation set of spatial and action tasks. Ensure Stage 1 converges fully before starting Stage 2. Use spatial prompting on the VLM's intermediate layers (not the input) so high-level spatial reasoning is available to the action expert.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal end-to-end demo would train a small version of the model on a subset of robot data (e.g., 1,000 pick-and-place trajectories, 500 spatial grounding labels). Split into Stage 1 (spatial grounding only, 50 epochs) and Stage 2 (action learning with spatial prompting, 100 epochs). Evaluate both spatial perception (using a held-out spatial grounding test set, measure IoU at intersection-over-union threshold 0.5) and action success (measure pick success rate on held-out robot trajectories).

spatial_integrity = (spatial_iou_end_of_training / spatial_iou_start_of_stage2); action_gain = (action_success_end / action_success_start); gradient_alignment = cosine_similarity(spatial_gradients, action_gradients)

The demo is successful if spatial integrity stays above 0.8 (spatial grounding does not collapse) while action success rises above 70%, and gradient alignment (PSS) remains above 0.5 throughout Stage 2. Without spatial prompting, spatial integrity should drop below 0.5 and action success should stall.
