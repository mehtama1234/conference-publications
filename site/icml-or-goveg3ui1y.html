Error Amplification Limits ANN-to-SNN Conversion in Continuous Control

Small action errors in converted spiking networks stack up across decision steps, but carrying over residual electrical charge suppresses the amplification.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Spiking neural networks (SNNs — a type of neural network that communicates through discrete electrical pulses rather than smooth signals) can inherit the skills of already-trained traditional neural networks (ANNs) without needing to retrain. This is valuable for robotics, where training is slow and risky. But in continuous control tasks, tiny action errors become correlated in time, snowballing into catastrophic failures. This paper shows why that happens and proposes a simple fix: carry over the residual electrical charge between decision steps to dampen the amplification.

Zero assumption version

Converted spiking networks fail in continuous control

A traditional neural network (ANN) trained to control a robot learns how to map observations (camera images, joint angles) to smooth control signals (desired forces on motors). To save energy and run fast on neuromorphic hardware, engineers convert these trained networks to spiking networks (SNNs) that communicate with discrete pulses. The weights stay the same, but the computation changes fundamentally.

In simple classification tasks, this works well. But in continuous control — guiding a robot arm or quadruped through a sequence of steps — tiny conversion errors create a cascading problem. When the SNN outputs an action slightly off, the robot moves to a slightly wrong state. At the next decision step, the SNN's input is now corrupted by that state deviation, so its output is off again. Small uncorrelated errors would average out, but these errors correlate in time: each step's error biases the next step's input in the same direction. The robot drifts further and further from the intended trajectory, and performance collapses.

A beginner might try dampening the error with classical control (adding a corrective term proportional to state deviation), but that requires modifying the policy, which breaks the whole point of conversion—inheriting a pre-trained network without retraining.

In one sentence. Carry the residual electrical charge (membrane potential) from one step to the next to counteract the build-up of correlated action errors.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Run the SNN for T internal time steps

The spiking network fires discrete pulses for a fixed number of internal simulation steps, then outputs an action.

Measure the residual membrane potential

At the end of those T steps, neurons have electrical charge that did not fully reset. This 'leftover' charge reflects the error between ANN and SNN.

Carry the residual to the next decision step

Instead of resetting to zero, initialize the next decision step's neurons with that residual charge.

Residual acts as a corrective bias

The leftover charge nudges the next step's computation in the opposite direction of the accumulated error, suppressing the correlated bias.

Error amplification is broken

Correlated errors are no longer free to compound; the carried-over charge dampens them before they grow.

What the paper changes

Membrane potential initialization

The key object the paper changes is the starting electrical charge (membrane potential) of spiking neurons at the beginning of each decision step. In standard conversion, neurons always start reset to zero. CRPI initializes them with a fraction of the residual charge left over from the previous step, directly feeding the error signal backward to the next step.

The hidden quantity

Residual membrane potential: the hidden amplifier

measured quantity  =  residual membrane potential

The residual membrane potential is the electrical charge left in a neuron after the SNN finishes its internal T simulation steps at a decision step. It represents the accumulated but not-yet-fired signal — the 'ghost' of the difference between what the ANN wanted and what the SNN computed. In a traditional SNN, this charge is thrown away and the neuron resets to zero. But this residual is exactly the error signal that needs to be suppressed at the next step.

The paper quantifies it using cosine similarity: measuring how aligned the action-error lists (sequences of numbers capturing the direction and size of each action mistake) are across consecutive decision steps. If errors point in the same direction repeatedly (positive correlation), they amplify. CRPI measures this correlation and uses the residual as a direct counter-signal.

Why it works

Why residual carry-over stops error amplification

Here is the actual causal chain: When an SNN runs for T internal time steps, neurons accumulate and reset electrical charge. Because the SNN's weights differ slightly from the ANN's, the accumulated signal at the end is off by some amount—the residual. This residual is the sum of all the small unresolved biases during that step.

At the next decision step, the neuron starts over with fresh charge (normally zero). But the error that created the residual is still present in the problem: the observation is corrupted by the previous step's action error, so the same bias will compound again. CRPI's insight is to initialize with a fraction of the previous residual. Because the residual itself encodes the direction and magnitude of the unresolved error, using it as a starting point forces the neuron's early dynamics to tilt against that error. When the neuron then processes the corrupted observation, the residual 'pre-compensates' for the expected bias, smaller. The errors no longer align step after step; instead, each residual partially cancels the next error.

The mechanism works because error in continuous control is dominated by state distribution shift—the robot being in wrong states due to accumulated action errors. By carrying the residual forward, CRPI keeps the neuron's dynamics aligned with the ANN's trajectory longer, breaking the temporal correlation chain.

Math after the picture

Carrying the residual forward

First in words:

The membrane potential at the start of step k is initialized as one-half of the residual from step k-minus-one. Residual is the difference between the spiking network charge and what the traditional network would have produced.

Then the compact form:

v_l[0, k] = (1/2) * residual_l[k-1]

Every symbol:

v_l[0, k]starting electrical charge of layer l at step k
residual_l[k-1]unresolved charge in layer l at the end of step k-1
1/2the carry-over fraction (empirically tuned)
A tiny worked example
Step 1: ANN wants neuron to fire at charge 100. SNN fires at 95. Residual = 100 - 95 = 5.
Step 2: Without CRPI, neuron starts at 0. SNN fires at 90 (another small error, plus corrupted input). Residual now 10.
Step 2 with CRPI: Initialize at (1/2)*5 = 2.5. SNN fires at 96 (the residual nudged it closer). Residual = 4.
Step 3: Residual was 10 without CRPI, only 4 with it. Errors stopped amplifying; CRPI reduced error growth by 60%.

The fraction of residual to carry is chosen to be large enough to suppress correlation but small enough not to over-correct and create new errors.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Carry-over fraction too large. If the residual is carried with weight > 0.7, neurons are over-corrected. The neuron swings past the correct value, creating large errors in the opposite direction. The temporal correlation flips sign but still amplifies.
Residual not actually residual. If the leftover charge is computed incorrectly (e.g., only from some neuron layers), the feedback signal is noisy and may not counter the true error direction. A partial residual points in a random direction relative to the true error, so carrying it forward adds noise rather than cancelling error. This noise amplifies over steps just like the original uncorrelated error, making performance worse than leaving neurons reset to zero.
Works only for smooth continuous control. In highly chaotic tasks (e.g., tasks where a single-step decision error completely changes future state distribution), the residual from one step may be irrelevant to the next. CRPI assumes some continuity in how errors propagate.
Done right. Measure true residual when the SNN finishes computing an action, apply fraction 0.5 at initialization of the next step, and test on smooth control tasks. CRPI works across different RL algorithms and neuron types.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple continuous control task: train a small ANN policy to control a simulated arm or quadruped with reinforcement learning (using an algorithm like TD3 or SAC). Convert the trained ANN to an SNN using standard methods. Measure performance degradation. Then apply CRPI: initialize membrane potentials with residuals using the equation above. Re-run the same SNN. Measure error correlation magnitude across decision steps using cosine similarity of action-error sequences (lists of numbers showing how much each action deviated). Measure final task reward.

Error amplification reduction = (correlation_without_CRPI - correlation_with_CRPI) / correlation_without_CRPI; Performance recovery = (reward_with_CRPI - reward_baseline) / (reward_original_ANN - reward_baseline)

The demo must show that error correlation is measurably lower with CRPI, and that this correlation reduction directly explains the recovery in task reward. A smooth trajectory should emerge instead of divergence.
