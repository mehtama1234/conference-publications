Combining information from different senses

Merging information from images, sound, text, and other data sources so models understand situations from multiple angles at once.

What this is

Images and text are two modalities, but the world has more. Video has images plus time and motion. Audio has sound. A self-driving car has cameras, lidar sensors that measure distance, radar, and microphones. A robot in a factory has vision and touch sensors. If you want a model that understands a scene the way a human does, it needs to digest all of these at once. The challenge is that each modality comes with its own representation: images are spatial (two dimensions plus color channels), audio is temporal (sound waves over time), text is discrete symbols in sequence, and sensor data from a lidar is a point cloud in three dimensions. Merging them is like translating between different languages and then finding the common meaning.

The difficulty is that modalities don't always agree, and they provide complementary information. In a video, you might see a person's mouth moving and hear speech. If they match, great — you have confirmed information. But if they don't match (the lips and sound are out of sync), that tells you something important — maybe the audio is dubbed or the video is corrupted. Audio alone tells you someone is speaking, but video tells you who and which direction they're facing. Text tells you what was said, but audio tells you the emotion and emphasis. A good multimodal model has to understand when modalities agree and learn from all of them, and when they conflict, understand why. This requires not just stacking separate encoders but building real fusion mechanisms.

Research in this space tackles multimodal fusion at different levels. Some work focuses on early fusion, where information from different modalities is combined before being processed deeply. Others use late fusion, where each modality is processed separately and then the results are combined. Still others build more intricate architectures where modalities can query and focus on each other — imagine the visual encoder asking the audio encoder 'what is the speaker saying?' and using that to interpret the visual content better. This cross-modal focusing works because each modality picks up details the others miss, so querying one fills in gaps in the other. Some work focuses on learning a shared representation where all modalities are projected into a common space, the way image-text models work, but extended to three or more modalities. The goal is to build models that can take a video with sound and text captions and understand the event as a unified whole, not as separate streams of data.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five directions. First, researchers study how multimodal models actually work inside, which helps predict when fusion will succeed and how to improve it. Second, they tackle incomplete or missing data from some modalities, since real data is messy — a video camera might fail or a sensor might break. Third, they keep fusion stable and trustworthy when modalities conflict, because ignoring conflicts leads to wrong answers. Fourth, they deepen audio understanding by combining it with language, since audio carries emotion and emphasis that text misses. Fifth, they build practical systems that combine information for real-world tasks, proving multimodal fusion works beyond academic benchmarks.

How multimodal models learn internally

When you train a model on multiple sources of information, you need to understand what it actually learns. Do the different modalities teach the model different things, or do they learn the same idea in different ways? How does the model figure out which parts of each modality to focus on? These questions matter because if you don't know how multimodal learning works inside, you can't predict when it will fail or how to make it better.

Dissecting Multimodal In-Context Learning shows that multimodal models lean on induction circuits — internal pathways that recognize patterns and repeat them — but these circuits work differently across modalities. Images, text, and audio each leave their own fingerprint on how the model reasons. This means the model doesn't just translate everything into one universal language; each modality adds its own flavor. MultiLoReFT takes this further by explicitly separating shared information from modality-specific information. Rather than letting the model muddle them together, it uses low-rank fine-tuning to reveal exactly which learned features are used by all modalities and which ones belong to one modality alone. This split is useful because it shows you where modalities genuinely overlap and where each one knows something unique.

Working with incomplete or partial data

In real life, you rarely have perfect data from all modalities at the same time. A video might be missing audio. A person speaking might not have a visible face. Medical imaging might have some scans but not others. So multimodal models need to work even when some data is broken, incomplete, or simply not there. The challenge is that when a modality is missing, the model can't just ignore it — it needs to recognize the gap and still make sense of what remains.

LIMSSR handles the case where training data itself is incomplete. You might have action videos where some have audio and some don't, or some frames lack motion labels. Rather than throwing out incomplete training examples, LIMSSR learns from all of them, even the partial ones, and uses a sequence-to-score reasoning approach that figures out which missing pieces matter most for understanding quality. MER-DG goes further by thinking about domain shift — what happens when a model trained on one source of data (say, kitchen videos with both vision and audio) needs to work on a different one (medical surgery videos with a different audio setup). By penalizing models that lean too heavily on one modality, it helps the model use all modalities equally instead of relying too much on whichever modality happened to work best during training. Stable Spectral Copula Alignment tackles a tougher problem: when modalities are distorted or out of sync, traditional fusion fails, but spectral alignment treats the modalities like different languages and finds the real shared meaning underneath, even when things are skewed or warped.

Fusion under conflict and noise

When combining modalities, you often hit a hard problem: what happens when they disagree? An infrared camera and a regular camera see the same scene in completely different ways. A person's lips might move at the wrong time relative to their voice. Sensor data might be noisy or corrupted. Naive fusion — just averaging or stacking everything — fails because it tries to combine contradictory signals without understanding why they conflict.

Text-Driven Fusion for Infrared and Visible Images solves the disagreement problem by using text as a bridge. Instead of forcing infrared and visible images to fit into one space, it projects both into a special curved space (where distant items stay far apart) guided by text descriptions. This way, the fusion respects what makes each modality unique while finding genuine agreement. Schrödinger Bridges for Trustworthy Multimodal Fusion uses a different approach: it treats fusion as a flow problem, where you're trying to move what each modality believes toward their shared truth. When one modality is unreliable or noisy, this method can flag it independently of how confident the classifier sounds. It's like having a referee that doesn't just watch the final score but checks whether the evidence was solid. The Self-Consistent Task approach handles the practical case where you have missing data and unequal input sizes simultaneously, and it avoids fusion conflicts by making sure each modality's learning updates work in the same direction, so they cooperate rather than pull against each other.

Audio and language understanding

Audio carries information that text and images alone cannot. Speech has emotion, rhythm, stress patterns, and prosody — the rise and fall of voice. Music has timbre, harmony, and structure. These audio-specific features are often invisible in written transcripts. Models need to learn to extract this information and use it alongside what they know from text and vision.

Music Flamingo is a generalist model that treats music understanding as a multimodal problem: given both the audio waves and semantic questions about the music, it learns to reason about composition, style, mood, and meaning. It works better than previous approaches across multiple music benchmarks because it fuses audio analysis with language understanding. VowelPrompt tackles a narrower but deep problem: hearing emotion from text. Emotion in speech is encoded in vowels — the way they're stretched and stressed. Rather than trying to teach a model to infer emotion from bare text, VowelPrompt augments text with vowel-level information about how those vowels sound, creating a bridge between the acoustic and symbolic worlds. This means a text-only model can now access speech emotion information. AVERE improves audiovisual emotion reasoning by using preference optimization: instead of just classifying emotions, it learns to rank different emotion explanations and uses this ranking to guide multimodal fusion.

Real-world applications in specialized domains

Beyond general-purpose multimodal understanding, researchers are applying fusion to specialized tasks: medical diagnosis, field biology, real-world search, and translation. Each domain has its own data quirks and fusion challenges. Bioacoustic Geolocation shows that animal sounds reveal where you are on Earth, which means conservationists can track species without visual monitoring. By treating bioacoustic signals as a modality alongside geographic data, researchers can predict location from sound alone — useful when cameras aren't available in remote habitats. Interactive Person Retrieval via Multi-Turn Multimodal Conversation solves a practical search problem: how do you find a specific person when you can describe them in words ('person in red jacket'), show an image, or ask follow-up questions? The model needs to fuse text descriptions, images, and reasoning across multiple turns of conversation to zero in on the right person, which matters for security and identification tasks. LMM4-IC4K applies multimodal understanding to PCB design because circuit board components need both visual accuracy and semantic precision — the model learns to read images of circuit footprints alongside text labels, which automates a task that was manual and error-prone. Modeling Long-Tail Relations in the Operating Room tackles a healthcare domain challenge: surgeries have many rare events (specific combinations of instruments and actions that happen infrequently), but multimodal in-context learning lets the model reason from video and metadata even when examples are sparse, which improves surgical planning. Omni-Captioner creates detailed captions for video by fusing audio, vision, and text in a unified framework, which helps accessibility, and Scalable Multilingual Multimodal Machine Translation fuses speech and text to translate across 108 language pairs, showing that audio helps even in symbolic tasks like translation because it carries speaker intent.
