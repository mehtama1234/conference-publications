CellDuality: Self-Supervised Reasoning in LLMs for Biology

An LLM learns coherent biological reasoning by predicting outcomes and then checking its own predictions by working backward to the original conditions.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Large language models can memorize facts about cells and drugs, but generating real biological explanations from partial information is hard because verifying whether a predicted outcome is correct usually requires a wet-lab experiment. This paper shows how an LLM can learn without ground truth by using a clever loop: predict forward (what happens to this cell when we add this drug), then predict backward (can you reconstruct which drug caused the outcome you just predicted). The fidelity of the backward reconstruction acts as a truth signal, training the model to think coherently about biology.

Zero assumption version

Why LLMs struggle with biological reasoning

Large language models excel at recognizing patterns when you can check the answer: is this cell type annotation correct? You can look it up or count cells under a microscope. But biological reasoning is different. Suppose you ask the model to predict what happens to a human cell when you expose it to a specific drug, or to trace back from an observed cellular change to infer which genes were perturbed. There is no simple yes-or-no label, because running the experiment is expensive or impossible.

The standard fix is Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards: train the model by rewarding it when its predictions match ground-truth labels, the way you reward a chess engine when it finds the right move. But in biology, you rarely have labels for the tricky open-ended questions. You cannot easily verify a prediction, so you cannot build a reward signal.

A beginner might try: just fine-tune the model on whatever biological examples you have, then hope it works on new cases. But this breaks when the model encounters a cell type or drug combination it has never seen. The model has no internal compass pointing it toward logically consistent reasoning; it just echoes the training data.

The bottleneck. Most biological reasoning outcomes are non-verifiable, so you cannot use reward signals that require a human oracle to say whether the answer is right.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Forward pass

LLM predicts the biological outcome (e.g., gene expression level) given initial conditions (drug, cell type).

Inverse pass

LLM takes its own forward prediction and tries to reconstruct the original conditions.

Measure fidelity

Compare the reconstructed input to the true input; large match means the reasoning is self-consistent.

Reward signal

Fidelity score becomes the reward; high fidelity means the model thought coherently.

Reinforce with learning

Use the fidelity reward to adjust the LLM weights via reinforcement learning, steering the model toward internally consistent reasoning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

CellDuality modifies how the LLM learns by introducing a second task that the model must solve as a check on itself. Instead of training only on the forward prediction (input to outcome), the training loop now also requires the model to reverse its own prediction (outcome back to input). The model's weights are adjusted based on how well the backward reconstruction matches the real input.

The hidden quantity

Reconstruction fidelity: the internal consistency score

measured quantity  =  Fidelity (how well reconstructed input matches real input)

Fidelity is a score between 0 and 1 that measures how closely the reconstructed input resembles the true starting conditions. In practice, the paper computes this as a similarity score (e.g., a measure of how close two lists of numbers are, or exact match for discrete outcomes) between the input the model reconstructed and the actual input. High fidelity means the forward and inverse tasks are logically consistent; low fidelity means the model's predictions are internally contradictory.

The paper uses fidelity as a reward signal without ever needing a human label or a verified ground truth. Instead of asking an oracle whether the prediction is correct, it asks: did the model's own reasoning loop close consistently?

Why it works

Why self-checking reasoning works

The core insight is that if a model's reasoning is truly coherent, it must be reversible. If you know the drug is aspirin and you predict the cell will express certain genes, and someone then shows you those gene levels, you should be able to work backward and agree it was aspirin. If your reasoning is sloppy or hallucinatory, the backward step will fail. The model cannot trick itself into high reward by cutting corners.

Here is why this creates learning pressure: when the model makes a forward prediction, it commits to an answer. The backward task immediately tests whether that commitment was logically sound. If the reconstruction is poor, the model's weights receive a small reward signal. The reinforcement learning process adjusts the weights to favor predictions that close the loop consistently. Over time, this loop trains the model to generate predictions that hang together logically, not predictions that are individually plausible but mutually contradictory.

Critically, this works without requiring a wet-lab experiment or a human label. The model bootstraps its own feedback from the structure of the problem itself. This is why CellDuality succeeds on unseen cases (drugs and cell types not in training): the model has learned to think coherently about biology, not to pattern-match training labels.

The fidelity-based reward equation

First in words:

The reward for each prediction is the fidelity score, which measures how well the reconstructed input matches the real input. The model is trained to maximize expected cumulative reward over many predictions.

Then the compact form:

R = fidelity(input_true, input_reconstructed) = similarity(input_true, input_reconstructed)

Every symbol:

input_truethe actual starting conditions (drug, cell type)
input_reconstructedwhat the model guessed the input was, after working backward from its forward prediction
similaritya score between 0 and 1, e.g., cosine distance or exact match
Rthe reward signal used to train the model via reinforcement learning
A tiny worked example
True input: drug=aspirin, cell_type=hepatocyte.
Forward prediction: gene_X=high, gene_Y=medium (this is the model's answer).
Inverse prediction: reconstructed_drug=aspirin, reconstructed_cell=hepatocyte.
Similarity: drug match 1.0, cell match 1.0, average = 1.0.
Reward R = 1.0 (perfect). Model weights are reinforced.
---
Counter-example: True input same, but model's forward was vague (gene_X=low, gene_Y=low).
Inverse prediction: reconstructed_drug=paracetamol, reconstructed_cell=neuron.
Similarity: drug match 0.0, cell match 0.0, average = 0.0.
Reward R = 0.0 (failed). Model learns to avoid vague forward predictions.

The reward directly measures self-consistency: how well the model's reasoning closes the loop from input to output and back again.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Circular reasoning trap. If the model learns to give vague forward predictions and vague inverse predictions, the loop closes trivially but the model has not learned anything. The paper guards this by requiring the forward prediction to be specific enough that the inverse task is genuinely hard.
Mismatch between forward and inverse domains. If the forward task predicts continuous gene levels but the inverse task reconstructs discrete drug names, the two tasks use different output spaces. The similarity score must be chosen to match the data types, or fidelity becomes uninformative.
Limited coverage on truly new cases. The method only works if the unseen examples (drugs and cells not in training) have logically coherent explanations that can be inferred from the biological mechanisms the model saw during training. If a drug behaves in a completely new way, self-consistency alone cannot predict it.
Done right. When the forward and inverse tasks are well-aligned, fidelity becomes a true measure of reasoning coherence. The model learns to think through causality rather than pattern-match, and it generalizes correctly to new data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal single-cell dataset with 100 drug-cell pairs and 5 genes. Train an LLM baseline via fine-tuning, then train the same LLM with CellDuality. On a held-out test set of 50 drug-cell pairs not in training, measure: (1) how often the model correctly predicts which genes are up-regulated, (2) how well the inverse task reconstructs the original drug from the predicted gene levels, and (3) whether the model's explanations are logically consistent (e.g., does it always say the same drug causes the same phenotype).

consistency = (correct_predictions / total_predictions) × (reconstruction_fidelity)

The demo should show that CellDuality models maintain high fidelity even on unseen drug-cell pairs, while fine-tuning baselines guess randomly.
