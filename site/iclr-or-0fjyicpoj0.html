Gaussian certified unlearning in high dimensions

To safely remove someone's data from a trained model without retraining it all, use one Newton step plus noise — it works even when the model has billions of weights.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

When someone asks a company to delete their data from a trained model, the safe way is to retrain the whole model from scratch without that person's data. But retraining is too expensive and slow. This paper proves that a cheap trick works instead: nudge the model weights once using Newton's method, then add random noise. For high-dimensional models where the number of weights is similar to the number of training examples, this achieves both privacy (you cannot tell the data was there) and accuracy (the model still works).

Zero assumption version

The expensive cost of forgetting

A person's data helped train your model. Now they ask you to delete it. The safe answer: retrain the entire model from scratch without their data. This ensures no trace remains. But retraining costs thousands of dollars and hours of compute.

A beginner might try: just adjust the weights a little in the direction away from that person's data. But this leaves hidden traces — an attacker can detect the person's data was there by asking the model clever questions. The traces hide in the weights, and nudging once is not enough to obscure them.

The paper's move: Use one Newton step on the removed data, then add calibrated random noise to the updated weights, to match what retraining would produce.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Start with trained weights

Take the model that has already learned from all data, including the person we want to remove.

Step 2: Compute Newton direction

Find how the loss curve bends near the current weights by computing the Hessian (a table of curvature values at each weight). This tells us which direction best undoes the influence of the removed data.

Step 3: Take one Newton step

Move the weights along that direction by one step. Unlike gradient descent (which is first-order), Newton uses the curvature to take a smarter, larger step.

Step 4: Add calibrated Gaussian noise

Inject random noise into each weight. The noise strength is tuned so the final weights are statistically indistinguishable from full retraining.

Step 5: Return unlearned model

The result is now provably private (adversary cannot distinguish from retraining) and accurate (still works on new data).

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper modifies the trained weights through a single Newton update and noise injection, rather than retraining from scratch. No other part of the model architecture or training procedure changes.

The hidden quantity

What gets measured: Privacy distance

measured quantity  =  Privacy distance (how much an attacker can detect)

When you remove someone's data, the question is: can an attacker tell? The paper measures this using hypothesis testing. Imagine the attacker has two candidate models: one trained with the person's data, one trained without. How often can the attacker correctly guess which is which by looking at the final weights? That error rate is the privacy distance. Lower error rate means better privacy, because the attacker is guessing wrong more often — they cannot distinguish our result from true retraining.

The paper estimates this distance by computing how much the Newton step pushes the weights away from the original trained model, then checking whether the added noise is strong enough to hide the direction of that push.

Plain reading. Privacy means an attacker cannot tell the data was ever in the training set. A good measure is: if the attacker knows data was removed and guesses whether our result matches 'model trained without' versus 'old trained model,' they should guess wrong half the time. If they guess right 90% of the time, privacy is broken.
Paper reading. The paper uses Gaussian-certifiability: a hypothesis test where the adversary observes the unlearned weights and tries to tell which of two distributions they came from. The two distributions are (1) retraining from scratch without the data, and (2) the original trained model. The noise is calibrated so the adversary's error probability is at least epsilon.
Why it works

Why one Newton step plus noise works

Full retraining from scratch is expensive because training iterates many times, slowly nudging weights down the loss curve to its lowest point. Newton's method is smarter: it looks at how sharply the curve bends and jumps closer to the bottom in one step. Removing someone's data changes which direction is 'down' — the paper computes that direction exactly, then takes one big Newton step toward it.

But one step leaves a trail: the direction of movement tells an attacker which data was removed. The paper adds Gaussian noise (random numbers) to hide that trail. The noise is calibrated so the final weights are statistically indistinguishable from retraining: no matter how much an attacker analyzes the weights, they cannot tell whether we removed data or genuinely retrained. This works in high dimensions (when the number of weights is comparable to the number of training examples) because the noise spreads evenly across all weights, and the attacker needs to detect a signal in very high-dimensional noise.

Math after the picture

The equation that makes it work

First in words:

Unlearned weights equal the trained weights minus the Hessian inverse times the gradient on removed data, plus Gaussian noise.

Then the compact form:

beta_unlearned = beta_trained - H_inv * grad_L + noise, where noise ~ N(0, sigma^2 * I)

Every symbol:

beta_trainedThe original trained weights
H_invInverse of the Hessian (curvature matrix of the loss)
grad_LGradient of the loss on only the removed data
noiseRandom vector from a Gaussian distribution
sigmaNoise standard deviation, calibrated for privacy
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight w = 5.0 before unlearning.
The gradient of the loss (excluding removed data) points in direction 0.2, so grad_L = 0.2.
The Hessian curvature at this weight is H = 4.0, so H_inv = 0.25.
Newton step moves us by: 0.25 * 0.2 = 0.05 in the gradient direction.
After Newton step: w = 5.0 - 0.05 = 4.95.
Now add noise: noise_w drawn from a Gaussian with mean 0, standard deviation sigma = 0.15.
Suppose noise_w = -0.08 (just one random draw).
Final unlearned weight: 4.95 + (-0.08) = 4.87.
The noise hides the fact that we moved 0.05 — an attacker sees 0.87 total change but cannot tell how much is Newton step versus random noise.

The Hessian inverse times gradient is Newton's exact solution for removing influence. The noise is the key: it scrambles the trail so the attacker cannot recover the real Newton step.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hessian inversion is unstable. In very high dimensions, computing the Hessian and inverting it can amplify numerical errors. If you compute H_inv wrong, the Newton step points in the wrong direction, and privacy breaks.
Noise calibration is delicate. If the noise is too weak, the attacker can still detect the Newton step direction. If it is too strong, the model becomes inaccurate. The calibration depends on knowing the true Hessian spectrum, which is expensive to estimate.
Works only when dimension is not tiny. In low dimensions (few weights), high-dimensional noise arguments fail — an attacker can resolve the signal through the noise. This method is designed for modern large models.
Done right: careful numerics and theory. Use stable algorithms for Hessian approximation (e.g., sampling or sketching), calibrate noise using the paper's formula for the regime you are in, and run privacy audits on the result.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple linear regression model on a small dataset (e.g., house prices). Train it normally. Then pick one person's data point and remove it. Retrain from scratch on the remaining data (measure accuracy on a held-out test set). Now apply this paper's method: compute the Hessian, take one Newton step, add noise. Compare your unlearned model to the retraining model — they should produce nearly identical predictions on the test set. Finally, run an attacker that tries to guess whether your unlearned model came from retraining or from the original trained model. The attacker should fail more than half the time (true privacy).

privacy_score = 1 - (attacker_accuracy - 0.5), where attacker_accuracy is measured over many trials

The demo must show three things: (1) unlearned accuracy matches retraining, (2) attacker cannot distinguish unlearned from retrained, and (3) all this happens in milliseconds, not hours.
