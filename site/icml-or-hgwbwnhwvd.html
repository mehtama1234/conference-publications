CoIRL-AD: Dual-Policy Driving from Expert Examples and Task Rewards

When teaching a self-driving car to steer, split the job into two actors that learn from expert videos and reward signals separately, then let them compete to catch each other's mistakes.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Self-driving cars usually learn by copying expert drivers in recorded videos. This works well for common driving, but fails on rare tricky situations where the expert videos thin out. Reinforcement learning (learning by trial and error in simulations) can help overcome rare-scenario blindness, because it finds high-reward actions that the expert data never showed. However, mixing copy-from-videos with trial-and-error creates conflicting goals: the copy-loss pulls the model toward expert actions, while the trial-and-error loss pulls it toward high-reward actions, and both cannot be satisfied at once. This paper shows how two separate actors—one copying, one exploring—can work together on the same recorded data without a live simulator.

Zero assumption version

Copying videos alone misses rare cases

A self-driving model learns to steer by watching a human driver on recorded routes. Each video frame becomes an example: given the camera view, predict the steering angle the human chose. This works until the car faces a situation the training videos never showed (heavy rain, blocked lanes, a jaywalker at the edge). The model has no example to copy, so it freezes or steers wrongly.

A beginner's fix is to add exploration: let the car try steering moves and measure which ones succeed using a reward signal (did it reach the target; did it collide?). But in real driving, you cannot safely test moves; you need an offline simulator trained from past data. Once you train in a simulator, you have a second problem: the exploration phase and the copying phase want opposite things. The copy-phase wants the model to reproduce the exact human action. The explore-phase wants the model to deviate and find better paths because deviation is how you discover higher-reward actions the expert never showed. This conflict makes the model unstable and lose both skills.

The problem in one sentence. Imitation learning and reinforcement learning have conflicting goals, so combining them in offline training breaks both.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train two separate actors

Actor A learns by copying expert steering from videos; Actor B learns by predicting which actions lead to reward in a learned world model.

Build a simulated world

Use recorded driving data to train a neural world model that predicts the next camera frame and reward from the current frame and action.

Imagine future rollouts

Have each actor predict several steps ahead in the simulated world to see long-term consequences before committing to an action.

Compete to filter moves

When choosing which action to take in training, run both actors' predictions and take the move that both agree is good, or the one that passes a quality gate.

Update on agreement

Strengthen both actors only when their predictions align; this keeps the RL actor anchored to expert-like behavior.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper splits the learning process into two separate neural networks (actors) that work in parallel: one that copies expert driving, one that learns from simulated rewards. Normally these two goals fight. Here they are decoupled, so each can optimize separately and then compete to pick the best moves.

The hidden quantity

Behavioral alignment: how much do both actors agree?

measured quantity  =  Agreement score between actors

The paper introduces a competition mechanism that measures how often the two actors propose the same steering action or similar confidence levels. This score—call it the alignment measure—tracks whether the imitation actor and the reward-learning actor are on the same page.

The paper uses this alignment to gate the RL actor's updates. When alignment is high, both actors see the move as good; the RL training reinforces it. When alignment is low, the RL actor is drifting away from expert behavior, so the paper discounts that update or skips it. This keeps the RL actor anchored to realistic driving rather than chasing unlikely high-reward fantasies in the simulated world.

Why it works

Why splitting into two actors fixes the conflict

Normally, a single model trained on both imitation and RL objectives receives contradictory nudges. The imitation loss (a penalty that gets smaller when the model copies the expert) pulls the weights toward expert actions; the RL loss pulls them toward high-reward actions. In the early training phase, when the model has not yet learned what is rewarding, these nudges point in different directions. The model zigzags and forgets both skills.

By splitting into two actors, each one optimizes a clean objective without interference. The imitation actor focuses solely on matching expert behavior; it converges to reliable expert steering. The RL actor focuses on maximizing long-term reward in a learned world model; it finds improvements over the expert. Separately, each is stable.

The competition mechanism then runs a post-hoc filter: when it is time to commit to an action during training, it checks both actors' predictions. If the RL actor is drifting—proposing actions that the imitation actor rates as unlikely—that means the reward signal is leading into simulator error or into actions far from any expert example. The paper downweights or skips that RL update. The RL actor only learns from moves that also look expert-like, so it stays grounded.

Over time, the RL actor does find improvements, but only ones that the expert data also suggests are plausible. This is how a model can beat the expert on long-tail scenarios: the RL actor learns to generalize the expert's principles (e.g., stay in lane, avoid objects) in new situations, rather than memorizing specific expert steering angles.

Math after the picture

Balancing two objectives with a gate

First in words:

The imitation loss pulls toward expert actions; the RL loss pulls toward high rewards. The paper weights the RL update by how much the two actors agree on the move, measured as the overlap in their confidence scores or by an explicit alignment loss.

Then the compact form:

loss_total = loss_imitation + lambda * agreement * loss_RL

Every symbol:

loss_imitationhow far the model's steering is from the expert's (copied from video)
loss_RLhow much reward the imagined future rollout predicts the action will yield
agreementoverlap score: 1 if both actors propose the same action, 0 if they disagree
lambdahow much weight the RL objective gets (typically 0.1 to 0.5)
A tiny worked example
Scenario: At a crossroad, the expert video shows the car staying in lane (steering 0.0 degrees).
Imitation actor predicts steering = 0.0 (matches video). Loss_imitation = 0.
RL actor, in the learned world model, sees a reward signal for going straight. It predicts steering = 0.0 (same as imitation). Agreement = 1.0.
Combined: loss_total = 0 + 0.5 * 1.0 * 0.1 = 0.05 (small penalty). Both actors agree, so the RL signal reinforces the move.
Alternative: At a different crossroad, the RL actor learns in the simulator that swerving left yields higher reward (reward = 5). It predicts steering = 15 degrees.
Imitation actor still predicts 0 (all training videos show going straight). Agreement = 0.0.
Combined: loss_total = 0 + 0.5 * 0.0 * (high) = 0 (the RL loss is zeroed out). The RL actor's update is suppressed because it disagrees with expert behavior.

The agreement score acts as a gate: RL improvements only stick if the imitation actor also endorses the move.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

World model is inaccurate. The RL actor learns in a simulator trained from recorded data. If the world model is wrong (e.g., it predicts collisions will not happen), the RL actor learns high-reward behaviors that fail in real driving. Alignment helps catch this: if the reward-based move looks unlike any expert action, alignment is low and the update is suppressed, because the disagreement signals that the move is likely based on simulator error rather than real skill. But a subtle world-model error can still sneak through if it happens to align with expert actions; the error will not be caught because both actors agree.
Long-tail scenarios stay too rare to learn from. If a rare scenario appears only once or twice in the training data, even the RL actor may not get enough signal to learn the right move. The agreement gate will keep both actors conservative (neither will commit to a rare move unless both actors strongly support it), which is safe (fewer crashes) but may not be much better than imitation alone, because the model will still avoid rare moves even when the RL signal says they would work.
Two actors overfit to recorded data. Both actors train on offline data, so they can both memorize rare patterns that happen to correlate with high reward by accident, even if there is no causal relationship. The agreement gate cannot distinguish this from genuine improvement.
Done right. The paper tunes the alignment weighting (lambda and agreement thresholds) on a held-out validation set. It tests on new cities (cross-city generalization) and reports improvement over imitation-only baselines. The RL actor learns genuine improvements, not artifacts, because the expert data is large and representative.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show a simulated self-driving car trained on recorded nuScenes videos and rewards. Split the training: build a baseline model that copies videos only, and a dual-policy model (CoIRL-AD) with the two actors and competition gate. Test both on a held-out set of scenarios, including common driving and rare edge cases (e.g., a pedestrian stepping into the road unexpectedly, a lane suddenly blocked). Measure the success rate on each.

robustness = (common_success + rare_success) / 2; improvement = robustness_CoIRL - robustness_imitation_baseline

The demo must show that CoIRL-AD handles rare scenarios better than imitation alone, without sacrificing common-case performance. The simplest test: the dual-policy model should not crash more often on edge cases than the expert videos did (because the imitation actor keeps it anchored to expert-like steering), and should succeed on at least some rare scenarios where the expert is silent (because the RL actor can invent steering moves that the expert never showed but that look expert-like). Measure this by counting success rate on rare-case test scenarios before and after adding the RL actor.
