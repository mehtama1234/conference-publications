SAOT: Structure-Aware Optimal Transport for Continual Graph Learning

When graphs change over time, preserve the bonds between nodes by measuring how they should move to stay consistent with the previous task.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graphs are networks of connected things—social networks, molecule structures, recommendation systems. As new data arrives, you need to learn from the new graph without forgetting what you learned from the old one. The problem: most methods track individual nodes in isolation, so the relationships between nodes—the global structure—get twisted and lost. This paper measures and preserves those relationships across tasks using a mathematical tool called optimal transport.

Zero assumption version

Nodes drift apart when you learn from new graphs

A graph is a set of points (nodes) connected by edges (links). Social networks, molecule atoms, recommendation links—all graphs. In continual learning, a new graph arrives, and you need to learn from it without erasing what the old graph taught you. A naive approach: update the node embeddings (the list of numbers that represent each node) to fit the new graph data, then later update them again for the next graph. But when you optimize each node in isolation—only thinking about how that single node should move to reduce error on the new graph—the global structure falls apart. The connections that used to be clean and consistent start to cross and tangle.

Traditional self-supervised methods encourage individual nodes to stay similar between tasks: keep node A's embedding stable, keep node B's stable. This prevents catastrophic forgetting of individual node identities. But it ignores the space between nodes. If node A drifts left and node B drifts right, the relationship between A and B—which depends on their relative positions—breaks, and the graph structure becomes incoherent.

In one sentence. Track not just individual nodes, but the matching between old and new node positions—use optimal transport to find the cheapest way to move nodes from their old positions to new ones while preserving how they relate to each other.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies how node embeddings are updated across tasks. Instead of only pushing individual nodes to stay stable, it applies a penalty if pairs of nodes move in ways that would twist their relationship. The specific object: the inter-node correspondence loss—an additional term added to the training objective that measures and penalizes distortion of the distance relationships between nodes.

the object it moves  =  inter-node correspondence penalty
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Structure distortion

measured quantity  =  inter-node correspondence drift

The hidden quantity is how much the pairwise relationships between nodes have changed from one task to the next. In plain terms: if node A and node B were close together in the old graph, are they still close in the new one? If they were far apart, are they still far? A measure of this drift is the sum of squared differences between old distances and new distances, across every pair of nodes. The paper uses optimal transport theory to estimate this—a mathematical framework that finds the cheapest way to move a whole population of points while preserving their relative positions.

The paper estimates this quantity by solving an optimization problem: given the old node positions and the new node embeddings, find the best matching of old nodes to new positions that minimizes total movement cost. This matching reveals which nodes have drifted too far and need to be pulled back.

Plain reading. In a graph, every pair of nodes has a distance (how far apart they are in the embedding space). When the graph changes and node embeddings update, those pairwise distances shift. If node A and node B were far apart, they should stay far apart. If they were close, they should stay close. Optimal transport measures the total cost of rearranging node positions while keeping those distances intact, and the method charges a penalty for large rearrangements.
Paper reading. The paper uses optimal transport to compute a Wasserstein distance—the cost of moving one cloud of points to match another cloud—between the old node embeddings and the new ones. This distance captures how much the global correspondence (the matching of old nodes to their updated counterparts) has shifted. By minimizing this distance during training on the new task, the model is incentivized to update nodes in ways that preserve the inter-node relationships from the previous task.
Why it works

Why it works: Coupling structure constraints to learning

Continual learning on graphs has two competing goals: (1) adapt to new graph edges by updating node embeddings, and (2) do not erase the global structure learned on the old graph. Naive methods achieve (1) but sacrifice (2). This paper achieves both by adding a constraint that lives at the graph level, not the node level. When each node updates, it now "feels" pressure not just to fit its own local edges, but to stay in a correspondence with its neighbors in the embedding space that matches the old structure.

Optimal transport provides the glue: it measures the global cost of rearranging the new node positions to match the old ones. During training on the new task, the model computes this cost and adds it as a regularization penalty to the loss function (the total error being minimized). This penalty is strongest when node correspondences get scrambled. Knowledge distillation—copying some of the learned weights from the old model into the new one—reinforces this by seeding the new model with structure knowledge. The result: nodes can drift to fit new edges, but the overall geometric structure of the graph survives intact.

The math: Blending three losses

First in words:

Total loss = task loss plus optimal transport cost plus knowledge distillation penalty

Then the compact form:

L = L_task + lambda_ot * W(E_old, E_new) + lambda_kd * L_distill

Every symbol:

A tiny worked example with four nodes
Old task: node embeddings are at positions [0.1, 0.2], [0.3, 0.1], [0.8, 0.9], [0.9, 0.8].
New task arrives: after initial training, new embeddings drift to [0.2, 0.25], [0.35, 0.15], [0.75, 0.85], [0.85, 0.75].
Compute L_task: new edges predict loss = 0.4 (example error on the new graph).
Compute W(E_old, E_new): sum of squared distances of optimal matching = 0.06.
Compute L_distill: KL divergence of predictions = 0.08.
With lambda_ot = 1.0 and lambda_kd = 0.5: Total loss = 0.4 + 1.0*0.06 + 0.5*0.08 = 0.50.
The model nudges embeddings slightly to reduce W and L_distill, keeping overall movement cost low.

The equation says: optimize the new task while penalizing large rearrangements (optimal transport) and keeping outputs similar to the old model (distillation).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stale optimal transport solution. If the optimal transport computation uses old node positions that have now become outdated, the correspondence penalty may pull nodes in the wrong direction. The paper must re-compute the transport distance frequently during training to stay in sync with the evolving embeddings.
Hyperparameter tuning is brittle. The balance between lambda_ot and lambda_kd controls how much structure is preserved versus how much the model can adapt. If structure preservation is too strong, the model cannot learn from new edges. If too weak, structure is lost. Different datasets may require different balances, making the method less plug-and-play.
Scalability to large graphs. Optimal transport requires comparing every pair of nodes, which costs time proportional to the number of nodes squared. On graphs with millions of nodes, this computation becomes expensive. The paper demonstrates results on moderate-scale datasets; scaling to billion-node graphs remains an open question.
Done right. The method works well when the graph structure is genuinely important (e.g., recommendation systems where item-to-item relationships matter), the datasets have a moderate number of nodes, and the hyperparameters are tuned to the problem domain.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small graph with five nodes and two tasks. Task 1: learn embeddings to predict a initial set of edges (say, a triangle plus one extra link). Task 2: introduce new edges and retrain. Measure two things: (1) prediction accuracy on the new edges, and (2) how much the pairwise distances between nodes have changed from Task 1 to Task 2. Compare three methods: naive node-level stability, a baseline that just uses knowledge distillation without optimal transport, and SAOT. The demo should show that SAOT keeps accuracy high on both tasks while minimizing unwanted changes in pairwise distances.

structure_preservation = 1 - sum_i_j(|distance_old_ij - distance_new_ij|) / sum_i_j(distance_old_ij)

The demo must make it visually obvious that the pairwise relationships (which nodes are close to which) stay intact through the continual learning process when using SAOT.
