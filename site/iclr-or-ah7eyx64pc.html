OCR-Reasoning Benchmark: Testing MLLMs on Text-Rich Image Problems

A benchmark dataset with step-by-step reasoning traces that reveals how much multimodal language models struggle with images full of text.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

When you show a large language model an image that has lots of text in it, plus a reasoning question, what does it really do? Most image-understanding models are tested on final answers alone. This benchmark adds something new: the actual step-by-step reasoning the model wrote to get there. By evaluating both the answers and the reasoning traces on 1,069 annotated examples, researchers can see exactly where models fail when images contain dense text and symbols.

Zero assumption version

Why is reasoning hard to measure when images contain text?

Multimodal language models (models that read both images and text) are everywhere. But when you test them on images packed with text—like chemistry problems on a chalkboard, invoices with small numbers, or logic puzzles on a worksheet—something strange happens: existing benchmarks only grade the final answer. If a model writes out the reasoning steps that led to the answer, nobody actually checks whether those reasoning steps make sense.

A beginner would try this: collect images with text, ask a model to answer questions about them, and measure accuracy on final answers. The problem is this misses the real issue. A model might guess the right answer by accident, or write reasoning that is completely nonsensical but happens to land on the correct final response. You are grading the destination but ignoring the journey.

Without seeing the reasoning traces, you cannot tell whether a model truly understands the problem or just memorized surface patterns. This means you cannot design better models, because you do not know what part of reasoning actually breaks.

In one sentence. The benchmark grades both the final answer and the step-by-step reasoning the model actually wrote, revealing how carefully a model thought through the problem.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect images

Gather college-level problems from chemistry, physics, spatial reasoning, and statistics with real text.

Annotators write questions

Humans design reasoning questions for each image that require multiple steps to solve.

Record step-by-step traces

Annotators write out the complete reasoning path from setup to final answer.

Run models and collect output

Feed each model the image and question; record the answer the model writes and the steps it took.

Grade both answer and reasoning

An expert grades whether the final answer is correct and whether the steps make logical sense.

What the paper changes

What the benchmark changes

Most image benchmarks annotate one thing: the correct final answer. This benchmark adds a second layer of annotation that most others lack: the correct step-by-step reasoning path. This dual annotation is the key difference. It means that for every question, humans have written down not just what the right answer is, but how a human would actually reason through the problem step by step.

the object it moves  =  step-by-step reasoning traces
The hidden quantity

Reasoning soundness: the hidden quality being measured

measured quantity  =  step-wise reasoning correctness

Reasoning soundness is the degree to which each intermediate step a model writes logically follows from the previous step and correctly applies the right principle or calculation. It is not about the final answer alone; it is about whether the model's chain of thought makes sense at each point.

The benchmark measures this by having an expert read the model output step by step and check: Does this follow from the last step? Is the math right? Does this fact apply here? Are there logical jumps or unsupported claims? A model might score high on reasoning soundness even if it misses the final answer—or score low even if it guesses right.

Why it works

Why evaluating reasoning traces reveals what models actually understand

A model can guess a correct final answer without understanding the path to get there. Imagine a student who memorized: whenever you see chemistry on a chalkboard, the answer is usually C. The student guesses C and is right, but has learned nothing about chemistry. By grading the reasoning steps, you force the model to show its work. If the steps are logical and precise, the model actually understood the problem. If the steps are vague, contradictory, or make unsupported leaps, you know the model is just pattern-matching.

Reasoning on images that contain dense text is harder than pure visual reasoning, because the model must read and parse text, connect text to visual elements, and chain multiple steps together. Seeing the reasoning trace reveals where this breaks: Did the model misread a number? Did it forget a constraint from an earlier step? Did it apply the wrong formula? Without step-by-step visibility, you cannot fix these gaps. With it, you can target improvements at the exact failure point.

Scoring both answer and reasoning

First in words:

overall_score = (answer_correct × 0.5) + (reasoning_sound × 0.5)

Then the compact form:

score = (acc_final * 0.5) + (acc_reasoning * 0.5)

Every symbol:

acc_finalfraction of final answers correct
acc_reasoningfraction of reasoning steps that are logically sound
0.5equal weight to answer and reasoning quality
A tiny worked example
Model A: final answer correct (100), reasoning steps all sound (100). Score = (100 × 0.5) + (100 × 0.5) = 100. True understanding.
Model B: final answer correct (100), reasoning steps nonsensical (20). Score = (100 × 0.5) + (20 × 0.5) = 60. Lucky guess.
Model C: final answer wrong (0), but reasoning steps are mostly sound (80). Score = (0 × 0.5) + (80 × 0.5) = 40. Close to understanding, made an error at the end.
The metric exposes that Model B got lucky; only Model A truly reasoned.

This metric prevents models from gaming the benchmark by guessing right without reasoning, because half the score comes from verifiable reasoning traces.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Models guess correct answers without sound reasoning. A model may stumble into the right answer by accident while writing nonsense reasoning steps. Old benchmarks would miss this; the new trace-based metric catches it.
Images with dense text are inherently harder to parse. Models struggle to read small text, extract numbers accurately, and track multiple constraints in a single image. Performance on images packed with text can be much lower than on simple visual scenes.
Reasoning coherence is subjective at the margins. When steps are partly correct or use alternative valid approaches, human annotators and LLM judges may disagree on soundness. The benchmark uses majority voting to mitigate this, but edge cases remain.
Done right: benchmark motivates model improvements. By exposing both answer gaps and reasoning gaps, the benchmark makes it clear where to focus: improve text parsing, multi-step logical chaining, or constraint tracking.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take any multimodal language model. Feed it an image with text (e.g., a math worksheet, an invoice, a chemistry problem). Ask it a reasoning question. Collect its written output: the steps it takes and the final answer. Now grade two things: Is the final answer correct? Are all the reasoning steps logically sound? Run this on at least 20 examples from diverse problem types (math, chemistry, spatial reasoning, logic puzzles). The demo must show that a model can score high on answer accuracy but low on reasoning soundness, proving that answer alone is not enough to measure understanding.

reasoning_soundness = (steps_with_sound_logic / total_steps)

If answer accuracy and reasoning soundness are not highly correlated, you have proven the benchmark reveals something new.
