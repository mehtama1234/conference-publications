MutAtlas: A PDB-Wide Energy-Guided Atlas of Protein Mutation Effects

Build a very large mutation table from structure-based and sequence-based models, then distill their relative mutation preferences while explicitly keeping track of where they disagree.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Protein mutation work suffers from a basic data problem. Laboratory measurements of which amino-acid substitutions help or hurt a protein are accurate, but there are far too few of them to cover the space of possible mutations. This paper builds a large supplementary signal source by enumerating single-site substitutions on resolved protein structures and scoring them with several model families. The central point is not that these model scores are identical to experiments. It is that they contain useful ranking information, and that their disagreements follow systematic patterns. The paper then trains a model that learns from those ranked preferences while treating disagreement as information to model rather than noise to average away.

Zero assumption version

Mutation data are sparse, but model-based substitutes disagree

At one site in a protein, there are many possible amino-acid replacements, and each replacement can change stability, binding, folding, or function. Experimental assays cover only a thin slice of that space, so zero-shot prediction methods are often asked to work with little direct supervision.

There are already several ways to score mutations without new experiments. Physics-based energy models use three-dimensional structure. Protein language models use sequence regularities learned from large corpora. Inverse-folding models score how compatible a sequence is with a structure. These sources do not simply repeat the same answer. They often rank substitutions differently, because each source is sensitive to different regularities. A method that averages them blindly can erase the very structure that could make them jointly useful.

The paper's move. Use many model-generated mutation rankings as a training resource, but learn the shared preference signal without pretending the sources agree everywhere.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Enumerate single-site substitutions.

For each resolved protein structure, list the candidate amino-acid replacements site by site, because the training signal needs broad coverage.

Score each substitution with multiple sources.

Use physics-based energies, protein language models, and inverse-folding models to produce mutation preferences, because each source sees a different part of the problem.

Convert raw scores into relative preference signals.

Train on which substitutions rank above others at a site rather than on one absolute number, because relative order is more comparable across sources.

Model disagreement explicitly.

Let the distilled model keep track of where sources line up and where they diverge, because cross-source conflict reflects different inductive biases rather than pure measurement error.

Test without experimental labels in training.

Evaluate the distilled preference model on experimental mutation benchmarks to see whether the synthetic rankings carried useful biological structure.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  site-specific relative mutation preference

The important quantity is not one universal mutation score that means the same thing across all models. It is the ordering of candidate substitutions at a given site: which replacements look more compatible with that local structural and sequence context than others.

That quantity matters because many downstream tasks only need a reliable ranking. In protein engineering, one often wants the top few candidate mutations to test. In disease interpretation, one wants to know which variants are more likely to be damaging than alternatives at the same position. Relative preference is the common object that different model families can contribute to even when their raw score scales are incompatible.

Why it works

Why relative preference distillation can help

Each source family emphasizes different constraints. Structure-based energies are sensitive to clashes and stability around the resolved fold. Protein language models capture regularities from natural sequence variation. Inverse-folding models ask whether a sequence fits a structure. When those sources disagree, the disagreement is often local and patterned, because they are looking at different evidence rather than flipping coins.

Training on relative preferences matters because it strips away part of the calibration mismatch. A source may produce harsher numbers than another while still ranking good and bad substitutions in a biologically useful order. By learning those within-site orders, the distilled model receives a more stable training signal than it would from raw score fusion.

Explicit disagreement modeling matters because naive consensus can be wrong for systematic reasons. If one source is consistently better for buried residues and another is better for surface positions, a fused model should learn that split because the local context determines which source is more trustworthy there rather than compress both into one average preference. The paper's reported gain comes from keeping both the shared signal and the conflict structure.

The distilled target

First in words:

Learn one preference model whose output agrees with the relative ordering induced by several sources, while assigning separate terms for shared agreement and disagreement across those sources.

Then the compact form:

distill_loss = shared_preference_loss + lambda disagreement_loss

Every symbol:

shared_preference_losspenalty when the distilled ranking disagrees with the common ordering signal across sources at a site
disagreement_losspenalty or uncertainty term that records structured conflict among the sources instead of forcing collapse to one vote
lambdathe weight that controls how strongly cross-source disagreement shapes the final model
distill_lossthe training objective minimized without experimental mutation labels
A tiny worked example
At one site, three sources rank substitution A above B above C.
At another site, the energy model ranks D above E, but the language model reverses them.
The first site contributes mainly to the shared preference term, because the sources agree on the ordering.
The second site contributes to both terms, because the model should learn the candidate ordering while also learning that this context triggers source conflict.
A distilled model that ignores the conflict term may overtrust the average ranking even where one source family is systematically misleading.

The training signal is about ordering candidates at a site, because that is easier to align across model families than their raw numeric scales.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

All sources can share the same blind spot. If every zero-shot source misses an important functional constraint, the distilled model can inherit that miss, because more synthetic coverage does not create evidence that none of the sources contain.
Synthetic rankings may reflect solved structures more than real cell context. A mutation that looks favorable in a resolved structure may still fail in vivo, because expression, binding partners, and regulation may be absent from the input models.
Disagreement can be hard to interpret. A conflict term may capture source-specific strengths, but it can also absorb unhelpful noise if the sources disagree for shallow reasons.
Done right. The approach is most persuasive when evaluation shows that relative preference distillation transfers to experimental benchmarks, because that is the check that the synthetic atlas carried real biological order.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a protein benchmark with site-level mutation measurements. Build synthetic rankings from at least two zero-shot sources, then train one model on raw averaged scores and another on within-site preference distillation with a disagreement term.

The demo should test top-k mutation ranking and pairwise ordering accuracy against the experimental labels. A useful result is that the preference model ranks better even though it never saw those labels during training, because it learned the transferable part of the synthetic order rather than the accidental calibration of each source.

pairwise_rank_accuracy = correctly_ordered_mutation_pairs / total_test_pairs

The point is to show that synthetic mutation tables become more useful once the model learns from relative preference and from disagreement structure together.
