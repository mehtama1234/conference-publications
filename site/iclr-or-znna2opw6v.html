KnowledgeSmith: Unified Editing and Unlearning

Editing and unlearning are the same optimization problem in two disguises: one nudges knowledge toward a target, the other nudges it toward neutral silence.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

When an LLM learns something new, or needs to forget something wrong, it does not work like a human brain. This paper asks: are edits (teaching new facts) and unlearning (removing old ones) really different? Or are they the same mechanism used backward? KnowledgeSmith shows they are the same constrained optimization problem, which lets researchers study how edits ripple through connected knowledge.

Zero assumption version

Knowledge in a black box stays broken

An LLM is a mass of numbers (weights). When you want to correct a fact or erase a memory, you have to jam a corrected fact in, or run an expensive unlearning algorithm. But the LLM's internal knowledge is hidden — it is tangled up with connected facts, like a web. Editing one node (say, correcting 'Paris is the capital of France') might accidentally wreck a neighbor (say, the model forgets that Paris is in France). Unlearning one fact might leave breadcrumbs everywhere.

A beginner might try to just fine-tune the model on new examples, but that is slow and memory-hungry, and it often breaks unrelated knowledge. The real problem: existing editing and unlearning techniques are studied in isolation, on single facts, so nobody knows if they have the same structure or if they work the same way at scale.

The method in one sentence. KnowledgeSmith unifies editing and unlearning as one constrained optimization: both change model weights to align knowledge with a target pattern (a new fact for editing, silence for unlearning), subject to a preservation cost for related knowledge.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Cast as constrained optimization

Both editing and unlearning are written as: minimize distance from a target pattern (a new fact for editing, silence for unlearning), subject to preserving unrelated knowledge.

Generate structured test sets

Automatically build a knowledge graph (root concepts, mid-level categories, specific facts) and generate 360,000 test cases across multiple levels and data scales.

Apply editing or unlearning

Run the actual method (ROME, LoRA, or another technique) on targeted facts while measuring collateral damage to neighbors.

Measure propagation and trade-offs

Watch how edits ripple across the knowledge graph. Measure the trade-off: strong brakes preserve neighbor facts but also prevent the model from learning new things, so it trades off between reliability and general capability.

Compare across LLMs and scales

Test on multiple language models and data sizes to reveal which methods are stable, which are drifty, and how humans would differ.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the model's weights (the numbers inside the LLM) using editing or unlearning. The specifics differ by method (ROME moves MLPs, MEMIT scales them, LoRA adds a low-rank adapter), but all are instances of the same framework: change weights to align internal representations with a target pattern, while paying a cost for collateral damage.

the object it moves  =  weights and representations
The hidden quantity

Knowledge plasticity and propagation

measured quantity  =  how far an edit ripples

KnowledgeSmith introduces a hidden quantity: the plasticity of a knowledge fact — how easily the model's internal understanding changes when you edit it. For example, a broad concept like 'France' might be baked into many weight positions and harder to change than a narrow fact like 'the capital is Paris'. The paper also measures propagation: when you edit one fact, which neighbors get corrupted?

The paper estimates this by building a knowledge graph (root concepts linked to mid-level categories linked to specific facts) and testing edits at each level. It measures how much the model's answer changes on related queries. This reveals that LLMs do not behave like humans: editing a narrow fact at the leaf level does not propagate cleanly, and there is no consistent hierarchy of 'importance'.

Plain reading. Plasticity is how much a part of the model's understanding will shift if you edit it. A loose, general concept (like 'a city') shifts easily because it is mentioned in many weights. Paper reading. The paper defines propagation as the performance change on queries related to the edited fact (siblings in the knowledge graph). Plasticity scaling refers to how the sensitivity to edits varies across hierarchical levels: in human memory, editing a root concept is hard but affects many descendants; in LLMs, the pattern is messier and inconsistent.
Why it works

Why the same math works for two opposite goals

Editing and unlearning solve the same constrained optimization problem. The key is the target: for editing, the target is a new fact ('Paris is now the capital of Germany'); for unlearning, the target is neutral (the model gives no answer, or equally likely answers for all options). Both minimize the distance from that target, plus a preservation cost that penalizes changes to unrelated weights. The preservation cost acts as a brake: the larger it is, the more the model refuses to change, and the safer the edit becomes.

Why does this work? Because editing is essentially pulling the model's internal representations toward a new target, while unlearning is pushing them toward noise. The same optimization machinery handles both. But the trade-off is clear: a strong preservation cost (which keeps unrelated knowledge safe) also makes editing weaker and slower. So there is a hard trade-off between consistency (reliability of the edit) and capacity (overall model performance). LLMs also differ from humans: they do not update knowledge hierarchically the way a human would, so edits at different levels have unpredictable ripple effects.

Math after the picture

The optimization equation

First in words:

Minimize task alignment loss plus preservation regularization, subject to tolerance bounds.

Then the compact form:

minimize L_task(q_target, theta_prime) + lambda * R(theta_prime, theta) + I(preserve_constraint)

Every symbol:

L_taskdistance between model output and target (new fact for editing, silence for unlearning)
q_targetthe target distribution (corrected fact for editing, neutral for unlearning)
theta_primethe new, modified weights
thetathe original weights
lambdahow much to penalize deviation from original (higher = safer but slower edit)
R(theta_prime, theta)regularization cost, e.g., norm of weight changes
I(preserve_constraint)indicator that related knowledge must stay above a threshold
tolerance bounds eta+ and epsilonhow much change is allowed in target accuracy and preservation accuracy
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model has 5 weights: w1, w2, w3, w4, w5. Current outputs: 0.8 correct on target fact.
Edit goal: output should be 0.1 (for unlearning) or 1.0 (for editing).
Distance from 0.8 to 0.1 is 0.7. Cost to move weight w1 by 0.1 is 0.01 (squared change). Cost to move w2 by 0.05 is 0.0025.
With lambda = 10 (strong brake), total cost = 0.7 (task) + 10 * (0.01 + 0.0025) = 0.7 + 0.1125 = 0.8125.
With lambda = 1 (weak brake), total cost = 0.7 + 0.0125 = 0.7125 (edit is faster but nearby facts may break).
The trade-off: higher lambda means safer neighbors but slower convergence to the target.

The equation shows why editing and unlearning are the same: only q_target differs. The brake (lambda and R) controls how aggressively the model changes, balancing edit reliability against collateral damage.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Inconsistent hierarchies. LLMs do not respect the human idea of hierarchical knowledge. Editing a root concept like 'France' does not cleanly propagate down to leaf facts like 'Paris capital'. Different models show different propagation patterns, making it hard to predict collateral damage.
Capacity-consistency trade-off. The brake (lambda) that keeps neighbors safe also reduces the model's overall performance on new tasks, because the model refuses to change weights even when change is appropriate. A model with a strong brake is conservative and reliable but dumb. No single lambda works for all cases.
Drifty low-rank methods. LoRA-based editing seems flexible but is unstable at scale. Small edits compound unpredictably, and the model drifts away from its original behavior, because each edit moves a low-rank adapter that affects every forward pass without being tied to specific facts. Methods like ROME and MEMIT, which target specific weight positions tied to specific MLP layers, are more reliable because the edit stays localized.
Done right. Use a knowledge graph to test propagation. Choose a method (ROME, MEMIT) known to be stable. Measure the trade-off explicitly before committing to a strategy. Accept that LLMs require different tuning per hierarchy level.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small model or LLM, create a knowledge graph (3-4 root concepts, each with 3-4 categories, each with 3-4 facts), and edit one leaf fact using ROME or LoRA. Measure accuracy on the edited fact, its siblings, and distant facts. Increase the brake (lambda) and repeat. Plot the trade-off curve: as lambda rises, sibling accuracy stays high (good) but target accuracy plateaus (bad). Repeat with unlearning: unlearning should preserve more siblings from the start, even without a high brake.

consistency_gain = (neighbor_acc_after - neighbor_acc_before) / neighbor_acc_before; edit_success = target_acc_after - target_acc_before; plot lambda vs (edit_success, consistency_gain) to show the trade-off.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: you cannot have both a perfect edit AND perfect neighbors. The brake exists for a reason, and no lambda wins everywhere.
