Dyslexify: Typographic Defense for CLIP

Block text-in-image attacks by disabling the attention heads that read embedded text, without retraining the model.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

CLIP is a vision-language model trained to match images with text descriptions. Attackers exploit this by hiding text inside images; CLIP reads the hidden text and misclassifies the image or generates harmful captions. Dyslexify finds which attention heads (decision circuits inside the model) extract text information and surgically ablates (disconnects) them. The result: the model becomes blind to text in images, blocking the attack while keeping normal vision tasks working.

Zero assumption version

Text hiding in plain sight

CLIP vision encoders read both images AND any text embedded in those images. An attacker writes a prompt like 'a dog' in tiny letters inside a picture of a cat. CLIP sees the cat pixels, but also reads the hidden text, and since it learned that text is a strong signal, it classifies the image as a dog instead. Worse, CLIP is used in caption systems and language-model jailbreaks, so hidden text can trigger unsafe outputs.

A naive defense is to blur or remove text from images before they reach CLIP. But that damages normal vision tasks — medical images, screenshots, and educational diagrams all contain text that humans and the model should read. You cannot simply scrub all text. The real problem is that the attacker can sneak text in where nobody expects it.

The method in one sentence. Find the specific attention heads (internal decision circuits) that extract text, then disconnect them. Without these heads to read and forward text information, the model has no way to know text exists in the image.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Probe: find text-sensitive heads

Run images with and without hidden text through CLIP. Measure which attention heads respond strongly to the pieces of text (text encoded into small numbers).

Rank by causal impact

For each candidate head, surgically disconnect it and measure: does hidden-text attack success drop? Keep heads with the strongest impact.

Identify the circuit

Collect all heads in the latter half of the model (layers 12–23 in a 24-layer model) that jointly transmit text info. This circuit is the target.

Ablate (zero out the circuit)

Permanently set these head outputs to zero. No retraining needed; just clip the wires.

Test clean and attack

Measure normal ImageNet accuracy (should barely drop) and attack success rate (should collapse to near random).

What the paper changes

Dyslexify changes the internal wiring of the model: it permanently disconnects specific attention head outputs before they flow to the rest of the network.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: typographic signal flow

measured quantity  =  text-to-summary pathway strength

Typographic signal flow is how strongly information about embedded text moves through the model's attention heads toward the final decision (the cls token, which is a summary of everything the model saw). In a normal CLIP model, this flow is high — text tokens activate specific heads, those heads pass the signal forward, and it influences the final classification.

Dyslexify estimates this flow by probing: it measures how much each attention head's output correlates with the presence of text tokens, then ranks heads by their causal impact (how much does disabling this head reduce attack success?). Heads with high typographic signal flow are the ones to ablate.

Why it works

Why disconnecting text heads stops attacks

CLIP's architecture flows like this: image tokens and text tokens both enter the model, pass through multiple layers of attention heads, and eventually reach a summary token (cls token) that represents the whole input. Each attention head is a small decision-maker that reads certain tokens and passes information forward.

The attacker's goal is to make text tokens (the hidden attack text) influence the cls token's final message about the image. This requires two things: first, a head must read the text token (pull its signal forward), and second, a downstream head must treat that signal as important. Dyslexify breaks the first link: by zeroing out the heads that read text tokens, it prevents text information from entering the pipeline at all.

Why doesn't this break normal vision? Because text is only a subset of what these heads do. A head might read text 20% of the time and read other visual patterns 80% of the time. When you zero it out, you lose the 20% but keep working on the 80%. The model's vision still functions because other heads (not text-specialized) carry the visual signals. The cls token still gets a complete picture of the image; it just cannot hear the hidden text whisper.

Math after the picture

The causal impact formula

First in words:

attack success before minus attack success after, divided by attack success before

Then the compact form:

causal_impact = (success_before - success_after) / success_before

Every symbol:

success_beforefraction of attack images misclassified before ablating a head
success_afterfraction of attack images misclassified after ablating a head
causal_impacthow much ablating this one head reduces the attack, as a percentage
A tiny worked example
Before ablating head: 100 attack images, 80 successfully trick CLIP into wrong label. Success rate = 80/100 = 0.80.
After ablating candidate head A: still 80/100 success. Impact = (0.80 - 0.80) / 0.80 = 0. This head does not matter.
After ablating candidate head B: only 20/100 success. Impact = (0.80 - 0.20) / 0.80 = 0.75. Ablating head B reduces attack success by 75%, so head B is text-critical.
Rank heads by impact score. Ablate the top-ranking heads until attack success approaches random (10% on ImageNet-100 with 100 classes).

Heads with high causal impact are the ones carrying text signal; ablating them breaks the attacker's pipeline.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Over-ablating harms vision. If you ablate heads too aggressively, you lose legitimate vision performance. The method must carefully rank heads by causal impact to keep the most important ones.
Adversary adapts to a known circuit. Once the typographic circuit is published, an attacker who knows which heads are ablated can craft images that rely on other heads or use adversarial pixels instead of text. The method is not bulletproof against attackers who see the defense in advance.
Distribution shift: model sees new text patterns. The identified heads are tuned to text in ImageNet-style images. If text appears in medical images or documents with different fonts, texture, or layout, a new circuit might emerge.
Training-free simplicity. Ablating without retraining is the key strength: drop-in replacement, no gradient descent, reproducible. The tradeoff is that finetuning (retraining for a bit) can achieve slightly lower attack success at the cost of setup work.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test: create 50 images of common objects (dog, cat, plane) and embed adversarial text in 25 of them. Run baseline CLIP on these images and measure attack success. Then swap in Dyslexify CLIP (with the typographic heads zeroed out) and measure attack success again. The demo must show a large drop (from 80% to near random 10%), while normal clean images (no hidden text) stay classified correctly.

attack_success = (wrong_answers_with_hidden_text) / (total_attacked_images)

The demo's single key finding: the attacker's hidden text stops working the moment the typographic circuit is disabled.
