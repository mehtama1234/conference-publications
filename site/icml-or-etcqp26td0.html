Klein Hyperbolic Metric Learning

Use Klein's curved space with straight-line shortest paths instead of Poincaré's curved paths, so the neural network's linear transformations match the actual distances.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

When you have hierarchical data—like image categories that nest inside each other—you want a space where things that share a parent live close together, and distance shrinks as you zoom in. Hyperbolic spaces (curved inward spaces like a saddle) give you that for free: they allocate lots of room at the edges for fine detail and less room in the center for coarse groupings. The standard approach, the Poincaré model, uses curved shortest paths. This paper uses the Klein model, which is also hyperbolic but has straight-line shortest paths, matching the linear transformations that neural networks use. Both capture hierarchy equally well because both are hyperbolic; Klein is cleaner and faster because its straight paths align with the network's own weight-table multiplications, so no correction overhead.

Zero assumption version

Hierarchies are hard to embed in flat spaces

Imagine sorting animals: dogs, cats, birds all sit under animals; poodles, labs sit under dogs. When you put these in a flat (Euclidean) space, you run into a problem: the distance from poodle to labrador must be smaller than the distance from poodle to cat. But in a flat space, you cannot cram all the distances correctly at once—you either compress the boundary or stretch the center.

A beginner tries: use a flat space anyway and just train hard. It fails because the space itself is the wrong shape: no amount of training fixes a mismatch between what your data looks like (deeply nested) and what your space can express (all directions treat distance the same).

Hyperbolic spaces (curved inward, like a saddle) solve this: space allocates lots of room near the edges for fine distinctions and less room in the center for coarse groupings. Standard work uses the Poincaré model, where the shortest paths between points curve (geodesics are bent). But these curved paths do not match the straight-line transformations that neural networks use by default.

In one sentence. Switch from Poincaré's curved shortest paths to Klein's straight-line shortest paths, so the curved space and the neural network's linear transformations work as one.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of computing distances via curved Poincaré paths, compute them via straight-line Klein paths. The Klein model is still hyperbolic—it still warps space inward—but its geodesics are straight. This means a linear projection through a neural network layer directly encodes a true distance in the space.

The hidden quantity

The geometric alignment score

measured quantity  =  alignment of projections to geodesics

The paper silently measures one quantity: how well do the straight-line projections in the neural network match the true shortest paths in the space? In Poincaré, they do not match at all—curved paths are not linear. In Klein, they match perfectly—a linear projection IS a shortest path (called a geodesic, the shortest route in a curved space).

The paper does not compute an explicit alignment score, but it uses it implicitly: Klein is chosen specifically because it is a projective space, meaning that straight-line transformations (matrix multiplications) preserve distances. If you apply a matrix to points in Klein space, you still get valid distances in the result.

Why it works

Why straight lines solve the problem

The neural network learns features by applying linear transformations—matrix multiplications in each layer. Each transformation is a straight line through the space of features (lists of numbers). In Poincaré space (a curved inward space), the true shortest path between two points curves; a straight line undershoots or overshoots the actual distance. This creates a gap: the network computes linearly, but the space is curved, and they do not agree. Training fights this mismatch constantly.

Klein space is different. It is still hyperbolic—space still curves inward, giving you room to encode hierarchy—but its shortest paths are straight lines. So when the neural network applies a straight-line transformation, that transformation IS a valid shortest path in the space. No mismatch, no fighting the space. The network and the space move together. The learning nudges (called gradients, which point the direction to move numbers to reduce error) flow cleanly because there are no hidden curvature corrections needed. The paper derives stable formulas so that numerical traps (like dividing by near-zero) do not wreck training.

Math after the picture

The Klein metric formula

First in words:

distance in Klein space = the hyperbolic distance between two vectors, computed by taking the inverse hyperbolic cosine of a dot product scaled by a curvature term. No curves, all straight lines.

Then the compact form:

d_K(u, v) = acosh(1 + 2 * ||u - v||^2 / ((1 - ||u||^2)(1 - ||v||^2)))

Every symbol:

u, vtwo feature vectors
||u||the length of vector u
||u - v||^2the squared distance between u and v in flat space
acoshinverse hyperbolic cosine, the function that measures distance in hyperbolic space
d_Kthe true Klein distance
A tiny worked example
Suppose u = (0, 0.3) and v = (0.1, 0) in the Klein model, both inside the unit ball.
||u|| = 0.3, ||v|| = 0.1, so (1 - ||u||^2) = 0.91 and (1 - ||v||^2) = 0.99.
||u - v||^2 = 0.1^2 + 0.3^2 = 0.01 + 0.09 = 0.10.
Numerator of the fraction: 2 * 0.10 = 0.20.
Denominator: 0.91 * 0.99 = 0.90.
Argument to acosh: 1 + 0.20 / 0.90 = 1 + 0.222 = 1.222.
d_K = acosh(1.222) ≈ 0.626.
Compare to flat Euclidean distance: sqrt(0.10) ≈ 0.316. Klein distance is larger, as expected in hyperbolic space—room for hierarchy.

The acosh of a scaled dot product replaces the curve-finding of Poincaré; it stays finite and avoids numerical cliffs.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Feature lists outside the unit ball. Klein space requires all feature lists (vectors, just ordered lists of numbers) to stay inside the unit ball (length less than 1). If the network projects a feature to length bigger than 1, the distance formula breaks—logarithms of negative numbers, division by zero. The paper uses clipping (capping values) and renormalization (rescaling to fit) to fix this; if not done right, training explodes.
Numerical underflow in the acosh. When two points are very close, the argument to acosh approaches 1, and acosh(1 + epsilon) becomes unstable for tiny epsilon. The paper derives alternative formulas for the near case; without them, distances become garbage.
No hierarchy in the data. If your dataset has no true hierarchy—if categories are independent—then curved inward space is wasted. The method assumes the structure exists; it just reveals it. On flat datasets with no nesting, Poincaré and Klein perform the same.
Done right: gradient flow and interpretability. When the alignment is correct, gradients flow back cleanly through the projections into the network weights. Hierarchical structure emerges visually in the embeddings—you can see the tree.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny hierarchical dataset: a few hand-drawn classes (e.g. triangles, squares, circles, and within triangles: pointy vs wide). Train two embedders side-by-side, one with Klein metric, one with Poincaré. Check that both converge (training is stable). Then measure how closely items with shared parents sit together: for each test image, find the closest 5 neighbors and count how many share a parent category.
