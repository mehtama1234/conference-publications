Pushing robustness to the limits

These papers build AI systems that keep working correctly even when faced with adversarial attacks, distributional shifts, or unusual inputs that weren't in training.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model trained to classify images looks at millions of pictures and learns patterns. It works great on the test set. But then you deploy it, and the real world has slightly different lighting, new objects, unusual angles. The model's performance drops. Or you have a text model trained on news articles, and you ask it to summarize scientific papers written in a different style. This is distribution shift—the test data comes from a different statistical distribution than the training data, and the model wasn't prepared for it. An even worse case is an adversarial attack: someone deliberately modifies an image by a tiny amount—invisible to humans—that causes the model to misclassify it. Or someone adds imperceptible noise to audio that changes speech recognition. These attacks show that models are brittle in surprising ways.

Why is this hard? Because models learn surface patterns, not deep understanding. A vision model might learn that 'curved white things near the top of the image means swan,' and it will see swans everywhere if you give it images with that pattern, even if it's a plastic toy. A language model trained on clean English text might struggle when faced with typos, slang, or writing from a different region. And defending against adversarial attacks seems almost paradoxical: the network can make tiny changes that flip predictions, but those changes are invisible to humans. The model has learned features that humans didn't use, and those features are fragile. Making a model resistant to one kind of attack often makes it vulnerable to another, because the model learns to use different features to compensate, and those new features might have their own vulnerabilities. There's a fundamental tension between accuracy on clean data and resistance to perturbations, because techniques that help ignore irrelevant small changes often also ignore real differences in the input that should lead to different predictions.

Researchers approach this on several fronts. One is adversarial training: teach the model to resist adversarial examples by training on them, so it learns to ignore irrelevant small changes. Another is certified defenses: prove mathematically that a model will work correctly within a certain range, using verification techniques that give guarantees rather than just hoping it works. Others study why models are brittle in the first place, trying to understand whether brittleness is an intrinsic limitation—something unavoidable because of how neural networks learn by fitting data—or something that can be overcome with better training techniques. Still others look at distribution shift directly: train on multiple domains at once, which forces the model to find general patterns that work across all of them rather than memorizing one domain's specific quirks, since features that only work on one dataset won't transfer. Some papers also study detection: can you recognize when the model is seeing out-of-distribution inputs and fall back to a safer strategy that admits uncertainty rather than guessing? The fundamental goal is to make models that work in the messy real world, not just on tidy test sets, because deployment conditions are always different from training and the model will encounter surprises.

How the work breaks down

The papers in this subtheme fall into five main directions. First, researchers are building mathematical guarantees that models will behave correctly within specific boundaries (certified defenses). Second, they are studying how adversarial examples break models and how to make models resist them through training and design. Third, they are tackling the unique safety challenges of large language models, where attacks try to trick models into saying harmful things. Fourth, they are protecting vision-language models that process both images and text, which have their own vulnerabilities. Fifth, they are defending against subtle attacks like backdoors that hide malicious behavior inside a model. Each direction addresses a different way a model can fail when facing real-world challenges.

Mathematically Certified Safety

The core challenge in this group is that adversarial training (training on examples that fool the model) and other defense approaches often don't provide guarantees. A model might work well in tests but fail against a new attack no one thought of yet. Researchers here pursue a different path: prove mathematically that a model will work correctly even if the input is perturbed (changed) by up to a certain amount. This mathematical proof is called certification.

HyCAS combines special attention mechanisms with randomized smoothing—a technique that wraps the neural network to certify it stays correct within a radius of the input. By mixing deterministic attention with randomness, it achieves certified accuracy 7.3 percentage points higher than previous work while keeping the clean accuracy intact. Dual Randomized Smoothing solves a different problem: previous methods use one noise level everywhere, but real-world changes to images vary. This paper uses different noise levels in different regions to certify safety at both tiny and large perturbation amounts simultaneously. Certified Safety under Heterogeneous Perturbations extends certification to mixed threats—when images and text are both attacked at once—by deriving closed-form certificates that generalize existing smoothing methods to joint perturbations. LipNeXt scales Lipschitz-based certification (constraining how fast the model output changes with small input changes) from tiny models up to billion-sized models on ImageNet by using efficient low-precision training, improving certified accuracy 8 percentage points at a specific perturbation size. Certifying the Full YOLO Pipeline proves that object detection pipelines remain correct within bounds, scaling certification to practical computer vision systems like YOLO that were too complex before. These five papers share a philosophy: if you can prove the model works, you don't have to guess whether a future attack will slip through.

These papers build on each other incrementally, each tackling a new barrier to certification. The shared lesson across all five is that certification requires understanding where the model switches from one answer to another, not just training it to output correct labels on test data.

Adversarial Training and Attack Understanding

Adversarial training—teaching a model to resist attacks by training on examples that fool it—is the oldest and most widely used defense. But it is imperfect because when you force the model to ignore small changes (by training on adversarial examples), it must learn to ignore many features, and this makes it less accurate on clean data and vulnerable to attacks it wasn't trained against. The papers here show that the model's architecture (how layers connect) and the details of training (which samples are used, how the learning signals propagate through layers) shape whether these trade-offs can be overcome.

TriQDef addresses a specific problem: quantized networks (models compressed to use lower precision numbers) are more vulnerable to adversarial patches—small physical objects that fool vision systems. It works by disrupting the mathematical signals (gradients) that attackers use to craft patches, breaking both the semantic pattern and the optimization path the attacker relies on, cutting attack success rate by 40 percent. Understanding Differential Attention reveals why attention mechanisms in transformers have their own vulnerabilities: differential attention (where different positions get different processing) is more sensitive to small input changes, leading to higher attack success rates than standard attention. This shows that the architecture choice itself shapes how fragile a model is. DRIFT uses filtered transformations—preprocessing that distorts images in strategic ways—to make adversarial examples less transferable across models, working for both convolutional networks (layered image-processing networks) and vision transformers (attention-based image models). Nasty Adversarial Training improves the training procedure itself by adding a probability sparsity regularization: this forces the model to build decisions using fewer features, which reduces the surface area for attacks. Toward Understanding Adversarial Distillation reveals a fundamental problem when transferring safety from a teacher model to a student: on samples the teacher never learned well, the student overfits and becomes fragile, so the paper proposes using teacher uncertainty to identify which training samples are genuinely learnable.

Together these papers show that safety isn't just about the defense—it's about the model architecture, the training dynamics, and understanding which parts of the problem are fundamentally hard to solve.

Large Language Model Safety and Alignment

Large language models are trained to be helpful, but they can be manipulated through jailbreaks—carefully crafted prompts that trick the model into saying unsafe things. The challenge is that removing harmful behavior during training often creates side effects: the model might become overly cautious and refuse benign requests, or it might learn spurious shortcuts that fail on similar-sounding new queries.

Aligner, Diagnose Thyself trains models to fix themselves using intrinsic feedback signals from the model's own outputs, rather than relying on human judgments alone. By fusing multiple diagnostic signals together, it outperforms methods that use any single signal, meaning the model learns a more nuanced understanding of safety. Dual-Space Smoothness tackles the problem of unlearning—removing knowledge from a model so it doesn't generate harmful content—by smoothing the model's representations in two spaces simultaneously. This prevents the model from just finding a different path to the harmful output. Safety Mirage uncovers a specific failure mode: when you fine-tune a model to refuse harmful requests, it sometimes learns spurious correlations (word patterns) rather than true safety principles, so new attacks with different wording slip through. The paper shows machine unlearning can cut attack success rate by 60 percent by erasing these spurious shortcuts. Antibody defends against harmful fine-tuning attacks, where an attacker fine-tunes the model on toxic data to corrupt it. By dampening the gradient signals from harmful examples during fine-tuning, Antibody lets benign fine-tuning proceed normally while blocking malicious changes. Sysformer shows that frozen models (models you cannot retrain) can be made safer by learning adaptive system prompts—invisible instructions prepended to user inputs that guard the model without modifying its weights, achieving up to 80 percent improvement in refusing harmful requests.

These five papers reveal a central tension in LLM safety: you want the model to refuse harmful requests, but you don't want it to refuse benign ones, and both behaviors use similar patterns that the model must learn to distinguish. The best defenses teach the model principles that generalize, rather than surface patterns that break on new variations.

Vision-Language Model Safety

Vision-language models process both images and text, making them richer than pure image or text models, but also creating new attack surfaces. An attacker can change the image, the text, or the alignment between them. Models must withstand adversarial changes while maintaining accuracy on clean data—a tension that is especially hard when processing two different types of input at once.

AdPO uses preference optimization—training the model to prefer safe outputs over unsafe ones—combined with transfer from smaller models to larger ones, achieving 23 percent accuracy gains on multiple benchmarks in both adversarial and clean settings. The paper on trading inference compute for safety finds that spending more computation at test time—running the model multiple times with different settings and picking the best answer—improves safety proportionally to how safe the base model already is, trading inference time for safety. Adversarial Attacks Already Tell the Answer (DBD) is the counterintuitive insight that adversarial examples, which fool the model, actually reveal the bias direction the attacker exploited; by using this directional bias at test time, the model can recover correct predictions and even sometimes beat clean accuracy. Strengthening Vision-Language Models via Test-Time Prompt Adaptation learns to adjust prompts at test time—dynamically rewording the query based on the image—to maintain safety without retraining. Self-Calibrated Consistency works across all 22 zero-shot benchmarks by making the model consistent: if the model is confident on a noisy version of the image, it should be equally confident on the clean version.

These five papers converge on a principle: rather than changing the model, they change how you use it—either by steering the preference during training, spending more inference compute, using adversarial insights, adapting the prompt, or enforcing consistency. This is often simpler than retraining and can be deployed immediately.

Specialized Threats: Backdoors, Fingerprinting, and Detection

Some attacks don't poison the training data or perturb inputs; instead, they embed hidden behaviors inside the model itself. A backdoored model might work normally 99.9 percent of the time, but when you ask it a question containing a secret trigger (a certain phrase or image pattern), it activates the malicious behavior. These hidden attacks are hard to detect because they don't affect normal operation. This group addresses how to detect backdoors, how to prove you own your model, and how to protect diffusion models from such attacks.

Auditing Black-Box LLMs detects if someone swapped out your model or fine-tuned it maliciously by running a rank-based uniformity test—a statistical test that looks at how the model's answers spread across possibilities, requires few queries, and works without seeing inside the model. Fingerprinting Deep Neural Networks establishes theoretical bounds on what makes a valid fingerprint and enables practical fingerprint generation, so you can mathematically prove you created a model. AEGIS hardens diffusion models (models that generate images by removing noise) against backdoor and adversarial attacks, improving safety and maintaining utility. Broadening the Backdoor Basin shows that backdoors in LLMs often collapse—the hidden trigger stops working after fine-tuning—and proposes a method to make backdoors stay active across multiple fine-tuning steps. From Internal Diagnosis to External Auditing detects backdoors in vision models in real-time using a vision-language model to audit the outputs, suppressing attack success rate to below 1 percent on CIFAR-10 across 11 different backdoor types. Improving the Sensitivity of Backdoor Detectors enhances existing detection methods with class subspace orthogonalization, a technique you can add to any detector that removes normal features so detectors focus on anomalies from actual backdoors.

Together, these papers treat backdoors as a cat-and-mouse game: attackers try to hide them, defenders try to detect them, and researchers develop new detection methods and new hiding techniques. The defense advantage is that you only need to find one backdoor to know something is wrong, while attackers need to hide all their triggers perfectly.
