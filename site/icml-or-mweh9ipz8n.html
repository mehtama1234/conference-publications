Neural Implicit Action Fields

Instead of predicting fixed lists of hand and arm positions, predict smooth continuous motion curves that work at any speed.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot arms learn from videos and instructions, but today's methods predict action as snapshots—discrete poses locked to whatever speed the training data used. If the robot speed changes or must be smooth (no jitter), the snapshots fail because they don't define what happens between poses or whether velocity changes abruptly. This paper replaces those snapshots with smooth mathematical curves that can be queried at any moment in time, naturally supporting fast and slow control, and mathematically guaranteeing smooth velocity profiles that prevent jerky motion.

Why snapshots break smooth motion

Today's robot models learn to predict actions as a series of snapshots: position 1, position 2, position 3, each occurring at fixed time intervals (say, every 0.1 seconds). These snapshots come from training data collected at a single fixed speed, at exactly 10 Hz. If a robot needs to move at 20 Hz or 5 Hz instead, or if you ask for the arm's position at some time between snapshots, there is no natural answer.

A beginner would try to guess: interpolate between the snapshots, filling in the in-between positions by assuming the motion continues at the same speed. But physical motion is not linear motion between discrete points. Real arms have smooth curves, with the velocity starting slow, speeding up, and slowing down. When you interpolate linearly, you get jittery, jerky motion. Worse, you cannot guarantee that velocity changes smoothly—the arm might accelerate abruptly, wasting energy and stressing the joints.

The method: Instead of snapshots, predict continuous smooth curves (mathematical functions) for position and velocity. These curves work at any speed and guarantee smooth motion.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts what the model predicts: not a discrete list of poses, but the parameters (weights and learnable settings) that define a smooth continuous curve. The vision-language model becomes a 'hypernetwork'—instead of outputting actions directly, it outputs numbers that configure a smaller neural network, and that smaller network computes the smooth curve on demand.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: motion continuity

measured quantity  =  Higher-order smoothness (how jerk-free the motion is)

The hidden quantity is how smooth the motion curve truly is. Mathematically, smoothness has levels: a curve can be continuous (no jumps), or have a continuous first derivative (velocity never jumps), or continuous second derivative (acceleration never jumps, meaning no jerk). Discrete snapshots fail on all three. The paper measures smoothness by computing derivatives—if position is a smooth curve, its derivative is velocity, and that derivative's smoothness is guaranteed by the shape of the curve itself.

The paper enforces this by using both position and velocity as supervision signals during training. When the model learns to output a smooth curve, the derivative of that curve is automatically smooth too. This is the key: with snapshots, you had to guess velocity from numerical differences (noisy). With a smooth function, velocity is analytically guaranteed to follow from position, and higher derivatives follow from that.

Why it works

Why continuous functions fix the problem

Snapshots tie you to one sampling rate because each snapshot represents a single time tick in the training data. If training used 10 Hz, you have 10 snapshots per second. To run at 20 Hz, you must guess the in-between poses. But physical motion does not interpolate linearly; it curves. The paper replaces snapshots with a mathematical curve (a neural implicit function, trained to pass through key motion points but free to curve smoothly between them).

Once you have a smooth curve, you can ask for position at any time—10 Hz, 20 Hz, or any moment in between. More importantly, the curve's derivative is analytically defined, meaning velocity is guaranteed smooth and consistent with position. No numerical jitter, no discontinuities. This is the causal chain: continuous curve gives resolution-independence, which gives smooth derivatives, which gives stable jerk-free control.

The equation: defining the curve

First in words:

The predicted action is a function of normalized time. Position is that function; velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time. Both are constrained during training.

Then the compact form:

A(tau) = Phi(tau; theta) velocity(tau) = dA/d(tau) loss = L_position + w_velocity * L_velocity

Every symbol:

A(tau)position as a function of normalized time tau from -1 to 1
Phi(tau; theta)neural network parameterized by theta, computing the curve
thetathe learnable weights and embeddings that define the curve shape
velocity(tau)rate of change of position, analytically the derivative of A
L_positionhow far predicted position is from real trajectory data
L_velocityhow far predicted velocity is from real velocity data
w_velocityweight (strength) of velocity supervision, typically 1 or higher
A tiny worked example
Suppose a robot arm takes 1 second to grab an object (normalized time from -1 to 1).
At time tau=0 (middle), the curve outputs position 50 (cm) and velocity 10 (cm/s).
At time tau=0.5 (later), the curve outputs position 65 and velocity 8 (slowing down).
Training data says position should be 65 and velocity should be 9.
Position error: (65 - 65)^2 = 0. Velocity error: (8 - 9)^2 = 1.
Total loss = 0 + 1.0 * 1 = 1. The network adjusts to boost velocity prediction.
Both signals pull the curve to match real smooth motion.

The loss term on velocity directly enforces smooth derivatives, which is impossible with snapshots.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Weak motion template. If the learnable motion template (the starting pattern the network shapes) is too small or poorly initialized, the neural network cannot approximate the true motion well, and the smooth curve will still be jerky or inaccurate.
Velocity supervision mismatch. If the training data's velocity is noisy (computed from positions via numerical differences), feeding that noise into velocity loss will teach the curve to be jerky, defeating the purpose.
Test motion far from training. If the test motion is very different from training (much faster or slower acceleration), the learned function may extrapolate poorly outside the normalized time range, because the curve was shaped only by the training data's acceleration patterns.
Done right. Use high-quality velocity labels (e.g., from computed positions of real robot logs), initialize the motion template from data examples, and train on diverse motion speeds. The curve will generalize smoothly to new speeds and unseen tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robot arm simulator. Collect training trajectories at 10 Hz (10 poses per second). Train both a discrete waypoint model and a neural implicit function model on this data. Then evaluate both at 10 Hz (original rate), 20 Hz (double speed), and 5 Hz (half speed). Measure motion smoothness as the variance of acceleration (how much the acceleration bounces around). Discrete waypoints will show large bounces at non-native speeds because the guessed in-between poses are incorrect; the implicit function will show small, consistent bounces at all speeds because the curve is mathematically smooth.

smoothness = 1 / (1 + mean_absolute_acceleration_variance). Higher is smoother.

The demo must show that a continuous curve predicts valid motion at any speed, while discrete snapshots only work well at the single speed they were trained for.
