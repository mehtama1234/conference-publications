MedAraBench: Arabic Medical QA Dataset

A rigorously built and evaluated 24,883-question dataset for testing language models on Arabic medical knowledge.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Arabic medical knowledge is locked in textbooks and exams, but no public dataset lets you test whether language models can understand Arabic medical problems. MedAraBench takes 25,000 real medical exam questions from Arabic-speaking doctors and educators, organizes them into 19 specialties and five difficulty tiers, and then measures each language model's accuracy against expert clinician judgment. This is the first large-scale Arabic medical benchmark.

Zero assumption version

A gap between Arabic medical knowledge and what we can measure

Doctors, teachers, and textbooks across the Arabic-speaking world have built vast repositories of medical knowledge — exam questions, training materials, case studies. But that knowledge lives on paper and in closed libraries. Meanwhile, large language models (machine-learning systems trained to understand and generate text) are deployed in hospitals worldwide, yet we have almost no standardized way to test whether these models actually understand Arabic medicine.

A naive attempt: take the English medical benchmarks, translate them to Arabic, and test models. This breaks because translation loses cultural context (a heart attack is called different things in different regions), expertise (the translators are not medical experts), and difficulty mapping (the same concept is easier or harder in Arabic versus English). You measure the wrong thing.

The paper builds one. MedAraBench is a 24,883-question Arabic medical dataset, stratified across 19 specialties and 5 difficulty levels, evaluated against real clinician judgment.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect

Gather digitized academic medical exam materials from Arabic-speaking educational institutions.

Step 2: Label

Inherit specialty classification (Cardiology, Surgery, etc.) from original document structure.

Step 3: Split

Stratified random split: 80% training, 20% test, preserving specialty proportions.

Step 4: Evaluate

Two expert Arabic-fluent clinicians score a sample (378 questions) on quality, accuracy, relevance, difficulty.

Step 5: Benchmark

Test eight language models (Claude, GPT-5, Gemini, open-source) and compare against expert judgment.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper takes unstructured, scattered Arabic medical exam materials and transforms them into a single, curated, stratified dataset with both training and test sets, rigorous metadata (specialty, difficulty, quality scores), and a standardized evaluation protocol.

the object it moves  =  Unstructured exam documents -> Structured dataset with expert validation
The hidden quantity

Expert agreement: the clinician baseline

measured quantity  =  Clinician judgment correlation

The hidden quantity is clinician agreement — how much two independent expert doctors agree on whether a question is medically sound, clinically relevant, and at the right difficulty. This is the human ground truth. The paper measures it by having two board-certified clinicians (Anesthesiology and Internal Medicine, each 20+ years experience, fluent in Arabic) independently score 378 sampled questions on four criteria: medical accuracy, clinical relevance, question quality, and difficulty level.

Why measure this? Because no language model is more trustworthy than the expert clinicians who actually practice medicine. If your model cannot agree with a real doctor, it fails the test. The paper uses Pearson correlation (a standard measure of how much two rankings agree) to measure this agreement between each model and each clinician.

Why it works

Why stratified expert evaluation catches what you cannot see

Language models are trained on patterns in billions of words. They learn associations (Arabic word for lung -> Arabic word for pneumonia) but do not reason like clinicians. A model might guess correctly by pattern-matching (seeing a symptom, outputting a treatment) without understanding the actual disease mechanism. An untrained dataset would hide this gap — you would measure pattern-matching accuracy, not medical reasoning.

Stratification (organizing questions by specialty and difficulty) reveals the gap because it forces the model to prove it understands each subspecialty separately. Cardiology is different from Neurology. A model that memorized statistically common questions might score 80% overall but only 40% on rare conditions. Expert evaluation (clinicians scoring whether a question is medically sound) adds another dimension: models can sometimes produce plausible-sounding text that is medically false, and pattern-matching does not catch this without expert judgment.

By splitting the dataset into train and test sets, keeping specialties evenly represented in both, and having clinicians evaluate quality on a sample, the paper creates a measurable ground truth: the clinician judgment becomes the benchmark, and each model's correlation with clinicians becomes the real metric. This makes gaps in medical reasoning visible, because a model that scores well on translated English benchmarks may score poorly on Arabic medical reasoning, and the stratified evaluation will catch this discrepancy.

Measuring agreement: Pearson correlation

First in words:

For each language model, compute the Pearson correlation coefficient (a number from -1 to 1) between the model's scores on 378 sampled questions and each clinician's independent scores on the same 378 questions.

Then the compact form:

r = sum_i (score_model_i - mean_model) * (score_clinician_i - mean_clinician) / sqrt(sum_i (score_model_i - mean_model)^2 * sum_i (score_clinician_i - mean_clinician)^2)

Every symbol:

score_model_ithe score (0 to 1) that the model gave to question i
score_clinician_ithe score (0 to 1) that a clinician gave to the same question i
mean_modelthe average of all model scores across the 378 questions
mean_clinicianthe average of all clinician scores across the 378 questions
rcorrelation: 1 = perfect agreement, 0 = no agreement, -1 = perfect disagreement
A tiny worked example
Suppose two clinicians scored 3 questions as: [0.8, 0.9, 0.6].
A good model scored: [0.85, 0.88, 0.55]. Model mean = 0.76, Clinician mean = 0.77.
Numerator: (0.85 - 0.76)(0.8 - 0.77) + (0.88 - 0.76)(0.9 - 0.77) + (0.55 - 0.76)(0.6 - 0.77) = 0.09*0.03 + 0.12*0.13 - 0.21*0.17 = 0.0027 + 0.0156 - 0.0357 = -0.0174.
Denominators: sqrt((0.09^2 + 0.12^2 + 0.21^2) * (0.03^2 + 0.13^2 + 0.17^2)) = sqrt(0.0666 * 0.0398) = 0.0515.
r = -0.0174 / 0.0515 = -0.34. The model and clinician disagree on this tiny sample.

Clinician scores range 0 to 1 for each of four criteria; the paper computes correlation per criterion and per specialty, showing where models diverge from expert judgment.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Multiple-choice only. The dataset consists only of multiple-choice questions, so it measures if a model can recognize the right answer among four options. It does not measure whether a model can generate a complete diagnosis or treatment plan from scratch. Real clinical deployment requires generative reasoning.
Expert sample size. Only 378 questions (1.5% of the dataset) are scored by expert clinicians. If those 378 questions are not truly representative of the full dataset, the clinician baseline may not hold across all 19 specialties. A rare specialty might have fewer expert-evaluated questions.
Difficulty tier imbalance. The dataset spans five difficulty levels (aligned with medical education stages). If one specialty (e.g., Psychiatry) has fewer high-difficulty questions than another (e.g., Surgery), a model might appear to excel at one but be weak at reasoning about the other.
Done right. Stratified splitting ensures each specialty appears proportionally in train and test. Clinician evaluation on a statistically sampled subset (using finite population correction) provides a ground truth. Publishing the evaluation scripts and dataset allows others to verify the results and add their own measurements, which is critical because hidden methodological flaws (such as sampling bias toward common diseases) can only be caught when multiple independent teams audit the benchmark.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a script that loads the MedAraBench dataset, samples 50 questions uniformly across the 19 specialties, runs three language models (e.g., Claude, an open-source model, and a baseline), and computes the accuracy (percentage of correct answers) per specialty. Then compare each model's accuracy to the expert clinician baseline (the correlation published in the paper's results table). The demo should show that a model trained on the MedAraBench training set outperforms zero-shot (untrained) models because it has seen similar questions and can learn specialty-specific patterns, and that models specializing in Arabic medical knowledge score higher than generic language models because specialized training builds stronger associations between Arabic medical concepts and correct treatments.

Accuracy per specialty = (number of correct answers in that specialty) / (total questions in that specialty sampled)

The clearest win: a language model fine-tuned on MedAraBench training data should show measurable gains in Arabic medical accuracy and clinician agreement compared to the same model before fine-tuning.
