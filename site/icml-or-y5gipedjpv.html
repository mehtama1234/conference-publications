Causal Discovery for Irregularly Sampled Time Series

When time series have missing chunks and uneven sampling, you must fix the gaps AND find the causal links together, or each mistake poisons the other.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Most causal discovery methods assume clean, evenly-spaced time series. Real data from hospitals and weather stations has holes: a patient skips appointments, a sensor dies for a week. Filling those holes badly breaks causal discovery, and finding causal links wrong breaks gap-filling. ReTimeCausal solves this by alternating: fill the gaps, then update what causes what, then fill again using better causal knowledge. The paper proves this dance actually works.

Zero assumption version

The tangled knot: gaps and causality confuse each other

In hospitals and weather monitoring, measurements come in at odd times. A blood test happens Tuesday, the next one Friday. A rain gauge breaks for three days. You want to know: does patient A's blood pressure cause their glucose level to rise? Does wind speed cause rain? But half your data is missing.

A naive first attempt: fill in the missing values (guess what happened on Wednesday), then run a standard causal discovery method on the completed data. This sounds reasonable. But the guesses are wrong (you have no idea what Wednesday's reading was), and those wrong guesses twist the causal structure. You find false links that do not exist.

Or try the reverse: ignore the gaps, run causal discovery on whatever data you have. But gaps create phantom patterns. If you measured Monday and Thursday but nothing in between, random noise on those days looks like correlation even if there is no causal link.

The core problem. Mistakes in gap-filling make causal discovery fail, and mistakes in causal discovery make gap-filling fail. You cannot solve them separately.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Start with a guess

Initialize a causal graph using the available data, gaps and all.

Step 2: Fill the gaps

Use kernel smoothing (a method that looks at nearby measurements to guess missing ones) guided by the current causal structure.

Step 3: Learn causal links

Run sparse regression on the filled data, but penalize structures that violate acyclicity (you cannot have A causes B and B causes A).

Step 4: Repeat steps 2-3

The algorithm alternates until the gaps and the causal graph stop changing.

Step 5: Validate

The paper proves mathematically that the final graph matches the true causal structure with high probability.

What the paper changes

The thing being changed: missing data AND causal links, in lockstep

Earlier work treats gap-filling and causal discovery as separate problems. ReTimeCausal couples them: when you estimate a missing value, you use your best current knowledge of causality; when you update causality, you use your best current gap estimates. The object is the pair (filled values, causal graph), and both move together.

the object it moves  =  imputed values and causal graph
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: structural consistency

measured quantity  =  Mutual information loss from misalignment

When gap-filling and causal discovery disagree, you lose information. If you think A causes B, your gap-filler will interpolate B's values based on A's history. But if the causal graph is wrong, those interpolations will be biased, and causal discovery will then find a different (wrong) structure. The hidden quantity is this feedback loop: how much information is lost because imputation and causal learning are out of sync.

ReTimeCausal tracks this by measuring how much the imputed values change after each causal update, and how much the causal graph changes after each imputation step. When both stabilize (stop changing), the system has found structural consistency—the imputation matches the causality, and vice versa.

Why it works

Why it works: alternation breaks the poison cycle

The poison cycle: bad imputation twists causal discovery, which twists the next imputation. Naively, you might think this gets worse and worse. But alternating imputation and causal learning reverses the damage. Here is why.

When you first impute, you make mistakes. When you then learn causality from the imputed data, you do discover some wrong links. But critically, you also learn the structure that is most consistent with the imputed values. In the next imputation step, you use this structure. Even though the structure is not perfect, it is better aligned with the data than a random structure would be, because sparse regression (the algorithm's learning method) chooses the causal links that explain the patterns in the filled data best—any guess is better than no structure at all. The next imputation is less wrong. This reduces the structural inconsistency—the mismatch between what the causal graph says about dependency and what the data actually shows.

Mathematically, the paper proves that this alternating process converges to a solution where the imputed values and the causal graph agree with each other, and under mild conditions (sparse true causal structure, smooth local relationship properties), this solution is close to the true causal graph. Why alternation works: each step pulls the solution toward a state where imputation and causality are in sync, like two gears meshing together. You cannot solve them separately because each mistake in one makes the other worse; by forcing them to improve together, you escape the poison cycle.

The equation: balancing imputation and structure

First in words:

The loss combines three terms: a data fit term (how close the imputed values match the observed data), a causal sparsity term (penalize complex graphs), and a consistency term (penalize mismatch between imputation and causal model).

Then the compact form:

Loss = ||Y_obs - Y_impute||^2 + lambda * ||graph_edges||_1 + mu * sum_i || impute_i - predict_i(graph) ||^2

Every symbol:

Y_obsthe measurements you actually collected
Y_imputethe guessed values for missing days
||·||_1count the number of causal links (sparsity penalty)
lambdastrength of the sparsity penalty
predict_i(graph)what the current causal graph predicts for day i
mustrength of the consistency penalty
Tiny worked example with 3 days and 2 variables
Day 1: measure A=10, B missing. Day 2: measure A missing, B=20. Day 3: measure A=12, B=18.
Guess imputation: A at day 2 = 11, B at day 1 = 19.
Try causal structure: A causes B.
Data fit: (10-10)^2 + (11-11)^2 + (12-12)^2 + (19-19)^2 + (20-20)^2 + (18-18)^2 = 0. (Guesses match observed perfectly.)
Sparsity: 1 edge (A->B), so lambda * 1.
Consistency: Does the causal model A->B predict the filled values correctly? If A and B are causally linked by a simple rule B~0.8*A, then at day 1 the model predicts B~8, but we guessed B=19. The error is (19-8)^2=121. At day 2 model predicts A~12.5, but we guessed 11. Error is (11-12.5)^2=2.25. Consistency penalty: mu * (121 + 2.25) = mu*123.25.
Total loss = 0 + lambda*1 + mu*123.25. The large consistency penalty means this causal structure (A->B) is not consistent with our imputations. The algorithm will either re-impute B to be smaller at day 1, or change the causal structure.

The algorithm converges when the imputations and the causal graph support each other, which means they are both close to the truth.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

High missingness breaks the kernel smoothing. If more than 70 percent of data is missing, the kernel cannot see enough neighbors to guess the gaps accurately. The algorithm will still run, but the initial imputations will be poor, and convergence may take longer or get stuck in a bad local optimum.
Non-linear causality confuses sparse regression. The algorithm uses linear sparse regression to learn causal links. If the true causality is highly non-linear (e.g., A causes B only when B is above a threshold), the regression will miss it and learn a linear approximation instead.
Hidden confounders create fake links. If an unmeasured third variable causes both A and B, the algorithm will see A and B move together and may incorrectly infer A causes B (or B causes A), even though there is no direct causal link between them.
Done right: sparse, linear causal structure with missing-at-random data. When the true causal graph is sparse (few links), the relationships are approximately linear, and data is missing randomly (not systematically), the theory guarantees ReTimeCausal recovers the true structure.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a synthetic time series with known causal structure (e.g., variable A causes B, B causes C, with no other links). Randomly remove 30 percent of the data points. Run ReTimeCausal and a naive baseline (impute missing values independently, then run standard causal discovery). Measure the accuracy of the learned causal graph against the true graph.

Accuracy = (true_edges_found + true_non_edges_found) / (total_possible_edges)

The demo must show that ReTimeCausal recovers the correct causal structure much more often than naive imputation-then-discovery, especially as missingness increases.
