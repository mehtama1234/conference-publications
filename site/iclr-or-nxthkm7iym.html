PaAno: Patch-Based Anomaly Detection

PaAno detects time-series anomalies by comparing patches, not by feeding entire sequences through slow large models.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When a sensor measures temperature over time, detecting when something goes wrong is hard if the model needs to remember months of history. PaAno instead works with short chunks (patches) of time-series data, each turned into a fingerprint by a lightweight neural network that only looks at nearby time steps, not the entire history. At test time, it finds anomalies by checking whether each patch's fingerprint matches normal ones from the training data. Because the model only processes local chunks, it runs fast and uses little memory. Because the triplet loss pulls all normal patterns together tightly, even subtle anomalies land far from the cluster and get caught.

Zero assumption version

Giant models waste computation on time series

Time-series anomaly detection means spotting when something unexpected happens in a stream of measurements. A factory sensor records vibration every second; a credit-card transaction log shows when you usually spend money. You want to flag when the pattern breaks.

Recent work uses transformers (models that look at every part of the sequence and decide which pieces matter) or foundation models (huge pre-trained networks). These work well but they swallow electricity: they need to store all historical context in memory, and they compute attention (a heavy step where each word in the sequence asks 'which past words do I look at?') across thousands of time steps. For a factory or an embedded device, this is impractical.

A naive approach: train a simpler recurrent model (one that processes the sequence one step at a time, forgetting old context). But even these struggle. They need to learn what 'normal' means from the training data, yet they throw away most of the signal while trying to predict the next value. Detecting anomalies this way is unreliable.

The core tradeoff. Heavy models remember everything but waste resources; simple models are cheap but forget what matters.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Chunk the data

Split the time series into overlapping windows of fixed length.

Train fingerprints

Pass patches through a 1D CNN. Triplet loss pulls fingerprints of normal patches together and pushes anomalies apart.

Learn the normal shape

After training, collect fingerprints of all normal patches. These become the reference.

Score new patches

At test time, embed a new patch and measure its distance to the nearest normal reference.

Flag anomalies

If distance is large, the patch is anomalous. Aggregate across overlapping windows to get a score per time step.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts the burden from the full-sequence model to a local patch encoder. Instead of a transformer that attends to every past time step, PaAno uses a small 1D convolution (a cheap operation that looks only at nearby values) to extract a fingerprint (a short list of numbers that represents the patch) of each short patch.

the object it moves  =  the model architecture: from whole-sequence attention to local patch fingerprints
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: patch fingerprint distance

measured quantity  =  distance from patch to normal reference

The hidden quantity PaAno measures is the distance in embedding space (how far apart two fingerprints are). A patch from normal operation has a small distance to other normal patches because the triplet loss during training pulled them all toward the center. An anomalous patch, never seen during training, lands far away.

The paper estimates this distance by computing the Euclidean distance (just like measuring inches on a grid) between the test patch's embedding and the closest training patch's embedding. Patches that are far from any training patch score as anomalies.

Why it works

Why patch embeddings catch anomalies

The insight: anomalies are patterns the model has never learned. By training fingerprints (learned vector representations) with triplet loss (which pulls together patches of normal data and separates them from random samples), the model learns to map normal patterns into a tight region. Any pattern far outside that region, when encoded, lands far away from all training patterns.

This works because the triplet loss is a local constraint: it says 'an anchor (a normal patch) should be close to other normal patches, and far from negatives (random corruption or rare patterns).' Over training, this crowds normal embeddings together. At test time, a new patch either fits the crowd (normal) or sits far away (anomalous). The model does not need to remember the entire history; it only learns the shape of normal.

The 1D convolution is efficient because it only looks at nearby time steps within each patch, never the entire sequence. This is why PaAno is fast: it avoids the quadratic cost (cost that grows as the square of the input size) of full attention.

Math after the picture

The triplet loss equation

First in words:

For each normal training patch (anchor), the loss penalizes the distance to other normal patches (positive) being larger than the distance to corrupted or rare patches (negative), plus a margin to keep them separated.

Then the compact form:

L_triplet = max(d(anchor, positive) - d(anchor, negative) + margin, 0)

Every symbol:

anchora normal patch from training data
positiveanother normal patch from the same region or context
negativea corrupted or out-of-distribution patch (noise or rare event)
d(x, y)the distance between embeddings x and y (straight-line distance in the learned space)
marginthe minimum gap we enforce; typically 1.0 or higher
A tiny worked example
Suppose d(anchor, positive) = 0.8, d(anchor, negative) = 2.5, margin = 1.0.
The triplet loss term: max(0.8 - 2.5 + 1.0, 0) = max(-0.7, 0) = 0. The nudge signal is zero; this example is satisfied.
Now suppose d(anchor, positive) = 1.8, d(anchor, negative) = 2.0, margin = 1.0.
The triplet loss term: max(1.8 - 2.0 + 1.0, 0) = max(0.8, 0) = 0.8. A nudge signal flows; training nudges to pull positive closer.
Over many epochs, the triplet loss spreads out negatives and clusters positives, so the learned embedding space groups normal patches tightly.

The triplet loss builds a region in embedding space where normal patterns cluster; anything outside that region is an anomaly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Patch size mismatch. If the anomaly is slower than the patch length (e.g., a gradual drift over 1000 steps, but patches are 10 steps), the embedding will smooth over the anomaly and miss it. Patch size must match the timescale of expected anomalies.
Multimodal normal data. If the training data contains many different kinds of normal behavior (morning vs. evening, weekday vs. weekend), the embeddings will spread out and the distance threshold will need tuning. One tight cluster assumes one kind of normal.
Test data looks different than training. If the test data has a different scale or frequency pattern than training, the distance metric becomes unreliable. A systematic drift in temperature, even if normal, may score as anomalous.
Done right. Choose patch length to capture the anomaly timescale. Collect training data that represents all normal modes. Monitor and re-tune the distance threshold if the real-world data pattern shifts.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small sandbox: generate a synthetic univariate time series (e.g., sine wave with a fixed period), train PaAno on the normal signal, then inject synthetic anomalies (sudden spikes, flatlines, or reversals). Plot the anomaly scores over time and verify that injected anomalies score high and normal regions score low. Measure precision and recall. Then compare inference speed and memory to a simple transformer baseline to show the efficiency gain is real.

F1 = 2 * (precision * recall) / (precision + recall); inference_speedup = baseline_time / paano_time

The demo must show that PaAno finds real anomalies at least as well as or better than heavy models (because triplet loss tightly clusters normal patterns) and does so in a fraction of the time (because local convolution is cheaper than whole-sequence attention).
