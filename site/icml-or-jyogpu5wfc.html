Enhancing Multi-Modal LLMs Reasoning via Difficulty-Aware Group Normalization

Stabilize reinforcement learning for multimodal reasoning by normalizing rewards within groups of similar difficulty instead of mixing easy and extreme examples together.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Recent reinforcement-learning methods improve reasoning by comparing several sampled answers to the same prompt and then updating the model from their relative rewards. A common step in that process rescales rewards using the group mean and standard deviation, which is a measure of spread. The paper argues that this step becomes fragile in multimodal settings because a few unusual examples can distort the spread estimate. A model that must both see and reason can fail in two different ways: it can misread the image or it can reason badly after reading it correctly. Durian tries to reduce training instability by regrouping samples according to estimated difficulty before computing the shared normalization scale.

Why ordinary reward normalization breaks in multimodal reasoning

In group-based policy optimization, several candidate answers are sampled, scored, and compared. The comparison depends on normalization because raw rewards can vary a lot across prompts. If one answer in the group has an extreme reward, the computed spread can become too large or too small, which changes the learning signal for every other answer in the group.

That problem becomes sharper for multimodal models because difficulty varies along at least two axes. One image may be visually cluttered, while another is visually simple but requires many reasoning steps. If those examples share one normalization pool, the scale used for updates can reflect the wrong source of variation. The model then receives unstable credit assignment because answers are being compared under a distorted estimate of difficulty.

The paper's move in one sentence. Estimate how hard each training case is, group cases by similar difficulty, and normalize rewards inside those difficulty groups so outliers do less damage.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Score candidate responses

Generate several responses per prompt and assign verifiable rewards because the training algorithm needs a direct signal for which sampled answers were better.

Estimate visual difficulty

Use visual entropy, meaning a measure of how visually complex or disordered the image content is, to capture perceptual difficulty.

Estimate reasoning difficulty

Use model confidence to detect uncertainty because low confidence often signals that the reasoning path is unstable.

Group by similar difficulty

Pool samples with similar perceptual and reasoning difficulty before computing the shared normalization scale.

Normalize and update

Apply the reward normalization inside each difficulty group and use the resulting values for policy updates.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the reward normalization stage inside group-based reinforcement learning. It keeps relative comparison within groups, but it replaces one global scale with scales shared by samples of similar estimated difficulty.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Sample difficulty

The key hidden quantity is how hard a training case really is for the model. Difficulty is not directly labeled in the data. The paper estimates it from two clues: visual entropy for perceptual complexity and model confidence for reasoning uncertainty.

This hidden variable matters because normalization is only informative when the compared samples come from roughly similar conditions. If easy and hard cases are mixed without accounting for that difference, the reward scale stops reflecting quality differences and starts reflecting difficulty mismatch.

Why it works

Why this works: normalization should compare peers

Normalization changes the size of the policy update. If the spread estimate is distorted by outliers, then ordinary good answers may look weaker than they should or ordinary bad answers may look too influential. That makes the update noisy because the algorithm is reacting to the wrong reference scale.

Grouping by difficulty fixes the reference problem. Samples inside the same difficulty band are more likely to have reward variation that reflects answer quality rather than raw task hardness. That means the shared standard deviation, which is the measure of spread used in normalization, is more representative of the local comparison set. The resulting updates are steadier because they are based on relative differences that come from comparable cases.

The multimodal setting especially benefits from this separation because perception and reasoning errors interact. A wrong answer may come from a visual miss, a logic miss, or both. Durian does not fully disentangle those causes, but it reduces one major source of instability by avoiding a single normalization pool for all cases.

Math after the picture

Difficulty-aware normalized reward

First in words:

Each reward is centered and rescaled using the mean and spread of the difficulty group it belongs to, rather than the full mixed batch.

Then the compact form:

r_hat_i = (r_i - mean(r in group_d(i))) / std(r in group_d(i)))

Every symbol:

r_ithe raw reward for sample i
group_d(i)the set of samples assigned to the same difficulty group as sample i
mean(r in group_d(i))the average reward inside that difficulty group
std(r in group_d(i))the reward spread inside that difficulty group
r_hat_ithe normalized reward used for the policy update
A tiny worked example
Suppose one hard group has rewards 2, 4, and 6. Its mean is 4 and its spread is 2.
A sample with reward 6 gets normalized value (6 - 4) / 2 = 1.
Now suppose an easy group has rewards 8, 9, and 10. Its mean is 9 and its spread is 1.
A sample with reward 10 also gets normalized value 1, even though its raw reward is larger, because it is only being compared with peers of similar difficulty.

The point is not to equalize all tasks. The point is to make each update reflect performance relative to a fair local comparison set.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Difficulty estimates can be wrong. If visual entropy or confidence is a poor proxy for true hardness, samples may be grouped badly because the method only works when the grouping reflects real task differences.
Groups can become too small. Normalization becomes unstable again if each difficulty band has too few samples, because the mean and spread estimates then become noisy.
Confidence can hide systematic bias. A model can be confidently wrong. If confidence is treated as difficulty evidence without calibration, the method may understate some hard reasoning failures because the model does not recognize its own confusion.
Done right. The method works when difficulty proxies are informative and each group has enough samples to provide a stable local reward scale.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small multimodal reinforcement-learning loop on image-question pairs with verifiable answers. For each prompt, sample several model responses, score them, and compare two update rules: standard group normalization and difficulty-aware normalization. Estimate visual difficulty from image clutter and reasoning difficulty from answer confidence or entropy over candidate answers.

The demo should plot reward variance, policy loss variance, and benchmark accuracy over training. It should show that the difficulty-aware version has fewer unstable swings because its normalized rewards are computed among peers rather than across a mixed batch.

stability_gain = variance(plain_updates) - variance(difficulty_aware_updates)

A convincing demo must make the normalization effect visible, not only the final score difference.
