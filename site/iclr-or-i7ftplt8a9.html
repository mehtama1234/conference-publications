WE-MATH 2.0: Teaching MLLMs to Reason Visually Through Math

Teach a visual AI system math by giving it a structured map of knowledge, harder problem variants, and a two-step learning process that starts with guided reasoning then gradually pushes it to solve harder problems on its own.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Multimodal large language models (AI systems that read images and text together) can do many things, but they stumble on hard math problems that require real reasoning. The problem is not just the model — it is how we teach it. WE-MATH 2.0 uses three strategies: (1) a structured hierarchy of math concepts in order, (2) problem variants that climb from easy to hard, and (3) a two-stage reward system that first rewards reasoning steps, then rewards correct answers. Together, these make the model build real understanding instead of pattern-matching.

Why current systems fail at math

A visual math problem is brutal: the model sees a diagram, reads the question, and must chain together reasoning steps to arrive at a numerical answer. The typical AI that does this is trained on a mixed bag of problems — easy ones and hard ones all at once. It learns to pattern-match easy examples but when the problem demands real logic (multi-step calculations, spatial understanding, connecting concepts across domains), the model takes shortcuts and guesses wrong.

The naive approach is to throw bigger datasets at the problem. Collect more math problems. But that misses the real issue: the model has never learned the underlying structure. It does not know that adding lists of numbers in any order gives the same result (the property called commutativity); it does not know how different problem types relate. It is like teaching someone to play chess by showing them 10,000 game positions without ever explaining the rules. The model never builds a mental scaffold.

The core move: Organize math knowledge as a hierarchy, train the model on problem variants in ascending order of difficulty, and use a two-stage process: first reward the model for following step-by-step reasoning, then gradually reward it only for correct final answers on harder problems.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build a knowledge hierarchy

Create a five-level tree: domains (algebra) — topics (linear systems) — concepts (Gaussian elimination) — principles (pivot swap rules) — worked examples.

Generate problem variants

For each concept, generate 7 versions ranging from plug-in-numbers easy to multi-step-chaining hard.

Cold-start fine-tuning

Show the model step-by-step solutions on easier problems and reward it for reproducing that reasoning chain.

Progressive alignment RL

Gradually shift training to harder problems and reward only correct final answers, not intermediate steps.

Evaluate on hidden benchmark

Test on a benchmark covering all 491 concepts to confirm the model generalizes, not just memorizes.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies two things: the training data (now organized by difficulty and knowledge structure instead of random mix) and the training reward signal (now two-stage: first reward intermediate reasoning steps, then reward correct answers on hard problems).

the object it moves  =  training curriculum + reward signal
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: reasoning-to-answer alignment

measured quantity  =  Reasoning quality under progressive difficulty

This is the paper's critical insight. During cold-start training, the model learns to output step-by-step reasoning. The paper measures how well that reasoning generalizes when the problem difficulty increases. It is not just whether the model gets the final answer right; it is whether the model's internal reasoning chain (the intermediate steps) stays coherent and sensible even when the problem becomes harder.

The paper estimates this by tracking how often the model produces a valid reasoning chain on each difficulty tier. If reasoning stays crisp at easy levels but breaks into incoherence at medium, the model is brittle. If reasoning holds even when problems become hard, the model has built real understanding. This hidden quantity guides when to switch from the first training stage (reward reasoning) to the second (reward only answers).

Why it works

Why this teaches real math reasoning

The classical mistake is to jump straight to 'get the right answer.' That works on pattern-heavy tasks but fails on reasoning-heavy tasks. Math requires chaining together logical steps, and skipping that chain means the model is guessing rather than computing. WE-MATH 2.0 fixes this by building understanding in two stages.

In stage one (cold-start), the model is shown step-by-step solutions on easy problems and rewarded when it reproduces those reasoning chains. Why does this work? Because the model learns what a valid reasoning chain looks like — the shape of 'first I identify the variables, then I set up the equation, then I solve.' This is not memorization; it is learning the grammar of mathematical thought.

In stage two (progressive alignment), the problem difficulty increases and the reward signal switches to 'final answer only.' Why can the model handle this? Because it already knows how to reason. Now it applies that reasoning to harder problems. The 'average reward' part is key: instead of asking 'did this one problem get solved?', the system asks 'on average, across this batch of problems, is the model improving?' This smooths out noise and lets the system notice genuine progress even when individual problems are hard.

The knowledge hierarchy ensures each new concept builds on earlier ones. A model learning 'Gaussian elimination' has already learned 'row operations' and 'array form' (organizing numbers in rows and columns). The difficulty space ensures the jump from medium to hard is not a cliff; there are seven gradations per concept. The result: the model chains reasoning steps not because it memorized them, but because the learning process rewarded them step-by-step until they became automatic.

The training objective

First in words:

In the first stage, the loss penalizes wrong reasoning steps. In the second stage, the loss penalizes wrong final answers. The penalty is weighted by the average performance across a batch of problems, adjusted based on problem difficulty.

Then the compact form:

Loss_stage1 = sum_i (log_likelihood_of_step_i) * reward_signal_step; Loss_stage2 = (1 - accuracy) scaled by average_reward_batch / difficulty_level

Every symbol:

Loss_stage1penalty for getting reasoning steps wrong in early training
log_likelihood_of_step_ihow well the model predicted step i (the i-th reasoning move)
reward_signal_stepbonus when the step matches the ground-truth reasoning
Loss_stage2penalty for getting the final answer wrong in later training
accuracyfraction of problems solved correctly
average_reward_batchaverage performance across all problems in this training batch (smooths noise)
difficulty_levelan estimate of problem difficulty; higher difficulty scales the penalty down (easier to forgive mistakes on hard problems early)
A tiny worked example
Early training on easy problem (solving 2x + 3 = 7): Model outputs step 'subtract 3 from both sides.' Ground truth matches. Reward = +1. Model outputs step 'divide by 2.' Ground truth matches. Reward = +1. Loss = -log(prob_step1) - log(prob_step2) [negative because rewards were positive].
Later training on hard problem (solving a system of 3 equations): Model outputs 'Set up array A' (arrange the numbers in rows and columns). Correct. Model outputs 'Swap rows for pivot.' Correct. Model outputs 'Final answer x=2, y=-1, z=3.' This is hard; the problem difficulty level is 6 out of 7. Even if a step breaks, it is down-weighted: average_reward_batch is 0.7 (70% correct on this batch). Penalty = (1 - 1.0) * 0.7 / 6 = 0 (perfect answer).
Same mistake on easy problem: If the answer were wrong, penalty would be (1 - 0) * 0.95 / 1 = 0.95 [higher penalty, lower difficulty]. The system is stricter on easy problems (where mistakes mean real misunderstanding) and more forgiving on hard ones (where struggle is expected).

Stage 1 rewards reasoning chains so the model learns to think step-by-step — by making each intermediate step explicit, the model builds a habit of logical progression rather than guessing. Stage 2 transitions to answers but uses adaptive penalties so hard problems do not break training early — the model already knows how to reason, so now it applies that reasoning to harder problems without the penalty crushing it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stage 1 too long. If cold-start fine-tuning lingers, the model learns to reproduce shown reasoning but never learns to adapt. It becomes a rote memorizer, not a reasoner. The transition to stage 2 must happen once the model demonstrates reasoning coherence on at least medium-difficulty problems.
Knowledge hierarchy misses connections. If the five-level hierarchy does not reflect true mathematical dependencies, the model learns concepts out of order. For example, teaching 'derivatives' before 'limits' breaks the logical flow. The hierarchy must reflect the actual learning sequence mathematicians use.
Difficulty space poorly calibrated. If the seven variants per problem are not truly ordered by difficulty (e.g., variant 4 is harder than variant 6), training becomes chaotic. The model needs a smooth climb, not a cliff. Variants must be truly progressive.
Average reward smoothing too aggressive. If the system smooths reward signals too much, the model does not get clear feedback on individual problem types. It becomes hard to know which concepts were actually learned. The smoothing window (batch size) must be small enough to preserve problem-level signal.
Done right. The knowledge hierarchy is validated by math educators. Each problem variant is tested to confirm it is harder than the previous one. The transition from stage 1 to stage 2 is triggered by a clear metric (reasoning coherence above 85% on medium problems). The average-reward window is set to 16 problems — large enough to smooth noise, small enough to preserve signal.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simplified version of WE-MATH 2.0 on a single math domain (e.g., linear equations). Create a small knowledge hierarchy (3 levels: intro, intermediate, advanced). Generate 3 problem variants per level from an LLM or a template. Train a small language model (e.g., Pythia-1B or a fine-tuned BERT) in two stages: stage 1 on intro problems rewarding step-by-step outputs; stage 2 on intermediate/advanced problems rewarding final answers. Track accuracy separately on each stage and each problem variant.

forgetting_rate = (accuracy_on_intro_after_stage2 - accuracy_on_intro_before_stage2), transfer_gain = (accuracy_on_advanced_stage2 - accuracy_on_intro_baseline * 0.5), reasoning_coherence = fraction_of_problems_where_steps_are_logically_valid

The demo must show: (1) the model does not forget intro concepts after training on advanced ones (forgetting_rate near zero), because the hierarchy ensures advanced concepts depend on intro ones so losing intro knowledge would make advanced problems unsolvable; (2) the model solves advanced problems better than a random baseline (transfer_gain positive), because reasoning learned on intro problems transfers directly to advanced ones in the same hierarchy; and (3) the model's reasoning stays coherent even on hard problems (reasoning_coherence above 70% on advanced), because cold-start training embedded reasoning as a habit before asking for answers. These three together prove the two-stage process works.
