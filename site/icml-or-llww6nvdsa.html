CyberJurors: Multi-Agent Dispute Resolution

When a marketplace needs to settle disputes between buyers and sellers, use a team of AI jurors debating the evidence in stages, each one answering a separate question before voting.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Online marketplaces have millions of disagreements each year: a buyer says an item never arrived; a seller says it was delivered. Humans can only read a few thousand of these per year. The paper shows that multiple AI agents discussing evidence in structured rounds — like real jurors — can handle the full messy pile: photos, shipping records, chat logs, and sales history all mixed together, making decisions that match what real jurors would actually choose.

Zero assumption version

The dispute pile never stops growing

Every day, millions of online shoppers open disputes: the package says delivered but never arrived; the seller shipped a broken item; the buyer opened a box and swapped the contents. Human moderators can read perhaps a few thousand a year. Even hiring a army of contractors is too slow and too expensive.

A naive first attempt: feed all the evidence to a single large language model — a trained AI that reads text — and ask it to pick a side. This breaks for three reasons. First, it misses the connections between different pieces: a shipping photo plus a chat message prove something that each alone does not. Second, it ignores what actually happened in similar past disputes; it does not learn from the jury pool's voting history. Third, when a model reads 50 messages at once, it can fixate on one early piece of evidence and ignore contradictory facts later on. It votes without explaining its thinking.

The move. Simulate a real jury: each AI agent reads the evidence in four structured stages (receipt, damage, intent, precedent), writes out its thinking, then the team votes with access to their previous similar decisions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Receipt verification

Each agent examines shipping tracking and photos to establish what the seller sent.

Stage 2: Damage assessment

Each agent checks photos and messages for signs of the item's actual condition.

Stage 3: Intent extraction

Each agent reads the chat history to infer whether buyer or seller acted in good faith.

Stage 4: Precedent lookup

Each agent retrieves three similar past cases and reads what the jury decided.

Consensus vote

All agents vote, majority wins; ties broken by citing stronger ground-truth evidence.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the way evidence is processed: from one big stack fed to a single model all at once, to four separate filtered questions asked one at a time to a team, with each agent writing its reasoning before voting.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Causal clarity

measured quantity  =  How clearly each agent traces cause to verdict

The paper measures something essential but invisible: how clearly each AI agent can show the chain of logic from a fact (the photo shows a dent) to its decision (refund the buyer). This is called causal clarity. It is invisible because a trained model can make good decisions for bad reasons — guessing right by accident — but a jury must explain why.

The paper estimates causal clarity in two ways. First, during the four-stage chain-of-thought, each agent writes out its reasoning step-by-step, which lets humans audit whether the logic is real or guessed. Second, after the vote, the paper compares the AI jury's decision against the actual human jury's vote on the same case. If they match, the clarity was real; if they diverge, the model was relying on hidden patterns, not genuine reasoning.

Why it works

Why splitting into stages works

When one AI agent reads fifty messages at once, it fixates. The first message might say the seller is a liar; everything else gets filtered through that lens. Humans do this too, but real jurors have a remedy: cross-examination. One juror notes the lie, but another insists on the shipping proof, and a third refuses to vote until she has seen the photos. Their disagreement forces the group to sort fact from hunch.

The four-stage structure mimics this. Stage 1 (receipt) forces the team to answer one narrow question: what was physically sent? Stage 2 (damage) asks only: what condition was it in? Stage 3 (intent) asks only: did someone act in bad faith? Stage 4 (precedent) asks: how did we vote on similar facts before? Each stage acts as a filter, preventing the agent from jumping to a conclusion before gathering all its evidence. When three agents disagree on intent, the paper's voting rule says the agent that cites the strongest ground-truth fact (a link in the chat, a photo timestamp) wins the vote. This makes fixation costly because an agent that is just guessing has no evidence to point to, and voters can check whether the agent's reasoning points to something real in the evidence or is a bare assertion.

Precedent-grounding adds a final layer. Before the jury votes, each agent retrieves three past cases it already voted on that are similar to this one. Seeing its own past decision on a nearly identical receipt dispute nudges the agent toward consistency. It cannot easily flip a verdict because the group would ask: why are we deciding this one differently than the last one? This is how real juries work: they build jurisprudence.

The voting rule equation

First in words:

Each agent assigns a belief score to each possible verdict (refund or reject) for each piece of evidence it observed. The final verdict is the one that gets the highest total belief score across all agents, weighted by how strongly each agent cited ground-truth facts (not guesses).

Then the compact form:

verdict = argmax_v sum_i w_i * P_v(agent_i) where w_i is the confidence weight for agent i based on how much ground-truth evidence they cited

Every symbol:

verdictthe final jury outcome: refund, reject, or partial
argmax_vpick the verdict option v that has the highest score
sum_iadd up the scores from all agents i in the jury
w_ihow much weight to give agent i's vote; higher if they cited more ground-truth facts
P_v(agent_i)agent i's own belief score for verdict v, stated during chain-of-thought
A tiny worked example
Case: buyer claims item never arrived; seller claims proof of delivery.
Agent-A (reads receipt stage carefully): Refund belief 0.8, Reject belief 0.2. Cites tracking timestamp + photo. Weight w_A = 0.9 (strong evidence).
Agent-B (doubts the photo is real): Refund belief 0.4, Reject belief 0.6. Cites only seller reputation. Weight w_B = 0.4 (weak ground-truth).
Agent-C (retrieves past case, similar buyer profile): Refund belief 0.9, Reject belief 0.1. Cites precedent. Weight w_C = 0.7.
Refund total = 0.9 * 0.8 + 0.4 * 0.4 + 0.7 * 0.9 = 0.72 + 0.16 + 0.63 = 1.51
Reject total = 0.9 * 0.2 + 0.4 * 0.6 + 0.7 * 0.1 = 0.18 + 0.24 + 0.07 = 0.49
Verdict: Refund (higher score).

Agents that point to real evidence (photos, timestamps, chat links) get higher weight, so their vote pulls the final verdict toward the ground-truth verdict, not just the agent's guess.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

No precedent for this type of dispute. If the current case is unusual (a seller from a new country; a first-time buyer), stage 4 retrieves past cases that are only weakly similar. The agent cannot anchor to its own history, so it must guess, and the guess may not align with the real jury's intuition.
All agents fixate on the same wrong fact. If the first message contains a persuasive lie that all agents believe, the four stages just give the same wrong answer four times in a row. The voting rule cannot overcome unanimous fixation. This happens when the fabricated fact is more salient than the ground-truth one.
Evidence is genuinely ambiguous. Some disputes have no clear answer: the shipping proof could be forged; the photo could be from a different box; the chat is vague. Real juries also disagree on these. The paper's method does not resolve ambiguity; it just captures the jury's split vote accurately.
Done right: clear chains, real precedent, diverse agents. When each stage uncovers a different angle of the evidence, when past precedents are on point, and when agents bring slightly different interpretations, causal clarity is high and the verdict matches the real jury.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny dispute-resolution game with 10 real cases from VerdictBench (the benchmark the paper uses). For each case, show the raw evidence (photos, chat snippets, shipping tracker), then run CyberJurors for 2 agents in four stages, outputting the chain-of-thought for each stage. After the verdict, reveal what the real human jury decided. Count how many match. Also log a case where the AI agents disagreed (one agent said refund, one said reject) and see how the voting rule broke the tie. Show the reasoning that won.

accuracy = number of AI verdicts matching real jury verdict / total cases

The critical moment is a tie-break: when two agents disagree, the one citing stronger ground-truth facts wins the vote because voters check whether the reasoning points to something real in the evidence. That outcome is impossible with a single AI model; it is the signature of this method.
