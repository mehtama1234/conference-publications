Dynamic Novel View Synthesis in High Dynamic Range

Render moving scenes in HDR (showing full brightness and darkness detail) from ordinary video by adapting the color translation rule to match how the lighting changes over time.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Ordinary cameras clip very bright and very dark parts of a scene—they can't show what your eye sees. HDR (High Dynamic Range) rendering shows the full range. The challenge: build a 3D model that renders any viewpoint and any moment from plain video, while fixing the clipping problem and handling moving objects and changing lights. This paper's move is to adapt the color-translation rule (tone-mapping) as time passes, so the image stays consistent as lighting evolves.

Zero assumption version

Real scenes change, but cameras clip brightness

Ordinary cameras cannot record very bright or very dark parts of a scene. A sunny window overexposes to white; a shadow underexposes to black. The data is simply lost. You end up with a flat, clipped image that hides detail.

Building a 3D model (to view from any angle or moment) from these clipped videos is hard enough. But real scenes also move: people walk, lights turn on and off, reflections shift. A naive approach would treat the scene as frozen in time and assume one fixed rule to un-clip the colors. That breaks immediately when lighting changes—the same pixel might be clipped for a different reason at a different moment, so one fixed rule cannot fix all of them.

The paper's move: Use a tone-mapping rule that changes over time, adapting to how the overall brightness in the scene is evolving.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure radiance signature

For each time step, compute the average HDR brightness across all 3D point-cloud pieces (Gaussians).

Tone-map to LDR

Use a color-translation function that reads the radiance signature and adapts itself for that moment.

Fit 3D model over time

Adjust the 3D point cloud to match both the observed clipped video and any paired HDR ground truth.

Optimize jointly

Update the 3D positions, colors, and tone-mapping settings together until rendered images match training data.

Render any view and time

Query the 4D model (space plus time) from any camera angle at any moment—tone-mapping adapts automatically.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The tone-mapping function—the rule that translates from deep, real-world colors (HDR) down to what the camera records (LDR, the clipped version). Normally this rule stays fixed across the entire video. The paper makes it change from moment to moment based on the radiance signature, because the optimal squeeze strength depends on how bright the scene actually is—high radiance means use aggressive squeezing to protect highlights, low radiance means use gentler squeezing to keep shadow detail.

The hidden quantity

The radiance signature: the hidden guide

measured quantity  =  Radiance signature (average HDR brightness per time step)

The radiance signature is the average color value across all 3D Gaussian points at a given moment. Think of it as a single number that captures how bright the entire scene is right now—brighter if the sun just came in, darker if a cloud passed. It is a fingerprint of the lighting at that moment.

The paper uses this signature to steer the tone-mapping function. Instead of a fixed rule, the rule reads the signature and adjusts itself: when the signature says the scene is very bright, tone-map more aggressively to protect highlights; when it says dim, less aggressively. This keeps the color-translation consistent with how reality is actually changing.

Why it works

Why dynamic tone-mapping fixes the problem

Tone-mapping is the act of squeezing the full range of brightness in the real world (HDR) into the narrow range a camera can store (LDR). A naive single rule says: 'always apply this one squeeze formula.' But the squeeze itself depends on the scene. If the scene starts dim and then becomes very bright, the one rule will preserve the wrong details—it was tuned for the old brightness level.

The paper's insight: instead of one fixed rule, let the rule adapt based on the radiance signature. When the signature signals that brightness is rising, dynamically adjust how aggressively the tone-mapping squeezes colors so the color-translation stays true to the new lighting. This works because the radiance signature captures exactly how the lighting is changing—high signature means bright moment, low means dim—so tuning the squeeze strength to the signature keeps the translation honest across all moments. The 3D point-cloud positions, their colors, and the tone-mapping function all update together during training. At render time, the point-cloud pieces know both where they are and their HDR colors; the tone-mapping layer automatically bakes in the adaptive function and produces a clipped LDR image—or, if you want full HDR, it skips the clipping. The result is that moving scenes with time-varying light render consistently from any viewpoint.

The dynamic tone-mapping equation

First in words:

tone-mapped color at time t equals a function applied to the HDR color, where the function parameters depend on the radiance signature at time t

Then the compact form:

LDR_color_t = f_tm(HDR_color_t; radiance_signature_t)

Every symbol:

LDR_color_tthe clipped color the camera stores at time t
f_tmthe tone-mapping function, which squeezes brightness
HDR_color_tthe deep, true color in the 3D model at time t
radiance_signature_tthe average brightness across the scene at time t, which steers the tone-mapping
A concrete example over three moments
Time 1 (dim light): radiance_signature = 50; tone-mapping is gentle (scale 0.5). A true HDR value of 200 becomes LDR = 0.5 * 200 = 100.
Time 2 (very bright): radiance_signature = 200; tone-mapping is aggressive (scale 0.2). Same HDR value 200 becomes LDR = 0.2 * 200 = 40 (more compressed).
Time 3 (back to dim): radiance_signature = 50; tone-mapping returns to gentle (scale 0.5). Same HDR value 200 becomes LDR = 0.5 * 200 = 100.
The radiance signature guides the scale factor: high signature means bright scene, tone-map more aggressively; low signature means dim scene, preserve detail with a gentler squeeze. This keeps the color-translation consistent with reality.

The key is that the tone-mapping function is not a constant—it reads the radiance signature and changes its behavior to match how the lighting is actually evolving in the scene.

What can go wrong

Sparse or missing HDR ground truth. The method learns tone-mapping best when paired HDR data is available during training. If HDR ground truth is scarce or absent, the tone-mapping must be inferred only from the clipped LDR video, which is ambiguous—multiple tone-maps could explain the same LDR output. The algorithm degrades to LDR-only reconstruction.
Sudden extreme illumination changes. If the radiance signature jumps abruptly (e.g., a light turns on instantly), the tone-mapping adaptation may lag or overshoot, causing flickering or color discontinuities at the transition. The method assumes gradual changes in the radiance signature.
High-frequency temporal flickering in the input video. If the input LDR video itself has noise or compression artifacts that change frame-to-frame, the radiance signature may pick up spurious fluctuations, causing the tone-mapping to wobble and produce jittery output.
Done right: smooth, well-lit captures. When the input video is clean, lighting evolves gradually, and some HDR reference frames are available, the dynamic tone-mapping adapts smoothly, and the 3D reconstruction is photorealistic and consistent across all views and times.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Record a short video of a dynamic scene with challenging lighting—e.g., a moving person under a window where sunlight comes and goes. Use a phone camera in HDR mode to capture ground-truth HDR reference frames; also save the phone's standard LDR video. Train the model on the LDR video, optionally with a few HDR frames. Then render from a camera angle not in the training data and show: (1) The reconstructed HDR at that new viewpoint matches the expected appearance (brights are detailed, darks are detailed, not clipped). (2) At different times in the video, the rendered colors stay consistent—no flickering as tone-mapping adapts. (3) Comparing naive static tone-mapping to dynamic, the dynamic version preserves detail in both bright and dark regions.

PSNR_HDR = 20 * log10(max_value / sqrt(mean_squared_error_hdr)); color_consistency = 1 - temporal_std_dev(pixel_colors); view_coherence = SSIM(new_viewpoint, reference_viewpoint_at_same_time)

The demo must make it impossible to miss that adapting the tone-map to the moment's lighting fixes flickering and color inconsistency, making moving scenes renderable in true HDR from any viewpoint.
