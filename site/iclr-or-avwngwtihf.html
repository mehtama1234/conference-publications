ASSESS: A Semantic and Structural Evaluation Framework for Statement Similarity

ASSESS evaluates formal statement translations by turning statements into operator trees and scoring how much semantic and structural editing is needed to transform one statement into another.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Autoformalization systems translate ordinary mathematical statements into formal language that proof assistants can check. Evaluating those translations is difficult. Exact string match is too brittle because equivalent formal statements can be written differently. Pure proof-based evaluation is also incomplete because a failed proof attempt gives almost no graded information about whether the translation was close or completely wrong. ASSESS fills that gap with a similarity framework that measures both semantics and structure. It represents formal statements as operator trees, allows meaning-preserving transformations inside those trees, and computes a real-valued similarity score, so near-correct translations receive partial credit because they require only small semantic or structural edits instead of being treated the same as unrelated ones. The paper also introduces EPLA, a benchmark of expert-annotated statement pairs, so the metric can be tested against human judgments of provability and structural likeness.

Zero assumption version

Formal translation needs a graded metric, not only exact match or proof success

A formal statement has both meaning and construction. Two statements may differ by variable names, commutative reordering, or other transformations that preserve meaning. A string-based metric treats those differences as large errors even when the mathematics is the same. That makes it unsuitable for evaluating generated formal statements because it punishes harmless rewrites as if they changed the theorem.

A proof-based metric goes to the opposite extreme. If a candidate statement can be proved equivalent to a reference, the score is essentially perfect. If proof search fails, the metric often collapses to a binary failure, even when the candidate is only one quantifier or operator away from being correct. For model development, that is not enough feedback. Researchers need a score that reflects how semantically and structurally close two formal statements are.

The paper's move. Represent each formal statement as an operator tree, allow meaning-preserving edits inside that tree, and score similarity by the minimum semantic-structural transformation cost, because low-cost edits correspond to small changes in meaning or structure.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Parse each formal statement into an operator tree.

Convert syntax into nodes for operators, predicates, quantifiers, and arguments so the metric can compare structure directly.

Define allowed transformations.

Specify semantic-aware edits such as renaming bound variables or reordering commutative expressions with lower cost than edits that change meaning, because those rewrites preserve the statement's logical content.

Compute transformation cost.

Search for the minimum-cost sequence of edits that turns one tree into the other.

Map cost to similarity.

Convert the edit cost into a real-valued score where smaller semantic-structural change means higher similarity.

Validate against expert labels on EPLA.

Check whether the metric agrees with human judgments of provability and structural likeness across annotated statement pairs.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  semantic-structural edit distance between formal statements

The hidden quantity is not the visible text difference. It is the amount of mathematically important structure that must change to convert one statement into the other. Two formulas may differ in many characters but require only trivial semantic edits. Another pair may share much of the same text while differing in one quantifier that reverses the meaning.

ASSESS captures that hidden quantity by comparing operator trees under transformation rules that know which edits are cheap and which are expensive. The resulting measure, TransTED Similarity, is informative because it combines three kinds of evidence at once: how the syntax tree is organized, where the semantic operators sit, and whether a transformation preserves or changes logical meaning.

Why it works

Why tree-based semantic edits give a better score

Formal statements are compositional. Quantifiers bind variables, predicates apply to arguments, and operators combine subexpressions recursively. A tree representation matches that structure naturally. Once the statement is in tree form, the metric can locate whether a difference appears in a harmless place, such as variable naming, or in a decisive place, such as the relation or quantifier that determines the theorem's meaning.

The semantic transformation rules are the second key piece. Standard tree edit distance would still over-penalize many mathematically equivalent rewrites. By assigning low cost to meaning-preserving transformations and higher cost to semantic changes, TransTED turns the edit process into a closer approximation of how a human expert compares formal statements.

This produces a more useful training and evaluation signal. A model that outputs a near-correct formalization gets partial credit rather than a flat failure. A model that preserves many surface symbols but changes the logical core is penalized appropriately. That balance is what earlier metrics lacked.

Math after the picture

Similarity from minimum transformation cost

First in words:

Build an operator tree for each statement, compute the least expensive semantic-aware edit path between the two trees, and map that cost to a similarity score. Small cost means the statements are close in both meaning and structure.

Then the compact form:

TransTED_similarity = 1 / (1 + min_edit_cost(tree_a, tree_b))

Every symbol:

tree_a, tree_boperator-tree representations of the two formal statements
min_edit_cost( , )the minimum total cost of semantic-aware tree edits needed to transform one tree into the other
semantic-aware editan insertion, deletion, substitution, or allowed transformation whose cost depends on how much meaning or structure changes
TransTED_similaritythe final graded similarity score, higher when fewer low-cost edits are needed
A tiny worked example
Suppose the reference statement says that for all x, P(x) implies Q(x).
A generated statement says that for all y, P(y) implies Q(y).
The operator trees differ only by a bound-variable rename, so the minimum edit cost is low and the similarity score stays high.
Now change the candidate to for all y, P(y) implies R(y).
That requires changing the predicate node itself, which carries a higher semantic cost, so the similarity score drops even though most of the text still matches.

The exact paper metric may normalize cost more carefully, but the central principle is unchanged: similarity is the inverse of the semantic-structural work needed to turn one statement into the other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The edit rules can miss equivalences. If an important meaning-preserving rewrite is not included in the semantic transformation set, the metric may still over-penalize an otherwise correct statement.
Parsing errors propagate directly. The whole framework depends on accurate operator trees. If the formal parser builds the wrong tree, the similarity score becomes unreliable.
Benchmark labels still reflect human conventions. EPLA provides expert annotation, but judgments of structural likeness can still depend on what experts consider a significant reformulation.
Done right. Use the metric alongside proof checking: the proof result tells you whether the translation is formally valid, and ASSESS tells you how close failed or alternative translations are.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small evaluator for formal statement pairs drawn from a benchmark such as miniF2F or ProofNet. For each pair, compute a symbol-based score, a proof-based signal if available, and the ASSESS tree-based similarity score.

The demo should include near-equivalent pairs, structurally similar but semantically wrong pairs, and clearly unrelated pairs. A convincing result shows that the tree-based score ranks those cases in the expected order and aligns more closely with expert labels than the baselines do.

ranking_quality = correlation(metric_scores, expert_similarity_labels)

The essential claim to verify is that a useful evaluator for autoformalization must provide graded semantic and structural feedback, not only exact string overlap or binary proof outcome.
