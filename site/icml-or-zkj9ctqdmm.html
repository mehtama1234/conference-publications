Zero assumption version

Measuring confidence in a model's predictions

Once a neural network is trained, it makes a prediction. But how confident should you be in that prediction? Is it likely to be wrong? A good way to measure this is to imagine training the model many times on slightly different data and watching where the predictions scatter. If they all cluster in one spot, you can be confident. If they scatter widely, the model is unsure.

The standard way to measure this scattering uses continuous-time math — it pretends the training algorithm takes infinitely tiny steps, so it slides smoothly. That works fine with small batches. But in real life, when you train on thousands of data points at once (a large batch), the algorithm takes big lumpy steps, not smooth ones. The smooth-motion math gives wrong answers about how much the prediction should scatter.

A beginner might try to ignore batch size and just apply the old theory anyway. But the old theory predicts too much certainty or too little, which makes the model's confidence estimates useless.

The paper's fix in one sentence: Use discrete-time math (modeling the actual lumpy steps) to predict the scatter in predictions, so the certainty estimates stay accurate even with large batches.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the continuous-time limit with a discrete-time model. Instead of pretending the algorithm takes infinitely small steps, it models them at their actual size. This tiny change in how you describe the algorithm cascades: it means the randomness from subsampling no longer averages away the same way, and the interplay between step size and batch size becomes visible.

the object it moves  =  the time model (discrete instead of continuous)
The hidden quantity

The stationary covariance: where predictions scatter

measured quantity  =  stationary covariance (prediction scatter in the long run)

The hidden quantity is the stationary covariance: a mathematical measure of how much the model's final predictions bounce around across many training runs. It captures the inevitable jiggling that happens when you train on random batches. A smaller covariance means the model is confident; a larger one means it is unsure.

The paper estimates this by building a mathematical model of the algorithm's discrete steps, then calculating (not measuring) where the predictions land in the long run. The formula takes the step size, batch size, and the data's noise profile as inputs, and outputs a prediction of the scatter.

Plain reading. When you train a model, the final answer wiggles slightly around its best value because batches are random. That wiggle is the stationary covariance. Large wiggles mean low confidence. Small wiggles mean high confidence.
Paper reading. The stationary covariance is defined as the long-run variance of the iterates under the invariant distribution of the SG(L)D Markov chain, given fixed step size and batch size.
Why it works

Why the discrete model works

The core insight is that large batches change how noisy the weight-change estimates are. With tiny batches, randomness averages away; with large batches, significant randomness remains. The old continuous-time theory assumed the noise shrinks to zero, which is false at realistic batch sizes. The discrete-time model keeps the noise explicit.

Here is the causal chain: when you use a large batch, the gradient computed from that batch is still random (different data, different gradient), but less random than with a tiny batch. This residual randomness is not captured by the continuous-time limit. The discrete model tracks it: the stationary covariance formula includes terms that account for the gradient noise at each step. Because the formula is discrete (not a limit), it remains accurate even when step size and batch size are not vanishingly small. You can then compute exactly how much the predictions scatter.

The algorithm proposed in the paper takes this formula and inverts it: given target levels of certainty and a batch size, solve for the right step size. This turns the formula into a tuning procedure. You no longer guess; you compute.

Math after the picture

The covariance formula

First in words:

stationary covariance = correction_for_batch_subsampling * (gradient_noise_at_convergence) + correction_for_step_size

Then the compact form:

C = (1/B) * I - (1/N^2) * sum_n J_n Sigma J_n + corrections_from_discrete_steps

Every symbol:

Cstationary covariance (how much predictions scatter)
Bbatch size (how many data points per step)
Imeasured Fisher information (curvature of error surface)
Ntotal data points
J_ngradient Jacobian for data point n
Sigmagradient noise covariance
discrete_correctionsterms that vanish only as step size shrinks to zero
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 100 data points, batch size 10, and gradient noise Sigma = 2.0.
Fisher curvature at convergence: I = 5.0 (Hessian of the parabolic error bowl).
Subsampling term: (1/10) * (1/100^2) * sum_n(J_n * 2.0 * J_n) ~ 1.5 (concrete value depends on Jacobians).
Step size correction: if step size is 0.01, discrete_corrections ~ 0.3.
Total covariance estimate: 1.5 + 0.3 = 1.8.
This means predictions scatter with standard deviation sqrt(1.8) ~ 1.34. Confidence interval is roughly +/- 1.34 from the mean prediction.

The formula captures two sources of scatter: noisy gradients from subsampling, plus the discrete jumps from a non-vanishing step size. The first term dominates at large batches; the second becomes visible when step size is not tiny.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Model misfit. If the error surface is not bowl-shaped (the quadratic assumption fails), the formula breaks down. Real neural networks have error surfaces that twist and bend in many directions. A parabola is only accurate in a small region near the minimum.
Very large step sizes. If the step size is so large that the iterates jump wildly across multiple different regions of the error surface, the discrete approximation becomes inaccurate. The formula assumes you stay in the quadratic bowl.
Non-independent batches. The theory assumes each batch is drawn independently. If batches are correlated (e.g., data is ordered by class), the gradient noise structure changes and the formula is wrong.
Done right. If you stay in the quadratic bowl, use reasonable step sizes, and shuffle batches, the formula accurately predicts how much the predictions scatter. Use it to set the step size for honest confidence intervals.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple regression problem on synthetic data: pick 100 points on a line with noise (varying noise, meaning the noise size changes along the line). Train SGD or SGLD with different batch sizes. For each batch size, run the algorithm many times with different random seeds and measure where the final predictions scatter. Then compute the covariance prediction from the paper's formula. Plot both the measured scatter and the formula prediction; they should match closely, especially at large batch sizes where the old theory fails.
