Risk-Averse and Optimistic Advertiser Incentive Compatibility in Auto-bidding

Refine incentive guarantees for auto-bidding by comparing truthful reporting and misreporting under the best and worst equilibria that can arise.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

In modern ad auctions, advertisers often do not submit a bid for each impression directly. They hand a goal and a budget-like constraint to an auto-bidding system, which then chooses bids on their behalf. That creates a new incentive question: should an advertiser report those constraints truthfully? The difficulty is that the same reported constraints can produce several equilibria, meaning several stable outcomes where no participant wants to deviate. This paper replaces one very strict truthfulness requirement with two weaker ones, one for risk-averse advertisers and one for optimistic advertisers, and then shows that the second-price auction satisfies both.

Zero assumption version

Why truthfulness becomes harder with auto-bidding

In a classical auction, truthfulness often means that stating your true value is the best strategy. In auto-bidding, the advertiser reports higher-level constraints such as return targets or spending limits, and the bidding agent turns those reports into many per-auction actions. The mapping from report to outcome is indirect, so incentive analysis must account for both the agent's behavior and the auction rule.

A second complication is equilibrium multiplicity. The same reports can support several stable outcomes with different utilities for the advertiser. A truthfulness definition that compares the worst truthful equilibrium to the best deviating equilibrium is so strict that it can classify a reasonable mechanism as non-truthful even when truthful reporting is preferable under more realistic attitudes toward equilibrium selection.

The paper's change. Judge truthful reporting against the worst truthful and worst deviating equilibria for risk-averse advertisers, and against the best truthful and best deviating equilibria for optimistic advertisers.

What it actually does

Advertisers report constraints, bidding agents respond, and the auction outcome is described as an equilibrium of that induced game.

Define the old benchmark

Start from Auto-bidding Incentive Compatibility, which compares the worst truthful equilibrium with the best deviating equilibrium.

Introduce RAIC

For a risk-averse advertiser, compare the least favorable truthful equilibrium with the least favorable equilibrium under any misreport.

Introduce OAIC

For an optimistic advertiser, compare the most favorable truthful equilibrium with the most favorable equilibrium under any misreport.

Prove the property for SPA

Show that the second-price auction preserves truthful reporting under both definitions because misreporting cannot improve the matched equilibrium benchmark.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the incentive question itself. Instead of asking for a single extreme guarantee that mixes the worst truthful case with the best deviating case, it separates advertiser attitudes toward equilibrium selection and evaluates truthfulness with a matched comparison.

the object it moves  =  the definition of incentive compatibility under auto-bidding and multiple equilibria
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  which equilibrium outcome the advertiser expects to matter

The main hidden quantity is not just utility from one auction result. It is the advertiser's stance toward equilibrium selection. If the advertiser plans conservatively, the relevant number is the lowest utility among equilibria compatible with a report. If the advertiser expects favorable coordination, the relevant number is the highest such utility.

Once that stance is fixed, the incentive test becomes clearer. Truthful reporting should beat misreporting under the same stance. Mixing a pessimistic truthful benchmark with an optimistic deviating benchmark builds in an asymmetry that is stronger than many applications require because it gives the deviation credit for a favorable equilibrium that truthful reporting is not allowed to use.

Why it works

Why the refined definitions give a sharper incentive picture

Multiple equilibria create an ambiguity that classical truthfulness definitions do not face. A report is no longer linked to one utility value. It is linked to a set of possible utility values. Any incentive statement must therefore explain how that set is summarized.

RAIC uses the minimum utility in each set. This fits a cautious advertiser because the relevant question is whether a misreport can protect against a bad outcome better than telling the truth. OAIC uses the maximum utility in each set. This fits an advertiser who expects the market and bidding dynamics to settle on the favorable equilibrium if one exists.

The second-price auction satisfies both because its payment rule already discourages strategic distortion of value information. When the auto-bidding constraint report is evaluated through matched worst-case or matched best-case comparisons, the truthful report still dominates: misreporting does not create a better equilibrium summary under the same attitude because the report change does not improve the advertiser's utility floor or utility ceiling relative to truthful reporting.

Math after the picture

The RAIC comparison

First in words:

A report satisfies the risk-averse notion if the least favorable truthful equilibrium gives at least as much utility as the least favorable equilibrium under any deviation.

Then the compact form:

min_(e in E(t)) u(e) >= min_(e in E(m)) u(e)

Every symbol:

tthe truthful report
ma possible misreport
E(t)the set of equilibria that can arise after the truthful report
E(m)the set of equilibria that can arise after the misreport
u(e)the advertiser utility at equilibrium e
A tiny worked example
Suppose truthful reporting can lead to advertiser utilities 7 or 10 across two equilibria.
Suppose one misreport can lead to utilities 6 or 11.
For RAIC, compare the minima: truthful gives 7 and the misreport gives 6.
Truthful reporting wins because the cautious advertiser cares about the floor, not the best lucky outcome.

OAIC uses the same structure with maxima instead of minima. The point is to compare truthful and deviating reports under one consistent equilibrium attitude.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Matched comparisons still depend on the equilibrium concept. If the market model uses an equilibrium notion that misses important bidder dynamics, the truthfulness guarantee may describe the wrong strategic object.
Real advertisers may not be purely risk-averse or purely optimistic. Some advertisers may weigh several equilibria probabilistically, so RAIC and OAIC bracket behavior but do not cover every attitude.
The proof is mechanism-specific. Second-price auction behavior does not automatically transfer to other auction rules, because the payment and allocation incentives can change the equilibrium utility sets.
Done right. Choose an equilibrium concept that matches the auto-bidding system, state which advertiser attitude is being modeled, and test truthfulness with a matched comparison because that separates reporting incentives from equilibrium-selection asymmetry.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small simulated ad auction with two advertisers, a second-price payment rule, and bidding agents that convert reported constraints into bids. For each advertiser, enumerate the equilibria induced by truthful reporting and by a few strategic misreports.

Then compute the RAIC and OAIC summaries for each report. The demo should show that truthful reporting remains preferred under both summaries because misreporting does not improve the advertiser's matched worst-case or matched best-case utility in the second-price setting.

raic_margin = min_utility_truthful - min_utility_misreport ; oaic_margin = max_utility_truthful - max_utility_misreport

The important output is the pair of margins, since they show directly whether truthful reporting survives the equilibrium multiplicity problem under cautious and optimistic views.
