Feasible Fusion: Constrained Joint Estimation under Structural Non-Overlap

When observational data covers cases that the randomized trial barely touches, the paper learns a representation where causal constraints can still be enforced while keeping the statistical strength of the large observational sample.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Modern causal estimation often combines two data sources with very different strengths. Observational data is large and covers many users, treatments, and contexts, but treatment assignment is biased. Randomized trial data is much smaller, yet it carries the identification signal needed for causal claims because treatment was assigned experimentally. Standard fusion methods try to reweight or combine these sources directly in the original covariates. This paper shows that a deeper failure appears when some treatment-context combinations exist in the observational data but are structurally absent from the randomized trial. In that regime, the experimental restrictions cannot simply be imposed by weighting. The paper therefore turns fusion into a constrained joint estimation problem: fit the broad observational sample while learning a representation in which experimental moment conditions are actually feasible to satisfy.

Zero assumption version

Why large observational data is not enough

Suppose a platform wants to estimate the effect of several pricing actions on driver behavior. It has a huge observational log with many contexts and many actions, so prediction looks easy. But those logs reflect the platform's old policy, not random assignment. Some actions were almost never shown in some contexts because the production policy avoided them. If you train only on that data, the estimated treatment effect is mixed with the platform's old decision rule.

A randomized controlled trial fixes identification but introduces a new bottleneck: the trial is small and expensive. In practice it may cover only a thin slice of the treatment-context space. Standard fusion methods assume the trial can anchor the observational estimator everywhere after reweighting. The paper shows this can fail under structural non-overlap, meaning the trial does not merely undersample some regions but leaves whole treatment-context structures in practice unavailable for the required causal restrictions.

The central obstacle. If the experimental sample does not provide the treatment-context support needed to enforce the causal restrictions, no amount of direct weighting in the original feature space can repair the fusion.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Fit the main prediction target on observational data

Use the large sample to reduce variance, because the observational source contains far more examples than the trial.

Write down experimental moment conditions

Encode the causal restrictions that should hold under random assignment, because those are the validity conditions the final estimator must respect.

Check feasibility under current representation

Measure whether those restrictions can actually be satisfied with the experimental support available, because structural non-overlap may make the raw space impossible.

Jointly learn representation and predictor

Move the data into a representation where observational risk stays low and experimental moments become enforceable.

Optimize with a penalized primal-dual procedure

Adjust model internal settings and constraint multipliers together, because the method needs to trade off fit, feasibility, and violation penalties during learning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the fusion objective itself. Instead of first fitting an observational estimator and then trying to patch it with weights or moment corrections, it solves one constrained learning problem that treats observational accuracy and experimental validity as joint requirements.

the object it moves  =  the fusion objective
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  moment feasibility under representation

The deciding hidden quantity is whether the experimental identifying moments are feasible in the current representation. A moment condition is a requirement that certain average residuals or correlations vanish under the correct causal model. In plain terms, the experimental data should not systematically contradict the estimator once treatment assignment is random.

Structural non-overlap makes feasibility the real issue. The problem is not only that the experimental sample is small. It is that the raw features separate cases in a way that leaves the trial unable to support the required equalities. By learning a representation, the method can merge irrelevant distinctions and preserve the distinctions that matter for treatment effect estimation. That can turn an infeasible constraint system into a feasible one without giving up the trial's identifying role.

Why it works

Why joint estimation succeeds where weighted fusion fails

A weighting-based fusion method assumes there exists some direct correction that makes the observational sample behave as though it came from the randomized design. That logic breaks when the experimental sample lacks the structural support needed for the identifying restrictions. Then the estimator can fit the observational risk well yet still have no way to satisfy the trial-based conditions. The failure is geometric in the sense that the feasible set defined by the moments does not intersect the estimator class in the raw feature space.

The paper's response is to learn the predictor and the representation together. The predictor still uses the large observational sample to reduce estimation noise. But the representation is shaped by the trial constraints, so it is not free to preserve every observational pattern. Distinctions that only reflect the historical logging policy can be compressed away, while distinctions required to keep treatment effects identifiable are retained. This is why the learned representation can recover overlap that was missing for constraint enforcement, even though it cannot invent experimental data.

The primal-dual algorithm matters because the objective has two competing terms. One term wants low observational risk. Another wants the experimental moments close to zero. The dual variables increase pressure when those moments are violated, so training repeatedly asks: can the current representation satisfy the trial restrictions without sacrificing too much predictive accuracy? The final error decomposition then separates three sources of failure: not recovering enough usable overlap, still violating moments, and ordinary finite-sample statistical error.

Math after the picture

The joint constrained objective

First in words:

The method minimizes observational prediction risk while penalizing violation of experimental moment conditions. The predictor parameters and representation parameters are trained together, and the penalty grows when the trial-based restrictions are not met.

Then the compact form:

min over theta, phi of R_obs(theta, phi) + lambda * ||m_exp(theta, phi)||^2

Every symbol:

thetaprediction model parameters
phirepresentation parameters
R_obsobservational risk term fit on the large observational sample
m_expvector of experimental moment violations computed from the randomized sample
lambdapenalty weight controlling how strongly trial validity is enforced
A tiny worked example
Model A has observational risk 0.12 and experimental moment violation norm 0.50.
With lambda = 4, its total objective is 0.12 + 4 * 0.50^2 = 1.12.
Model B has observational risk 0.18 and moment violation norm 0.10.
Its total objective is 0.18 + 4 * 0.10^2 = 0.22.
Model B fits the observational data slightly worse but respects the experimental restrictions much better, so the joint estimator prefers it.
Representation learning is useful exactly when it can move from an A-type solution toward a B-type solution.

The actual paper uses a constrained and primal-dual formulation, but this shorthand captures the main tradeoff: exploit the big observational sample without ignoring the experimental validity conditions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

No representation can recover the needed support. If the trial entirely lacks the treatment variation required for a causal comparison, representation learning cannot invent that information. The method can only recover feasibility when the missing support is structural in the raw space but recoverable after compression.
Penalty tuning can distort the tradeoff. If the constraint penalty is too weak, the model mostly follows observational correlations. If it is too strong, the predictor may overfit the small trial sample and lose too much statistical efficiency.
Representation collapse can hide real effect heterogeneity. Compressing features too aggressively may erase context distinctions that actually matter for treatment response, producing a feasible but oversimplified estimator.
Done right. The method is strongest when the randomized sample is small but still informative, the observational sample is rich, and the missing overlap arises from feature granularity that a learned representation can simplify without destroying treatment-effect signal.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic multi-treatment problem with two data sources. Generate a large observational dataset from a biased logging policy and a much smaller randomized dataset that only covers some treatment-context combinations. Then compare three estimators: observational-only, direct weighted fusion, and the paper's constrained joint estimator with learned representations.

The demo should measure both prediction error and experimental moment violation. It should also vary the severity of structural non-overlap. A convincing result is that direct fusion degrades sharply once overlap disappears in the raw feature space, while the learned representation restores low moment violation and lower-error treatment-effect estimates because the constraints are enforced in a space where the trial and observational samples overlap.

joint_score = treatment_effect_error + alpha * moment_violation

The important outcome is not only lower prediction error, but lower causal error while still extracting efficiency from the large observational source.
