Contact-guided Real2Sim from Monocular Video

When reconstructing a scene from a single video camera, the paper watches where a human body touches the ground to fill in hidden surfaces, then uses physics simulation to make sure the result is real-world accurate.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot needs to learn motion from a casual video. Traditional video-to-simulation pipelines extract blurry, incomplete scene shapes that break when a motion-tracking policy tries to use them. This paper introduces CRISP, which uses a key insight: where a human body makes contact with the scene (feet on ground, hands on table) reveals surfaces that the camera cannot see. By fitting clean, flat shapes to depth measurements and using contact clues to fill hidden areas, CRISP produces simulation-ready scene models that pass a physics realism check.

Zero assumption version

Noisy scene surfaces break simulation pipelines

Modern video analysis can extract where a human body is and how it moves frame by frame. But extracting scene surfaces from a single camera view is fundamentally hard: the camera only sees surfaces facing it, while interior corners, the underside of a table, or where a foot touches ground are hidden.

When researchers try to build simulatable scenes from video, they often get cloudy point clouds (millions of noisy depth measurements piled up) or incomplete mesh models. These contain artifacts—duplicate structures, floating fragments, disconnected patches. When a motion-tracking policy then tries to use these flawed surface maps in physics simulation, it fails catastrophically: the humanoid penetrates surfaces, slips off edges, or jitters.

The core problem: Hidden contact surfaces remain invisible to the camera, but the humanoid body needs exact scene surfaces to learn realistic motion in simulation.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Extract camera poses and depth

Run visual SLAM on the video to learn camera position/orientation per frame, then estimate depth (distance to surfaces).

Cluster depth into flat shapes

Group nearby depth points by their alignment (using surface normals—the direction each point faces) into compact planar clusters.

Use contact as a hint

Where the body's joints (feet, hands) touch the scene, assume those contact surfaces are real and refine the shape fit.

Check physics realism

Train a humanoid to imitate the reconstructed motion in simulation using reinforcement learning, discarding surfaces that cause penetration or instability.

Deliver simulation assets

Export clean, watertight geometric primitives (sealed, no holes) that physics engines can use directly.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the reconstruction target from dense, messy point clouds or polygon meshes to compact, flat, convex planar primitives—dozens of thin flat shapes that tile the scene like roof panels on a house. This trade-off (giving up fine detail like a curved armrest) buys a massive gain: the result is simulation-ready, fast to compute, and physically plausible.

The hidden quantity

The contact-surface consistency score

measured quantity  =  Contact surface agreement

A hidden quantity the paper measures is how well the reconstructed scene surfaces match where the human body actually makes contact. If the humanoid's foot is supposed to touch the ground at frame 47 but the reconstructed floor is 3 centimeters below, the motion tracking will fail. The paper estimates this by running a contact-prediction network (trained to detect which joints are in contact) and then checking if a surface exists at that location.

This score acts as a gate: surfaces that contradict observed contact are rejected or refined. Surfaces with high contact agreement are trusted and carried forward into simulation.

Why it works

How contact reveals hidden surfaces

The core mechanism is simple and physical: a human body has mass and cannot pass through solid objects. Every point of contact (foot on floor, back against a wall) marks a surface that must exist. When the camera sees a foot in motion but the depth sensor gives no reading (because the camera angle hides the ground plane), the contact signal fills the gap. Clustering then groups all confirmed contact points plus nearby depth measurements into a single flat primitive.

Why does flat surface representation work? Because scenes in video are dominated by large planar surfaces—floors, walls, table tops, chair seats. Rather than trying to capture every curve and contour (which requires dense, accurate depth), the paper bets that a small number of flat shapes covers 95% of what matters for motion simulation. The physics solver then validates this bet: if the humanoid penetrates the reconstructed floor, the surface refinement loop knows to adjust.

The reinforcement learning step at the end enforces a hard constraint: it forces the entire pipeline (camera calibration, motion extraction, surface reconstruction, contact model) to pass a physical realism test. A motion that looks good in video but violates physics (e.g., a foot phasing through the floor) is rejected by the policy reward signal. This closes the loop and ensures the output is truly simulatable.

Math after the picture

Measuring surface fit and contact agreement

First in words:

overlap_score = sum over primitive pairs of (depth_consensus × contact_consistency × normal_alignment) weighted by cluster size

Then the compact form:

score_b = sum_a [ overlap_ratio(S_i,a, S_j,b) × cos(normal_i,a, normal_j,b) × contact_weight(foot_at_b) ]

Every symbol:

overlap_ratio(S_i,a, S_j,b)fraction of points in cluster S_i,a that align with cluster S_j,b (larger = better match)
cos(normal_i,a, normal_j,b)how parallel two cluster surfaces are, ranging from -1 to +1 (closer to +1 = same orientation)
contact_weight(foot_at_b)confidence boost if a body joint (foot, hand) is recorded at location b; multiplier from 0.5 to 1.5
score_bfinal confidence that primitive b is real and useful for simulation
A tiny worked example
Cluster A (floor): overlap 0.92, normals align 0.98, foot contact present → score = 0.92 × 0.98 × 1.3 = 1.17 (high confidence)
Cluster B (noise): overlap 0.15, normals differ 0.20, no contact → score = 0.15 × 0.20 × 0.5 = 0.015 (discard)
Cluster C (wall): overlap 0.78, normals align 0.85, hand contact present → score = 0.78 × 0.85 × 1.2 = 0.79 (keep, but lower than floor)
Physics check: policies trained on A+C succeed (high score surfaces); policies trained on B fail (noisy surfaces discarded).

Surfaces with high contact agreement and depth consistency are kept; surfaces that contradict the body's motion are refined or removed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Contact model is only as good as the training data. If the neural network for predicting contact was trained only on sitting videos, it will mispredict contact during standing or jumping. A false contact signal can place a phantom surface where none exists, breaking the motion.
Curved surfaces cannot be represented. A person leaning against a cylindrical pillar or sitting on a rounded armchair will have poor surface reconstruction. The planar primitive model trades fine detail for efficiency and speed; scenes with intricate shapes need denser primitives, which slows simulation.
Occluded contact remains invisible. If a person sits in a chair with arms that fully hide the seat, and the camera cannot see the foot touching the floor, there is no contact signal to recover the seat surfaces. The method can infer surfaces from neighboring planes, but accuracy depends on how similar the scene structure is.
Done right: physics realism as a check. By training the humanoid control policy in simulation and measuring its success, the pipeline has a ground truth: if the humanoid falls through the floor or jitters violently, the surfaces are wrong and are rejected before export.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo takes a 3 to 5 second casual phone video of a person interacting with a chair or table (sitting, reaching, standing). It reconstructs the scene surfaces using CRISP, then trains a humanoid motion-imitation policy in simulation for 100 steps of reinforcement learning. The key metric is whether the humanoid successfully executes the recorded motion without penetrating surfaces or becoming unstable.

success_rate = (number of frames policy imitated without penetration) / (total frames) × 100%

If the humanoid sits stably on the chair seat and stands up without slipping, the reconstructed surfaces are real enough for simulation. If it falls through the seat or jerks violently, the surface refinement failed.
