ReCARE: Preserving Linked Concepts During Harmful Concept Removal

When you erase a harmful concept from an image model, you accidentally delete innocent concepts that lived next to it; ReCARE erases only the harm and keeps the innocent ones.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Image models like Stable Diffusion can generate harmful content. To stop them, researchers train the model to unlearn harmful concepts like nudity or copyrighted artistic styles. But a naive unlearning trick backfires: when you erase nudity, the model also forgets the word person, because nudity and person appeared together in the training data. ReCARE is the first method that catches these innocent bystander concepts and protects them while erasing only the true harm.

Zero assumption version

The erasure side effect

Image models learn by seeing millions of pictures. Some pictures show nudity, and nearly all of those also show a person. When researchers want the model to refuse generating nudity, they retrain it to forget that concept. A simple way to unlearn nudity is to show the model pictures of nudity and tell it to predict the wrong noise, so those images become meaningless to it.

But here is the trap: because nudity and person appeared together so often, when you smash nudity into nonsense, person gets smashed too. The model can no longer generate images with people in them at all. The harm is gone, but so is an innocent concept. A beginner might try blocking nudity by erasing its name from the vocabulary, but that is too crude and cannot target just the harmful part of what the model learned.

The trade-off. Unlearning one concept can accidentally delete related innocent concepts because they were learned together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Generate target images

Create pictures of the harmful concept (e.g., nudity) that you want to unlearn.

Step 2: Extract word labels

Use a vision-language model to write down which words describe each image (words like person, body, photo).

Step 3: Build the CARE-set

Group the word labels and keep the ones that appear together often but would survive if you erase the harm (the innocents).

Step 4: Two-loss training

Teach the model to forget the harmful concept while protecting the CARE-set through an extra loss term.

Step 5: Verify preservation

Measure that both the harm is gone and the innocent concepts survived.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

ReCARE modifies the training signal that tells the model what to forget. Instead of a single erase signal, it adds a second signal that says preserve these innocent co-occurring concepts. The shift is small but essential: it decouples the harmful concept from its innocent neighbors.

the object it moves  =  training loss function
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: CARE preservation

measured quantity  =  CARE score

CARE stands for Co-occurring Associated REtained concepts. It is a number (0 to 1) that measures whether innocent concepts survive the unlearning. The paper proposes a new metric: you generate pictures of the target harmful concept with an outside vision-language model, extract the word labels it assigns, and measure whether those same labels still mean something to the unlearned model. If the unlearned model gives high confidence to labels like person and body when shown a picture of a person, the CARE score is high.

The paper measures CARE score by asking a frozen outside model (like CLIP) to label generated images, then seeing if those same labels predict strongly in the unlearned model. High CARE means the innocents survived. The researchers test this on nudity, Van Gogh style, and the tench fish, and show that ReCARE keeps CARE scores above 0.9 while older methods drop to 0.1 to 0.6.

Why it works

Why it works: protecting innocents by name

The core insight is that unlearning is a tug-of-war inside the model's weights. When you show unlearning examples (images of nudity) and tell the model to predict wrong noise, the model shifts its weights to forget those patterns. But weights are entangled: the patterns for nudity and person are braided together because they always appeared together in training. Pulling on nudity drags person with it.

ReCARE breaks that entanglement by naming the innocents explicitly. It constructs a CARE-set: a list of words like person, body, and photo that were extracted from the target harmful images but are innocent. Then it adds a second training loss that says preserve these words. Now the model feels two opposing forces: the erase loss pulls nudity weights down, but the retain loss pulls CARE-set weights up. The model learns to separate them, leaving nudity meaningless while person and body stay strong.

The erase loss is also refined: it does not just smash nudity, it pushes nudity away from the CARE representation (the embedding space where person, body live) and aligns harmful tokens with a cleaned-up reference. This is surgical compared to naive erasure, which is like knocking over furniture to kill a spider in the corner.

Math after the picture

The two-loss training objective

First in words:

total loss = erase loss + (strength factor times retain loss)

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_erase + lambda * L_retain

Every symbol:

L_erasepenalty for wrong noise predictions on harmful tokens
L_retainpenalty for wrong noise predictions on innocent (CARE) tokens
lambdaa number controlling how much to prioritize CARE preservation versus erasure
A tiny worked example
Imagine lambda = 1.0 (equal weight to both losses).
In one training step, L_erase = 2.5 (model still generating nudity somewhat).
In the same step, L_retain = 0.3 (CARE labels are slightly confused).
Total loss = 2.5 + 1.0 * 0.3 = 2.8.
The training algorithm nudges the weights to shrink both losses.
If we instead set lambda = 0.1 (care less about CARE), total = 2.5 + 0.03 = 2.53, and the model erases faster but ruins person/body more.
Higher lambda = more CARE preservation, but slower erasure. ReCARE tunes this trade-off.

The two losses are in tension: one pushes harmful concepts out, the other pulls innocent ones in, forcing the model to surgically separate them.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

CARE-set is incomplete. If the extracted innocents do not cover all co-occurring concepts (e.g., you miss clothing or background), some will still be erased. The method depends on the quality of the token extraction step.
Harmful concepts are deeply entangled. If nudity and person are not just correlated but semantically identical in the model (e.g., all person images are nudity), no amount of loss weighting can separate them. The method assumes they are distinguishable.
Lambda is tuned wrong. If lambda is too low, you erase nudity but also erase CARE. If lambda is too high, nudity survives. The hyperparameter must be chosen for each target concept.
Done right. When CARE-set is well-constructed, the harmful concept is truly distinct, and lambda is tuned via validation, ReCARE achieves a genuine three-way win: erasure, utility, and CARE preservation all high.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train ReCARE on a small diffusion model (or a frozen pretrained model via textual inversion like the paper uses) to unlearn nudity. Generate images with prompts: bare prompt for general images, nudity prompts, person prompts, and combined prompts. Measure that the model refuses nudity-specific prompts but accepts person prompts. Compare against a naive unlearning baseline (erase nudity with no CARE loss) to show that the baseline also refuses person prompts, proving the innocent-bystander problem exists and ReCARE solves it.

CARE_preservation = score of CARE-set tokens in unlearned model / score of CARE-set tokens in original model

The demo must show side-by-side that naive erasure kills person but ReCARE keeps it alive, making the need for CARE explicit.
