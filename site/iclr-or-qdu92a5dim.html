Refine Drugs, Don't Complete Them: Uniform-Source Discrete Flows for Fragment-Based Drug Discovery

Generate new drugs by refining all parts of a molecule in parallel, instead of building it word by word.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Drug discovery starts with fragments—small pieces of molecules known to bind to disease targets. Chemists want to grow these fragments into full drugs while keeping desired properties like potency and safety. Current models work like writing: they predict one token (chemical piece) at a time, deciding what comes next. InVirtuoGen instead flips this: it starts with a completely scrambled molecule and refines all parts at once, like unblurring a photo. This parallel refinement is faster because all atoms refine in one pass instead of requiring one pass per atom, and it keeps better track of chemistry constraints because later refinement steps can fix early mistakes, preventing errors from cascading.

Why token-by-token drug generation gets stuck

In drug discovery, a chemist starts with a molecular fragment—a small piece known to bind a disease target. The goal is to grow it into a full drug by adding atoms and bonds while keeping potency (it still binds), safety (it is not toxic), and synthetic feasibility (a lab can actually make it). Current generative models treat this like language: they predict one chemical symbol at a time. After predicting the first symbol, they predict the second conditioned on the first, then the third, and so on. This sequential approach has two problems. First, early mistakes cascade—a wrong first symbol steers the entire generation down a bad path. Second, the model must make a decision about whether to continue for every single position in the final molecule, so a 50-token drug requires 50 decision steps, and each decision point is a chance to fail.

A beginner might try: just use a standard language model or autoregressive model (a model that predicts one token at a time). But chemists have learned that constraints on bond valence, aromaticity, and ring closure are fragile under sequential prediction. The model often produces chemically invalid molecules—structures with too many bonds on an atom or impossible ring patterns. It wastes generation steps on fixing mistakes instead of exploring promising regions of chemical space.

In one sentence. InVirtuoGen treats drug generation as refinement—start with noise, refine all atoms and bonds in parallel—rather than sequential completion.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Start with uniform noise

Begin with a scrambled random molecule (all tokens equally likely, no chemistry yet).

2. Refine all positions in parallel

Each denoising step improves all atoms and bonds simultaneously, not one at a time, allowing the network to correct mistakes by seeing the whole evolving molecule.

3. Measure chemistry validity at every step

Check that bonds follow chemistry rules; guide the model away from invalid structures.

4. Combine with genetic algorithm for optimization

Score candidates by potency and safety; breed the best pairs for multi-objective improvement.

5. Finetune with property-guided steering

Use proximal optimization to nudge the model toward desired binding or safety values without forgetting chemistry.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

InVirtuoGen changes the generation direction from token-by-token completion to full-molecule refinement. Instead of predicting what comes next, the model predicts the entire molecule in parallel refinement steps. This decouples the number of sampling steps (how many times we refine) from the sequence length (how many atoms the drug has). The model learns to map a uniform random source (pure noise, no structure) to the data distribution (valid drugs with known properties). By refinement rather than completion, every position can change at every step, correcting early errors and exploring chemistry space more flexibly.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: refinement trajectory

measured quantity  =  Validity and chemical coherence during refinement steps

Refinement trajectory measures how smoothly the model transforms noise into a valid, drug-like molecule. At each denoising step, atoms and bonds shift, and the model must maintain chemical validity (no atom with too many bonds, no broken rings) while gradually assembling a structure that matches the training distribution (real drugs). This is not just counting valid molecules at the end—it is tracking whether every intermediate step stays chemically sound.

InVirtuoGen estimates trajectory coherence by computing which positions change at each step, whether those changes respect chemistry constraints, and whether the resulting molecule grows closer to a valid drug structure. The paper uses this implicitly by training the model to predict all positions at every denoising step, forcing the model to learn smooth refinement paths. Molecules that maintain validity throughout refinement can continue learning; trajectories that collapse into invalid structures are down-weighted in the loss. This is why parallel refinement works: the model learns to avoid local chemical dead-ends by correcting all atoms together.

Why it works

Why it works: parallel refinement avoids early mistakes

Sequential models fail because the first prediction commits the model to a path. If token 1 is wrong, tokens 2 through N must compensate or fail. Refinement flips this: all tokens start equally uncertain (uniform noise), and every step improves all of them together. Early errors do not cascade because later steps can correct them. If the model predicts the wrong atom at position 5 in step 1, step 2 can fix it when the model sees the evolving context of the whole molecule.

The second advantage is computational: sequential generation needs one forward pass per token, so a 50-token drug requires 50 passes. Refinement can use the same number of denoising steps regardless of length—a 30-token fragment and a 50-token drug both use 10 steps. This is because refinement is not deciding how many steps to take; it is deciding how much noise to remove at each step. The model learns to prioritize: in early steps, rough shapes (where is the core ring structure); in late steps, fine details (which substituent side chain). This focus prevents the model from wasting computation on micro-decisions. The training loss also reflects this: by computing the loss at every position and every denoising step, the model learns to refine valid molecules smoothly, because invalid intermediate structures produce high loss across many positions and steps.

The refinement loss

First in words:

Total loss sums predictions at all positions during all denoising steps, favoring molecules with smooth valid refinement paths

Then the compact form:

L = sum_t sum_i log p(x_i^t | x_noised^t) where x_i^t is atom i at denoising step t

Every symbol:

x_i^tPredicted atom type at position i, denoising step t
x_noised^tPartially denoised molecule up to step t
tDenoising step (1, 2, ..., T; T is final step)
iPosition in the molecule (atom index, 1 to N)
p(...)Probability the model assigns to that prediction
log pLog likelihood (higher is better, used to measure prediction confidence)
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have a 5-token drug and 3 denoising steps (T=3).
Step 1 (rough): model predicts [C, C, X, X, X] where X means 'uncertain'. Loss_1 = log(0.8) + log(0.8) + log(0.1) + log(0.1) + log(0.1) ≈ 1.6 + 1.6 - 2.3 - 2.3 - 2.3 = -3.8.
Step 2 (refining): model predicts [C, C, O, N, X]. Loss_2 = log(0.9) + log(0.9) + log(0.7) + log(0.6) + log(0.2) ≈ 0.95 + 0.95 - 0.36 - 0.51 - 1.6 = -0.57.
Step 3 (final): model predicts [C, C, O, N, H]. Loss_3 = log(0.95) + log(0.95) + log(0.95) + log(0.85) + log(0.9) ≈ -0.26.
Total loss = -3.8 - 0.57 - 0.26 = -4.63. Smooth refinement (improving predictions at each step) yields less total loss than chaotic or invalid intermediate states, so the model learns valid trajectories.

By summing loss over all steps and all positions, the model is penalized for invalid intermediate molecules, not just wrong final answers. This teaches refinement coherence.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fragment not preserved. If the fragment (the starting chemical piece) is small or weakly anchored in the training data, refinement might drift away from it. The model may generate drugs that ignore the fragment's binding mode, wasting effort. Constraining fragment tokens is necessary but adds computational overhead.
Genetic algorithm collapse. If all top candidates converge to a narrow region of chemistry space (e.g., all have the same core ring), breeding no longer explores new structures. Diversity is lost. The algorithm needs mechanisms to preserve fitness variation or restart exploration.
Proximal optimization overfitting. Finetuning for a specific property (e.g., binding score) without brakes can cause the model to memorize a single high-scoring molecule instead of learning a distribution of good drugs. The finetuned model then fails on related but different targets.
Done right. When fragment anchoring is enforced, genetic diversity is maintained, and property-guided optimization uses careful braking (preventing the model from chasing a single solution too hard), InVirtuoGen generates new multi-objective-balanced drugs that pass chemistry validity and docking checks, improving on all benchmarks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo starts with one fragment (e.g., an indole known to bind a kinase) and runs InVirtuoGen to generate 100 drug candidates optimizing 3 goals: binding strength, low toxicity, and synthesis feasibility. It measures 5 metrics: coverage (percentage of generated drugs unseen in training), validity (percentage with correct chemistry), potency (average docking score, how well each drug binds), and multi-objective coverage (fraction of the Pareto frontier—candidates where no other candidate beats them at all three goals at once). A second demo shows lead optimization starting from a weak hit (a molecule that binds but is toxic) and breeding toward a balanced drug. Code should be under 300 lines: initialize fragment, run denoising loop with validity checking, rank by genetic fitness, apply property-guided finetuning, and compute metrics.

Pareto_coverage = count(candidates_on_frontier) / total_candidates; Validity = count(chemically_valid) / count(generated)

A successful demo makes clear: parallel refinement finds more unseen drug structures than sequential models, maintains chemistry validity throughout, and multi-objective optimization produces drugs excelling at all three goals—potent and safe and synthesizable at once.
