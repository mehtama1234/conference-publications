Why regular distance measures fail for SPD matrices

A symmetric positive definite matrix (a square grid of numbers where reading it top-to-bottom gives the same values as left-to-right, and multiplying it by any list of numbers always points in a consistent direction) appears constantly in machine learning: covariance matrices (grids measuring how variables correlate), diffusion tensors (3-by-3 grids used in brain imaging to track how water moves), and weighted graph structures.

A naive approach: treat the matrix as a flat space and use Euclidean distance (the familiar straight-line distance). This breaks catastrophically. SPD matrices do not form a flat space — they live on a curved non-flat surface. Moving in a straight line on this curved surface takes you off the valid surface entirely, into regions where the matrix loses its positive-definite property. When gradients (the directions that tell training which way to nudge the numbers) point in nonsensical directions, training becomes unstable, and classifiers trained this way forget what they learned or fail to converge.

Existing solutions use special distance measures tailored to this curved structure. The problem: current approaches like the affine-invariant metric require computing matrix exponentials and logarithms — operations that demand inverting large matrices at every step. On 512 by 512 matrices, a single training step takes 60 to 70 seconds. For deep learning, this is unbearably slow.

In one sentence. Rewrite SPD neural networks to use a product-based distance metric computed via Cholesky factors — this works because breaking an SPD matrix into Cholesky factors exposes two independent flat spaces, each with a cheap distance calculation, which sum to a valid distance on the original matrix.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Discover the product structure

Cholesky decomposition writes any SPD matrix as the product of a triangular matrix L and its transpose. The space of all L forms a simple Euclidean space; the space of all diagonal positive numbers forms another. Two independent, flat pieces.

Equip each piece with a simple metric

For the triangular part, use standard Euclidean distance. For the diagonal part, use a scaled Euclidean distance (power-deformed or Bures-Wasserstein metric). Both use only multiplication and addition, no inverses or matrix exponentials.

Combine metrics via the product

The metric on the original SPD matrices inherits from the product: distance between two SPD matrices measures distance between their Cholesky factors, then sums those two distances together.

Translate to neural network operations

Compute learning gradients (directions that training nudges), exponential maps, and parallel transport using only element-wise operations on Cholesky factors. No matrix inversion.

Train SPD classifiers and deep networks

Apply the new metrics to Riemannian multinomial logistic regression (classification where the weights are SPD matrices) and residual blocks in SPD neural networks. Speed improves because element-wise ops are cheap; stability improves because no matrix inversion accumulates rounding errors.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the distance measure (metric) used inside SPD neural networks. Instead of affine-invariant, log-Euclidean, or other existing Riemannian metrics, it uses two new metrics derived from Cholesky decomposition: Power-Cholesky Metric (PCM) and Bures-Wasserstein-Cholesky Metric (BWCM).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: computational cost per training step

measured quantity  =  Number of primitive operations (additions, multiplications, inversions) per metric computation

The hidden quantity is computational cost: how many arithmetic operations the distance formula requires per training step. Affine-invariant and other existing approaches rely on matrix exponentials and logarithms, operations that internally require many matrix inversions and breakdowns into eigenvalues. On a 512 by 512 matrix, this costs thousands of multiplications and dozens of inversions.

The paper measures this by wall-clock runtime on standard hardware and by counting operations. It shows that PCM and BWCM compute learning gradients using only element-wise multiplications and additions on Cholesky factors — no matrix inversion at all. At 512 by 512 dimension, the cost is roughly 30 times lower: 1.7 seconds instead of 60 to 70 seconds per step.

Why it works

Why it works: the product structure insight

Every SPD matrix can be written as L times L-transpose, where L is a lower triangular matrix (Cholesky decomposition). This is a fundamental fact from linear algebra, but the paper's insight is structural: the space of all triangular L matrices is itself a simple flat space with Euclidean distance. The space of all diagonal positive numbers is another flat space. Two flat spaces combined remain flat.

When you combine two flat spaces, you can measure distance in the combined space by treating each component separately. Instead of designing one complicated distance formula on the curved SPD surface, use the natural flat distance on Cholesky factors. This automatically gives a valid distance on SPD matrices because the map from Cholesky factors to SPD matrices preserves distances (a mathematical isometry).

Concretely: compute Cholesky factors L and D, measure distance between L and L-prime using simple Euclidean distance, measure distance between D and D-prime using a power-deformed or Bures-Wasserstein distance (still just multiplication and addition), then add those two distances. The result is a valid distance formula on SPD matrices using only cheap operations. Training operations (computing how to nudge the numbers, exponential maps, parallel transport) then reduce to element-wise operations on Cholesky factors, bypassing every expensive matrix operation.

The metric via product structure

First in words:

The metric on Cholesky factors combines two simple distances: a flat Euclidean metric on the triangular part L (class gE), and a power-deformed metric on the diagonal part D (scaled by a deformation parameter theta). The metric on the original SPD matrices inherits by computing distances on the factors, then summing.

Then the compact form:

metric_SPD(P, Q) = euclidean_dist(L, L') + power_metric(D, D', theta)

Every symbol:

P, Qtwo symmetric positive definite matrices
L, L'lower triangular Cholesky factors: P = L L^T
D, D'diagonal positive-definite parts
metric_SPDinduced Riemannian metric on SPD matrices
thetadeformation parameter (tunable exponent for stability)
euclidean_dist(L, L')standard Euclidean distance: square root of sum of squared element differences
power_metric(D, D', theta)scaled distance on positive diagonal: sum of (D_i / D'_i)^(1/theta) terms
A tiny worked example
Matrix P: 2×2 Cholesky gives L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]], D_diag = [2, 1].
Matrix Q: 2×2 Cholesky gives L' = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1.1]], D'_diag = [2, 0.9].
Euclidean distance on L: sqrt((0 - 0)^2 + (1 - 1.1)^2) = 0.1.
Power-scaled distance on D (theta=2): (2/2)^0.5 + (1/0.9)^0.5 = 1.0 + 1.054 = 2.054.
Total metric: 0.1 + 2.054 = 2.154. This distance is computed in 5 operations (multiplications and additions), whereas affine-invariant metric would require matrix inversion, exponential, logarithm — roughly 50+ operations.

The metric on SPD matrices is defined by measuring distance on Cholesky factors separately, then summing — a product metric that costs only element-wise arithmetic.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Diagonal elements becoming non-positive. During optimization, the diagonal D might dip below zero or to exactly zero, violating the positive-definite constraint. Fix: clip diagonal elements to stay above a small epsilon (e.g., 1e-6) after each update.
Cholesky decomposition failing on near-singular matrices. If an SPD matrix has very small eigenvalues (nearly singular), its Cholesky factor becomes ill-conditioned, and rounding errors corrupt the decomposition. Fix: regularize matrices toward identity (add a small multiple of I) or use higher-precision arithmetic.
Power-deformation number theta poorly chosen. If theta (the deformation exponent) is too large or too small, the distance formula becomes either too lenient or too strict, slowing learning or causing oscillation. Fix: tune theta on validation data, or use cross-validation.
Done right. Clip diagonals to stay positive, regularize near-singular inputs, tune theta once per problem. The resulting metrics are stable and fast across all tested dimensions (3 by 3 to 256 by 256 matrices) and datasets (diffusion tensor imaging, hand pose, covariance pooling).
