SpaCE-Eval: Spatial Reasoning Benchmark for Multimodal LLMs

SpaCE-Eval is a visual test that measures whether multimodal language models can reason about real-world spaces, understand how objects move and interact, and plan actions in complex environments.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Multimodal language models (models that read both pictures and text) give impressive answers on simple tests, but we do not have good tests to check whether they truly understand real-world spaces and how things work in them. A model might sound confident about how gravity, balance, or space work but get it wrong when the rules matter. SpaCE-Eval is a benchmark — a graded test set with visual diagrams and questions — designed to measure this gap honestly.

Zero assumption version

The gap between sounding right and being right

Today's multimodal language models are evaluated on simple multiple-choice tests: 'Does this image show a cat or a dog?' or 'What color is the wall?' They pass these easily. But the real world is far more complex. To survive in a room, build a structure, or drive a vehicle, a model needs to reason about space, understand how objects interact, and predict what will happen next.

The naive approach is to expand existing benchmarks — more images, harder multiple choice. But this does not catch the gap between pattern-matching and real understanding. A model can memorize that most pictures of a stack of books show them not floating in air, then confidently predict stacks will fall — without ever learning why gravity matters or what makes a structure stable. When the test flips to an alien planet, the model fails. The issue is that existing benchmarks do not ask the questions that force genuine spatial and physical reasoning.

In one sentence. Current multimodal LLM benchmarks do not measure whether models truly understand spaces and physical interactions in real environments.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect human diagrams

Humans draw scenarios showing rooms, buildings, or outdoor spaces with objects, paths, and spatial relationships.

Author paired questions

For each diagram, experts write questions about spatial layout, physics reasoning, and action planning.

Refine and validate

Curators filter out ambiguous questions and keep only scenarios that clearly separate genuine reasoning from guessing.

Test leading models

Feed each diagram-question pair to multiple multimodal LLMs and record whether they answer correctly.

Report the gap

Publish results showing which models and which reasoning types models struggle with most.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

SpaCE-Eval introduces a new kind of test set. Rather than tuning existing benchmarks, it invents a fresh category of questions: those that demand spatial reasoning (understanding how objects fit in rooms and buildings), commonsense physics (knowing how gravity, balance, and collision work), and environmental planning (deciding which actions lead to desired outcomes). The object is the question type itself.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Spatial reasoning gap

measured quantity  =  The difference between simple classification and spatial understanding

Spatial reasoning gap is not explicitly named as a single number in the abstract, but it is what SpaCE-Eval measures: for each model and each type of spatial question, how often does it answer correctly? This gap reveals whether the model has internalized how the real world works or merely memorized shallow patterns.

The paper estimates this by comparing a model's accuracy on straightforward visual questions (e.g., 'What is in this room?') to its accuracy on spatial and physics questions (e.g., 'If I move this object here, will it fall?'). A model that scores 80 percent on naming objects but 30 percent on predicting stability has a gap of 50 percentage points, signaling weak spatial understanding.

Why it works

Why honest tests reveal honest gaps

A model can pass simple benchmarks by learning shallow associations without ever understanding the underlying rules. If a test only asks 'Does this room have a window?' a model learns to spot window-shaped patterns without understanding how rooms are organized and how big they are. But if a test asks 'Will this book fall if I place it at the edge of this shelf?' the model must genuinely reason about balance, gravity, and object dimensions.

SpaCE-Eval works by asking questions that force this deeper reasoning. A human carefully designs each diagram so that the correct answer requires spatial understanding, not just pattern matching. For instance, a diagram might show an unusual object placement that would fall in the real world but looks superficially similar to a stable arrangement. The model must use its learned rules about physics, not just its memory of common image patterns, to answer correctly.

The benchmark becomes a diagnostic. If all major models score poorly on spatial questions, it signals a real gap in their training or architecture — they have not learned how objects fit together in space and how physical forces work. This clarity is what makes SpaCE-Eval useful: researchers can now target this specific weakness.

Measuring the gap

First in words:

For each model M and each test set C (simple or spatial), accuracy is the fraction of questions answered correctly. The spatial reasoning gap is accuracy_simple minus accuracy_spatial.

Then the compact form:

gap_M = accuracy_M(simple) - accuracy_M(spatial)

Every symbol:

accuracy_M(simple)fraction of correct answers on basic visual questions
accuracy_M(spatial)fraction of correct answers on spatial reasoning questions
gap_Mhow much the model's performance drops when reasoning is required
A tiny worked example
Model A on simple questions (naming objects): 80 out of 100 correct = 80 percent
Model A on spatial questions (predicting stability): 30 out of 100 correct = 30 percent
Gap for Model A: 80 - 30 = 50 percentage points
Model B on simple questions: 85 percent
Model B on spatial questions: 60 percent
Gap for Model B: 85 - 60 = 25 percentage points
Model B has closed half the gap, showing stronger spatial reasoning than Model A.

The bigger the gap, the more the model relies on visual pattern matching and the less it truly understands spatial reasoning.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

A model memorizes spatial stereotypes. If the benchmark always shows common layouts, a model might memorize 'living rooms usually have sofas on this wall' without learning how rooms are actually organized and how big they are. The fix: randomize object placements and test unusual but valid scenarios.
Questions are ambiguous. If a spatial question has multiple valid interpretations ('Does this shelf hold the books safely?' could mean different things), the model's answer is not wrong — just different. The fix: rigorous human curation and validation.
Diagrams do not match real-world physics. If hand-drawn diagrams use stylized perspectives or scales that do not match how real rooms look, models might not transfer their learning to actual robotic or navigation tasks. The fix: validate diagrams against reference images of real spaces.
Done right. SpaCE-Eval uses diverse, carefully vetted diagrams with unambiguous questions; results generalize to real-world spatial tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should measure spatial reasoning gap on a small but diverse set of scenarios: a simple room layout with a stability question, a multi-room building with a path-planning question, and an outdoor space with a collision prediction. For each scenario, ask a baseline model (e.g., a standard vision-language model) the spatial question and record whether it answers correctly. Then compare its accuracy on spatial questions to its accuracy on simple naming questions from the same diagrams. The gap is the proof.

spatial_reasoning_gap = accuracy(simple) - accuracy(spatial)

If the demo runs five diverse spatial scenarios and the model achieves 70 percent on simple naming but 35 percent on spatial reasoning, you have confirmed the benchmark works: it exposes a real, measurable weakness in spatial understanding that easy benchmarks miss.
