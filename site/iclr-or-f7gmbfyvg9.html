FlexiVoice: TTS with Style and Voice Control

A speech system that reads text and lets you say how to say it, using natural instructions and voice samples to control tone and timbre separately.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Today's speech systems can either clone a voice or change the speaking style, but not both at once — and they struggle when you want Chinese one moment and English the next. FlexiVoice changes that by building a single system around a language model that takes three inputs at once: the text to speak, an instruction (like 'speak angrily' or 'whisper'), and a voice sample (to sound like that person). A new training recipe called Progressive Post-Training untangles these three dials so each one controls what it should, not by accident but on purpose.

Zero assumption version

The world before: One system, one job

Speech systems today are specialists. Some clone your voice — play them a 5-second recording and they sound like you — but they cannot change how angry, sad, or whispery you sound. Others follow style instructions ('sound confident') but expect you to record the voice sample in the target language. And if you want both languages, you buy two systems. None of them do three things at once.

A beginner might try putting a language model in charge and saying 'just learn all three' — the text, the style instruction, and the voice. The problem is that all three signals fight. When the model hears 'speak angrily,' does it lighten the voice or deepen it? When it gets a happy voice sample, does it copy the tone or the timbre? The signals bleed into each other. The model learns to shortcut: it might ignore the instruction and just copy everything from the voice sample, or ignore the voice sample and guess an accent from the text. You lose control.

In one sentence. FlexiVoice trains its language-model core to separate style instructions, voice timbre, and text content into independent control channels, then uses a two-stage reward process to lock each channel in.

What it actually does

The model reads three separate tracks

Text input, natural language instruction (like 'speak gently'), voice sample.

Stage 1: DPO teaches obedience

Reward good outputs that follow both instruction and voice; punish outputs that ignore either one.

Stage 2: GRPO separates the signals

Fine-tune the language-understanding part to nail natural-language instructions (like 'sound like you are underwater').

Final model outputs sound that respects text, instruction, and voice in separate channels, not mashed together.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes the weights of a language model that outputs speech — not just to follow instructions, but to keep three separate dials independent. It does this through a reward-based training process that explicitly punishes interference: when changing one dial moves the others.

the object it moves  =  Language model weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Cross-talk leakage

measured quantity  =  Cross-talk: how much one control channel bleeds into another

Every speech system has a hidden problem: when you turn up the 'anger' dial, does the voice get higher, lower, or stay the same? Does it unconsciously shift to sound female or male? That hidden bleed is cross-talk. It is not measured in decibels or accuracy — it is the degree to which one input (style) accidentally changes the output of another input (voice timbre). FlexiVoice measures this by playing human listeners the same text with the same instruction but two different voice samples, then asking if the voices sound different. High cross-talk means the voices sound similar (the style instruction drowned out the voice). Low cross-talk means the voices stay distinct.

The paper estimates cross-talk by rewarding the system when different voice samples produce audibly different outputs — a multi-objective reward (GRPO) that explicitly penalizes cross-talk.

Why it works

Why it works: Decoupling through progressive rewards

The core trick is that you cannot teach a system to decouple three things all at once. If you do, it gets stuck in a local solution where each dial partly overrides the other.

FlexiVoice does it in stages. First, it uses Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) — a training method that compares a good output (one that followed the instruction AND matched the voice) to bad outputs, and nudges the model weights in the direction of the good one. This teaches raw obedience: the model learns 'when you get both signals, use both.' But at this point, they are still tangled; the model learned to follow both, but does not yet know HOW to separate them.

Second comes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a multi-objective training step. Instead of a single reward ('good' or 'bad'), the model now gets separate objectives: one reward for 'does the output match the voice sample?' and another for 'does the output follow the style instruction?' and a third for 'are the text words correct?' The model is forced to optimize all three at once. When the model finds a shortcut (like 'ignore the voice, just follow the style'), the voice-matching reward drops, and it gets punished. When it finds another shortcut (like 'ignore the style, just copy the voice'), the style reward drops. The model learns to satisfy all three because skipping any one costs more than the effort to separate them.

Finally, a third stage refines instruction-following for complex, natural-language descriptions. By this point, the dials are already decoupled, so fine-tuning only sharpens language understanding without re-tangling the channels.

Math after the picture

The reward equation: Separating three dials

First in words:

Total reward equals the voice-match score plus the style-match score plus the text-correctness score, minus a penalty whenever one dial changes affects another dial.

Then the compact form:

R_total = R_voice(x, ref) + R_style(x, inst) + R_text(x, toks) - lambda * I(voice_output; style_output | text)

Every symbol:

R_voice(x, ref)How much the output x sounds like the voice reference
R_style(x, inst)How well the output follows the style instruction
R_text(x, toks)How accurately the output speaks the text tokens
lambdaHow strongly we punish cross-talk
I(voice_output; style_output | text)Mutual information: how much the voice channel and style channel depend on each other
A tiny worked example
Imagine a voice reference with a deep, slow tone (R_voice = 8).
The style instruction says 'speak angrily' (R_style = 7).
The text is 'Hello' (R_text = 10).
Without GRPO: Output happens to speak quickly, not slowly. The deep voice is lost. I(voice; style) = 5 (high crosstalk). Total = 8 + 7 + 10 - 2 * 5 = 13.
With GRPO (after training): Output preserves the deep, slow tone AND speaks angrily. The two channels stay separate. I(voice; style) = 0. Total = 8 + 7 + 10 - 2 * 0 = 25.
The system earns 12 extra reward points by keeping the dials independent.

The system learns to maximize total reward by decoupling each channel — trying to satisfy each one independently yields more reward than using shortcuts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Weak voice reference. If the voice sample is only 2 seconds long, the model may not capture enough detail about that person's unique timbre, so voice cloning fails even after training.
Instruction too vague. Natural language like 'sound polished and refined' is ambiguous; if humans disagree on what it means, the model receives conflicting rewards and does not converge.
Language mismatch. If the voice sample is in English but the instruction is given in Chinese, the model may not connect them correctly, especially if the bilingual training was not balanced.
Overcorrection in GRPO. If the multi-objective penalty on cross-talk is set too high, the model may learn to decouple so hard that changing the voice reference has almost no effect (flattened voice identity).
Done right. Use voice samples longer than 5 seconds, concrete style descriptions ('angry' not 'polished and refined'), balance bilingual data, and tune the cross-talk penalty to neither entangle nor flatten the channels.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple test with three voice references (one fast, one slow, one nasal) and three styles (angry, gentle, whispery). For each pair, synthesize the same text. The demo should show: (1) When you switch the voice reference, the output timbre changes but the style stays consistent. (2) When you switch the style instruction, the tone changes but the voice identity holds. (3) Both changes happen in the same utterance, not in isolation.

Independence score = (variation in voice when voice reference changes) - (variation in voice when style changes). Higher = better decoupling.

The demo makes one thing unmissable: changing one dial does not spin the other.
