Local signals vs global structure

This page explains a core failure mode in representation learning: a model can satisfy many local training signals while still arranging the learned space in the wrong larger shape.

Core idea

A representation can win locally and still be wrong globally

A representation can satisfy many local training tasks and still have the wrong global shape.

Many training tasks look at small pieces of the problem. Predict this masked token. Rebuild this patch. Pull these two views together. Push this negative example away. Make this sparse dimension fire. Match this teacher target. These are local signals because each one gives feedback on a small relation: one missing part, one pair, one neighbor, one code, one view, one moment.

Local signals are useful. They are how most practical systems learn. But a learned representation is not only a collection of local wins. It is also a map. Points that should form a path should remain connected. Classes that should form a hierarchy should keep that hierarchy. Behaviors that unfold over time should not be scrambled. A graph role should not be reduced to a node label. A patient trajectory should not be broken into unrelated visits.

local question: does this pair, patch, token, frame, or neighbor relation look right? global question: does the whole learned space have the right clusters, paths, loops, hierarchy, timeline, or graph structure?

The deep issue is that local correctness does not automatically add up to global correctness. Many small pairwise decisions can produce a larger space that tears a path, crushes a rare cluster, invents a false shortcut, or loses a long-range relation.

Local signals

Most learning pressure is local because local feedback is easy to create

Self-supervised learning often creates supervision from small checks. A masked language model checks whether the hidden word was predicted. A masked image model checks whether a patch was recovered. A contrastive model checks whether two views are close and a negative is far. A retrieval model checks whether a query matches a document. A graph model checks whether nearby nodes, edges, or codebook assignments look plausible.

These signals are attractive because they scale. You can create millions of masked tokens, cropped images, video pairs, graph neighborhoods, and query-document pairs without hand labels. They also let the model learn from raw data directly. The problem is that each local task sees only part of the structure.

Masked tokenLocal pressure: recover one hidden word or subword. Risk: the model learns frequency and nearby phrase patterns instead of document-level meaning.
Masked patchLocal pressure: recover missing image content. Risk: the model learns texture and color continuation instead of objects, layout, or text.
Positive pairLocal pressure: make two views close. Risk: the chosen views erase a difference that the task later needs.
Negative pairLocal pressure: push two examples apart. Risk: false negatives split examples that belong to the same concept.
Teacher targetLocal pressure: match one target vector or code. Risk: the student copies local teacher behavior without inheriting the larger organization.
Codebook entryLocal pressure: assign one sample to one code. Risk: many samples collapse into a few codes, or nearby structure gets cut badly.
Global structure

Global structure is the shape created by all local decisions together

Global structure is what the whole representation space looks like after training. It is not one pair or one prediction. It is the arrangement of many examples: clusters, paths, loops, branches, hierarchies, timelines, graph neighborhoods, dense regions, sparse regions, and rare islands.

This matters because downstream tasks often depend on the larger map. Retrieval depends on the neighborhood around a query, not only one positive pair. Classification depends on class regions, margins, and overlap. Video understanding depends on the path from one behavior state to the next. Medicine depends on patient trajectories and disease stages. Graph learning depends on roles and communities. Scientific modeling depends on smooth physical or biological state spaces.

Clusters

Similar examples should group without crushing real subtypes.

Paths

Gradual change should remain connected through valid intermediate states.

Loops

Recurring cycles, rotations, rhythms, and seasons should not be torn into lines.

Hierarchy

Broad categories and subcategories should keep their nesting.

Neighborhoods

Nearby points should be nearby for the reason the task cares about.

Rare regions

Small but important cases should not vanish into common ones.

Why local success misleads

Local objectives can hide global damage

A model can make many local predictions correctly while damaging the larger space. Imagine folding a map so two distant cities touch. A local neighborhood may still look fine, but the global geography is wrong. Representation learning can do the same. It can place two locally similar examples together even when a longer path, timeline, or causal chain says they belong apart.

Contrastive learning shows this clearly. If the method only checks pairwise closeness, it may pull together augmented views but ignore the larger class shape. It may push apart examples that are actually two views of the same concept. It may separate points in a way that breaks a smooth path. It may overfit to whichever local differences are easiest, like background, scanner, camera, document format, or word frequency.

Masking has a similar issue. A model can recover many patches from nearby texture while never learning the whole object. It can predict many words from short phrases while failing to track a claim across a long document. It can reconstruct lab values from common co-occurrences while missing the disease trajectory. Local recovery is evidence, not proof.

local win: this small prediction was correct global risk: the learned space may still tear paths, merge rare cases, invent shortcuts, or lose long-range relations
Geometry

Geometry asks whether distances and directions mean the right thing

Geometry is about distance, direction, margins, and neighborhoods. If two examples are close in representation space, the model is saying they are similar in some usable way. If moving in one direction changes pose, sentiment, risk, or time, that direction has meaning. If class boundaries are clean, the geometry supports classification. If retrieval neighborhoods are coherent, search works.

Bad geometry happens when distance is controlled by the wrong factor. Images may group by background instead of object. Patient records may group by hospital instead of disease. Documents may group by writing style instead of answer content. Code snippets may group by variable names instead of behavior. The local training loss may look good because these factors are easy, but the representation space is organized around the wrong reason.

The geometric question is: when the model says two things are close, close in what sense? The answer has to match the use case.

Topology

Topology asks whether the larger shape survives

Topology is less concerned with exact distances and more concerned with durable shape: connected components, loops, holes, branches, and whether a path remains a path. This matters when the data has natural global structure. A rotating object can form a loop because after a full turn it returns to a similar view. A behavior can move through phases. A disease process can branch. A graph can have communities and bridges.

Topology-aware learning adds a warning: pairwise similarity is not enough. You can satisfy many local neighbor constraints and still tear the larger shape. If a loop becomes a line, the model may misunderstand cyclic change. If a bridge between clusters disappears, transfer across related cases may fail. If one connected behavior path is split into pieces, the model may treat phases of the same process as unrelated states.

This connects directly to the topology-aware pages. They are not just adding math. They are saying that representation learning should preserve the durable shape of the data when that shape carries meaning.

Time and trajectories

Temporal structure is global because meaning unfolds across steps

Video, robotics, patient records, and event streams cannot be judged only by isolated moments. A frame, visit, action, or event gets meaning from what came before and what can happen next. The same pose can mean reaching, falling, waving, or stopping depending on the trajectory. The same lab value can mean recovery or decline depending on trend and timing.

A local frame objective can learn appearance. A global trajectory objective asks whether the representation preserves state over time. Does the path through the representation space move smoothly? Does it respect phase order? Does it keep rare transitions? Can it forecast valid next states? Can it represent irregular timing without forcing fake equal steps?

BEAST sits here because animal behavior is not only visible appearance. Continuous-time latent models sit here because hidden state evolves between observations. Neural implicit action fields sit here because robot motion should support smooth behavior across time, not only a set of sampled points.

Graphs

Graph meaning lives in relation, not isolated nodes

Graphs make the local-global problem obvious. A node label can be local. Its meaning often comes from its neighbors, paths, edge types, communities, and role in the whole graph. Two nodes may have similar features but different roles. Two nodes may look different locally but serve the same structural role.

Graph codebooks and quantization methods must therefore avoid treating each node as an isolated item. A useful graph unit should preserve relation. If a codebook collapses, many graph roles share the same token. If it over-splits, similar roles scatter. If it ignores topology, the token assignment may be locally plausible and globally wrong.

The global test is whether graph structure transfers: node classification, link prediction, community structure, molecular property prediction, or relational reasoning should improve because the representation kept the shape of the graph.

Retrieval and sparse embeddings

Retrieval needs local matches that also form a useful search space

Retrieval looks local because the immediate task is query-document matching. But the full retrieval system is global. Every query lands in a neighborhood. Every document competes with nearby documents. A representation can match one positive pair and still create bad neighborhoods where common topics dominate, rare facts disappear, or close distractors outrank the right result.

CSRv2 makes this sharp. With only a few active dimensions, each active dimension must carry a distinction that helps organize the search space. A sparse representation that fires on generic features like "document" or "business" may look active but fail retrieval. A stronger sparse representation fires on distinctions that separate the correct answer from plausible wrong answers.

This connects back to bottlenecks. A bottleneck can improve global structure if it removes noise and keeps useful axes. It can damage global structure if it removes rare but decisive differences.

Concepts and hidden causes

Concept structure should reflect causes, not only surface strings

Concepts create another local-global issue. A local text match may connect the same word across examples. But a concept may appear under many surface strings, modalities, or measurements. "Heart attack" and "myocardial infarction" should connect conceptually. Two patient records may share a disease process even if their raw measurements differ. An image and caption may point to the same object even though their raw units are different.

Latent distribution matching is important here because it asks whether the learned space matches the hidden causes behind observations. If the representation groups by surface string, hospital style, camera setting, or background, it may pass local similarity tests while missing the true cause. If it groups by hidden state, it becomes more useful for transfer, explanation, and action.

The deeper question is: what larger structure should concepts form? A flat list is not enough when concepts have hierarchy, overlap, cause, sequence, or part-whole relations.

How people train it

Global structure usually needs extra pressure beyond local prediction

Local objectives are still necessary. The practical question is how to add pressure so local learning does not damage the larger map. Different papers do this in different ways.

Better positivesChoose views that preserve the right identity, not arbitrary augmentations that erase useful differences.
Hard negativesUse close distractors so the model learns fine distinctions, while avoiding false negatives that split one concept.
Teacher targetsUse a stable or richer teacher so local student updates inherit a better global organization.
Topology constraintsAdd pressure for paths, loops, connected components, or durable shape to survive training.
Temporal objectivesTrain on order, future prediction, state evolution, and smooth motion so trajectories remain coherent.
Graph structureUse neighborhoods, edge types, role similarity, and topology-aware codebooks to avoid isolated node reasoning.
Collapse checksEnsure codebooks, sparse dimensions, clusters, and embeddings use their capacity instead of shrinking to trivial states.
Behavior testsTest the final task because global damage may not show up in the local training loss.

Tests

How to tell whether the global structure is right

The right test depends on the kind of structure the domain needs. A single accuracy number can miss the failure. The test should ask whether the learned map behaves correctly when the local shortcuts are no longer enough.

Neighborhood auditFor a query or example, inspect nearest neighbors. Are they close for the right reason, or because of background, style, format, or frequency?
Cluster auditDo real subtypes form useful regions, or are rare cases crushed into common classes?
Path auditWhen data changes gradually, does the representation move through valid intermediate states?
Loop auditFor cycles, rotations, seasons, repeated behavior, or rhythms, does the representation preserve recurrence?
Timeline auditDoes the learned path respect event order, phase transitions, irregular timing, and long-range state?
Graph auditAre structural roles, communities, bridges, and edge patterns preserved?
Counterexample auditTry close examples that share a surface feature but differ in meaning. The representation should separate them.
Transfer auditMove to a new domain where the shortcut changes. If performance collapses, the global structure was probably organized around the wrong factor.
Takeaway

The first-principles line

A representation can win many local training tasks and still have the wrong global shape.

Granularity chooses the pieces. Training pressure teaches those pieces what to carry. Local signals provide scalable supervision. Global structure asks whether the learned space becomes the right map for the task: the right neighborhoods, clusters, paths, loops, hierarchies, timelines, graph roles, and rare regions.

good representation = locally useful signals + globally correct organization + behavior tests that expose shortcuts
