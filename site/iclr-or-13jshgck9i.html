D-REX: Differentiable Real-to-Sim-to-Real Engine for Learning Dexterous Grasping

A robot learns to grasp objects by building a digital twin in simulation whose physics parameters (like mass) are automatically tuned from real videos and then used to train safer grasping policies.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots must learn to grasp objects safely without dropping them. Simulation lets engineers test thousands of grasps cheaply, but simulated robots behave differently from real ones—the digital world does not match reality. D-REX fixes this by watching a real robot try to grasp real objects and automatically measuring what the object weighs, then creating a high-fidelity digital copy that teaches a policy how to handle that specific object's weight and physics.

Zero assumption version

Why robots drop things

A robot gripper must know how heavy an object is to grasp it safely. If a robot thinks a cup of coffee is as heavy as a brick, it clamps too hard and breaks it. If it thinks a brick is as light as a cup, it does not grip hard enough and drops it.

Engineers usually train robot grasping policies in a simulator (a computer program that mimics physics), then run that trained policy on the real robot. But here is the problem: the simulator rarely matches reality exactly. The object's weight in the simulator might be wrong, or the friction between the gripper and the object might be off. A policy trained on wrong physics learns to grasp wrong.

Getting the physics right is hard. You could manually measure each object, but that is slow and you need those numbers before training starts. You could run the grasp many times on the real robot and learn from failures, but real hardware is expensive and slow—you can only afford limited tries.

The real world has unknown physics; simulation has wrong physics; they do not match. D-REX measures the object's real physics from watching a real robot, then builds a matching simulator, then trains a safe policy on that accurate simulator.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

D-REX tunes a single hidden parameter: the object's mass. In traditional simulators, you guess the mass when you set up the scene. D-REX makes this mass tunable—it is a knob that the computer can adjust automatically by watching what actually happened in the real world.

the object it moves  =  the object's mass
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: object mass

measured quantity  =  mass that explains the motion

In D-REX, the object's mass is a hidden quantity you cannot see just by looking at the object. You see the object move in real videos, but not its weight directly. D-REX solves this backwards problem: given the video (what happened), what mass in the simulator would have produced exactly that motion?

The paper measures mass by comparing real motion to simulated motion. It starts with a guess (say, 500 grams), simulates what the gripper and object would do with that mass, and checks if the simulated motion matches the real video frame by frame. If the simulation lags behind (too heavy) or rushes ahead (too light), the mass guess is adjusted. This happens automatically—the computer treats mass like a weight dial that it tweaks thousands of times until the simulator motion and real motion line up.

Plain reading. Mass is how much 'oomph' it takes to push the object. Heavy objects move slowly when pushed; light objects zip away quickly. By watching the object move and asking 'what mass would move this speed?', D-REX back-calculates the true weight.
Paper reading. D-REX uses Gaussian Splat representations (a way to encode 3D scenes from images) to build a differentiable physics engine. The mass is a learned parameter optimized to minimize the pixel-space distance between real observations and simulated renderings of the same scene.
Why it works

Why it works: the feedback loop

D-REX solves two linked problems at once. First, it tunes the mass by running simulations and comparing them frame-by-frame to real video. Each time the mass is tweaked, the simulator re-runs and the computer measures how close the simulated frames are to the real frames. After thousands of adjustments, the mass converges to a value where simulation and reality almost perfectly match.

Second, once the mass is correct, the simulator is now trustworthy. The paper then takes videos of a human grasping the same object and plays that human grasp motion into the accurate simulator. The human shows a good grasp strategy, but human arms and robot arms are shaped differently. The computer translates the human's arm angles into robot arm angles that do the same motion—then records what forces the robot gripper needed to match that grasp. This becomes safe training data for the robot policy. The policy learns 'when my gripper feels this much force, success happens', and because the simulator is tuned to real physics, this training actually works on the real robot.

Math after the picture

How mass is tuned

First in words:

Total error equals pixel difference between real frames and simulated frames. We minimize this by adjusting mass. The gradient of this error with respect to mass tells us which way to change mass.

Then the compact form:

L = sum over frames of (real_frame_pixels - simulated_frame_pixels)^2; d(L) / d(mass) points the direction to reduce L

Every symbol:

Ltotal pixel mismatch between real and simulated video
real_frame_pixelsthe actual color values from the real camera
simulated_frame_pixelsthe color values rendered by the simulator
massthe object's weight, which we are tuning
d(L) / d(mass)the slope; tells us which way to nudge mass to shrink L
A tiny worked example
Suppose we are comparing one frame. Real frame has pixel values [200, 150, 100]. Simulated frame (with mass=500g) has [180, 170, 110].
Pixel errors: (200-180)^2 + (150-170)^2 + (100-110)^2 = 400 + 400 + 100 = 900.
We compute d(L)/d(mass) and find it is -2.5 (negative means 'increase mass'). So we update mass to 510g.
Simulated frame with 510g now has [195, 160, 105]. Pixel errors: (200-195)^2 + (150-160)^2 + (100-105)^2 = 25 + 100 + 25 = 150.
Error dropped from 900 to 150. Keep adjusting until error is very small.

The paper uses Gaussian Splat rendering (a fast way to draw 3D scenes) so changes to mass propagate backward from pixels all the way to mass updates.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fast motion hides mass errors. If the object moves very quickly, small changes in mass produce tiny changes in motion (over those short time steps). The computer cannot tell what mass is correct. With slow, careful motion, mass errors are obvious.
Friction and grip slip are hard to measure. The paper measures mass from motion, but the motion also depends on friction and how much the gripper slips. If friction is wrong, the measured mass will be wrong too. D-REX assumes friction is known.
Limited human demonstration data. The method needs real videos of humans grasping the same object. If you only have one grasp video, the robot policy sees only one strategy. Many grasps teach it more ways to succeed.
Done right. With careful recording (clear lighting, stable grasp angle), known gripper friction, and several human grasp examples, D-REX builds an accurate digital twin and trains a policy that works reliably on the real robot.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A real demo of D-REX would record a robot gripper grasping a mystery object three times, measure its mass automatically, then show that a policy trained in the corrected simulator successfully grasps that object on a real fourth attempt without dropping it.

grasp_success_rate = (number of grasps that do not drop object) / (total number of grasps attempted)

The demo must show that the trained policy grasps the real object safely, proving that the automatically-measured mass and the human-demo-trained policy together bridged the sim-to-real gap.
