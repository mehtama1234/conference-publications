D-REX: Real-to-Sim-to-Real Grasping

Find an object's weight by comparing real video to simulated physics, then teach a robot to grip it without dropping.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Real robots struggle to grip unfamiliar objects because they do not know how heavy each one is. This paper shows how to measure weight from a video of the real robot trying to move the object, then use that weight to train a gripper policy that works first time. The method builds a virtual copy of the scene (using point-cloud reconstruction), runs simulated physics until it matches what the real robot did, and reads the object mass directly from that mismatch.

Zero assumption version

Why robots drop objects they have never seen

A robot hand has sensors that measure joint angles and motor effort, but no direct sensor for object weight. When you hand it a coffee mug, a brick, or a glass of water, it must guess. A naive gripper squeezes the same way for everything: push down with X newtons of force, close the fingers at Y speed. This breaks. A light mug cracks under the same grip that barely holds a brick. A coffee cup spills if the robot squeezes too hard or grips too loose. The grip force depends on mass.

Humans solve this by picking something up, feeling its weight, then adjusting our grip. Robots trained in simulation never touch a real object before deployment, so they have no way to learn object-specific grip forces. A policy trained to grip simulated blocks will drop real metal rods because the real objects have different weights and surface friction than the simulation assumed.

The solution. Watch the real robot move the object once, infer its mass by comparing that motion to physics simulation, then train a policy that controls grip force based on the inferred mass.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Reconstruct the scene

Take a video of the robot and object from a camera view, convert it into a detailed 3D point cloud.

Run trial physics

Simulate the robot's arm and object inside a physics engine, using the same joint commands the real robot executed.

Measure the mismatch

Compare the simulated trajectory to the real trajectory (frame by frame). How far off is the simulation?

Optimize mass

Adjust the simulated object mass up and down. Find the mass that makes simulation match reality best. That is the true mass.

Train grip-force policy

Train a neural network to predict grip force as a function of object mass. Deploy to the real robot.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the physics simulation engine so that you can compute how sensitive the simulated motion is to the input parameters (like object mass). This means an optimizer can automatically adjust mass until the simulation matches the real video. It also trains the gripper to output force commands directly, not just position commands, making those forces depend on the measured mass.

The hidden quantity

The object mass: the one number that changed everything

measured quantity  =  Object mass (inferred from video)

The hidden quantity is the true mass of the object, measured in kilograms. The robot never touches a scale. Instead, D-REX infers mass by asking: what mass, plugged into our physics simulator, makes the simulated motion most closely match the real video we recorded? The paper estimates it by running physics forward (given a guessed mass), comparing the resulting trajectory to reality, and then using calculus to compute which direction to nudge the mass to reduce that mismatch.

The paper measures this inferred mass against ground-truth weights for test objects, showing it stays accurate even when objects have different shapes or materials. Once you have the true mass, a gripper can adjust its grip force accordingly—light objects get a weak grip (to avoid crushing), heavy objects get a strong grip (to avoid dropping).

Why it works

Why matching simulated physics to real video reveals mass

Physics is predictable: if you know an object's mass, material, shape, and the forces applied to it, calculus tells you exactly how it will move. A robot arm applies a known sequence of forces (the joint motor commands); the only mystery is the object's mass. In the real world, the robot and object follow a particular trajectory. In simulation, if you guess wrong on mass, the trajectory will be different. A lighter simulated object accelerates too fast; a heavier one moves too slowly. By measuring the mismatch (comparing frame-by-frame positions) and repeatedly nudging the mass in the direction that shrinks that mismatch, you drive the mismatch to zero. At that point, the guessed mass equals the true mass.

Once you have the mass, a gripper policy can compute the right grip force. Heavy objects need more grip force to prevent slipping (because gravity pulls them down harder); light objects need less (to avoid crushing). The policy learns to predict grip force as a linear function of mass: force equals the mass times gravity, divided by the number of contact points. This is the same formula humans use intuitively.

Math after the picture

The formula: trajectory loss shrinks as mass improves

First in words:

Total mismatch equals the squared distance summed over all timesteps, between where the real object actually moved and where simulation predicted it would move, given a guessed mass.

Then the compact form:

L_traj(m) = sum_k ||z_k(m) - zhat_k||^2

Every symbol:

L_traj(m)Total mismatch between real and simulated motion, as a function of mass m
z_k(m)Actual position of the real object at timestep k
zhat_kSimulated position at timestep k, computed using the physics engine with guessed mass m
kTimestep index (frame 1, frame 2, ..., frame T)
||...||^2Squared distance (how far apart two points are)
Tiny worked example
Suppose an object moves along a table. Real motion: positions 10cm, 20cm, 30cm (three frames).
Sim guess 1: mass too low, predicted 12cm, 24cm, 36cm. Mismatch is (10-12) squared plus (20-24) squared plus (30-36) squared equals 4 plus 16 plus 36 equals 56.
Sim guess 2: mass too high, predicted 8cm, 16cm, 24cm. Mismatch is (10-8) squared plus (20-16) squared plus (30-24) squared equals 4 plus 16 plus 36 equals 56.
Sim guess 3: mass just right, predicted 10cm, 20cm, 30cm. Mismatch is 0 plus 0 plus 0 equals 0.
Optimizer finds guess 3 automatically by computing how the mismatch changes with mass and walking downhill.

The mass that makes the loss zero is the true mass; at that point, simulation perfectly predicts reality.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Video blur or motion discontinuity. If the video is blurry or has frame drops, the measured real trajectory becomes noisy. When trajectory is noisy, the mass estimate becomes noisy, and the gripper does not know the true grip force. Solution: steady camera, high frame rate.
Simulator does not match physics reality. D-REX assumes the simulated physics engine is accurate (friction coefficients, contact model, elasticity). If real objects have unusual friction or deformation, the simulator will predict a different trajectory even with the correct mass. The optimizer will then infer a wrong mass to compensate. Solution: calibrate the simulator against multiple test objects, check for unmodeled effects like squeeziness or sliding.
Gripper force formula is too simple. The paper assumes force scales linearly with mass: the required grip force is the mass multiplied by gravity and divided by the number of contact points. Real materials require different forces: sandpaper needs more grip than smooth plastic, even at the same mass. Solution: condition the policy on both mass and material (if observable), or learn a nonlinear force function.
Done right: end-to-end. When the camera is steady, the simulator is tuned to match real physics, and the gripper learns to produce variable grip forces, D-REX succeeds. Tested on eight objects of diverse shapes and weights; success rate > 80%.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple gripper (or use a simulation). Give it five objects of unknown mass: a rubber ball, a wooden block, a metal cylinder, a glass jar, a foam cup. Have it lift each one by watching a video of a human moving it around. Measure the inferred mass against a digital scale. Then retrain a gripper policy conditioned on those inferred masses and measure grasp success (does it hold the object for 10 seconds without dropping or crushing).

grasp_success_rate = (objects_held_without_drop_or_crush) / (total_objects_grasped)

By running physics backward from real motion, you can read off the one number that was hiding: the true mass. Once the gripper knows the mass, it can squeeze with exactly the right force.
