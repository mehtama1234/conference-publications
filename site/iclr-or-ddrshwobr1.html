Disentangled Robot Learning via Separate Forward and Inverse Dynamics Pretraining

Train a robot policy by splitting video prediction and figuring-out-the-action into two separate experts, then gluing them together to exploit both labeled robot data and vast unlabeled human videos.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot learning from videos faces a brutal tradeoff: predicting what the world looks like next (2D image forecasting) trains very differently than predicting what action to take (3D robot movement). Combining them in one model makes training unstable and wastes billions of human videos that have no action labels. This paper proposes DeFI: train two separate specialists first—one learns to predict future frames, the other learns to infer hidden actions from video alone—then bolt them together into a complete robot policy.

Zero assumption version

The two-headed monster: image vs. action

Robot learning needs two things from videos: first, seeing what happens next (a truck rolls forward, a block falls, a gripper closes); second, figuring out which action caused it (turn the wheel, no input, pinch fingers). A naive approach trains one big model to do both at once. But the training gets stuck because these two objectives compete: image prediction wants to blur details to smooth out noise, while figuring out the hidden action wants to sharpen fine-grained changes. The model can't win both ways, so it learns neither well.

Even worse, there are billions of hours of human videos on the internet—people walking, cooking, fixing cars. These cost nothing. But they have no robot action labels, so a standard robot-learning pipeline just throws them away. Meanwhile, labeled robot data is expensive and sparse. The model trains on crumbs when it could use an ocean.

The paper's one-sentence idea: Split the job: train a vision model to predict frames on any video, train a separate model to infer hidden actions from video alone, then merge them into a robot policy.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Pretrain forward model

Feed frames and task instructions into a video generator; teach it to predict the next H frames from millions of unlabeled human and robot videos.

Step 2: Pretrain inverse model

Look at two video frames separated by a delay; use a self-supervised trick to teach the model to infer the action that must have occurred between them, without any action labels.

Step 3: Freeze forward, keep inverse flexible

Lock the forward model's weights; use its predictions of future frames as input to the inverse model during the next stage.

Step 4: Merge and fine-tune

Connect forward and inverse as one pipeline; train on small labeled robot datasets; both halves adjust their learning nudges toward the real downstream task.

Step 5: Deploy unified policy

The frozen forward module predicts what the robot's action will lead to; the inverse module reads that prediction and infers the best action to take next.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper splits the policy into two separate knowledge modules instead of forcing them to share weights from day one. Forward and inverse dynamics are decoupled during pretraining, then fused into one end-to-end architecture at fine-tuning time.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: task-aligned forward state

measured quantity  =  Predictable action-relevant visual change

The core insight is that the video frames the forward model learns to predict are not arbitrary—they should change in ways that encode the action that was just taken. A good forward prediction model captures visual consequences of actions (gripper opens, object moves, light changes). But raw pixel prediction alone does not force this alignment; the forward model could predict blurry, averaged-out frames and still minimize loss.

What the paper measures is whether the forward model's predicted frames contain enough visual signal for the inverse model to reliably infer the action. If the frames are too blurry or averaged, the inverse model cannot work. If they contain sharp, action-aligned changes, the inverse model thrives. This hidden quantity—whether the forward predictions encode actionable visual information—is never explicitly named in the abstract, but it is what makes the decoupling work.

Why it works

Why it works: separation enables specialization

The root problem is that image prediction and figuring out the hidden action pull in opposite directions. If you train them together on the same learning step, one objective always loses. The paper breaks this by training each specialist alone, on the data it does best with. The forward model sees billions of human and robot videos—action-free web data included—and learns to predict frames. The inverse model sees video pairs and must infer the hidden action; it gets good at detecting the micro-changes in scenes that encode actions (finger position, object location, lighting). Each specialist absorbs the knowledge its data rewards it for learning.

Then the magic: when you freeze the forward model and fine-tune on robot tasks, the inverse model reads the forward model's predictions and learns to map those visual changes to actual robot commands. The forward model's predictions are not random—they contain rich, action-aligned visual information because they were trained on real video. The inverse model now has a clean target: make sense of the forward model's predictions and output actions that align with the downstream robot task. Both halves train without conflict because their objectives are now sequential, not competing. Forward model predicts; inverse model infers from that prediction. No more tug-of-war.

The decoupled training equations

First in words:

Forward training uses video diffusion loss to predict future frames. Inverse training uses vector-quantized action reconstruction loss to infer actions from frame pairs. At fine-tuning, they merge: forward outputs embed future states; inverse reads embeddings and produces actions; robot loss flows back through both.

Then the compact form:

L_forward = MSE(predict_frame(o_t, l), o_t+H) + L_diffusion. L_inverse = MSE(infer_action(e_t, e_t+n, l), a_t->t+n) + L_vqvae. L_finetune = L_robot(a_inferred, a_true) -- gradients backprop through both.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forward model predicts action-irrelevant changes. If the forward model learns to predict furniture, lighting, and camera jitter but not the fine-grained hand-object interactions that encode actions, the inverse model will have no signal to infer from. The model can still predict frames and minimize loss—just not action-aligned frames. Fine-tuning on robot tasks then fails because the inverse module cannot read action information from a blurry prediction.
Inverse model overfits to pretraining video style. Human videos show different hand sizes, camera angles, and speeds than robot manipulation. If the inverse model trains only on human video dynamics and never sees robot data during pretraining, it may infer actions that work for hands but not grippers, or predict action timings that don't match robot actuators. Fine-tuning helps because the downstream task loss reorients the model, but starting from a human-only inverse model means the first few training steps waste time unlearning hand biases instead of learning robot mechanics from the start.
Frozen forward model diverges from downstream task. The forward model is pretrained on general video prediction (anything can happen next). If the downstream task requires very specific, task-determined action effects (e.g., 'push block exactly to the goal'), the forward model's generic predictions may not capture the precise visual outcomes the inverse model needs to deduce the right action. The model must balance two pulls: using diverse web data to gain broad knowledge, versus specializing to the robot task so the forward predictions are actually useful for figuring out what action to take.
Done right. Train the forward model on a mix of human and robot videos so it learns diverse, action-aligned frame changes. Include robot data in pretraining so the inverse model sees the embodiment it will control. Then fine-tune on the downstream task with a learning rate that lets the inverse module adapt while keeping forward model insights.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy robot task: a gripper on a 1D rail, a target position on the rail, and random starting positions. Collect a small labeled dataset (current frame, action, next frame, task instruction, success/failure). Train GFDM on 1000 unlabeled video clips of random gripper motion (no action labels). Train GIDM on the same 1000 clips by hiding the action and inferring it from frame pairs. Freeze GFDM; attach GIDM; fine-tune on the labeled dataset. Measure: (1) how often the inverse model infers the correct action from GFDM predictions, (2) how often the robot task succeeds on held-out test positions, (3) contrast against a baseline that trained one joint model on the labeled data alone.

Task success rate = (episodes where gripper reached target) / (total test episodes). Action inference accuracy = (times GIDM guessed the right action from GFDM output) / (total inferences). Expected result: DeFI success ~85%, joint-baseline success ~65%; DeFI accuracy ~90%, baseline ~60%.

The demo makes visible why decoupling helps: when both prediction and figuring-out-the-action are trained separately on large data before merging, each one is already strong at its job because neither lost ground fighting the other; the joint model then inherits that strength and fine-tunes cleanly toward the robot task, whereas a joint baseline starts weak (both objectives compromised) and the weak foundation never improves enough to match.
