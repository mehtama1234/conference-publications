GeoDPO: Geometric Perception via Translator-Guided RL

Vision models learn to perceive shapes, angles, and spatial relationships better by practicing on real diagrams while a trusted translator scores how accurately they describe the diagram elements.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Vision-language models can read text in images and answer questions, but they struggle with spatial reasoning—they do not correctly describe circles, rectangles, angles, and how shapes connect. The reason: no one has separated teaching them to see and describe the shapes from teaching them to reason about them. This paper builds a benchmark of diagrams paired with formal descriptions, then teaches models to perceive shapes and their spatial relationships better by rewarding answers that match those formal descriptions.

The Perception Blind Spot

Vision-language models are good at reading captions and answering simple questions about pictures. But they fail at spatial reasoning tasks. Show them a diagram with circles and rectangles and ask what shape touches what, and they get it wrong—not because they cannot reason, but because they do not perceive the diagram correctly in the first place.

The deeper problem: when you train a model on diagrams and spatial questions mixed together, you cannot tell if it is bad at detecting shapes or bad at reasoning about them. A beginner might try to fix this by just showing the model more diagrams and correct answers. But this teaches the model to recognize answers better, not to perceive the shapes better, because the reward only grades the final answer correct-or-wrong, not whether the intermediate shape descriptions are accurate. The model learns the wrong lesson.

The paper's move. Separate shape perception from reasoning: teach the model to see shapes and describe them accurately by comparing its descriptions to formal rules, independently of reasoning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build the referee

Collect diagrams and translate each into formal descriptions (positions, sizes, connections).

Train the translator

Teach a small model to convert natural language descriptions to formal rules using the paired data.

Ask the model

Show the vision model a diagram and have it describe what it sees in natural language.

Score with the referee

Run the translator on the model's description to get formal rules; compare to ground truth.

Reward correct perception

Use the match score to reward the model's weights via reinforcement learning.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper changes the rewards the vision model receives during training. Instead of rewarding whether the final answer is correct (which mixes poor perception with poor reasoning), it rewards whether the model's description of the shapes and their positions is accurate according to formal rules.

the object it moves  =  reward signal
The hidden quantity

Alignment Score: How Well Does the Model Describe Shapes?

measured quantity  =  Alignment score (shape description accuracy)

This is a single number that measures whether the model's description of a diagram matches the formal truth. For example: does the model correctly identify that a circle is at position (100, 200) with radius 50? The paper does not measure correctness by the final reasoning answer—it measures perception directly by comparing the model's natural-language description to formal shape rules.

The paper estimates this by having a smaller translator model convert the vision model's description back into formal rules, then comparing those rules to the ground truth. A high alignment score means the model described the shapes correctly.

Why it works

Why Translating Shapes into Rules Works

The core insight: a model can learn to perceive and describe shapes (seeing accurately) separately from reasoning (using that knowledge to answer a question), because both need the same foundation—an accurate description of what the diagram contains.

Here is the causal chain: When the model describes a diagram poorly, the translator catches the error (e.g., 'you said the circle is at 150, but it is actually at 100'). The reinforcement learning system then makes the model's weights slightly less likely to produce that wrong description next time. Over many examples, the model learns to describe shapes accurately. Once perception is sharp, reasoning about spatial relationships becomes much easier—the model has correct building blocks to work with.

The Scoring Equation

First in words:

Reward equals the fraction of formal geometry facts the model got right, minus a small penalty for being too confident when wrong.

Then the compact form:

R(d) = (1 - alpha) * align(translate(desc(d)), truth(d)) - alpha * confidence_penalty(desc(d))

Every symbol:

R(d)the reward for describing diagram d
desc(d)the model's natural-language description of diagram d
translate(...)the translator's conversion from words to formal rules
truth(d)the ground-truth formal description of diagram d
align(X, Y)the fraction of facts in X that match Y (0 to 1)
confidence_penalty(...)a small cost if the model sounds certain but is wrong
alphahow much weight we give the confidence penalty (typically 0.1)
A tiny worked example
Diagram: two circles, positions (50,50) and (200,150), radii 25 and 40.
Model says: 'Two circles at roughly (50,50) and (200,150), radii about 25 and 40, touching closely.'
Translator extracts: (50,50,r=25), (200,150,r=40), touching=yes.
Truth: (50,50,r=25), (200,150,r=40), touching=yes.
align score: 5 out of 5 facts correct = 1.0.
confidence_penalty: model was precise, no penalty = 0.
R = (1 - 0.1) * 1.0 - 0.1 * 0 = 0.9. Strong reward.

The higher the alignment between what the model said and ground truth, the bigger the reward, pushing the model to describe shapes more accurately.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The translator itself is wrong. If the small translator model misinterprets the vision model's words, the reward signal is garbage. The translator must be trained very carefully on high-quality paired data.
Formal rules do not cover all diagrams. Some diagrams (e.g., freehand sketches, ambiguous hand-drawn shapes) may not have clean formal descriptions. The method works best on structured, rule-based diagrams.
Reward signal is too noisy. If alignment scores wiggle randomly because of translator errors or diagram ambiguity, the model gets contradictory feedback and learning slows or fails.
Done right: ground truth is consistent. When diagrams are synthetic or carefully annotated, and the translator is reliable, rewards are clear and the model learns sharp perception.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of 100 simple diagrams (colored circles, rectangles, line segments in known positions). Write formal descriptions for each. Train a tiny translator on 80 of them. Use the remaining 20 as test. Run reinforcement learning on a pretrained vision model with this reward signal. Measure how accurately the model describes the test diagrams before and after training.

perception_gain = (accuracy_after - accuracy_before) / accuracy_before multiplied by 100

The demo must show that the vision model's descriptions of shapes become more precise after receiving rewards tied to formal truth, even on diagrams the translator has never seen.
