Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back

Self-organizing language-model teams often underuse their best member because the group drifts toward compromise instead of giving enough weight to the expert's view.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

It is tempting to assume that a team of language-model agents will beat its best member. This paper tests that assumption in unconstrained team discussions. The result is uncomfortable: self-organizing teams often perform worse than their strongest individual member, even when the team is told who the expert is. The central failure is not expert identification. It is expert use. Team discussion tends to average opinions together, which can protect against hostile members but can also dilute the best answer.

Zero assumption version

Why more agents can mean worse decisions

A multi-agent system is often sold on the idea that multiple members should outperform one member because the group can pool useful knowledge. Different agents bring different strengths, and discussion should combine those strengths into a better answer because the group can weigh specialized knowledge during disagreement.

The paper shows that unconstrained teams often do something else. They move toward compromise. When the expert disagrees with weaker members, the group often softens the expert's answer instead of following it. That hurts performance because averaging strong and weak judgments can be worse than simply trusting the strongest judgment.

The paper's move. Measure whether teams match their best member, then analyze whether failure comes from missing the expert or from failing to follow the expert.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Assemble unconstrained teams

Let several language-model agents discuss tasks without forcing a fixed workflow.

Measure best-member versus team outcome

Compare the final team answer to the strongest individual member on the same task.

Separate identification from use

Check whether the team recognized who the expert was and whether it actually followed that expert when disagreement appeared.

Analyze discussion patterns

Look for compromise behavior, such as blending incompatible answers instead of weighting the best-supported answer more heavily.

Test the trade-off

Measure whether compromise harms performance while also improving resistance to hostile or adversarial team members.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the main evaluation target for multi-agent teams. Instead of asking only whether a team beats a single baseline, it asks whether the team can at least match its own best member.

the object it moves  =  the success criterion for a self-organizing team
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  how much weight the team gives to genuine expertise

A team transcript can look collaborative while still discarding the best answer. The hidden quantity is expert weight: when one member is more reliable on the task, how much extra weight does the team give that member's judgment because that member has earned more trust on this task?

The paper finds that teams often identify the expert correctly but still fail to use that information. The group moves toward integrative compromise instead. That lowers task performance because the final answer drifts away from the most reliable source. It can still improve safety against malicious members because compromise reduces any one member's ability to dominate.

Why it works

Why teams hold experts back

Unconstrained discussion encourages social-style consensus behavior. When several agents speak, the system tends to treat agreement as a sign of quality. That can be helpful in noisy settings, but it becomes harmful when the members have unequal expertise. The final answer drifts toward the center because the discussion gives weaker views more influence than their evidence deserves.

This is why the paper separates expert identification from expert use. A team may correctly say which member is strongest and still ignore that fact in the final synthesis. Identification alone does not help unless the coordination process gives the expert's judgment more weight at the moment of choice because that is the step where the final answer is locked in.

The trade-off with adversarial resistance follows from the same mechanism. Consensus weakens the expert, but it also weakens a malicious member. The team becomes harder to hijack because no single voice dominates. That safety benefit comes at a performance cost whenever the expert's view genuinely should dominate.

Math after the picture

A simple team-quality summary

First in words:

Team quality depends on the best member's quality times how much of that expertise the final decision actually preserves.

Then the compact form:

team_quality = best_member_quality * expert_weight_retained

Every symbol:

team_qualitythe final accuracy or score of the whole team
best_member_qualitythe quality of the strongest individual member on the task
expert_weight_retainedthe fraction of the best member's strength that survives team discussion and final synthesis
A tiny worked example
Suppose the best individual member answers correctly on 90 out of 100 tasks.
If the team preserves only 0.7 of that expertise because compromise weakens the final choice, then team quality is 90 * 0.7 = 63.
A stronger coordination rule that preserves 0.95 of expert strength would raise the team result to 85.5.
The paper's main claim is that current self-organizing teams often preserve too little expert weight even when the expert is known.

The paper reports detailed benchmark measurements, but this compact summary captures the core point that performance hinges on whether expert strength survives the group process.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Expert quality may vary by subtask. A fixed weighting rule can also fail if the expert on one subtask becomes weak on another, so coordination should adapt to local expertise rather than assuming one permanent leader.
Too much deference can reduce resilience. If the team always follows one member, a mistaken or manipulated expert can mislead the whole group because no other voice has enough influence to correct the error.
Transcript analysis may miss silent failure modes. A team can appear to compromise in language while actually failing earlier through poor task decomposition or poor evidence sharing.
Done right. The paper is most useful as a warning that coordination rules must preserve genuine expertise while still limiting damage from hostile members.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create a benchmark where one agent scores higher than the others on a subset of tasks by a clear margin. Run three systems: the best individual alone, an unconstrained team discussion, and a team with an explicit expert-weighting rule at the final decision step.

Measure final accuracy, best-member gap, and resistance to one adversarial member. The result should reveal whether unconstrained teamwork helps or merely smooths away the strongest answer because compromise gives weaker views too much influence.

best_member_gap = best_individual_accuracy - team_accuracy ; expert_use_gain = weighted_team_accuracy - unconstrained_team_accuracy

The demo should show that a team is only helpful when it preserves expertise strongly enough to avoid dragging the best member downward.
