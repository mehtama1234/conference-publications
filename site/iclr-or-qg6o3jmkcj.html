SurvHTE-Bench: Benchmarking Treatment Effects from Incomplete Medical Data

A benchmark that lets researchers fairly test methods for figuring out which patients benefit most from a treatment, when some patient outcomes are unknown.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

In medicine and policy, doctors often ask: 'Will THIS patient benefit from this treatment?' But real-world data has a hard problem: some patients' outcomes are never observed because they left the study or the study ended. SurvHTE-Bench is the first benchmark that lets researchers systematically test their answer-methods on data with known ground truth, where outcomes are deliberately hidden, to see which approaches work when information is incomplete.

Zero assumption version

Why existing methods are hard to compare

Imagine you're a doctor testing a new cancer drug. Half your patients get it, half don't. Some patients recover, some relapse. But here's the catch: your study ended before every patient's outcome was known. Some people survived past the end date — you only know 'they were still alive when the study stopped', not 'they recovered'. That's called censoring, and it's real.

A researcher might build a method to predict 'Which patients will benefit most from treatment?' But how do you test if the method is right? You can't just see if it predicted well on your own data — maybe it got lucky. Maybe it works on your data but fails on another dataset with different patient types. Different researchers use different test datasets, different medical datasets, different metrics. So it's impossible to know which methods truly work best.

The problem. Researchers have no fair, shared way to compare methods for predicting individual treatment benefits when patient outcomes are partially unknown.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Create test scenarios with known ground truth

Build synthetic datasets where ALL outcomes are known, deliberately censor some, force methods to infer them.

Vary the rules of the world

Change which assumptions hold (e.g. are outcomes truly random, or biased by patient type?).

Pair real covariates with simulated truth

Use real patient data (age, health history) but fake treatment and outcome, for realism without privacy risk.

Run existing methods on all scenarios

Test forest-based, meta-learner, and outcome-imputation approaches on every test case.

Measure where each excels and breaks

Show how each method fares when assumptions hold and when they break.

What the paper changes

What changes: a shared, modular testing ground

The paper creates a reusable benchmark — a collection of datasets and rules — not a new method. The object is the testing process itself. Before SurvHTE-Bench, each researcher tested on their own data, using their own metrics, without a common baseline. Now, all methods are tested on the same set of scenarios: synthetic datasets (where ground truth is 100% known), semi-synthetic datasets (real covariates, fake outcomes), and real-world datasets (like a twin study where ground truth is confirmed). This shared ground is the change.

the object it moves  =  shared testing scenarios
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Individual treatment benefit under censoring

measured quantity  =  true individual treatment effect

The hidden quantity is the answer to 'How much better would THIS patient have done on treatment than on control?' — it's called the individual treatment effect, because it measures the boost or harm specific to each person. In survival analysis, you measure it as the gap in survival time or probability. You can't directly observe it, because each patient gets either treatment or control, not both — so you never see their outcome under both arms. Add censoring on top — you don't know the true outcome even for the arm they received — and the quantity becomes doubly hidden. SurvHTE-Bench recovers it by working backwards from the data, comparing methods' predictions against synthetic ground truth where the answer IS known.

SurvHTE-Bench estimates it by comparing methods' predictions against synthetic ground truth. It builds scenarios where the true treatment effect IS known, hides some data, and watches each method try to recover it. The paper measures success by comparing predicted vs. true effects on the withheld synthetic data.

Why it works

Why a shared benchmark improves the field

Without a benchmark, each researcher tests their method on their own data with their own metrics. Two researchers publish papers saying their method works — but maybe one used an easier dataset, or only tested when assumptions held. A doctor reading both papers can't tell which method is truly better, because they have no way to compare — one paper used data from hospital A with a certain type of patient, the other used data from hospital B with different patients and different challenges. Disagreement about assumptions, datasets, and metrics hides the truth.

SurvHTE-Bench fixes this by building a common, modular testing ground. Every method is tested on the SAME synthetic data (where the paper knows the correct answer), the SAME semi-synthetic data (realistic covariates, controlled outcomes), and the SAME real datasets. The paper also varies assumptions on purpose: 'What if censoring is not random?' 'What if treatment assignment is biased?' This forces each method to show where it excels and where it breaks. A reader can now see: 'Method A does best on random censoring, but Method B handles biased censoring better', because both methods are measured side-by-side on the same test. That clarity lets researchers pick the right tool for their setting, rather than guessing which published paper to trust, and the field make real progress by knowing which problems are actually solved.

Math after the picture

How accuracy is measured

First in words:

integrated absolute error = average of absolute difference between predicted and true treatment effect

Then the compact form:

IAE = (1/n) * sum_i |tau_pred_i - tau_true_i|

Every symbol:

tau_pred_ithe method's prediction of individual treatment effect for patient i
tau_true_ithe true effect (known in synthetic data, hidden in practice)
|difference|absolute value, always positive
(1/n) * sumaverage across all n patients in the test set
A tiny worked example
Patient 1: predicted effect 8 months, true effect 10 months. Error = |8-10| = 2 months.
Patient 2: predicted effect 5 months, true effect 4 months. Error = |5-4| = 1 month.
Patient 3: predicted effect 12 months, true effect 11 months. Error = |12-11| = 1 month.
Average error = (2+1+1) / 3 = 1.3 months. Lower is better.

Each method's predictions are scored against ground truth only on synthetic data where the truth is known; real data measures reproducibility and real-world performance.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Censoring is not random but tied to patient health. If sicker patients drop out, methods that don't account for this bias will guess wrong about treatment effects. SurvHTE-Bench includes this scenario so you can see which methods handle it.
Treatment is assigned based on patient type, not randomly. If doctors give treatment only to sicker patients, naive comparison fails. Methods that ignore this pattern will draw false conclusions, because they can't separate the effect of treatment from the effect of who received it.
Real-world covariates are different from the benchmark data. If patients in your hospital differ in age, disease stage, or other factors from the benchmark, methods optimized for the benchmark may transfer poorly.
Done right: using the benchmark to find your method's blind spots. The modular design means you can test on scenarios matching your real data — same covariate distribution, same censoring pattern — to see if your method will work before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should take one method (e.g. causal survival forest) and one SurvHTE-Bench scenario (e.g. semi-synthetic data with random censoring). Split the dataset: train the method on the censored data (hiding some outcomes), then test on withheld data where outcomes ARE known. Measure the integrated absolute error (IAE) — how far the method's predictions are from truth.

IAE = average absolute error between predicted and true individual treatment effects on test set

Run the same method on three scenarios (random censoring, biased censoring, and realistic twin-study data) and show the error rising as assumptions break — proving the method is sensitive to hidden bias, not magic.
