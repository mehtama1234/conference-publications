TRACE: Recovering Smooth Mechanism Transitions

When causal mechanisms shift gradually instead of flipping on and off, you can recover the exact mixing path by training separate expert models and interpolating between them.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Real-world systems change gradually. A turning car does not snap from straight driving to cornering—its dynamics morph smoothly. A person does not switch gaits instantly—they blend walking and running fluidly. Yet causal discovery methods assume mechanisms flip instantaneously. TRACE models these smooth transitions as weighted combinations of fixed atomic mechanisms and recovers their exact mixing trajectory, even for intermediate states never seen in training.

Zero assumption version

The discrete assumption breaks in smooth transitions

Causal representation learning discovers hidden causes behind what you observe. A causal mechanism is the rule that explains how hidden variables drive the observed data. In static systems, this rule is fixed. But real data changes: markets crash, weather patterns shift, vehicles maneuver. Existing methods assume these changes snap—mechanism A turns off, mechanism B turns on. Instantaneous.

In reality, many systems transition smoothly. A vehicle's steering gradually changes its dynamics during a turn. Human gait morphs continuously from walking to running. A factory machine ramps from low speed to high speed by small increments. The problem: if you train a causal discovery method on data that blends mechanism A and B but the method assumes one or the other, it fits an impossible ghost mechanism—neither pure A nor B—and fails to identify either.

The method in one sentence. Train separate expert models for each pure mechanism, then at test time recover what fraction of each expert was active at each moment by measuring how well their predictions combined match the observed data.

What it actually does

Separate hidden causes from blended data

What the paper changes

The single thing the paper changes

TRACE changes what the training signal contains. Instead of using time-varying causal mechanisms (which vary within each training batch), TRACE learns separate mixture-of-experts (multiple independent models, each expert specializing in one pure causal rule). At test time, the mixing weights—how much each expert contributes—are recovered by solving an inverse problem: find the weights that make the blended experts' prediction match the observed data.

the object it moves  =  Mixing trajectory
The hidden quantity

The mixing trajectory: how much of each mechanism is active

measured quantity  =  Mixing weight per expert per timestep

The paper introduces a named hidden quantity: the mixing trajectory—a set of time-varying weights that answer the question: at each moment, how much of expert A is the system using, how much of expert B, and so on? These are fractions between 0 and 1 that add up to 1 (because the blended mechanisms must account for 100 percent of what is happening).

The paper measures this by solving a least-squares problem at test time: for each observed data point, find the mixing weights such that combining expert A's prediction, expert B's prediction, and any others in the right amounts matches the actual observation as closely as possible. This is an inverse problem with a closed-form solution.

Why it works

Why recovering the mixing weights reveals the truth

At test time, every observed data point is a noisy read of the true hidden variables driven by some blend of expert mechanisms. If expert A predicts observation O_A and expert B predicts O_B, and the true observation comes from blending 60 percent of expert A's prediction with 40 percent of expert B's prediction plus noise, then solving for the mixing weights tells you those percentages—60 percent and 40 percent—revealing how much of each causal rule was running.

The paper proves a key mathematical fact: if the atomic mechanisms (the pure rules) are sufficiently different from each other, and if the observed data comes from a true blend of those mechanisms, then the mixing weights are identifiable—they are the unique solution. You cannot fit the data any other way. This means your recovered trajectory is not just any solution; it is the true path the system took.

Math after the picture

The mixing problem solved at each timestep

First in words:

The observed data equals the true hidden variables mixed through expert models, plus noise. Solve for the mixing weights that make the blended expert predictions closest to the observation.

Then the compact form:

x_t = A_1(z_t) * w_1(t) + A_2(z_t) * w_2(t) + ... + A_K(z_t) * w_K(t) + noise

Every symbol:

x_tthe observed data at time t
z_tthe true latent hidden variable at time t
A_kexpert k's causal mechanism: a function that takes the hidden z and outputs a prediction
w_k(t)the mixing weight for expert k at time t, a number between 0 and 1
sum of w_k(t)always equals 1 (weights form a probability distribution)
noiserandom measurement error
A tiny worked example
Suppose at one moment the true hidden variable z = 5.
Expert A (walking gait) predicts: stride length = A_1(5) = 1.2 meters.
Expert B (running gait) predicts: stride length = A_2(5) = 1.8 meters.
The actual observed stride = 1.5 meters.
Solve: w_A * 1.2 + w_B * 1.8 = 1.5. Since w_A + w_B = 1, set w_A = 0.75 and w_B = 0.25.
Check: 0.75 * 1.2 + 0.25 * 1.8 = 0.9 + 0.45 = 1.35. Oops, that is 0.15 short.
True solution: w_A = 0.5, w_B = 0.5. Check: 0.5 * 1.2 + 0.5 * 1.8 = 0.6 + 0.9 = 1.5. Correct.
The mixing weights (0.5, 0.5) reveal that at this moment the system blends gait mechanics evenly.

At each test moment, the mixture of experts' predictions must sum to the actual observation, which uniquely pins down the mixing weights if the experts are different enough.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mechanisms too similar. If expert A and expert B make nearly identical predictions for all hidden states, the mixing weights become ambiguous—you can shift weight from A to B and barely change the error. The paper assumes distinctness; if mechanisms overlap heavily, identifiability breaks.
High noise drowns out differences. When measurement noise is large, the experts' individual predictions are unreliable. The mixing problem becomes ill-conditioned (small changes in noise cause large swings in recovered weights). The recovered trajectory will be noisy.
Missing an expert. If the true system uses three pure mechanisms but you train only two experts, you miss the third. The two experts will try to compensate, and the recovered mixing weights will be misleading.
Mixing weights outside valid range or not summing to 1. If the optimization is done carelessly, the solver might return negative weights or weights greater than 1. The fix is to constrain the solver to enforce that all weights stay between 0 and 1 inclusive, and that they sum to 1.
Done right. Train experts only on data where you know which mechanism was pure (e.g., early walking, clearly in walking regime). At test time, solve the constrained least-squares problem: minimize error subject to weights being valid probabilities. Validate by checking that recovered mixing trajectories are smooth and plausible.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate synthetic data where you blend two known causal mechanisms (e.g., a linear rule with slope 2 and a linear rule with slope 0.5) by a smooth trajectory (e.g., 90% first rule at t=0, 50-50 at t=50, 90% second rule at t=100). Train two experts: one on pure first rule, one on pure second rule. At test time, recover the mixing weights using least-squares. Plot the recovered weights against the true weights over time. Compute the correlation.

correlation = Pearson(recovered_weights, true_weights) over all timesteps

A high correlation (greater than 0.95) proves that TRACE reliably uncovers smooth mechanism transitions invisible to discrete-switching methods, even on intermediate states never encountered during training.
