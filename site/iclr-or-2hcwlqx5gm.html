Numerion: Hypercomplex Spaces for Time Series

Break down a time series into multiple layers by mapping it through different hypercomplex spaces, each one naturally capturing a different pattern frequency.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting usually starts by building a complex model with many special-purpose parts for different patterns. Numerion takes a different route: instead, it shows that when you write time series data in a special number system (called hypercomplex), different layers naturally pick up different frequencies on their own. The paper builds a multi-layer machine that maps the raw numbers into several of these spaces, lets each one do its job independently, then intelligently mixes the results.

Zero assumption version

The decomposition trap

Time series forecasting is hard because a single sequence hides multiple patterns at once. A stock price bounces with daily noise, swings with weekly trends, and drifts with annual cycles. Most models build separate parts to grab each pattern—one module for short-term wiggles, one for medium swings, one for slow drifts. This works but creates a brittle machine: you have to guess which patterns matter, build special code for each, and the whole thing breaks if the data changes character.

A naive approach: use one big neural network and hope it learns all the patterns at once. That almost never works well. The network tends to focus on the easiest patterns (usually the fast wiggles) and misses the slow trends, or vice versa.

The paper's move: Instead of building separate modules by hand, mathematically transform the raw numbers into special number systems where different frequencies naturally separate themselves.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Map to hypercomplex

Take raw numbers, embed them into complex, quaternion, and 16-dimensional hypercomplex spaces.

Generalize operations

Define addition, multiplication, and the tanh squashing function to work in each space.

Run independent networks

Each space gets its own chain of linear layers and nonlinear functions, processing the pattern independently.

Mix predictions

An adaptive fusion layer learns what weight each space should have in the final answer.

Forecast the future

Combine all weighted forecasts and measure error (how far off the guess was), then adjust the weights and network numbers to shrink that error next time.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper changes where the network does its calculations. Instead of working in regular real numbers, it shifts the computations into hypercomplex spaces. A hypercomplex space is a generalization of complex numbers: regular numbers have one dimension (just the value itself), complex numbers have two (a real part and an imaginary part), quaternions have four, and you can keep going to 8, 16, or any power-of-two dimensions. Each higher dimension adds a new imaginary axis with its own multiplication rule.

The hidden quantity

Frequency decomposition

measured quantity  =  Natural frequency separation across spaces

The paper measures something that usually requires careful hand-tuned design: which patterns each layer actually picks up. The hidden insight is that when you transform a time series into a higher-dimensional hypercomplex space and train a network there, the network naturally gravitates toward capturing lower-frequency components (slow trends and cycles). Real-valued networks in regular space grab high-frequency noise; complex networks land on mid-frequency patterns; quaternion and higher spaces automatically focus on low-frequency long-term drifts. This is not designed in—it emerges from the mathematics of how multiplication works in each space.

The paper quantifies this by analyzing trained networks: measuring which layers contribute most to the final forecast and visualizing the frequency content of what each space captures. In their experiments, the 16-dimensional (hexadecimal) space contributes about 40 percent of the final prediction on its own, and all high-dimensional spaces together account for roughly 60 percent.

Why it works

Why separating spaces works

When you use regular real numbers, a single neural network has to learn to ignore noise, pick up trends, and capture drifts all at once. It almost never does this cleanly. The network either jumps at every wiggle or misses the real patterns entirely.

Hypercomplex numbers fix this because multiplication in higher dimensions works differently. A complex number i has the property i times i equals negative one. A quaternion j times k equals i, and i times i equals negative one, but you lose commutativity (j times k does not equal k times j). These rules mean that when you combine numbers and lists using hypercomplex multiplication, the algebra naturally amplifies some frequency ranges and dampens others. The real-valued network inside that space then only has to learn the subset of patterns that the space naturally emphasizes. A quaternion network does not have to figure out how to ignore high-frequency noise—the quaternion algebra already suppresses it. This drastically shrinks what the network has to learn.

The paper generalizes this further: it builds multiple networks in parallel, one for each space (real, complex, quaternion, 16-dimensional). Each space handles the frequencies it is naturally good at. Then, instead of needing hand-tuned weights or separate loss terms for each, a single adaptive fusion layer learns how much each space should contribute. If the time series is mostly trend with little noise, the fusion layer upweights the high-dimensional spaces. If it is noisy with fast swings, it upweights the real and complex networks.

The fusion equation

First in words:

Final forecast equals the weighted sum of predictions from all spaces, where each space gets its own learned weight.

Then the compact form:

y_pred = sum_k alpha_k * y_k, where sum_k alpha_k = 1

Every symbol:

y_predthe final forecast value
alpha_kthe learned weight (importance) for space k
y_kthe prediction from network k running in space k
sum_k alpha_k = 1weights are learned to sum to one, like a probability
A tiny worked example
Real space predicts: 100 (good at noise, not trends)
Complex space predicts: 105 (catches mid-frequency swings)
Quaternion space predicts: 103 (sees slow patterns)
16D space predicts: 102 (sees very slow drifts)
Learned weights (after training): alpha_real=0.1, alpha_complex=0.3, alpha_quat=0.4, alpha_16d=0.2
Final forecast: 0.1*100 + 0.3*105 + 0.4*103 + 0.2*102 = 10 + 31.5 + 41.2 + 20.4 = 103.1
The model weighs each space by how reliable it is for this particular time series.

Each space naturally suppresses some frequencies because of its multiplication algebra, so the fusion layer just has to learn the final mix.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overhead without enough data. Running five parallel networks (one per space) costs more compute than a single network. If your time series is very short or you have few examples, the overhead may not be worth the gain. The method shines when you have enough data for each space to specialize.
No acceleration from native hardware. Computers have fast hardware for regular matrix multiplication (real numbers), but not for hypercomplex multiplication. The paper simulates hypercomplex operations using multiple real-valued matrices, which is slower in practice even though mathematically it is the same computation. A GPU with native hypercomplex support would fix this.
Fusion layer has to learn the right weights. If the adaptive fusion mechanism gets stuck or if learning rates are wrong, the mixture weights may not reflect the true frequency content of the data. A poorly tuned model might downweight the spaces that matter most.
Done right. When you have a time series with multiple clear frequency components (noise, trends, and slow drifts), and enough data and compute to train five networks, Numerion naturally decomposes it and forecasts accurately.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic time series that mixes three clear frequencies: high-frequency random noise (jittering every step), mid-frequency sine wave (a 12-step cycle), and a slow linear drift (growing 0.1 units per timestep). Train a simple real-valued network to forecast five steps ahead. Measure its error. Then train Numerion on the same data. Measure its error on each frequency component separately: how much does it misforecast the noise, the mid-cycle, and the drift?

error_per_frequency = (forecast_value - true_value)^2, computed separately for high/mid/low components

The demo should show that Numerion's error on low-frequency drifts is much smaller than the baseline, because the high-dimensional spaces naturally handle them, while the real-valued network struggles.
