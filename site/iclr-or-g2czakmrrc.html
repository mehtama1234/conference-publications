Retention-Optimized Two-Sided Matching

Instead of maximizing the count of matches or spreading exposure fairly, focus on which matches actually keep users coming back.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Dating and job platforms show people recommendations to make matches. Most algorithms chase two broken goals: either show the most-likely matches (which piles all recommendations on popular users while others get almost nothing), or spread exposure equally (which sounds fair but does not keep users engaged). This paper asks a harder question: what if you learned how many matches each user needs to stay, then targeted those numbers exactly?

Zero assumption version

Why match-counting and fairness both miss the mark

Dating and recruiting platforms live on the idea that matches keep people coming back. The simple approach: rank all possible partners by how likely they are to match with the incoming user, then show those ranked results. This gives more matches to already-popular users and leaves unpopular users with almost nothing. So people with few matches abandon the platform.

A second camp tries fairness: ensure every person gets shown to enough incoming users so they have equal exposure. This sounds right, but it misses a deep fact—being shown to more people does not guarantee someone stays. What matters is whether they actually get enough real matches to feel valued. Fairness spreads visibility, but does not target what users actually need: real connections at the rate that keeps them engaged.

The core problem. Algorithms optimize for the wrong goal. Platforms succeed when users stay, not when algorithms satisfy abstract rules.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn retention curves

From each user's past matches, fit a curve showing how retention probability rises with match count.

Score each possible pair

For the arriving user and each candidate, compute how much each new match would lift retention.

Joint optimization

Score each candidate twice: retention gain for the receiver, and retention gain for the candidate.

Rank and recommend

Sort candidates by combined score; show top K.

Adapt over time

As new users arrive and match, update their retention curves; the algorithm learns.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most matching algorithms choose recommendations by sorting candidates by match probability (how likely they are to match with the arriving user). MRet changes this: instead of sorting by probability, it ranks candidates by their impact on user retention on both sides. The paper replaces the matching metric with a retention metric.

The hidden quantity

Retention gain: how much does this match help?

measured quantity  =  Retention gain

The paper measures a quantity called retention gain: for a given user who has received some number of past matches, how much does one more match increase their probability of staying on the platform? It is estimated from each user's profile and match history—users with few past matches often show a steep curve (one more match matters a lot), while users with many matches show a flat curve (one more barely moves the needle).

MRet learns a personalized retention curve for each user from their interaction history. Once learned, when a recommendation must be made, the algorithm looks up the curve and computes: if this user currently has N matches, and we give them one more, how much higher is their stay probability? That is the retention gain.

Paper reading. The user's probability of staying is f of (user, cumulative_matches). When we add one more match, the gain is the new stay probability minus the old one.
Why it works

Why targeting retention works better

On any platform, there are always more potential matches than you can show. You might show 5 recommendations, but there are 1000 candidates. Someone has to be left out. The real question is: which 5 will keep people coming back, and who should receive each?

Match-probability ranking (the baseline) assumes every recommendation is equally good if it has a high relevance score. But in reality, the same match has wildly different value to different users. A date recommendation might be the seventh match for one person (they might stay anyway) but the second ever for another person (they would stay only if they get it). By learning retention curves, MRet discovers this difference. Then the algorithm makes a smarter trade-off: it sends a high-relevance candidate to the person it matters most for, even if someone else would have a marginally higher match probability.

MRet does this on both sides of the platform. When user X arrives and needs a recommendation, the algorithm does not just ask 'which candidate gives X the highest retention boost?' It also asks 'which candidate would benefit most from being recommended to X?' For instance, a candidate might not have received many recommendations yet, so being shown to X is very high-value to them, because they have a steep retention curve and one more recommendation pushes them higher on the stay probability. This two-sided accounting works because when you send a good match to someone who really needs it (instead of to someone who already has plenty), you lift platform retention more than a single-sided algorithm would.

The retention-gain equation

First in words:

For each user, compute their stay probability given a total match count, then measure the marginal gain.

Then the compact form:

retain_gain(user, count) = f(user, count+1) - f(user, count)

Every symbol:

f(user,count)the probability the user stays, given they have received count matches
usera specific person on the platform
countthe total number of matches they have received so far
retain_gain(...)how much the stay probability rises if they get one more match
A tiny worked example
User A: f(A, 2 matches) = 0.60 (60% chance they stay)
User A: f(A, 3 matches) = 0.75 (75% chance they stay)
Retention gain = 0.75 - 0.60 = 0.15 (one more match lifts stay probability by 15 points).
User B: f(B, 8 matches) = 0.90, f(B, 9 matches) = 0.91
Retention gain for B = 0.91 - 0.90 = 0.01 (one more match barely moves the needle, since B already has many).
In ranking: A's gain (0.15) beats B's gain (0.01), so if the algorithm can only show one person a good match, it chooses A.

The math captures the intuition: early matches are gold, late matches have diminishing returns. An algorithm that sees this can spend matches wisely.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Retention curves are noisy for new users. A brand-new user has no history, so their curve is unfit. The algorithm must use a guess based on users like them, or pool estimates across similar people. If the guess is wrong, early recommendations are misdirected.
Cold-start : who gets shown to new candidates? New candidates have no retention history either. The algorithm must still decide whether to recommend them. If it avoids them, they stay invisible forever. If it recommends them to everyone, it wastes scarce slots.
Gaming retention curves. If users learn that the algorithm favors people with few past matches, they might briefly abandon and return to reset their count, artificially gaming their retention curve.
Done right: hybrid cold-start. Use an ensemble: estimate the curve from the user's profile features (e.g., age, location) plus their match history, weighted by confidence. New users fall back to the demographic baseline; as they accumulate history, the measured curve takes over.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small two-sided matching simulator: generate 50 users per side, each with a synthetic retention curve (random breakpoints: some are hungry for matches, others are picky). Run three algorithms: (1) sort by match probability (ignore retention), (2) fairness-based (equal exposure), and (3) MRet (score by retention gain on both sides). Run for 100 time steps, tracking each user's matches and stay/abandon decision. At the end, measure overall platform retention: what fraction of users stayed until the end?

platform_retention = (number of users who stayed) / (total users)

MRet's retention must exceed both baselines. The win should be largest for the unpopular and the hungry—exactly the users the algorithm is designed to protect.
