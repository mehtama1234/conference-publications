CHESS: Chebyshev Spectral Synthesis for Trajectory Condensation

Instead of storing individual sensor readings, store the smooth continuous mathematical curves that produced them, so compressed data stays physically realistic and works across different models.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you compress lots of sensor measurements from a physical system (say, a robot or weather station), you often end up with tiny gaps and spiky noise that look nothing like the original smooth process. CHESS fixes this by working backwards from raw data points to discover the smooth continuous curves that generated them, then uses those curves instead of storing individual numbers.

Zero assumption version

The compression trap: smoothness gets lost

When you have thousands of sensor readings from a physical system and need to compress them down to just a few examples, the naive approach is to simply pick every hundredth data point and throw away the rest. This saves space, but selecting scattered points instead of storing the smooth curve discards the true physics, because the real signal is the gradual change from point to point (the curve), and the points themselves are just noisy samples of it.

A physical system (a robot arm, a weather pattern) produces trajectories (paths through space over time) that are inherently smooth — the values change gradually, not in jagged jumps. But when you store only scattered sample points and ignore their smooth connection, two problems appear. First, the random noise in any real sensor recording gets frozen into the compressed data, making it look choppy. Second, the compressed data starts to overfit — it only captures the exact sampling rate and model that created it. Swap to a different model or change how often you collect readings, and those frozen points no longer make sense.

In one sentence. The paper stores the smooth curves (functions) that produced the data, not the individual jagged points.

The paper shifts from storing discrete numbers (individual sensor readings) to storing continuous-time functions (smooth mathematical curves). Instead of asking 'what is the value at time 47.3?', it asks 'what is the smooth curve that fits the pattern?'

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: smoothness and stability

measured quantity  =  spectral coherence across time

The paper measures a hidden property: how smooth and physically realistic the compressed data is. This is not the usual loss (distance from original values) — it is something deeper. When a physical system evolves, the values at nearby points in time are correlated and predictable; if a sensor suddenly jumps, that violates the physics.

Chebyshev polynomials are a specific family of smooth mathematical curves (built from cosines). They have a special property: they stay well-behaved across a range — they do not wildly spike or collapse. By forcing the compressed data to fit into Chebyshev form, the paper guarantees that the curves stay physically plausible. The hidden quantity is how tightly constrained the solution is to the Chebyshev manifold (the space of all possible smooth curves of that type).

Why it works

Why continuous curves fix the problem

Here is the causal chain: When you store individual points from noisy sensor data, you are storing the noise as well as the true signal. That frozen noise only makes sense for the exact model and sampling rate that created it. If you switch models or sample faster, the noise is now misaligned because it was tailored to the old setup — the model expects patterns at the old resolution, but now you have different spacing or a different task, and the noise patterns no longer align with what the model needs.

The fix is to fit a smooth curve to the data. A smooth curve averaging out the noise and capturing only the trend — the slow, large changes that reflect the true physics. A Chebyshev curve is specially chosen because it has two properties: it is smooth (no spikes), and it is numerically stable (small changes in the coefficients do not cause wild swings in the curve). This means that when you later sample the curve at a different rate or plug it into a different model, the physics-based pattern is preserved.

Because the curve is defined by just a few numbers (the coefficients), compression becomes extreme: instead of storing thousands of points, store a few dozen coefficients. Generalization becomes automatic: because you are working with a mathematically smooth object, not frozen noise, any model and any sampling rate can read off values.

Fitting the curve

First in words:

For each sensor or spatial dimension, find coefficients (a1, a2, a3...) such that the curve a1*T0(t) + a2*T1(t) + a3*T2(t) minimizes both distance from the original data points AND a penalty that keeps coefficients small (preventing overfitting).

Then the compact form:

loss = sum over time points (curve value - original value)^2 + lambda * sum (coefficient^2)

A tiny worked example
Original noisy sensor: 10, 11, 9, 12, 8 (5 points, mostly noise around true value ~10)
Fit Chebyshev curve: a1=10.0, a2=0.3, a3=-0.1 (3 coefficients instead of 5)
Curve values at same times: 10.0, 10.2, 9.9, 10.1, 10.0 (smooth, noise averaged out)
Compression: 5 numbers -> 3 numbers; generalization: curve now works at any sampling rate.

Minimizing the squared distance pulls the curve toward the data; the coefficient penalty keeps it smooth and prevents memorizing noise.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing too few coefficients. If you save only a1 and a2 but the true curve really needs a1, a2, and a3, then you fit something too simple and lose the important details. The curve becomes overly smooth and misses genuine motion.
Choosing too many coefficients. If you save 50 coefficients for a signal that only needs 5, you are memorizing noise again and the curve becomes spiky. Compression fails and generalization breaks. The right number depends on the physics of the system.
Abrupt transitions in the trajectory. Chebyshev polynomials assume smoothness across the whole domain. If a sensor reading has a hard step (a break in the process), a global polynomial cannot capture it. CHESS handles this with piecewise curves (splitting time into intervals) but choosing breakpoints is an extra design choice.
Done right. The paper shows that with proper coefficient selection and piecewise boundaries, the curve stays smooth, generalizes across models and sampling rates, and achieves massive compression without resorting to stored noise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple trajectory: a sine wave or robot arm moving over time, sampled at 1000 points. Add noise. Compress it to 50 points using CHESS (fit a Chebyshev curve). Then measure how well the curve predicts data sampled at a different rate (say, every third original time point) or on a different model (pass the curve through a neural network trained on a different task).

generalization ratio = (accuracy on original model at original rate) / (accuracy on different model or different rate)

If generalization ratio is close to 1.0, CHESS truly reuses knowledge across setups instead of memorizing the exact data.
