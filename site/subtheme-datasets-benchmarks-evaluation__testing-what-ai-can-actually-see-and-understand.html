Testing What AI Can Actually See and Understand

Papers build benchmarks to test whether vision-language models can handle real images, spatial reasoning, and visual understanding tasks that humans find natural — from design aesthetics to ancient pottery.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Vision-language models—systems that look at images and answer questions about them—sound simple in theory: show the model a picture, ask what it sees, check if it is right. But in practice, vision understanding is far trickier than it seems. A model might recognize objects but miss spatial layout; it might handle photos taken in studios but fail on blurry, messy real-world images; it might spot a dog but not understand whether the dog is happy or scared, chasing or being chased. This subtheme is about building tests that expose what vision models can and cannot actually do.

The core difficulty is that human vision understanding is so automatic and intuitive that we forget how much is involved. Understanding a picture means parsing objects, their positions, colors, sizes, actions, relationships, context, and emotions all at once. Tests have to slice through that complexity and ask: does the model truly see spatial reasoning, or just memorize patterns? Can it understand design aesthetics, or just label surfaces? Does it work on ancient pottery the same way it works on modern photos? The papers here build benchmarks that test these specific kinds of understanding, often catching places where models that seem impressive at first actually fail on details humans find natural.

Researchers build these benchmarks by carefully collecting images paired with questions that probe a specific skill. Some focus on spatial reasoning—asking how objects relate to each other in 3D space. Others test visual understanding of domains people have studied for centuries: art history, architecture, ancient objects. Still others gather real, messy images from the internet or cameras rather than clean studio photos, to test whether models work in the wild. The goal is to move beyond generic vision tests toward benchmarks that measure real-world visual understanding.

How the work breaks down

The papers here split into five directions: testing whether vision models truly understand how objects relate to each other in 3D space (not just pattern matching), handling specialized tasks like reading text in images and detecting deceptive intent, parsing scenes that unfold over time in videos, understanding pictures in specific domains like medicine and ancient history, and catching moments when models fail on details that humans find simple—whether that is thinking through complex scenarios step by step, or maintaining spatial sense when images get messy and real.

Spatial Reasoning in Real Scenes

The core problem here is that a model can look at a picture and name the objects in it, but that does not mean it understands where those objects are or how they relate to each other in 3D space. A dog and a ball might both be in an image, but does the model know the dog is to the left of the ball or approaching it? Does it understand distance, angle, perspective—the layout that makes a scene make sense? These papers expose a deep gap: models that seem good on photos of objects often fail badly when you ask them to reason about space.

The Face to Face benchmark tests whether models can look at an image of two people and answer questions about their relative positions, emotions, and what they are doing—things that depend entirely on spatial and social understanding. The finding is stark: existing models fall far behind human performance, and fine-tuning can shrink but not close the gap because the skill is harder to teach than model builders expected. Spatial CAPTCHA uses a simple idea: ask the model to solve spatial puzzles that humans find easy—rotating objects, judging containment, understanding perspective. The best model gets only 31% right, while humans ace them because rotating objects in your mind is how humans naturally think about space. SpaCE-Eval and SpatiaLab test the same skill on real environments: asking whether models understand the layout of rooms, streets, and scenes. InternVL, one of the stronger models, scores 55% where humans hit 88%—a gap that exists because the model reads pixel arrangements instead of building a 3D mental model. Seeing Across Views tests whether models can reason about space when they see the same scene from multiple angles at once. When a single view poorly predicts multi-view performance, it tells us that models are not truly reasoning about the 3D world behind the images—they are reading surface patterns that differ when the angle changes. CitySeeker asks models to navigate city streets by looking at photos and answering questions about where to go, what to see, and what they just passed. The models accumulate errors, lose track of where they are, and struggle to recall what they observed—a failure in spatial memory and planning that humans do continuously. Together, these benchmarks agree: spatial reasoning is a core gap in today's vision models.

The papers build on each other by showing the same weakness appears whether the model is asked about positioned people, shaped objects, room layouts, robotic scenes, or navigable environments. None of them invented a new architecture—they all measure the same fundamental skill: does the model truly see space or just match patterns in pixels.

Text, Intent, and Complex Information Extraction

Many real-world images contain text—signs, labels, documents, maps, news articles. When a vision model looks at such an image, can it read the text accurately and use it to answer questions? This is harder than it sounds. The text might be small, blurry, sideways, or mixed with other graphics. The model has to not only see the pixels of each letter but also parse meaning from the text and combine it with visual understanding. Beyond text itself, some images contain layers of intent: does a news photo pair with its headline in a way that manipulates viewers, or truthfully represents the story?

The OCR-Reasoning benchmark presents images with text, then asks the model complex questions that require both reading and reasoning: if a menu item costs X and you order Y of them, how much do you pay? Leading models achieve below 50% accuracy because they fail at both steps—they misread text and they stumble on the calculation. FRIEDA tests a harder variant: understanding maps and geographic reasoning. Maps contain symbols, scale, overlap, and spatial relationships written in visual form. Models have to ground information from multiple parts of the map to answer where something is or how to get there. Results show models struggle at the cross-map reasoning that humans do naturally. LiveWeb-IE tackles a different extraction problem: when given a screenshot of a live website, can the model pick out the information you asked for—a phone number, a price, a link? This tests whether the model understands the page structure and can locate elements among noise. Deception Decoded looks at video and images with text and asks: does this creator intend to mislead the viewer? This requires reading the text, understanding the image, and inferring the creator's hidden goal. Current models rely on shallow cues and often miss manipulation. Together, these papers show that when text, structure, and intent mix with images, today's models often fail at each component and almost always fail at the combination.

All four papers test reasoning over visual information that is not just about objects and space but about language, meaning, and intent embedded in the image.

Video, Time, and Multimodal Reasoning

A video is a sequence of images over time, but understanding it requires something more: tracking changes, noticing cause and effect, remembering what happened before, and connecting sound or speech to what you see. Models trained on single images sometimes fail when they have to think across frames or combine multiple modalities into one answer.

VUDG tests whether a model trained to understand videos on one kind of domain (say, sports) stays accurate when moved to a different domain (say, cooking). Most models do not. Performance drops sharply under domain shift because the model learned surface patterns specific to the training data rather than true understanding of how actions and objects work. Video-LevelGauge looks at a subtle but revealing problem: does a model focus equally on frames from the beginning, middle, or end of a video, or does it favor certain positions? The findings show that open-source models often rely too heavily on early or nearby frames, while commercial models like Gemini 2.5 Pro treat all frames more equally. This reveals whether the model truly reasons about the temporal sequence or just shortcuts by looking at a few frames. VideoMathQA takes videos that contain mathematical reasoning—someone solving a problem on a board, or quantities changing—and asks the model to answer math questions. This requires temporal reasoning (knowing the sequence of steps), visual reasoning (seeing the symbols), and mathematical reasoning all at once. Current models struggle because they cannot integrate these three forms of understanding.

Each of these papers reveals that adding time to visual understanding exposes new gaps that single-image benchmarks miss.

Domain-Specific Visual Understanding

Vision models are usually tested on generic images—objects, scenes, everyday photos. But real experts use visual understanding in specialized domains where decades of human knowledge matter: a doctor reading an X-ray, an art historian analyzing a painting, an archaeologist studying ancient pottery. Can models perform at an expert level in these domains, or do they stumble because the visual patterns are unfamiliar or the reasoning is too specialized?

MME-Unify evaluates models that try to both understand images and generate new ones. Testing on both directions reveals that models struggle with following detailed instructions when generating images and produce poor quality overall. The Graphic Design Aesthetics benchmark asks whether a model can judge a design the way a professional designer would: Does the color palette work? Is the composition balanced? Are the types readable? Models fail to align with human aesthetic judgment because they lack the training or reasoning to understand design principles. Fetal Ultrasound (FETAL-GAUGE) tests vision models on medical imaging from a domain where mistakes matter because wrong answers could affect patients. The benchmark uses ultrasound images from pregnancy assessment and asks models to answer questions about what they see. The best model hits only 55% accuracy, nowhere near the level a doctor needs to trust the answer. Doctors see structures and measurements that current models miss because they trained for years on thousands of scans. VaseVQA-3D goes further back in time: given 3D models of ancient Greek pottery, can a model answer questions about the shapes, decorations, and styles? This requires recognizing visual patterns that span centuries and understanding how archaeologists classify and name them. Together, these benchmarks show that general vision ability does not transfer automatically to specialized domains—models need domain-specific training or reasoning to reach expert performance.

All four papers measure the same idea: visual understanding at scale does not mean visual understanding everywhere, especially in fields where human expertise has accumulated over decades.

Durability and Reasoning Under Pressure

Real visual reasoning is not just about getting things right on clean, well-lit photos. It is about staying accurate when images are blurry, rotated, partially hidden, upside down, or require you to mentally manipulate them. It is also about working through multi-step logic and not relying on shortcuts. These papers test whether models hold up when conditions are hard or when asked to reason deeply.

BabyVision shows a gap that should not exist: when asked simple visual questions that a 2-year-old can answer correctly, advanced models like Gemini score under 50% while humans hit 94%. The questions test basic visual primitives—shapes, counting, spatial relations—without heavy domain knowledge. This is not a failure of training data size; it is a failure of fundamental visual perception. KidGym uses children's intelligence tests: grid-based puzzles and pattern matching that a school-age child finds straightforward. Models stumble because they do not reason step-by-step through the logic the way humans do. Real-3DQA tests whether a model truly understands 3D spatial relationships or just memorizes patterns. When researchers remove the easy visual cues, model accuracy crashes, confirming that they are using shortcuts rather than reasoning because they were not forced to build a real 3D mental model. FloorplanQA shows inconsistency: models answer simple spatial questions on floor plans correctly but fail when you add constraints or ask them to think about whether a layout is feasible. SpatialViz-Bench looks at perspective-taking: can a model mentally rotate objects or see them from different angles? Open-source models show wild variation in accuracy, and strangely, adding chain-of-thought prompting actually makes some models worse because the models make up explanations instead of carefully rotating the object mentally. Unfolding Spatial Cognition tests the ultimate challenge: visual simulation, asking the model to imagine how a scene would look if objects moved or rotated. Models perform near random chance while humans solve the puzzles easily, suggesting they cannot mentally simulate spatial changes. The corrupted-image benchmark measures whether models stay accurate when images are corrupted—blurred, darkened, rotated, or changed. The finding is counterintuitive: models are hurt more by small spatial changes (a rotation) than by large visual damage (heavy blur or color shift), revealing that models rely on the arrangement of pixels rather than understanding the 3D scene underneath. Together, these papers expose a pattern: models often succeed on pattern-matching but fail at reasoning, mental simulation, and holding steady when the image's spatial structure changes.

All of these benchmarks converge on one insight: the gap between what models score on standard tests and what they actually understand shows when you remove the crutches or ask them to imagine and reason.
