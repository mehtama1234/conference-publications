Vulcan: Compact Class-Specific Vision Transformers

The paper keeps only the knowledge a model needs for its specific classes and throws away the rest, using a two-step trick: first introduce controlled redundancy, then prune ruthlessly.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Imagine you train a model to recognize all 1000 dog breeds, but your phone only needs to spot 5 specific ones. Today's compression methods still keep the knowledge about all 1000 breeds, wasting space. Vulcan discovers that different parts of a Vision Transformer (a neural network that reads images) do different jobs: some parts remember which class an image belongs to, others see general patterns like edges and textures. By deliberately adding redundant copies of the class-specific parts before trimming, Vulcan can compress the model to one-fifth its original size while keeping or even improving accuracy on just those 5 breeds.

Zero assumption version

Compression throws away the wrong knowledge

A Vision Transformer trained on ImageNet learns to recognize all 1,000 classes—but if you deploy it on a phone that only needs 5 classes, you still carry the weight of knowledge about the other 995. Standard compression methods (pruning: cutting neurons, quantization: using smaller numbers) shrink the model uniformly, keeping both the class-specific and class-irrelevant knowledge.

A naive approach: just prune neurons that seem unimportant. But pruning is irreversible—once you delete a neuron, you cannot restore it if training later realizes it was actually useful. The result: compressed models lose accuracy on the target classes because they also lost useful general patterns.

The problem in one sentence. Standard compression keeps useless knowledge and loses useful details, because it cannot distinguish which neurons encode class-specific facts from which encode reusable patterns.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Analyze the transformer.

Measure which neurons light up for each class and which fire for all images alike.

Collapse and fold.

Copy the neurons that care about your target classes; stack them on top of each other; train to merge them.

Inject low-rank constraints.

Force attention weights to lie in a thin plane (low-rank), so they cannot store irrelevant complexity.

Prune safely.

Now delete neurons that add nothing, because the important facts have been crystallized.

Deploy and measure.

Check the shrunken model on target classes; it often scores higher because redundant class-specific neurons merged efficiently, crystallizing the pattern.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Vulcan modifies two parts of the transformer before pruning: the feed-forward network neurons (which encode which class an image belongs to) and the attention weights (which encode general visual patterns). By deliberately creating redundancy in class-specific neurons and constraining attention to low-rank, Vulcan makes those parts easier to compress without breaking them.

The hidden quantity

The class-specific importance signature

measured quantity  =  How much class-specific neurons respond

Not all neurons in a transformer care about class identity equally. Some neurons in the feed-forward blocks fire strongly when they see your target classes and weakly otherwise. Vulcan measures this by recording how much each neuron activates when processing images from your 5 target classes versus other images.

The paper estimates this by running the model on labeled data and recording how much each neuron fires per class. A neuron that fires strongly for your target classes gets kept during the collapse step; neurons that barely fire get pruned away.

Paper reading. The paper measures how much each neuron fires on your target classes versus other classes, computing a score that ranks neurons by how much they care about class identity. This ranking decides which neurons to keep and collapse.
Why it works

Why collapsing then pruning works better than direct pruning

Direct pruning is irreversible: once a neuron is deleted, the model cannot recover it even if training later discovers it was useful. Vulcan inverts this problem. By duplicating class-specific neurons before training, Vulcan gives the optimization algorithm a chance to discover which copies can merge without loss, and which matter. Low-importance neurons can still disappear—but now their deletion is guided by redundancy, not blindness.

Second, attention weights capture visual patterns (edges, shapes, textures) that work across classes. Vulcan forces these weights into a low-rank form: instead of storing arbitrary numbers in a big matrix, it stores only the essential directions (the thin plane in high-dimensional space). This prevents attention from wasting capacity on class-specific quirks and keeps it focused on reusable patterns. When you later prune the class-irrelevant parts, attention survives because it was never bloated to begin with.

Math after the picture

The redundancy-and-prune equation

First in words:

Minimize the model's error on target classes plus a penalty that encourages redundant class-specific neurons to overlap and attention weights to stay in a low-rank space, while gradually removing unhelpful neurons.

Then the compact form:

L = cross_entropy(predictions, targets) + lambda_collapse * sum(distance between duplicate neurons) + lambda_rank * rank_penalty(attention_weights)

Every symbol:

cross_entropy(predictions, targets)how wrong the model is on the target classes
lambda_collapsehow strongly we push redundant neurons to merge
distance between duplicate neuronshow far apart the copied neurons have drifted during training
lambda_rankhow strongly we force attention weights to lie in a thin subspace
rank_penalty(attention_weights)a measure of whether attention weights are truly low-rank
A tiny worked example with three neurons on five target classes
Neuron A (class-specific): activation per target class = [0.9, 0.85, 0.88, 0.1, 0.12]. High for targets 1-3, low for 4-5. Class-specific importance = 8.
Neuron B (class-specific): activation = [0.92, 0.87, 0.91, 0.11, 0.13]. Similar pattern. Importance = 7.5.
Neuron C (generic): activation = [0.5, 0.55, 0.48, 0.52, 0.51]. Equal everywhere. Importance = 1.
Vulcan duplicates A and B, creating copies A' and B'. During training with lambda_collapse = 5, distance penalty for (A, A') = |0.9-0.9| (small cost). Neuron C, uncopied, faces pruning pressure.
After fine-tuning: A and A' have merged (both ≈ 0.88), B and B' have merged (both ≈ 0.89). Neuron C still fires equally everywhere. Pruning step deletes C entirely.
Final size: 2 neurons (A and B) instead of 3. Accuracy on target classes: unchanged (A and B do the work). Size reduction: 33%, class-specific knowledge intact.

The collapse penalty makes redundant copies slide toward each other during training; once merged, they encode class-specific knowledge compactly. Low-rank attention and pruning of unmerged neurons shrink the model without destroying the important patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Collapse weight chosen wrong. If lambda_collapse is too weak, redundant neurons never merge and pruning loses information. If too strong, duplicate neurons collapse before training realizes they encode subtly different patterns, and the model oversimplifies. Each dataset needs its own collapse strength setting.
Low-rank constraint too aggressive. Forcing attention to very low rank can destroy cross-class visual patterns (e.g., if targets are similar, low-rank attention may discard fine-grained distinctions between them). The rank must be calibrated to your class diversity.
Class-specific vs. generic boundary blurs. Some feed-forward neurons encode facts that are partly class-specific and partly general. The paper's method uses a hard threshold to classify neurons; neurons near the boundary may be misclassified and chosen for deletion by mistake.
Done right. Vulcan works best when your target classes are truly distinct (so a clear set of class-specific neurons exists) and your resource budget is moderate (20-40% of original size). With good hyperparameter tuning per dataset, compression stays lossless or beneficial.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a proof-of-concept: (1) Train a small Vision Transformer on CIFAR-100 (100 classes) end-to-end. (2) Pick 5 target classes. (3) Measure class-specific importance for each neuron via activation statistics on labeled data. (4) Duplicate the top-50% important neurons and add low-rank constraints to a random attention layer. (5) Fine-tune for 5 epochs. (6) Prune neurons that fell below a threshold during fine-tuning. (7) Measure accuracy on original 5 target classes and on a held-out non-target class.

Compression ratio = (original parameter count) / (final parameter count); class-specific accuracy = target-class test accuracy after compression; irrelevant-class accuracy drop = (original non-target accuracy) - (final non-target accuracy)

The demo must show that class-specific accuracy stays high or improves (because redundant neurons crystallize the pattern) while the model shrinks to 20-40% of its original size. The irrelevant-class accuracy should drop by at least 30 percentage points, showing that class-irrelevant neurons were cleanly purged.
