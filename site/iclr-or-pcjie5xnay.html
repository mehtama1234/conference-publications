Verbal Technical Analysis: Reasoning on Stock Prices

VTA teaches an LLM to reason about stock prices in natural language, then uses that reasoning to guide time-series forecasts.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Stock prices move in patterns. A human trader might say, 'The 50-day moving average just crossed the 200-day one, and volume jumped. This looks like an uptrend forming.' An AI forecaster should be able to reason that way too. This paper shows how to combine natural language reasoning with neural time-series forecasting: convert historical prices into text descriptions (like that trader would), let a language model think through the patterns aloud, and then feed that verbal reasoning back into a time-series model as a constraint. The result is predictions that are both accurate and explainable.

The Domain Gap: Prices to Words and Back

Neural networks are good at forecasting time-series: feed in 60 days of price history, get out tomorrow's price. Language models are good at reasoning: feed in text, get out sensible text. But stock prices are neither words nor abstract numbers—they are financial objects with names (high, low, close) and patterns (trends, reversals, support levels). The challenge is that a human who knows finance can look at a chart and narrate what they see ('This is a double bottom, sellers are exhausted') in a way that a pure number-crunching time-series model cannot. And a pure language model, if you feed it raw numbers, is 'remarkably bad' at reasoning about time-series without training.

A naive approach: just feed a language model the price time-series and ask it to forecast. This fails because language models were trained on text, not numerical sequences. Another naive approach: use a time-series neural network alone, black-box, with no human interpretation. This works for accuracy but fails for trust—traders and regulators want to know why the model thinks the price will move.

The paper's core move: Convert prices to text so the LLM can read them (language models understand words better than raw numbers), let a language model reason in natural language about what it sees, then feed that reasoning as a condition into a neural forecasting model. The forecasting model learns to produce predictions that match both the true future price and a human-sensible explanation, because it is scored on both accuracy and alignment with the reasoning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Text conversion

Extract statistics from 60 days of prices (mean, min, max) and technical indicators (moving averages, momentum). Write them as annotations.

Step 2: Language reasoning

Feed the annotations plus the price history to an LLM. The LLM writes out its reasoning: 'I see this trend, here's why, I expect this outcome.'

Step 3: Reward signal

Score the LLM's reasoning using a time-based reward: the difference between predicted price and actual next price. Reasoning that led to an accurate forecast gets a high score; reasoning that led to a wrong forecast gets a low score, because we measure reward by how close the forecast came to the true price.

Step 4: Reinforce reasoning

Use reinforcement learning to nudge the LLM toward reasoning that predicts accurately. The LLM learns which explanations lead to correct forecasts.

Step 5: Condition the forecaster

Pass the LLM's reasoning attributes into a separate neural time-series model. This model learns to produce forecasts that match both the true price and the reasoning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: The reasoning trace

The paper moves one thing: the verbal reasoning output from the language model. Normally, an LLM would produce reasoning that is fixed or random. Here, the reasoning is optimized—trained to be explanations that actually lead to correct price forecasts. The model learns which stories about price patterns are true stories and which are fiction.

the object it moves  =  reasoning trace
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Reasoning-to-forecast alignment

measured quantity  =  How well reasoning predicts price

The paper measures something usually invisible: how much does the LLM's reasoning actually explain the price movement? This is captured in the inverse MSE reward. MSE is 'mean squared error'—take the predicted price, subtract the true price, square it, average over all predictions. Inverse means: the lower the error, the higher the reward. So the LLM's reasoning is scored by how accurately the reasoning led to a price prediction.

The paper estimates this by running the LLM, collecting its reasoning, using that reasoning to generate a price forecast, and comparing the forecast to what actually happened. Each piece of reasoning gets a score: the forecast error. The LLM is then nudged (via reinforcement learning) toward reasoning that produces low forecast errors.

Why it works

Why it works: The reasoning-guided feedback loop

A time-series forecasting model (the neural network backbone) can learn to predict prices by pattern-matching: if it sees a shape, it learns to output a number. But that number has no story. An LLM can tell a story, but it does not naturally connect that story to numbers.

The paper's move is to couple them: (1) The LLM reasons over text versions of the price patterns. (2) That reasoning is fed as input to the forecasting model, acting like a set of constraints: 'If the reasoning says the trend is up, the forecast should be higher.' (3) The LLM's reasoning is scored by how accurate the resulting forecast is. (4) Reinforcement learning nudges the LLM to produce reasoning that, when fed into the forecaster, produces accurate predictions. The loop is: reasoning gets fed into the forecaster; forecaster predicts; prediction is compared to ground truth; if wrong, the reasoning was probably unhelpful—so nudge the LLM to try different reasoning. Over time, the LLM learns which verbal descriptions of price patterns are actually predictive.

Why does this work? Because the LLM's language model powers are unleashed on the right task: not forecasting raw numbers (hard for LLMs), but reasoning about patterns and connecting those patterns to price directions. The technical indicators (moving averages, momentum) are translated into readable text; the LLM can think step-by-step through them; its reasoning then guides a specialized time-series model. Neither is doing the other's job badly—they are working together, each on what it is good at.

The equation: Inverse MSE reward for reasoning

First in words:

The reward for a single piece of reasoning is 1 divided by (1 plus the squared forecasting error). When the forecast is accurate, the error is small, so the reward is large. When the forecast is wrong, the error is big, so the reward shrinks. The LLM learns to output reasoning that makes the error small.

Then the compact form:

r_MSE = 1 / (1 + MSE), where MSE = (predicted_price - actual_price)^2

Every symbol:

r_MSEreward score for this reasoning (higher is better)
MSEhow far off the price forecast was
predicted_pricewhat the time-series model output
actual_pricewhat actually happened next day
1 + MSEnormalizer to keep reward between 0 and 1
A tiny worked example
Suppose the true price tomorrow is $100.
The LLM reasons: 'trend is up, I expect $102.' The forecaster outputs $102. Error = (102 - 100)^2 = 4. Reward = 1 / (1 + 4) = 1/5 = 0.20.
Alternative reasoning: 'sideways, I expect $99.' The forecaster outputs $99. Error = (99 - 100)^2 = 1. Reward = 1 / (1 + 1) = 1/2 = 0.50. Second reasoning is better (higher reward), so the LLM learns to reason like the second one.
The reinforcement learning nudges the LLM: 'Reasoning that predicts $99 is rewarded 0.50; reasoning that predicts $102 is rewarded only 0.20. Say things like the second one.' Over many examples, the LLM learns which patterns matter.

The inverse MSE turns prediction error into a score: better forecasts mean better reasoning. The LLM learns this because reinforcement learning penalizes bad explanations and rewards good ones.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reasoning may overfit to noise. If the LLM's reasoning is rewarded only by forecast accuracy on the training set, it may learn spurious patterns: 'Every time I see this weird combination, the price jumped.' This pattern may not hold on new data. Fix: validate reasoning on held-out test data and filter training reasoning for quality (the paper uses a 10th percentile filter).
Text conversion may lose precision. Converting prices to text (e.g., 'mean $101.50') throws away decimal information. The technical indicators are hand-crafted, so if the choice of indicators is wrong, the LLM has no good patterns to reason about. Fix: include more varied indicators and validate that the text version captures the signal.
LLM may produce reasonable-sounding but incorrect reasoning. Language models are good at producing fluent text. The reasoning may sound plausible ('I see a reversal forming') even if the forecast is wrong. The reward is only the price error, so inaccurate but eloquent reasoning is not caught by the model. Fix: have humans spot-check the reasoning and flag false narratives.
Done right. Train on one market, validate on a different time period in a different market. Check that the reasoning is consistent (e.g., 'trend up' reasoning is not used to forecast down prices). Have industry experts review reasoning on real trades and mark which explanations they trust.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy version on a single stock over 500 days. Collect 100 days of historical prices. Convert them to text (mean, min, max, 20-day moving average, momentum). Initialize a small language model (e.g., a model with 1.3 billion weights, or a pre-trained model adjusted to your task). Train it using the inverse MSE reward: for each day, the LLM writes reasoning, a time-series model makes a forecast conditioned on that reasoning, and the reward scores how close the forecast is to the true next-day price. Run for 1000 iterations. Then evaluate on a held-out 100-day test set: measure forecast accuracy (what fraction of forecasts were within 2% of the true price) and reasoning quality (ask a human: does this explanation make sense for this prediction?).

Accuracy = fraction of forecasts within 2% of true price. Interpretability = fraction of reasoning explanations that an expert agrees with.

The demo must show that (1) forecast accuracy improves as the LLM's reasoning improves, because better reasoning feeds into the forecasting model and steers it toward accurate predictions, and (2) the reasoning is human-readable and consistent (same pattern always gets the same explanation), because the LLM learns to map repeating price patterns to repeating verbal descriptions.
