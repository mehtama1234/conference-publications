ProfBench: Professional-Domain Evaluation with Expert Rubrics

ProfBench is a benchmark that measures whether LLMs (language models, AI systems that generate text) can handle professional-level tasks—like answering physics questions or writing financial reports—using detailed checklists created by real experts, while reducing the cost of grading by using AI judges trained to be fair.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Most LLM benchmarks test easy-to-verify tasks like math and coding. But real professional work—synthesizing research papers, writing financial analyses, solving advanced physics problems—is much harder to evaluate fairly. ProfBench solves this by collecting over 7,000 professional tasks (across physics, chemistry, finance, and consulting), each graded using detailed human-expert rubrics. To make this scalable, the paper builds AI judges that grade fairly without favoring certain models, and cuts evaluation cost by 100–1,000x.

Why existing benchmarks miss the hard part of professional work

Today, most LLM benchmarks test writing code, doing math, or answering factual questions—tasks where you can check the answer automatically or with a simple lookup. But real professional work (analyzing medical scans, writing legal briefs, synthesizing business research) requires judgment. A financial report might be correct in its numbers but confusing in its structure. A physics solution might list all the right steps but miss explaining the intuition. These tasks cannot be graded by a simple right-or-wrong checker.

When researchers try to evaluate LLMs on real professional tasks, they hit a wall: they have to hire experts to grade each response, which is slow and expensive. Even then, different experts might disagree on what counts as 'good'. So researchers fall back to testing only math and code, ignoring whole categories of work that matter to the real world. The field knows models are weak at complex professional judgment, but nobody has a way to measure it fairly at scale.

The core problem in one sentence. Professional tasks need judgment from domain experts to grade fairly, but expert grading is expensive and biased, so nobody has built a large-scale benchmark.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect rubrics from experts

Each expert writes 15–60 detailed criteria for tasks in their domain (e.g., 'Did the physics answer correctly identify the boundary conditions?').

Step 2: Create the benchmark

Gather 7,000+ professional tasks and responses, each one graded by an expert using their rubric.

Step 3: Train fair AI judges

Build LLM judges that score each criterion. Detect if a judge favors one model brand over another (bias), then correct for it.

Step 4: Measure model bias

Calculate how much each judge's grade leans toward models from one company. Choose judges that grade fairly across all vendors.

Step 5: Score models cheaply

Use the bias-corrected judges to grade many LLMs on ProfBench without hiring experts for each one.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: LLM judges that measure bias

Before ProfBench, when researchers used LLMs as judges (to automate expert grading), they didn't check if the AI judge was fair. One judge might give higher grades to Anthropic's Claude, another to OpenAI's GPT, purely by quirk—not because one is actually better. ProfBench's move: build a way to measure judge bias (how far each judge leans toward each vendor), detect it, and either discard biased judges or correct them.

the object it moves  =  LLM judge bias measurement and correction
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Judge bias per vendor

measured quantity  =  How much an AI judge favors one vendor's model

Normally, a judge just outputs a score. ProfBench measures something invisible: the judge's bias. Bias is the average difference between how the judge scored a model and how a human expert scored the same model. If the judge gave a GPT-4 response 8/10 but a human expert gave it 6/10 on average across many tasks, the judge has a +2 bias toward GPT-4. If the judge gives Llama responses 4/10 but humans average 6/10, it has a −2 bias against Llama.

To compute it: for each model M, calculate (average AI-judge score on M) − (average human score on M). A judge with big positive bias toward one vendor is unreliable. A judge with balanced bias near zero is trustworthy. The paper defines a 'bias-index' as the span from the judge that favors one model most to the judge that favors it least—a high span means judges disagree about who's best.

Why it works

Why measuring and correcting judge bias actually works

The core insight: an AI judge is just another model, so it has implicit preferences. It might give higher grades to responses that match its own training style, or it might unconsciously favor the models it was shown most often during training. These biases are invisible unless you measure them directly. By comparing the AI judge's scores to human expert scores on the same tasks, you can see the distortion—it's mathematical, not opinion.

The mechanism works in two steps. First: use human-expert grades as the ground truth. For each judge and each vendor's models, calculate the average difference between AI score and human score. If a judge's scores for Model A average 8/10 but humans averaged 6/10, that judge has a stable +2 bias. Second: either discard judges with big biases, or normalize their scores to match human-expert averages. This turns an unfair judge into a fair one. The unfair judge becomes calibrated—its grades now track what a real expert would think.

The bias calculation in plain form

First in words:

For each model, the bias is the average grade from the AI judge minus the average grade from human experts on the same criterion. Then the bias-index is the difference between the judge that favors any model most and the judge that favors it least.

Then the compact form:

bias_index = max_model [ (1/N * sum_criteria (score_AI_judge[model,criterion] - score_human[model,criterion])) ] - min_model [ (1/N * sum_criteria (score_AI_judge[model,criterion] - score_human[model,criterion])) ]

Every symbol:

score_AI_judge[model,criterion]The grade (0 or 1) that the AI judge gave for this model on this criterion
score_human[model,criterion]The grade (0 or 1) that human experts gave for this model on the same criterion
NTotal number of criteria
bias_indexThe span from most-biased to least-biased judge, a sign of unfairness
A tiny worked example
Suppose Judge A grades three GPT-4 answers on Physics criteria: 1, 1, 1 (all correct per the judge). Humans graded them: 0, 1, 1. Judge A's bias = (1 + 1 + 1)/3 - (0 + 1 + 1)/3 = 1.0 - 0.67 = +0.33 (judge favors GPT).
Judge B grades the same three answers: 0, 1, 1 (matches humans). Bias = (0 + 1 + 1)/3 - (0 + 1 + 1)/3 = 0.67 - 0.67 = 0.0 (fair).
Judge C grades them: 1, 1, 0 (gives an easy criterion but docks another). Bias = (1 + 1 + 0)/3 - (0 + 1 + 1)/3 = 0.67 - 0.67 = 0.0 (also fair by average).
bias_index = 0.33 - 0.0 = 0.33. A span of 0.33 means judges have wide disagreement about fairness. Discard Judge A or recalibrate it.

Judge bias is the systematic tilt between what an AI judge thinks and what a human expert knows to be true, measured directly on shared data.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Judge bias might not be consistent across domains. A judge might be fair on Physics tasks but biased on Finance tasks. ProfBench measures aggregate bias, which might hide domain-specific distortions. Solution: compute bias separately per domain and discard judges that are fair in one domain but unfair in another.
Too many input words can overwhelm retrieval. The paper notes that when models retrieve documents for a task, they often pull in too many words (4–12 times the original document size). Excess words drown the signal because the model's context window (its working memory) fills with junk, leaving little room for reasoning. Solution: improve retrieval to fetch only the most relevant document excerpts, because fewer words allows the model to allocate more brain space to understanding and generating an accurate answer.
Rubric quality depends on the expert. A careless expert might write vague criteria like 'well-written'. Then no two judges (human or AI) will ever agree, and bias measurement becomes noise because the true disagreement is not vendor bias but rubric confusion. Solution: have a reviewer approve or improve each rubric before using it, because a crisp criterion like 'lists all atoms in the molecule' is graded consistently by any competent judge.
Done right. Use domain-specific experts, have a reviewer approve rubrics for clarity, measure judge bias per domain, discard outlier judges, and use only judges with bias-index near zero.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small benchmark of 20 professional tasks in one domain (e.g. Finance MBA). Have two human experts grade all 20 using a rubric. Pick two LLM judges (say, Claude and GPT-4). For each judge, on each task, calculate whether it agrees with the human graders (a binary 0/1 score). Across all 20 tasks, measure the judge's bias: average difference between LLM and human on each task. Plot the bias for both judges. The demo should show that one judge is more biased than the other—its average score diverges from the human consensus. Then, recalibrate the biased judge by subtracting its observed bias from all its future grades. The recalibrated judge should now align with human grades.

bias per judge = average of (judge grade − expert grade) across all tasks; unfairness = biggest bias − smallest bias

The demo makes it impossible to miss: bias is real (you can plot it), measurable (as the gap between judge and expert), and fixable (a simple adjustment restores accuracy), so once fixed, LLM judges become trustworthy partners for professional domain evaluation because their grades now reflect what domain experts actually think.
