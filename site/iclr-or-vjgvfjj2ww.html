EAMET: Robust Massive Model Editing

When you edit thousands of facts at once in an LLM, pieces of information point in different directions inside the model—EAMET fixes this mismatch so all edits hold together.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models store knowledge in their weights like a filing cabinet. When you need to update thousands of facts at once—say, correcting outdated information—a naive approach fails because different facts end up pointing in conflicting directions. EAMET solves this by aligning two separate internal spaces that the model uses to store information, so all the edits reinforce each other instead of colliding.

Zero assumption version

Why does editing thousands of facts at once break?

Knowledge in a language model lives in the weights (the numbers the model multiplies and adds with). When you want to update a single fact—like 'The CEO of Tesla is now X instead of Y'—you can find exactly which weights store 'Tesla' and 'CEO' and tweak them. This works fine for one fact.

But when you edit 10,000 facts at once, something goes wrong. The model's internal representation starts to crumble. Different facts, now encoded with different goals, begin pointing in separate directions. Some facts retrieve correctly, some forget old answers poorly, and the model becomes unreliable in complex settings.

The root cause: the model uses two separate internal spaces to organize information. One space (the 'key' space) points to where facts are stored. The other space (the 'residual' space) holds corrections to add on top. When you edit many facts, these two spaces drift apart—they no longer align. This mismatch cascades into failure.

In one sentence. EAMET ensures that when you edit thousands of facts, the two internal spaces the model uses to store information point the same direction, not against each other.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: extract the hidden states for each fact

For a fact like 'Jeep Commander is made by Jeep', feed the model a templated question and grab the raw numbers from mid-layer computation.

Step 2: compute the key space (where facts live)

Project those raw numbers into 'key' space and find how similar each fact's representation is to every other fact's.

Step 3: compute the residual space (the corrections)

Extract the 'residual' path (the add-on corrections) and measure its similarities the same way.

Step 4: measure the mismatch between the two spaces

Compare key-space similarities to residual-space similarities; if they differ, the spaces are misaligned.

Step 5: nudge both spaces into agreement

Update the weights to make both the key-space and residual-space similarities match, so all edits point the same way.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

EAMET changes a single thing: the weights in the feed-forward layers (the MLPs, the computational modules inside the model that transform information from one form to another). When you edit, EAMET does not edit weights blindly. It edits them in a coordinated way—ensuring that both the 'key' representation space and the 'residual' space that the model uses stay aligned as you make thousands of changes.

the object it moves  =  Feed-forward layer weights, coordinated
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: embedding space misalignment

measured quantity  =  Embedding space misalignment

Inside the model, there are two separate internal representations—two 'spaces' where information lives. One is the 'key' space (where the model looks up 'what fact is this?'), and the other is the 'residual' space (where corrections get added on top). When a single fact is edited, both spaces naturally align. But when 10,000 facts are edited together, each edit pushes the spaces in slightly different ways. Eventually they point in completely different directions.

EAMET measures this misalignment by computing cosine similarity (a number from 0 to 1 showing how much two directions agree) between every pair of facts in the key space, then doing the same for the residual space. If the two lists of similarity numbers differ, the spaces are misaligned. The paper reduces this misalignment by adding a loss term (a penalty during training) that forces the two sets of similarities to match.

Why it works

Why fixing alignment rescues massive editing

When you edit a weight, it changes how the model retrieves information. In a single-fact edit, changing a weight naturally updates both the 'where does this fact live' (key space) and 'what correction applies' (residual space) in the same direction. But with 10,000 facts, each edit pushes differently. One fact may need its key to move left, while the edit also moves residuals right—creating internal conflict.

EAMET solves this by making both spaces have the same internal structure. If the key space says 'Facts A and B are similar', the residual space must agree. This agreement acts like a constraint: it limits how wildly the weights can be changed without breaking one space or the other. With this shared structure in place, edits to thousands of facts become stable. When the model retrieves a fact, both the lookup (key space) and the correction (residual space) point to the same answer, so they reinforce instead of cancel.

The mechanism is alignment as a stability anchor. By forcing the two spaces to match before any individual fact update, EAMET ensures that scaling from 1 fact to 10,000 facts does not introduce cascading failures. Each edit lands in a space where the model's internal logic is consistent.

Math after the picture

The alignment loss that forces both spaces to match

First in words:

For each fact i, compute how similar its key embedding is to every other fact's key embedding, then compute the same similarities for residual embeddings. Penalize cases where a pair of facts is similar in key space but not in residual space, or vice versa. Use two penalties: one on the full distributions (KL divergence), one on the top M largest similarities (MSE loss).

Then the compact form:

L_align = KL(P_k^(i) || P_r^(i)) + MSE({P_k^(i,j)}_top_M, {P_r^(i,j)}_top_M)

Every symbol:

L_aligntotal penalty for misalignment
P_k^(i)distribution of how similar fact i's key embedding is to all other key embeddings
P_r^(i)distribution of how similar fact i's residual embedding is to all other residual embeddings
KL(...)divergence (penalty for differing distributions), ranges 0 to infinity
{...}_top_Mthe M largest similarity values from the distribution
MSE(...)mean squared error (sum of squared differences divided by count)
A tiny worked example with 3 facts
Key-space similarities for Fact A: [A-vs-A: 1.0, A-vs-B: 0.8, A-vs-C: 0.3]
Residual-space similarities for Fact A: [A-vs-A: 1.0, A-vs-B: 0.2, A-vs-C: 0.7]
Top-2 for key space: [1.0, 0.8]; Top-2 for residual: [1.0, 0.7]
MSE of top-2: (1.0-1.0)^2 + (0.8-0.7)^2 = 0 + 0.01 = 0.01
This penalty tells the optimizer: make residual's second-strongest similarity closer to key's second-strongest (0.7 -> 0.8).

The two loss terms together force the model to organize both key and residual spaces the same way, so when weights are edited, both internal paths agree on which facts are related and which are distinct.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Editing very deep layers fails harder than shallow layers. The paper finds that similarity among different facts' representations increases as you go deeper into the model. At layer 8, average similarity is nearly double that at layer 4. This means deeper edits have less 'separation' between facts—they blur into each other. EAMET still works but is less effective at very deep layers because there is less distinct structure to align.
Computing pairwise similarities scales poorly with number of facts. To align the spaces, EAMET must compare every fact against every other fact. For 10,000 facts, that is 100 million similarity values. This is expensive. Future work may need to sample similarities instead of computing all of them.
Complex factual relationships (A is related to B, which is related to C) are harder to edit. When facts form chains or loops of meaning, aligning the two spaces becomes ambiguous. The paper is measured in 'context-rich' settings but does not fully address scenarios where facts are deeply interdependent.
Done right: use alignment before editing. Align the key and residual spaces first (making them have matching internal structure), then update weights for the target facts. This order prevents the spaces from drifting during editing and is what lets you edit thousands of facts without the model becoming unstable or forgetting old answers.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model (or load a pretrained one like LLaMA-2-7B). Pick 100 factual statements: 'Paris is the capital of France', 'The Great Wall is in China', etc. Manually edit 10 of these facts to new (false) values in the weights using EAMET. Then test the model on 20 new questions about these facts: some ask directly about the edited facts, some ask about related facts, some ask about facts you did not edit. Measure how many direct edits stick, and whether editing a fact breaks nearby knowledge that was not edited.

efficacy = number of direct edits retrieved correctly / total direct edits; collateral_damage = number of unedited facts that now give wrong answers / total unedited facts

Run the same test WITHOUT alignment (naive editing) and with alignment (EAMET). EAMET's efficacy should stay above 85% and collateral damage below 20% even at 100 edits; naive editing should show efficacy dropping below 70% and collateral damage climbing above 40%.
