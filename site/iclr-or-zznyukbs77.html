Debugging Concept Bottleneck Models through Removal and Retraining

Convert expert feedback on which concepts are wrong into sample labels that fix the model's biased reasoning.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Concept Bottleneck Models let experts look inside and even steer a model at test time, but only if the model learned concepts the way an expert would. When data is biased (e.g., a bird classifier learns that waterbirds have water backgrounds), the model's concepts go haywire and expert steering fails. CBDebug fixes this by translating expert feedback from the concept level down to the data level, then reweighting and augmenting the training samples to teach the model the right concepts.

Zero assumption version

Expert steering breaks on biased shortcuts

A Concept Bottleneck Model (a type of interpretable model) works like this: an encoder reads an image and assigns strength scores to human-friendly concepts like waterside, feathers, wing shape. A small linear layer then reads those scores and predicts the label. Experts can watch this happen and even change the concept scores by hand at test time to steer the model.

But if the training data is biased — for example, a dataset where waterbirds are always photographed on water and landbirds on grass — the model takes a shortcut and learns waterside as the main way to separate the two. The encoder's waterside concept actually encodes background, not the bird itself. Now when an expert steps in and says 'ignore the waterside concept, focus on feathers,' the model still fails, because feathers never mattered for the original biased training. The model learned the wrong concept-to-label link.

Previous retraining methods fail here because they don't use what the expert already knows: which specific concepts are broken.

The fix: Treat each concept the expert marks as bad as a signal about which samples are tricky, then reweight and augment the data to push the model toward truthful concepts.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Encode and label

Run the broken model on all training images; collect concept scores; mark samples with high scores on bad concepts.

Step 2: Learn which samples are confounded

Train a small classifier that predicts whether a sample is confounded (background is the signal) or clean (real features matter).

Step 3: Reweight samples

Upweight clean samples and downweight confounded ones, so retraining sees mostly honest examples.

Step 4: Augment for coverage

Cut and mix parts of confounded images to create new examples where bad concepts are removed.

Step 5: Retrain the model

Fine-tune the concept extractor and the scoring layer on the reweighted and augmented dataset until it relies on true concepts.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

CBDebug converts expert feedback from the concept layer down to individual training samples. The expert says 'concept C is bad,' and CBDebug uses the model's own strength scores on C (how much the model activates that concept for each image) to label each sample as confounded or clean. This label becomes an auxiliary signal that guides reweighting and augmentation.

the object it moves  =  Concept feedback to sample labels
The hidden quantity

The confounder score

measured quantity  =  Confounder risk per sample

A confounder is something in the data that correlates with the label but is not a real feature (e.g., water backgrounds correlating with waterbird label). The paper estimates this risk per sample by running the trained model's encoder and measuring how strongly each sample activates the concepts the expert marked as bad. High activation on a bad concept means that sample is likely confounded.

CBDebug uses a learned classifier, eta, to convert those activation scores into a probability: what is the chance that this sample's label comes from a true feature versus a shortcut? Samples with high confounder risk are downweighted during retraining so they do not mislead the model.

Why it works

Why reweighting and augmentation fix the model

The root cause: during the original training, the model saw more confounded samples (bird with background) than clean samples (bird without background). The shortcut was the easiest path to memorize the labels. Reweighting flips this imbalance by increasing the weight on clean samples and decreasing the weight on confounded ones, so the algorithm sees mostly honest examples during retraining. Now the model must learn the real features because the fake shortcut path no longer leads to low error.

Augmentation (cutting and pasting image patches) removes the shortcut from confounded samples, forcing the model to extract other cues. Together, these two techniques act as a corrective bias: the model can no longer succeed by relying on backgrounds; it must rely on wings, feathers, and shapes. The expert's feedback bridges from human intuition (this concept is wrong) to machine-readable signals (these samples are unreliable) to algorithmic fixes (reweight and augment).

Math after the picture

The reweighting equation

First in words:

sample weight equals the odds that it is clean over confounded, based on the model's prediction

Then the compact form:

u_i = eta(y_i, v_i) / (1 - eta(y_i, v_i))

Every symbol:

u_iweight assigned to sample i, from 0 to infinity
eta(y_i, v_i)the model's estimated probability that sample i is from the clean set, given its label y_i and bad-concept activations v_i
v_ithe activation scores of bad concepts on sample i
y_ithe true label of sample i
A tiny worked example
Sample A: waterbird with water background. Model predicts eta = 0.2 (80% chance it is confounded). Weight = 0.2 / 0.8 = 0.25. Downweighted.
Sample B: waterbird, no water background. Model predicts eta = 0.9 (90% chance it is clean). Weight = 0.9 / 0.1 = 9.0. Upweighted.
Same image feature, but clean sample gets 36 times higher weight. Retraining sees mostly clean examples, so it learns the real pattern.

The weight is an odds ratio: higher values mean the model trusts that sample's label came from a real feature, not a shortcut.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Expert marks the wrong concepts as bad. If the expert misdiagnoses and removes a concept the model actually needs, retraining will hurt performance on that feature. The method trusts expert labels entirely.
The bad concepts are deeply mixed with good ones. If background and bird features are tangled in the same concept representation, removing one damages the other. Concept-level intervention alone cannot disentangle them.
The confounder score classifier mislabels samples. If eta predicts confoundedness poorly, reweighting will upweight misleading samples and downweight honest ones, making performance worse.
Done right: iterative human feedback. Experts watch the retraining results, verify that concepts have become more honest, and repeat removal if a concept still encodes shortcuts.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple image classifier on a dataset with a known shortcut (e.g., a bird dataset where a particular species always appears with a specific background, or a pneumonia detector trained mostly on images from one hospital). Train a concept bottleneck model, identify the spurious concept, run CBDebug with expert feedback marking that concept as bad, and measure whether balanced accuracy across groups improves after retraining. This works because reweighting changes which samples the model learns from, and augmentation removes the fake signal from confounded images, so the model has no choice but to learn the real pattern.

Balanced accuracy = (accuracy on group A + accuracy on group B) / 2, comparing before and after CBDebug

The metric must show that the model stops exploiting the shortcut and learns to handle both groups equally, even though retraining only saw expert feedback at the concept level.
