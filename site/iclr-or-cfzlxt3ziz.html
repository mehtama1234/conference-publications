FIRE: Balancing Stability and Plasticity in Continual Learning

FIRE finds the sweet spot for restarting weights when learning new tasks, by measuring how far they drifted and how tangled they became.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When a neural network (a system of interconnected math operations) learns task after task on changing data, it faces a tension: save the old knowledge (stability) or make room to learn new things (plasticity). Most reinitialization methods—rewinding weights to their starting values—are too crude: timid resets fail to free up learning power, aggressive ones erase what the network already knows. FIRE proposes a balanced reinitialize that measures two quantities at once: how far the weights have strayed from start (using a metric called Squared Frobenius Error) and how warped their directions have become (using Deviation from Isometry). It solves for the reinitialize point that minimizes drift while keeping directions honest.

Zero assumption version

The stability-plasticity trap

A network trained on task A learns patterns by adjusting millions of numbers (called weights). When task B arrives—with new data, new questions—the network must adapt. But here is the trap: every change to any weight risks erasing patterns learned from task A. If you leave the weights alone, task B suffers. If you rewind them too aggressively toward their original values, task A deteriorates.

The naive approach: reset every weight toward its starting value, hoping to restart learning capacity. But which direction? By how much? Conservative resets fail to free plasticity; aggressive ones crater old-task accuracy. There is no principled way to dial it in.

The problem in one sentence. Standard weight resets are guesswork: tune them too gently and new learning stalls; tune them hard and old knowledge vanishes.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure drift

After learning task A, compute how far each weight has moved from its start.

Measure tangling

Check whether the weight directions are stretched or rotated (isometry means honest length; loss means twist).

Set the constraint

Solve for a reset point that shrinks drift while forcing direction-honesty back to zero twist.

Execute efficiently

Use a fast iteration (Newton-Schulz) to find that point without running many epochs.

Start task B

Train on new data from the reset position. Old knowledge stays; plasticity is unblocked.

What the paper changes

The one thing being changed

FIRE picks a better reinitialize point for the weights—not a blind reset to start, but a targeted intermediate position that jointly satisfies two requirements, because solving for both drift and twist at once blocks the usual tradeoff of losing old memory or strangling new learning.

the object it moves  =  weights (at reinitialization)
The hidden quantity

Two hidden quantities FIRE measures

measured quantity  =  Drift (Squared Frobenius Error) and Twist (Deviation from Isometry)

After training on task A, every weight has moved from its starting value. Drift (or Squared Frobenius Error) is the sum of how far each moved—a single number measuring total distance traveled. A small drift means weights did not wander; a big drift means they reorganized heavily.

Twist (or Deviation from Isometry) measures whether weight matrices have been stretched, squashed, or rotated in weird ways during training. Isometry means 'length-preserving'—if the weights stay honest in their shape and angles, twist is zero. Real training warps them. FIRE measures that warp, and aims to erase it by the reinitialization point. Drift and twist are two separate problems, and FIRE solves them together—shrink drift while forcing twist to zero.

Why it works

Why it works

Standard reinitialization does one thing: nudge weights toward their starting point. That is one-dimensional. FIRE solves a two-dimensional problem.

When you shrink drift alone, the weights spiral back toward start, but their internal angles and scales—the directions between them—gets tangled. That tangles task B training, because tangled weight directions make learning signals noisy and unreliable. If you restore angles and scales (isometry) without shrinking drift, weights stay far from start and look 'corrupted' to task B's learning signals. FIRE finds the sweet spot: the reinitialize point at which both drift and twist vanish together. This works because the two quantities live in separate mathematical spaces—one captures magnitude (how far), the other captures shape (how bent). By solving the constrained problem 'minimize drift while setting twist to zero,' FIRE decouples the two stressors and balances them exactly.

The practical win: because both conditions are met at the reinitialization, task A memory is maximally preserved (drift is as small as it can be given the twist constraint), and task B learns fast (directions are honest, so learning steps point clearly toward lower error). Neither stability nor plasticity is sacrificed; both are optimized jointly.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

minimize the squared difference between current weights and original weights, subject to the constraint that the weight matrix has zero isometry deviation

Then the compact form:

minimize SFE(W) = ||W - W_init||_F^2 subject to DfI(W) = 0

Every symbol:

Wcurrent weight matrix after training task A
W_initoriginal weights at the start of training
||·||_FFrobenius norm (the root of the sum of squared matrix elements)
SFESquared Frobenius Error, measuring how far weights have drifted
DfIDeviation from Isometry, measuring how tangled directions have become
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight matrix (a grid of numbers) started as [2.0, 0.5; 1.0, 3.0] and after task A training is [2.5, 1.2; 0.8, 3.5].
Drift (SFE) = (2.5−2.0)^2 + (1.2−0.5)^2 + (0.8−1.0)^2 + (3.5−3.0)^2 = 0.25 + 0.49 + 0.04 + 0.25 = 1.03.
Twist (DfI) = how much the matrix's row and column lengths and angles deviated from staying honest (computed via singular values; here suppose it is 0.8).
FIRE solves: find a reset point W* between W_init and current W such that ||W* − W_init||_F^2 is minimized AND DfI(W*) = 0.
Newton-Schulz iteration steps toward that W* efficiently. The reset point trades: it may not recover all the way to W_init, but it gains clean angles and scales, freeing task B.

The equation trades off: smaller drift means staying closer to start (preserving task A knowledge), but the constraint forces angles and scales to be honest (enabling task B to learn with clean signals). The solution balances both at once.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Isometry is not always the right target for all tasks. Some tasks may benefit from weight angles and scales that are intentionally warped or stretched. FIRE enforces zero twist universally; it may be too strict for domains where non-uniform stretching helps learning, because the constraint ignores task-specific shape that might be useful.
Newton-Schulz iteration converges slowly on some matrices. If weights are very ill-conditioned (some directions much smaller than others), the iteration may need many steps, adding compute cost.
The constrained problem is not unique when drift is zero. If the network has already learned an isometric configuration, FIRE has no constraint to solve and may stall. This is rare but possible in very small models.
Done right: FIRE shines when continual tasks accumulate. On vision and language continual-learning benchmarks where weights drift a lot and angles naturally stay honest during good learning, FIRE outperforms naive training and single-tuning-dial resets, because it solves two separate problems (drift and twist) instead of guessing one compromise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small network (e.g., ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10) and train it on two sequential tasks: class-split-A (classes 0–4) then class-split-B (classes 5–9). At the boundary, measure old-task accuracy before and after reinitialization, then train on the new task and measure new-task accuracy. Compare three strategies: no reset (baseline), linear interpolation reset (reset toward start by a hand-tuned fraction), and FIRE's computed reset. Plot accuracy on both tasks after task B training.

stability_loss = task_A_acc_before − task_A_acc_after; plasticity_gain = task_B_acc_after; tradeoff_score = (task_A_acc_after + task_B_acc_after) / 2

FIRE should score highest on tradeoff_score, meaning it preserves task A better than aggressive resets (which erase early learning by resetting too hard) and enables task B learning better than timid ones (which fail to free plasticity by barely moving), because solving both drift and twist together beats hand-tuning a single compromise.
