DECO: Decoupled Multimodal Diffusion for Bimanual Robot Hands

A robot-control system that learns bimanual manipulation by treating vision, touch, and joint feedback as separate instruction channels that guide a denoising process.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot with two hands to perform complex tasks like picking, placing, and rotating objects requires the robot to understand what it sees, what it feels touching the object, and where its joints are positioned. DECO learns all these at once by using a diffusion transformer — a model that starts with random motion and gradually sharpens it into the right action — but keeps each type of sensory input (visual, tactile, proprioceptive) in its own separate pathway instead of forcing them into one shared stream.

Zero assumption version

Robots struggle when multiple senses must agree

A robot hand picking up a glass needs to see where the glass is (vision), feel the contact and pressure as fingers touch it (tactile sensing), and know exactly where its own joints are pointing (proprioception). Training systems must combine all three types of information at the same time.

A naive approach mixes all three streams into a single channel — concatenate all the numbers together and feed them into the model. This fails because each modality has a different structure: an image is a 2D grid of pixel colors, touch is contact pressure at specific finger locations, and joint positions are just angles. Forcing them into one bag loses which signal came from where, and the model wastes capacity figuring out how to separate them again.

The method in one sentence. Keep each sensor stream in its own processing pathway, then combine their outputs only at the decision point.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Separate the streams

Vision goes to a vision decoder, touch to a touch decoder, joint angles to a proprioception decoder.

2. Start with random noise

The diffusion process begins with a random motion sequence, not a blank slate.

3. Condition each pathway

Each decoder tells the denoising step: 'vision says reach left', 'touch says grip softer', 'joints say straighten up'.

4. Merge at the denoiser

The central denoising step listens to all three channels and picks the motion that satisfies them together.

5. Iterate to clarity

Repeat the denoising 20-50 times until the motion is specific and executable.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The object is the architecture of how sensor information flows through the model. Instead of concatenating all inputs into one fat list of numbers (called a vector, just a sequence of values) at the start, DECO routes each modality through its own specialized encoder-decoder pathway, keeping them decoupled until the very last step where they vote on the final action.

the object it moves  =  sensor pathway routing
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: modality coherence

measured quantity  =  modality coherence

Modality coherence is how well each sense contributes to a single, unified action without drowning out the others. If vision alone predicts motion and touch is ignored, coherence is low (one sense dominates). If all three senses together predict the same action with confidence, coherence is high.

The paper measures this indirectly by observing task success — when all three modalities are kept separate until fusion, each one trains on its own clear signal instead of a muddled mix. The touch decoder learns pure contact patterns, the vision decoder learns pure visual cues, and the central fusion step learns when to trust each one.

Why it works

Why decoupling the streams makes the model learn better

Learning to control a robot from multiple sensors is a noisy problem. Vision pixels contain shadows and reflections that do not matter; touch has noise from electrical sensors; joint angles drift slightly. If you mash all three into one list from the start, the model's learning algorithm must figure out which numbers came from which modality, and noise from one corrupts the learning signal (the nudge direction that tells weights which way to adjust) for the other.

By keeping the three pathways separate, each decoder specializes: the vision decoder learns to ignore shadows, the touch decoder learns to filter electrical noise, and the joint decoder learns its own stability tricks. When they merge at the final decision step, they meet clean, so the central model can simply learn the rule: 'if vision says left and touch agrees, move left; if they disagree, trust touch because the object is small'.

The lightweight adapter for touch works the same way — it injects new tactile signals into an already-trained model. Because the touch pathway was kept separate, the adapter can nudge just those weights without retraining everything. This is like adding a specialist advisor to a meeting that already has other experts; the new advisor does not corrupt the others' expertise.

Math after the picture

The denoising equation

First in words:

The model predicts the clean motion by removing noise, guided by each sensor pathway. Each pathway contributes a weight that says how much to trust it in this moment.

Then the compact form:

motion_clean = motion_noisy + sum_k ( w_k * guidance_k ) where w_k is the trust weight for modality k (vision, touch, joints)

Every symbol:

motion_cleanthe robot action we want to execute next
motion_noisythe random or partially-denoised motion from the previous step
guidance_kthe direction and strength each modality suggests (vision says move left 0.3, touch says grip 0.8, etc.)
w_khow much to believe this modality right now (learned during training)
kindex over the three modalities: vision, touch, proprioception
A tiny worked example
Start with noisy motion: [wrist tilt 0.2, grip 0.5, fingers spread 0.1]
Vision guidance: move left 0.8, w_vision = 0.6, contributes 0.6 * 0.8 = 0.48
Touch guidance: grip tighter 0.9, w_touch = 0.7, contributes 0.7 * 0.9 = 0.63
Joints guidance: straighten arm 0.5, w_joints = 0.4, contributes 0.4 * 0.5 = 0.20
Clean motion = [0.2, 0.5, 0.1] + [0.48 left, 0.63 tighter, 0.20 straight] = robot moves left, grips hard, and straightens

Each pathway votes on the action with a weight learned from past successes, so they cooperate instead of fight.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pathways learn incompatible goals. If vision is trained on tall objects and touch is trained on small objects, their guidance vectors point different directions. The model must learn a gating weight to switch between them — if that fails, the action wavers.
One modality is missing at test time. If the model learned to fuse all three and touch suddenly has no signal, the touch weights become useless. DECO must be retrained or use a graceful fallback.
The lightweight adapter overfits to new touch data. Adding a small adapter to inject touch into a pretrained model works only if you have enough new touch data. With too little, the adapter learns noise instead of signal.
Done right. Test the system on held-out objects and gripper styles; ensure each modality is present in training; validate the adapter on diverse contact types before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple two-finger gripper on a robot arm. Train on 10 reach-and-grasp tasks with vision, proprioception, and force feedback from the fingers. First, train a baseline that concatenates all three input streams at the start. Then train DECO with separate pathways. Record the success rate and the variance (how often does each method succeed on repeated tries?) for each task.

success_rate = tasks_succeeded / total_tasks_attempted; coherence = fraction of tasks where all three modalities agree on the direction (motion matches all three guidance vectors)

The demo must show that DECO succeeds more often and that removing any one modality at test time hurts DECO less than the baseline, proving the pathways learned independent, reusable skills.
