Optimizing through simulation and search

Using AI to explore enormous design spaces—finding the best drug or molecular design—by combining learning with search and reinforcement learning techniques.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Drug discovery, materials design, and protein engineering all pose the same underlying problem: find the best solution in a gigantic search space. Does this change to a protein make it more stable? Does this new alloy conduct electricity better because of different atomic packing? Does this drug candidate hit the target and avoid side effects? Traditionally, chemists narrow the search space using chemical intuition, make a few candidates, and test them. But what if there is a much better solution that no human would think to try, because it requires a combination of properties that contradicts human intuition?

The space of possibilities is so enormous that brute-force testing is impossible. There are more possible small molecules with drug-like properties than atoms in the universe. Even if we train a model to predict drug activity, we still face the combinatorial explosion: which of a billion candidates should we actually synthesize and test? And how do we explore regions of the space that have never been tried, where the model is uncertain?

This cluster combines learning with search. Some use reinforcement learning — a technique where the AI learns to make decisions that maximize a reward (binding strength, stability, low toxicity) by trial and error. Others use active learning: generate many candidates, test a small number that the model is most uncertain about, and then retrain the model with the new data, focusing the search. Some interleave simulation and learning: run expensive physics simulations on the most promising candidates and use those exact results to refine the model. The common strategy is that the model is not trying to predict every property perfectly — it is trying to narrow down the search to the most promising few candidates, so that expensive testing focuses on winners.

How the work breaks down

The work in this subtheme splits into five natural directions. First, multi-agent frameworks where teams of AI agents reason together to solve complex molecular and clinical problems, because different expertise is needed to evaluate molecules that must be both effective and manufactureable. Second, active learning systems that intelligently choose which candidates to test, building models more quickly with fewer experiments, because expensive testing budgets make random exploration wasteful. Third, reinforcement learning approaches where AI agents learn by trial and error to design better molecules and proteins, because the reward function (binding strength, stability) can be shaped to optimize whatever outcome matters most. Fourth, search and evolution strategies that explore large design spaces efficiently by iteratively refining promising candidates, because evolutionary pressure focuses exploration on regions that have already shown promise. Fifth, simulation-grounded methods that combine physics simulations with learning to find designs that actually work in the real world, not just theoretically, because predictions made by simple models often fail when put into actual chemistry.

Multi-agent reasoning for molecular and clinical design

One challenge in drug and protein design is that the decisions are interconnected—choosing the right molecular structure depends on understanding chemistry, biology, and synthesis constraints all at once. Single models tend to miss these connections. The papers in this group tackle this by building teams of AI agents that reason together, each bringing different expertise to the same problem.

The AI-for-Science Low-code Platform paper builds a framework where multiple agents work together on earth science tasks, with each agent checking the others' work to catch errors before they compound. The multi-agent chemical reaction system papers explain each step—rather than just saying "this reaction works," it walks through why the conditions are right, giving chemists something they can trust and learn from. The language agents for clinical decisions paper shows how to train a team of agents to make diagnoses step by step, testing hypotheses and asking the right questions before settling on a treatment. TusoAI extends this to arbitrary scientific tasks, showing that agent collaboration beats specialist models on diverse problems from genetics to materials. The MAC-AMP system coordinates multiple agents to design antimicrobial peptides that hit multiple goals at once: the molecule must kill bacteria, avoid hurting human cells, and actually be possible to make.

Active learning and intelligent sampling

Testing candidates is expensive—you cannot try a billion molecules. The problem is that your model is least confident about the regions that might hold breakthroughs, because nobody has tested them yet. Active learning solves this by having the model predict not just which candidate is best, but which candidates it is most uncertain about. You test those uncertain candidates, get real data, retrain the model, and search gets sharper with each cycle.

PepCompass navigates peptide space using Riemannian distance metrics (a way to measure similarity between peptides that accounts for how they actually fold) to find the most uncertain unexplored regions. It achieved 100% validation when actually testing peptides it designed in the lab. BioBO adds biology rules to the active learning loop—if you know that certain genes control a pathway, you design perturbations that test that pathway directly, cutting the number of experiments needed by 25-40%. LABO takes the approach further by combining language models to propose candidate families with Bayesian optimization to pick which family member to test next, beating fixed optimization budgets. Target-Aware Bandit Allocation tackles the case where you have a billion molecules but can only afford to screen a tiny fraction by learning which regions are most likely to have good solutions, letting you optimize even when the library is impossibly large.

Reinforcement learning for molecular and protein design

Reinforcement learning trains an AI agent by rewarding it when it makes good designs and penalizing bad ones. The agent learns a policy—a procedure for building molecules or proteins piece by piece—that maximizes total reward. This works well for design because you can define reward however you want: binding strength, stability, toxicity, or even manufacturability.

The Unified Biomolecular Trajectory paper generates protein dynamics that reproduce what happens in physics simulations, then uses reinforcement learning to refine designs by having the model learn from trajectories that match real molecular behavior. CocoRNA uses cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning where agents design RNA structures together, with policies that generalize to new structures without retraining. DePO shows that seeding reinforcement learning with a few human-designed examples beats pure learning, hitting 13% improvement over standard fine-tuning on molecular optimization tasks. FIDIA grounds the reward function in real sequence function—you are not just optimizing binding, you are optimizing for designs that actually fold correctly and maintain their function. LithoGRPO applies reinforcement learning with flow matching to semiconductor mask design, speeding up the evaluation loop 130 times by learning to predict manufacturability.

Search and evolutionary strategies for design spaces

Large design spaces are too big to search directly. Evolutionary approaches use a trick from nature: keep the best candidates from each generation, mutate them to create variation, and repeat. This works well when you can evaluate candidates quickly (or via a learned model) because iterating many times becomes feasible, and when you do not need the absolute best solution, just something much better than what humans would try, because local optimization can beat global optimization if you start in a good region.

MATHMO discovers Pareto frontiers of models by adaptively searching over model structure and weight settings (the numbers that control how the model behaves), balancing different objectives like accuracy and interpretability, because real applications need to trade off competing goals. The genetic algorithm paper applies classic evolutionary methods to molecular design, mixing synthesis rules with Bayesian optimization to find candidates that are both good and makeable, because integrating chemical constraints reduces wasted effort on designs you cannot build. Deliberate Evolution uses language models to propose variations instead of random mutations, cutting the sample budget to 40% of standard evolutionary search while maintaining quality, because intelligent mutation beats random perturbation when you have structure to learn. DeltaEvolve applies momentum to evolution—if a direction kept producing good candidates last round, explore it more aggressively next round—cutting computational cost (measured in processing steps) across diverse scientific domains, because staying in promising regions longer beats equal exploration everywhere. LLEMA combines language-guided suggestions with evolutionary search to design materials, achieving higher hit rates and better Pareto fronts than pure generation or pure search, because language models capture domain intuition while search handles the combinatorial explosion. Principle-Evolvable Scientific Discovery minimizes uncertainty across the search trajectory, keeping all promising regions open instead of prematurely committing to one solution, because early convergence trades away future discovery. RetrOrchestrator orchestrates multiple retrosynthesis models dynamically, picking which model to use for each step based on confidence, reaching 94% success on real synthesis planning benchmarks, because different models have different strengths and you should use each where it is most reliable.

Simulation-grounded design and refinement

The real test of any design is whether it works in the actual world, subject to real physics and chemistry. Simulation bridges the gap: run expensive physics calculations on the most promising candidates to get exact energies and forces, then use those ground-truth results to train the model further. This grounds learning in reality and avoids the trap of optimizing an approximate model that never translates to real molecules.

SYNC learns to predict not just binding strength but synthesizability—how hard the molecule is to make—and then guides generation to prefer highly bindable, highly synthesizable designs without compromising either. Elign aligns diffusion models to follow physics simulations by generating conformations that lower DFT energies while improving stability; running the model stays as fast as unguided sampling because the model learns the alignment during training, not by adding extra computational steps at test time. Testing and refinement for protein ensembles uses real experimental validation data (X-ray and NMR) to guide the model toward structures that match what nature actually produces, beating computational estimates. Sim2Reason applies physics simulators to train reinforcement learning agents on hard reasoning problems (International Physics Olympiad), then shows the learned policies transfer zero-shot to new problems—evidence that the agent learned physics principles, not just Internet facts.
