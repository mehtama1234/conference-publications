Cross-Embodied Co-Design for Dexterous Hands

Design and control robot hands together by training one control policy that works across many hand shapes, then search for the best shape.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot hands are usually designed first, then a control policy is trained on that fixed design. But optimal control for a task often requires a hand shape tailored to that task. This paper trains a single control policy that works across many different hand shapes at once, then uses that policy to quickly evaluate which hand shape is best. This avoids months of iterative design-retrain cycles because each new candidate hand can be evaluated in minutes (using the pre-trained policy) instead of days (retraining from scratch), enabling end-to-end design from CAD to real robot in under 24 hours.

Zero assumption version

Why hand design and control are stuck in a loop

Robot hands are built one way: an engineer designs the hand first (how many fingers, how many joints per finger, what material), then a roboticist trains a control policy on that fixed hand. The problem is that the best hand shape depends on what task you want to do. A hand optimized for spinning a coin looks different from one optimized for precision assembly. But if you want to find the best hand for a task, you have to design a hand, train a policy on it, test it, and repeat—this takes months.

A naive approach would be to try many hand designs and train a separate control policy for each one. But that multiplies the work: if you have 1,000 possible hand designs and training takes a day per design, you need 1,000 days. Even with parallelization, the search space of morphologies (finger count, joint angles, finger placement, palm size) is huge and training is slow.

The method in one sentence. Train one control policy that works on many hand shapes simultaneously using a morphology-conditioned encoder, then use that policy to rapidly evaluate candidate hand designs without retraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Generate hand shapes by the rules

Randomly sample hand parameters (finger count, joints, palm size and shape, finger placement) subject to real manufacturing constraints—no impossible designs.

Encode each hand shape into a descriptor

Use a graph neural network (a learnable encoder that reads the hand's connectivity structure—which fingers connect where) to turn each morphology into a fixed-size list of numbers that captures the hand's structure.

Train one policy on all hand shapes together

Pre-train a control policy on 2,000–8,000 hand samples, grouped by family (symmetric vs asymmetric). The policy is told which hand it is controlling (via the encoded descriptor) and learns a unified strategy.

Evaluate candidate hands using the pre-trained policy

During co-design search, generate a new hand, encode it, pass it to the pre-trained policy, and score how well it performs on the task. No retraining needed.

Search and manufacture the best hand

Use heuristic search (Graph Heuristic Search) to iteratively propose and evaluate candidate hands, keeping the best one. Fabricate the winning design with modular off-the-shelf components.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of training a separate control policy for each hand design, the paper changes the policy training process to condition on morphology: one policy learns to control many different hand shapes by receiving the hand's shape descriptor as input.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: morphology-conditioned action

measured quantity  =  How well the policy adapts to each morphology

The hidden quantity is how well the policy can generalize its control strategy across different hand shapes without retraining. This is measured implicitly by the performance of the pre-trained policy when it is applied to new, unseen hand designs during the search phase. A good generalization means the pre-trained policy performs well on new shapes; poor generalization means it fails on shapes it has not seen before.

The paper estimates this by tracking task performance (e.g., rotation angle achieved) of the pre-trained policy on each candidate hand during search. Hands that perform well with the pre-trained policy are more likely to be good designs overall.

Why it works

Why it works: the morphology-conditioned encoder

The bottleneck in hand design search is that each candidate hand requires days of policy training to evaluate. The paper breaks this bottleneck by separating the search problem into two phases.

Phase 1 (Pre-training): Train one control policy on a large, diverse set of hand shapes—group similar hands together by their kinematic structure (symmetric vs asymmetric, different finger counts) and train the policy to handle all of them. The policy is given the hand's structure as input (encoded by a graph neural network that reads the connectivity of joints and fingers). This teaches the policy a general strategy: given any hand shape, produce reasonable actions.

Phase 2 (Search): Now, when you propose a new candidate hand, you do not retrain anything. Instead, you encode the new hand and pass it to the pre-trained policy. The policy already knows how to control that type of structure, so it can generate actions immediately. Test the hand on the task, record the score, and move on. This is minutes per hand instead of days.

Why does this work? Because hands with similar structures (same finger count, similar size and shape) often benefit from similar control strategies. By grouping and pre-training on families of hands, the policy learns generalizable principles rather than memorizing one hand. The graph encoding ensures that structurally isomorphic hands (e.g., two 4-finger hands with different sizes) are treated as variations on the same theme.

Math after the picture

The policy objective equation

First in words:

The policy learns to maximize the expected sum of future rewards, conditioned on the hand morphology. For a given hand structure G and policy pi, the objective is the expected cumulative reward over time.

Then the compact form:

J(π, G) = E_τ [sum_t γ^t R(s_t, a_t) | G]

Every symbol:

J(π, G)The expected total reward for policy π on morphology G
E_τExpected value over all possible sequences of states and actions
sum_tSum over all time steps in an episode
γDiscount factor (0 to 1): how much we care about future reward versus immediate reward
R(s_t, a_t)The reward at time t for being in state s_t and taking action a_t
s_tThe current state (joint angles, positions, sensor readings of the hand)
a_tThe action (command to motors) at time t
GThe hand morphology (structure and geometry)
A tiny worked example
A hand rotates a coin for 10 steps. At each step it receives reward R = 0 (no progress) or R = 1 (moved coin 1 degree closer).
Discount factor gamma = 0.9 (we care less about far-future reward). Steps: R = [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1].
J = 1 × 0.9^0 + 1 × 0.9^1 + 1 × 0.9^2 + 0 × 0.9^3 + 0 × 0.9^4 + 1 × 0.9^5 + 1 × 0.9^6 + 0 × 0.9^7 + 1 × 0.9^8 + 1 × 0.9^9
= 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + 0 + 0 + 0.59 + 0.53 + 0 + 0.43 + 0.39 = 5.65 total discounted reward.
The policy for this hand morphology learns actions that maximize this sum. A different morphology might achieve J = 4.2, so the first morphology is better for this task.

The policy is conditioned on the hand shape (told which morphology it is controlling), so the same policy learns to generalize across many morphologies by receiving different hand-shape descriptors, and we can evaluate performance for new hand shapes without retraining the policy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor generalization to very different morphologies. If a new candidate hand is structurally very different from those in the pre-training set (e.g., a 6-finger hand when pre-training only had 3–5), the pre-trained policy may not adapt well and the evaluation will be unreliable. The method groups hands into families (symmetric, asymmetric) to mitigate this, but outlier designs can still confuse the policy.
Manufacturing constraints not captured by the model. The method uses real CAD specifications and collision geometries to avoid obviously impossible designs, but subtle manufacturing issues (vibration, play in joints, material fatigue) only show up on the real hand. A hand that scores well in simulation might fail in the lab due to assembly errors.
Heuristic search may miss the global optimum. Graph Heuristic Search uses a value network to guide candidate proposal, but heuristics are greedy. It is possible that the search gets stuck in a local optimum and never proposes the truly best hand design, especially early in the search when the value network is poorly trained.
Done right. Pre-train on a diverse set of hands within a kinematic family, validate the policy on held-out designs before co-design, test the top-5 ranked hands on real hardware, and engineer the grammar to exclude physically impossible designs.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show the three stages: (1) generate and visualize 20 random hand morphologies satisfying the grammar constraints; (2) run the pre-trained policy on each and collect task scores (e.g., in-hand rotation angle); (3) perform Graph Heuristic Search to propose 10 new candidate hands and show that the score improves with each proposal. The demo should make clear that no retraining happens during steps (2) and (3)—only encoding the hand structure and running the policy forward—so each evaluation completes in seconds or minutes, not hours or days.

design_search_speedup = (time to evaluate 100 hands with separate policies) / (time to evaluate 100 hands with co-design policy)

The metric must show at least 100-times speedup in design evaluation (days per hand becomes minutes per hand) when using the morphology-conditioned policy, proving that pre-training generalizes and avoids redundant training.
