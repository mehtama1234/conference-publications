Generalizable Coarse-to-Fine Robot Manipulation via Language-Aligned 3D Keypoints

A robot learns complex tasks by breaking them into step-by-step language instructions, each pointing to where the gripper should go next, using a language model fine-tuned on real 3D scenes.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots today struggle to adapt to new objects or new instructions even with pre-trained models. This paper proposes CLAP, which splits each manipulation task into steps—each step is a written instruction aligned with a specific 3D location (a keypoint). A language model learns to read the current scene, predict the next step instruction and keypoint, then a separate module converts that keypoint into precise gripper movements. Training on just 10 demonstrations, it generalizes to unseen objects and new task variations.

Zero assumption version

Why robots fail at new objects and new tasks

Today, robots learn manipulation (picking and placing objects) from human demonstrations. A robot watches a person pick up a cup and place it on a table, or push a block to a target. The robot then builds a policy, a decision-making rule, that predicts: given the current camera view and the goal instruction, what should the gripper do next?

The naive approach: train one big neural network to predict the final gripper action directly from the image and instruction. This often fails because the task is too hard to do all at once—the robot overshoots, grasps the wrong part, or misaligns with the target. Even if you give the network pre-trained models (vision models trained on millions of images), it still crashes on new objects or new wordings of the task.

Why? Because pre-trained models are tuned for different scenes, and the leap from reading a picture to commanding a gripper's exact 3D position is enormous.

The paper's move: Break each task into a sequence of language descriptions (steps), each aligned with a specific 3D location on the object. Train a language model to predict both the next step description and its 3D target, then a separate module turns that target into precise gripper commands.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard coarse-to-fine policies predict where to look (a 3D region) and then how to act. CLAP adds language-aligned keypoints: each 3D location is now paired with a written step description. The language model is fine-tuned to predict both together from the scene.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: step-instruction alignment

measured quantity  =  How well each keypoint matches its step instruction

The paper measures an invisible quantity: alignment. For each predicted 3D keypoint, how well does it correspond to the step instruction the language model just generated? A keypoint that matches the instruction means the model is reasoning compositionally—it understands that to grasp the handle, it needs to point to the handle, not the body of the cup.

The paper estimates this by fine-tuning the language model on trajectories where the human-labeled step and the 3D target are both known. During training, the model learns to predict step instructions that make sense in the context, and keypoints that make sense for that step. The fine-tuning pipeline uses a sequential reasoning procedure: first predict the task plan (all steps), then for each step generate the instruction and keypoint.

Why it works

Why step-language alignment helps robots learn faster and generalize

Language naturally breaks tasks into reusable pieces. When you say 'grasp the handle', you implicitly define a location. By forcing the model to predict both the instruction and the location together, you tie the language to a real place in the scene. This makes the model reason about objects and their parts, not just memorize camera images, because during training the model learns that the word 'handle' always pairs with a specific 3D region, so it extracts handle-ness as a concept rather than pixel patterns.

Normally, a robot learns a monolithic policy: entire camera view maps to entire action. That's brittle—if the object moves or the lighting changes, the camera pixels look different and the learned mapping breaks. But if the robot reasons in language-aligned steps, it can track objects by name (handle, body, table edge) and reuse those concepts across new objects. A handle is a handle whether it's on a red cup or a blue cup.

The fine-tuning pipeline reinforces this. The language model first plans all steps (thinking through the whole task), then predicts each step and keypoint in sequence (tying words to 3D spots). Training on this multi-part loss means the model learns object semantics from the text and task structure from the step decomposition. On an unseen object, the model already knows what a grasp, a lift, and a placement are in language, so it can transfer those concepts immediately.

The fine-tuning objective

First in words:

Total loss = loss of step prediction + loss of keypoint prediction + loss of task plan generation. The model is rewarded when the predicted step instruction makes sense for the image, the keypoint is close to the correct target, and the full task plan covers all steps.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_plan(L_pred, L_true) + L_step(ell_pred, ell_true) + L_keypoint(p_pred, p_true)

Every symbol:

L_planloss when predicted task plan differs from ground truth
L_steploss when predicted step instruction differs from ground truth
L_keypointloss when predicted 3D keypoint differs from ground truth target
L_pred, ell_pred, p_predmodel predictions (plan, step, keypoint)
L_true, ell_true, p_trueground truth from human demonstrations
A tiny worked example
Suppose the true task plan is: [grasp-handle, lift, move-to-table, release].
The model predicts: [grasp-handle, lift-from-side, move-to-table, release]. L_plan loss = difference in sequence.
For step 2, true instruction is lift, model predicts lift-from-side. L_step loss = 0.3 (similar but not exact).
True keypoint for step 2 is [handle, y=50cm], model predicts [body, y=48cm]. L_keypoint loss = 0.8 (location is close but object part is wrong).
Total loss = 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.8 = 1.3. Training nudges the model to predict cleaner steps and better-aligned keypoints.

The multi-part loss forces the model to align language (step instruction) with 3D locations, so both the concept and its real-world grounding improve together, because a mismatch in any one part—wrong step, wrong keypoint, or wrong plan—increases the loss, pushing the model to fix all three simultaneously.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor task decomposition. If the human labels steps badly (e.g., grouping grasp and lift into one step when they should be separate), the model learns confused alignments. The paper's fine-tuning helps, but garbage labels stay garbage, because the multi-part loss only works if the ground-truth step label and keypoint are both correct—if the label is wrong, the model tries to align nonsense with a location.
Language model does not ground to 3D. If the pre-trained language model is weak at 3D reasoning, fine-tuning alone may not fix it. The paper mitigates this by using a strong VLM pre-trained on diverse images, but the base model matters.
Keypoint outside the object. If predicted keypoints fall in empty space (e.g., behind the object), the action module has no valid target and the gripper hangs.
Done right. With clear step labels, a capable base model, and proper fine-tuning validation, the policy transfers cleanly to new objects because it reasons in language concepts that are grounded in 3D.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a robot or simulation with a gripper. Collect 10 demonstrations of a task (e.g., pick a red cup and place it on a table). Decompose each into steps with labels (grasp, lift, place). Fine-tune a language model on those demos to predict step and keypoint together. Then test on a blue cup or a green cup (same task, new object) and a new phrasing (put the cup over there instead of on the table). Measure success rate: what fraction of trials end with the cup on the table?

Generalization success rate = (trials that completed the task on new objects) / (total trials on new objects), in percent.

The critical test: trained on only red-cup demos, does it grasp and place a blue cup? If yes, the model is reasoning in language-grounded space, not memorizing pixels. The paper shows this holds even with just 10 demonstrations per task.
