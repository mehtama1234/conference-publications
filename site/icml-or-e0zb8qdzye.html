CoSpec: Cross-City Road Representation via Spectral Alignment

When learning road networks across cities, split the graph signal into shareable low-frequency patterns and city-specific high-frequency details to avoid the spectral mismatch that breaks standard methods.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Cities have different street layouts: Manhattan's grid looks nothing like Boston's winding streets. When you try to teach a single model to understand all road networks, it fails because the graph structure varies wildly city-to-city. This paper identifies the real culprit: the Fourier spectrum (the composition of slow-changing and fast-changing patterns in the street graph) differs sharply across cities. CoSpec fixes this by separating the universal slow patterns from the local fast details, so the model can learn once and work everywhere.

Why standard graph models fail across cities

A road network is a graph: intersections are nodes, streets are edges. A graph neural network learns by looking at each node and its neighbors, and gradually updating what it knows based on the neighborhood patterns. This works well inside one city.

But cities differ fundamentally. Manhattan's grid has 4-way intersections; Venice has canals; Tokyo has winding alleys. When you feed all three cities' road graphs to one model, the node-neighbor patterns are incompatible. A node with 4 neighbors in one city is rare or meaningless in another. The model sees contradictory lessons and fails.

A beginner might try: use a standard graph neural network and just mix data from all cities together. This breaks because the model learns weights assuming a consistent neighborhood structure, but the structure keeps changing.

In one sentence. CoSpec separates each city's road graph into universally-shareable slow patterns (which highways connect to highways everywhere) and city-specific fast details (which street layout quirks are unique to that place).

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decompose the graph signal

Split road features into low-frequency (slow, city-agnostic) and high-frequency (fast, city-specific).

Align low frequencies across cities

Use learned prototypes to match highway-like patterns everywhere, forcing the model to recognize universal road type (highway, arterial, local) the same way in every city.

Modulate high frequencies per city

Let each city adjust the fast-varying residual signal to fit its own street layout and local patterns.

Combine with dual-path network

Run two processing paths in parallel: one stabilizing shared semantics, one capturing local details.

Measure how similar the cities now are

Use Wasserstein distance (a metric for how far apart two data patterns are) to confirm cities are now compatible, not contradictory.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

CoSpec modifies the graph signal itself: instead of feeding the raw road network directly to a single model, it first decomposes the signal into slow and fast components (low-frequency and high-frequency Fourier modes). The slow component is processed via one path of the network (shared across cities) and the fast component via another (city-specific). The network learns to combine them intelligently, adapting its behavior per city while reusing the global lessons.

The hidden quantity

Spectral misalignment: the quantity at the root

measured quantity  =  Spectral pattern mismatch across cities

The hidden quantity is the Fourier spectrum (the distribution of slow vs. fast-varying patterns) in each city's road graph. A road network has a spectrum just like sound: the spectrum tells you how much slow change (like the overall grid orientation) versus fast change (like local twists and turns) is present.

Normally, the model learns weights assuming the spectrum is consistent. When it sees Manhattan's spectrum (highly regular, low-frequency dominant) and then Boston's spectrum (irregular, high-frequency rich), the learned weights no longer make sense. CoSpec measures this mismatch and forces the model to separate the frequency bands before learning, so each band gets its own aligned treatment.

Why it works

Why splitting by frequency works

Here is the mechanical reason it works. A road network in any city serves the same high-level function: connect origins to destinations efficiently. That function (the low-frequency signal) is universal. But each city implements it differently: some use grids, others winding alleys. Those details (the high-frequency signal) are specific.

When you train on mixed city data without splitting, the model must choose weights that work for both signals at once. It cannot: a weight that captures Manhattan's grid inevitably misses Boston's curves. By splitting first, you let the low-frequency path learn the universal function (highway connects to highway; major roads have stable properties) independently of the high-frequency path. The high-frequency path then learns the local residuals (the deviations from the universal pattern). When you combine them, the model has learned both the global lesson and the local variation, so it transfers to new cities: given a new city's road graph, the shared low-frequency path immediately recognizes the highway structure, and the high-frequency path adapts to the street layout.

The Wasserstein distance bound ensures that after alignment, the two city distributions are mathematically compatible, meaning the learned weights will not contradict themselves on new data.

Math after the picture

The decomposition and alignment equation

First in words:

Total loss equals (reconstruction error on low frequencies plus alignment error on low frequencies) plus (reconstruction error on high frequencies, weighted per city) plus a regularizer keeping the two paths cooperative.

Then the compact form:

L = ||s_low - s_low_pred||^2 + Wasserstein(dist_low_city1, dist_low_city2) + sum_c w_c * ||s_high_c - s_high_pred_c||^2 + lambda * KL(pred_combined || true)

Every symbol:

s_lowthe low-frequency signal (slow patterns shared across cities)
s_low_predthe model's prediction of low-frequency signal
dist_low_city1, dist_low_city2the distribution of low-frequency components in city 1 and city 2
Wasserstein(...)distance between two distributions, how different they are
s_high_cthe high-frequency signal specific to city c
s_high_pred_cthe model's prediction of city c's high-frequency signal
w_cweight adjusting how much the model focuses on city c
pred_combinedthe model's full prediction combining low and high
truethe actual observed signal
lambdaa knob controlling how much the two paths should agree
KL(...)divergence (a measure of how different two probability distributions are)
A tiny worked example
Manhattan low-frequency vs. Boston low-frequency: Wasserstein distance 0.8 (fairly different).
After alignment: Wasserstein distance drops to 0.2 (much more compatible).
Manhattan-specific high-freq residual prediction error: (actual residual 5.2) vs. (predicted 5.0) = 0.04 squared.
Boston-specific high-freq residual prediction error: (actual residual 3.1) vs. (predicted 3.3) = 0.04 squared.
Combined loss for this training example: (low reconstruction 0.01) + (low alignment 0.2) + (0.5 * 0.04 + 0.5 * 0.04) + (lambda * 0.15) = roughly 0.35 per example, improved from 1.2 without splitting.

The equation shows why splitting works: aligning low frequencies (the Wasserstein term) is a separate objective, allowing the model to learn universal patterns without being dragged into city-specific contradictions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incorrect frequency split. If the cutoff between low and high frequencies is set wrong (too high or too low), the model might put city-specific details into the shared path or vice versa, breaking both transfer and local accuracy.
Prototype initialization. If the adaptive prototypes (the learned templates for low-frequency patterns) start far from the true shared semantics, the alignment step may converge slowly or to a poor local solution, defeating the purpose.
Imbalanced city data. If one city has far more training samples than another, the high-frequency path will overfit to that city, and the low-frequency path will be biased by its spectrum, reducing cross-city fairness.
Done right. CoSpec avoids these by using learned frequency cutoffs (not fixed), warm-starting prototypes from the pooled data, and per-city weighting to balance training signals.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy example with three synthetic road graphs: one a perfect grid (Manhattan-like), one a spiral (Rome-like), one a random walk (organic growth). Train a standard graph neural network on grids and spirals mixed, measure its accuracy on each city separately and on a held-out random-walk city. Then train CoSpec on the same data and measure again. The demo should show: (1) standard model's test accuracy on the grid and spiral drops compared to the training accuracy (overfitting to one city), (2) standard model's accuracy on the random-walk city is very low (poor transfer), (3) CoSpec's accuracy on all three cities is high and stable (successful separation and transfer).
