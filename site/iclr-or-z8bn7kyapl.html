RAVEN: End-to-end Equivariant Robot Learning with RGB Cameras

A robot learns to grasp and move objects from RGB camera images alone by learning to rotate and shift predictions the same way the world rotates and shifts.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching robots to manipulate objects usually requires either expensive structured sensors, like cameras pointing straight down, or point clouds (precise 3D coordinates). RAVEN shows that robots can learn from plain RGB images—the video feed you would see on a cheap webcam—if you teach the model to understand how images change when the robot and objects rotate or move in 3D space. This cuts costs because you can now use any camera angle, and it works in changing environments because the model generalizes to new viewpoints without retraining.

Why robots cannot learn from cheap cameras

Most robot learning systems that work well require one of two things: either a camera mounted directly above the scene looking straight down (a top-down view), or sensors that measure the exact 3D coordinates of every object (point clouds). These setups work because the system knows in advance that the input will always look the same from that fixed angle. But real robot tasks happen in kitchens, warehouses, and homes where you cannot bolt cameras to the ceiling. If you move the camera even a few inches, the entire input looks different, and the robot forgets everything it learned.

A naive attempt: take a regular robot learning system, point a camera from the side, and collect data. What happens? When the robot learns to grasp a cup from the left, it memorizes: 'pixels in this region mean cup, move gripper to that location.' But if you rotate the view 10 degrees, the pixel pattern changes completely. The robot has no idea that it is still the same cup. Every new camera angle is a brand-new problem. You would need to collect separate training data for every possible viewing angle—thousands of cameras and hours of human demonstrations.

In one sentence. RAVEN learns a policy that automatically flips and rotates its predictions whenever the camera angle flips or rotates, so it works from any viewpoint.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Interpret image as rays

Each pixel is a ray cast from the camera into the 3D scene.

Build an SE(3)-aware encoder

A neural network (a learned pattern-detector) processes rays in a way that respects 3D rotations and shifts: if the world rotates, the detector's internal state rotates the same way.

Decode to robot action

Another network reads that state and predicts the next robot move: which direction to push, how wide to open the gripper, or how far to reach.

Action rotates with the camera

If the camera view rotates, the predicted action automatically rotates too—no extra logic needed.

Train on multi-view data

Collect trajectories (video clips of successes) from different camera angles. The model learns that the same task looks different from each angle, but the same rotation-pattern solves it everywhere.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which inputs the model uses: from structured, pre-processed data (like point clouds or top-down images) to raw RGB camera images. Instead of throwing away information (compressing a 3D scene into a single bird's-eye view), the model keeps the full RGB view and learns how that view transforms when the world rotates.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Equivariance strength

measured quantity  =  Equivariance strength

Equivariance strength measures how much the model's internal predictions rotate and shift in sync with the camera angle. A model with perfect equivariance: if the camera rotates 45 degrees, every prediction also rotates 45 degrees. A model with zero equivariance: camera rotates, predictions stay frozen. RAVEN's learned encoder is designed to maximize this quantity—every layer enforces that rotations in, rotations out.

The paper estimates equivariance strength by testing the model on the same task from many camera angles and measuring how well predictions align across views. If a grasp from the left camera matches (when rotated) a grasp from the right camera, equivariance is high. The paper also uses ablations: remove equivariance constraints from the architecture and measure performance drop.

Why it works

Why it works: Rotation-aware learning

A ray in 3D space—a line from the camera through a pixel into the scene—transforms predictably when you rotate or translate the camera. A 2D pixel changes whenever the view angle changes, but a ray's direction in 3D space rotates in the same rigid way that the world rotates. RAVEN uses this: it builds a neural network encoder that treats image data as rays and processes them in a way that respects 3D rotations. Mathematically, this means the encoder is 'equivariant to SE(3) transformations'—a fancy way of saying 'rotations in become rotations out.'

Here is the payoff: Suppose the model learns a grasp from one camera angle: 'push down and close fingers.' If the camera rotates 90 degrees clockwise, the internal representation of the scene also rotates 90 degrees (because rays rotate). The encoder then outputs a grasp direction that is also rotated 90 degrees—still down and close fingers, but in the new frame of reference. The robot does the right action. No relearning needed. One learned policy works from any angle, because the model is built to understand that when the camera rotates, the entire scene rotates together with it.

This costs much less than collecting data from every possible viewing angle, because during training the model sees episodes from only a handful of camera positions but automatically works on unseen angles. The ray-based representation and the equivariant architecture prevent overfitting to one viewpoint—when you change the viewpoint, the inputs and outputs change in a predictable, symmetric way that the model has learned to handle. So the model generalizes to new angles without extra data, because the equivariance constraint forces the model to learn patterns that stay the same across all rotations, instead of memorizing details tied to one camera angle.

The equivariance constraint

First in words:

If you rotate the camera by an amount R, and the model's feature representation changes from F to F-rotated, and the predicted action changes from A to A-rotated, then A-rotated must equal R applied to A (the rotation 'commutes' with the model).

Then the compact form:

phi(R * x) = R * phi(x)

Every symbol:

phi(x)the model (encoder + decoder) applied to input x
Ra 3D rotation (a matrix that rotates points in space)
R * xthe input rotated by R
R * phi(x)the output rotated by R
A tiny worked example
Input: camera at 0 degrees, scene shows cup at position (1, 0, 0) to the right.
Model output: grasp action = 'move right 5 units, close gripper.'
Rotate everything 90 degrees counterclockwise: cup is now 'up', camera rotated 90 degrees.
Input rotated: camera now sees cup above, rays rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise.
Model output on rotated input: 'move up 5 units, close gripper' (rotated from 'right' to 'up').
Check: rotating the original output 90 degrees gives 'move up 5 units' — it matches. Equivariance holds.

The equation says: rotate your input, apply the model, and you should get the same result as applying the model first and then rotating the output.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Occlusions break the assumption. If one part of the robot or object blocks the camera's view of another part, the ray representation breaks—you cannot know what is hidden. Equivariance assumes full observability.
Non-rigid objects are harder. A cup holds its shape (rigid), so rotations work. A cloth deforms, and 'rotate the input' does not capture cloth crumpling. The method works best on rigid bodies.
Lighting changes trip up RGB encoding. If you rotate the camera, shadows and reflections move. The ray-based encoder learns from pixels, so drastic lighting changes can still confuse the model.
Done right. Train on 3-4 diverse camera angles and test equivariance on unseen angles in the same lighting. Use rigid objects. The model will learn to grasp from any viewpoint without retraining.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a robot arm with a gripper and a cheap RGB camera. Train RAVEN on grasping data collected from camera angles 0, 90, 180, 270 degrees around the table. Then test on angles 45 and 135 degrees (never seen before). Measure grasp success rate on each unseen angle. Also measure a baseline model (standard CNN, no equivariance constraints) on the same angles to show the gap.

Generalization gap = grasp success at unseen angle with RAVEN minus grasp success at unseen angle with baseline model

The demo must show that RAVEN's grasp success stays above 80% on unseen camera angles, while the baseline drops to below 40%.
