Decoding Environment State to Pick Better Visual Representations for Robots

Instead of training robot policies to test if a visual encoder is good, predict whether the encoder can decode the full state of the environment from images—a much faster test that correlates with policy success.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot policies need visual encoders (models that convert images to useful numbers) that capture what matters in the environment. Testing encoders by training full robot policies is slow and expensive. This paper shows a shortcut: measure whether an encoder can predict the complete state of the environment (object positions, shapes, materials, lighting) from images. When an encoder can decode full state accurately, it contains all the information the policy needs to decide what to do. Testing state-decoding takes minutes (train a small prediction head on simulated data with ground truth), while testing a full policy takes weeks (train the entire system). Because state-decoding ranking correlates with policy success, you get the same insights 100 times faster.

The encoder-testing bottleneck

A robot policy is a decision-maker: given an image, it says what to do next. The quality of that policy depends heavily on the visual encoder—the front piece that turns raw pixels into numbers the policy can understand. A naive approach: test each encoder by training a policy on top of it and running that policy in the real world or simulation. If the policy succeeds at grasping, placing, or stacking, the encoder is good.

But this is slow. Training a full policy takes hours or days, even in simulation. If you have 20 candidate encoders, that is weeks of compute. Also, an encoder might work well for one task but fail for another—generalization is hard to predict. Existing shortcuts (like measuring how well an encoder segments objects or estimates depth) focus on one narrow skill and miss the full picture of what the environment looks like.

In one sentence. Predict whether an encoder can decode the complete state of the environment (positions, shapes, materials, lighting) from images—if it can, the encoder will make good policies.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Pick an encoder to test

Take a pretrained visual encoder (ResNet, a patch-based image model like ViT, CLIP backbone, etc.).

Step 2: Hook a prediction head onto the encoder

Attach a small network that reads the encoder's output and predicts every aspect of the environment: object positions, bounding boxes, shapes, materials, lighting, joint angles.

Step 3: Train the head in simulation with ground truth

Feed images from simulator with access to true state. Train the head to predict that true state. Measure prediction error (how wrong the guesses are).

Step 4: Average prediction accuracy across all state dimensions

Combine position accuracy, shape accuracy, material accuracy, etc., into one score for this encoder.

Step 5: Rank encoders by score

Compare scores across all candidate encoders. The encoder that decodes state best also trains the best policies.

What the paper changes

What changes: the state-prediction head

The paper adds a small neural-network head (a few layers) to the end of each pretrained visual encoder. This head reads the encoder's output and predicts environment state. The encoder itself does not change; only the head is trained. This is fast because you are not retraining the expensive encoder.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how much environment information the encoder captures

measured quantity  =  state-decoding accuracy

The paper measures one concrete quantity: the accuracy of predicting environment state from the encoder's representation. This is the fraction of state variables (object positions, shapes, materials, etc.) that the head predicts correctly. High accuracy means the encoder is already capturing the information needed to describe the full scene.

The paper estimates this by training the state-prediction head in simulation (where ground truth is free) and measuring prediction error on held-out test images. A normalized score combines accuracy across all state dimensions—positions, shapes, materials, lighting, joint angles.

Why it works

Why this ranking predicts policy success

A robot policy makes decisions by reading encoded images. When an encoder captures the true state of the world—where things are, what shape they are, what they are made of—it provides the policy with information it needs to make good choices. A policy trying to grasp an object needs to know where the object is, what shape it is, and whether it is already being held. An encoder that misses this information forces the policy to guess, wasting learning cycles and making mistakes.

State-decoding accuracy is a direct measure of how much of the world the encoder captures. An encoder that can reconstruct object positions, shapes, materials, and lighting from its internal representation is encoding the information the policy will need. This is faster to test than training a policy because you only train the small prediction head, not the full policy. The correlation holds across different environments and learning algorithms because the underlying principle is the same: if an encoder can output all the facts about the environment, the policy will have the facts it needs to decide what to do.

The state-prediction loss

First in words:

For each state variable, compute prediction error (squared difference for continuous values like position, cross-entropy for categories like material type). Average all errors into a single score. Normalize so 1.0 means perfect prediction, 0.0 means random guessing.

Then the compact form:

score = 1 - (mean_squared_error_positions + cross_entropy_shapes + cross_entropy_materials + ... ) / normalizer

Every symbol:

scoreDecoding accuracy (0 to 1, higher is better)
mean_squared_error_positionsAverage squared difference between predicted and true object positions
cross_entropy_shapesPrediction error for object shape category (cat vs mug vs block)
cross_entropy_materialsPrediction error for material type (wood vs plastic vs metal)
normalizerConstant to rescale errors to 0-1 range; chosen so worst baseline gives score 0
A tiny worked example: three state dimensions
Suppose an encoder must predict three things: object X position, object shape (3 categories), material (3 categories).
Position error: true=50, predicted=52, squared error = (50-52)^2 = 4.0
Shape error: true=mug, predicted=cat, cross-entropy = 0.8 (wrong category is penalized)
Material error: true=plastic, predicted=plastic, cross-entropy = 0.0 (correct)
Average error = (4.0 + 0.8 + 0.0) / 3 = 1.6
If normalizer = 3.0 (worst-case error), then score = 1 - (1.6 / 3.0) = 1 - 0.53 = 0.47
Interpreting the score: this encoder gets state right just under 50% of the time; a different encoder with score 0.85 is much better and will likely train a better policy.

The score aggregates prediction accuracy across all environment dimensions, so an encoder strong at predicting positions but weak at shapes gets a middle score. This prevents one strong dimension from hiding a weak one that might matter for the policy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Task-specific encoders may not transfer. An encoder trained on internet-scale egocentric human video (like R3M) might decode state well in simulation but still struggle on real robots. State decoding in simulation is not a guarantee of real-world performance, only a proxy for simulation-to-simulation ranking.
Simulation-to-real domain gap. The evaluation uses simulation with perfect ground truth. Real images have lighting variation, blur, and occlusion that the simulated state-prediction head never sees, so the ranking might shift.
Hidden task-relevant state is not in the proxy. The state-prediction head predicts position, shape, material, lighting. If the policy also needs to predict whether a surface is slippery or how an object will roll, the proxy misses that dimension, and ranking can become less reliable.
Done right: multi-environment validation. Test the encoding quality across several different simulation environments (different object sets, lighting conditions, clutter levels) so the ranking holds across environment-specific variations and is not just lucky for one scenario.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a test setup in simulation: (1) pick 3-5 pretrained visual encoders (at least one general-purpose like ResNet, at least one robotics-specific like R3M); (2) for each encoder, attach a state-prediction head and train it on a dataset of simulation images with ground-truth state (100-1000 image-state pairs); (3) measure state-decoding accuracy on a held-out test set of images; (4) separately, train a robot policy (behavior cloning) for each encoder on the same environment; (5) measure final policy success rate on a holdout test set of trajectories or rollouts.

correlation = Pearson correlation between [encoder state-decoding scores] and [encoder policy success rates]

If correlation > 0.85, the proxy is reliable. A lower correlation (0.6-0.85) means the proxy is useful for rough ranking but not bulletproof; a correlation < 0.6 means the proxy is not predictive for this task.
