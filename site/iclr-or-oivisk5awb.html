Compose and Fuse: Revisiting the Foundational Bottlenecks in Multimodal Reasoning

Show that multimodal reasoning often fails not because models miss the facts, but because they do not separate fact recognition from logical combination and they fuse modalities too early.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Reports on multimodal large language models have been inconsistent. Sometimes extra modalities help reasoning because they provide an independent path to the answer when one source is weak. Sometimes they hurt it because the model mixes recognition and logical combination in one pass and lets one modality dominate too early. This paper argues that the confusion comes from mixing different kinds of tasks under one label. The authors build a controlled evaluation where facts are distributed across modalities in specific logical patterns, then inspect what the models actually do. Their conclusion is sharp: many models can recognize facts inside each modality, and they can reason from text alone, but they struggle when they must first recognize facts and then combine them across modalities in one pass. They also show that early fusion can inject bias, so one modality can dominate before the model has decided which evidence is actually useful.

Zero assumption version

Why adding modalities can make reasoning worse instead of better

A multimodal model must solve at least two subproblems. First it must recognize facts from each input source, such as a word in text, an object in an image, or a cue in audio. Then it must combine those facts according to the task logic. Many evaluations only report the final answer, so they do not reveal whether the model failed at recognition, logical combination, or evidence fusion.

This paper shows that extra modalities help only in a specific case: when they provide independent and sufficient paths to the answer, because one path can compensate when another path is noisy. When one modality depends on another in a chain, or when modalities contain conflicting or redundant cues, performance often drops. That means the bottleneck is not raw perception alone. It is the composition process that turns recognized facts into a joint conclusion.

The paper's move. Separate reasoning tasks by how facts are distributed across modalities, then test whether models can first recognize the right facts and only after that combine them.

Construct controlled logic tasks

Build evaluation cases where the answer depends on specific logical combinations of facts across modalities.

Measure recognition separately

Ask whether the model can identify the relevant fact in each modality before testing full reasoning.

Measure joint reasoning

Ask the model to solve the full multimodal logic task and compare this with its isolated recognition ability.

Check whether early layers keep modality identity and whether attention, which is the weighting rule that decides which earlier features each position reads, focuses on useful facts or only on dominant modalities.

Use two-step prompting and softened early fusion to see which failure mode each fix addresses.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper mainly changes how multimodal reasoning is evaluated and interpreted. Instead of treating every multimodal benchmark result as one number, it decomposes performance into recognition, composition, and fusion behavior. That reveals two separate bottlenecks that were previously mixed together.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: fact usefulness

measured quantity  =  Whether the model knows which recognized facts should enter the reasoning step

A model can notice several facts and still fail to reason correctly if it does not know which facts matter for the current question. The hidden quantity here is usefulness selection: after recognition, can the model keep the relevant facts active and ignore irrelevant or biased cues while it computes the final answer?

The authors show that attention patterns do not reliably encode this usefulness signal. Early layers still preserve modality identity, which means the system knows whether a cue came from text, image, or audio, but that identity can itself become a bias. If one modality is generally stronger, the model can over-trust it even when another modality carries the decisive fact, because the shared representation has already been pulled toward the stronger source.

Why it works

Why this works: split the failure into composition and fusion

The paper's diagnosis works because it isolates two different operations that had been conflated. Task composition asks the model to recognize modality-specific facts and then apply logic over those facts. If the prompt asks for both at once, the reasoning trace can collapse because the model has to decide what to extract and how to combine it in a single forward pass. Two-step prompting helps because it externalizes the intermediate facts, so the second step reasons over an explicit fact list instead of over raw mixed inputs.

The fusion bottleneck is different. Even before reasoning begins, representations from different modalities are mixed. If this happens too aggressively in early layers, a strong modality can bias the shared representation before the model has inspected whether that modality contains the needed evidence. Softening early fusion helps because it preserves modality-specific evidence longer, which gives later layers a cleaner chance to compare and combine facts rather than inheriting one modality's bias.

The controlled task taxonomy matters because it reveals when extra modalities should help at all. If two modalities independently contain enough information to solve the task, an additional path can rescue errors from one modality. If the answer requires chained entailment across weak cues, the model has more chances to fail because every missing step breaks the chain.

Math after the picture

A simple decomposition of reasoning success

First in words:

Final task success depends on both fact recognition and fact combination. If either stage fails, the final answer fails even when the other stage is strong.

Then the compact form:

success = recognition_quality * composition_quality

Every symbol:

successprobability of solving the full multimodal reasoning task
recognition_qualityhow often the model extracts the needed facts from each modality
composition_qualityhow often the model combines the extracted facts correctly
A tiny worked example
Suppose a task needs one fact from text and one fact from an image.
If the model recognizes the needed facts 90 percent of the time but combines them correctly only 50 percent of the time, full-task success is 0.9 times 0.5 = 0.45.
If two-step prompting raises composition quality from 0.5 to 0.8 while recognition stays at 0.9, full-task success rises to 0.72.
This explains why a model can look strong on isolated recognition probes but still fail many full multimodal reasoning cases.

The paper uses richer analyses than this scalar product, but the equation captures the main point: good perception does not guarantee good multimodal reasoning.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The controlled tasks may still miss real-world messiness. A clean logic taxonomy is useful for diagnosis, but open-ended multimodal tasks can include ambiguity, weak supervision, and noisy relevance signals that the benchmark does not fully reproduce.
Two-step prompting can propagate early extraction errors. If the recognition step writes down the wrong fact, the reasoning step may confidently reason over that wrong fact because the intermediate list now looks authoritative.
Softening fusion too much can delay necessary interaction. Some tasks need early cross-modal alignment, such as matching a caption phrase to a visual region. If fusion is postponed too aggressively, the model may fail to connect cues that should interact early.
Done right. Use the taxonomy to diagnose where the model fails, then choose the fix that matches that failure, because recognition errors need better fact extraction support while fusion errors need a cleaner separation of modalities before joint reasoning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a benchmark with synthetic but controlled multimodal logic tasks. Include at least three settings: independent evidence paths, chained evidence paths, and conflicting evidence paths. Run a multimodal model in one-pass mode and in two-step mode where it first lists facts and then reasons over them.

Also vary the amount of early fusion in the architecture or prompt setup if the system permits it. The demo should show that the best intervention depends on the failure source, because two-step prompting targets the recognize-then-combine bottleneck while fusion control targets modality dominance and conflict.
