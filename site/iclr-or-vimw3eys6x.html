Dual-Space Smoothness for Robust LLM Unlearning

To safely forget harmful information from a language model without breaking its other abilities, smooth out the model's weights in two separate ways at once: in the hidden layers and in the stored numbers.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Language models sometimes need to forget things — a user asks to remove their data, or a company wants the model to stop regurgitating copyrighted books. But when you try to erase that knowledge, the model often suffers catastrophic forgetting: it stops being able to do other tasks altogether. Meanwhile, a determined attacker can still jailbreak the model with small tricks, or retrain the erased knowledge in just a few steps. This paper proposes PRISM, which works by smoothing the model in two places at once: in the hidden-layer representations (using an adversarially trained classifier) and in the weight parameters themselves (by decoupling conflicting changes). The result is a model that truly forgets the target information while staying useful and harder for attackers to trick back into remembering.

Zero assumption version

Why forgetting is hard

When you have a language model trained on public data, sometimes you need to remove specific knowledge. A user asks you to delete their private information. A publisher demands you stop using their copyrighted text. So you take the model and try to unlearn: you show it the forget-set (the data to remove) and nudge it to give wrong answers to those inputs, while keeping it accurate on everything else (the retain-set). But this creates two traps. First, the forget objective and retain objective pull in opposite directions: if you push hard on forgetting, the model's general performance collapses — it forgets the forget-set, yes, but it also stops understanding basic language. Second, if an attacker perturbs the model's hidden layers slightly or retrains it, they can bring back the erased knowledge in seconds.

A naive approach tries to optimize both objectives equally. But the math creates a tug-of-war: the forget objective and the retain objective pull the weights in opposite directions. Suppose a weight helps both tasks. If you nudge it to erase harmful knowledge, the weight moves in a way that harms benign output quality, because both use that same weight. If you nudge it to keep utility, you move away from the erasing goal. One weight cannot satisfy both. Existing methods swing too far one way or the other, creating an irreducible trade-off.

In one sentence. Make the model forget harmful data without breaking its ability to answer benign questions, and make it so resistant that attackers cannot easily undo the forgetting.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Train a guard

A small classifier learns to recognize when hidden layers are being attacked or corrupted.

Step 2: Smooth in representation space

Use adversarial training to make the hidden layers hard to fool; a small nudge by an attacker changes nothing.

Step 3: Decouple conflicting objectives

Split the forget and retain objectives so they update different weights in different directions.

Step 4: Smooth in weight space

Apply a sharpness-minimization technique; weights settle into a flat region where small changes have tiny effects.

Step 5: Test against attacks

Verify the model truly forgot, retains utility, and cannot be tricked back into remembering.

What the paper changes

The single thing the paper changes

This paper does not change what data the model sees (the datasets stay the same). Instead, it changes how the optimization process treats conflicting objectives and where the final weights end up. By separating the conflicting update directions during the optimization step and applying sharpness minimization, the paper ensures that the final weights sit in a flat, calm region instead of a sharp peak where small nudges would make the erased knowledge spring back to life.

the object it moves  =  The final position of weights (the region they occupy)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how far you must push to undo the unlearning

measured quantity  =  The push distance to reactivation

The paper measures something invisible: how far an attacker must push the weights before the erased knowledge comes back. On a sharp peak, a tiny push (a single training step) can bring back harmful outputs. On a flat, calm plateau, even a big push does almost nothing. PRISM estimates this by looking at how the forget-set loss changes when you nudge the weights by small amounts. It then adjusts the unlearning process to move weights into a wide, calm region where perturbations have tiny effects.

The paper uses two approaches to measure and enforce this: (1) in the hidden layer space, by training a small classifier on perturbed hidden states and measuring how big a perturbation it needs to fool the classifier, and (2) in the weight space, by using a sharpness-minimizing technique that explicitly keeps slopes small while still achieving the forgetting goal.

Why it works

Why dual-space smoothing stops attacks

PRISM works by addressing two distinct attack vectors: jailbreaks (small changes to the hidden layer representations that trick the model) and relearning (retraining the model to recover the erased knowledge). A single defense in one space is not enough. If you only smooth the weights, a jailbreak can still manipulate the hidden layers. If you only smooth the hidden layers, an attacker can still retrain the weights.

The mechanism: First, the probe (trained with adversarial perturbations) learns a decision boundary in the hidden-layer space that is hard to fool. When the unlearning process updates the weights, it is guided by this probe's loss — it must move weights in a direction that keeps the hidden representations safe from small tricks. This is the representation-space smoothing. Second, the decoupling step separates the forget and retain update directions so they no longer fight each other for the same weights. Third, sharpness minimization (a min-max optimization) pushes the weights into a flat, calm region. In a flat region, an attacker trying to reactivate the erased knowledge would need to move far in weight space — the slope is nearly zero everywhere, so small training steps achieve little. Together, these three moves make the model harder to fool in both ways: jailbreaks fail because the hidden layers are hard to trick, and relearning fails because the weights are in a calm region.

Math after the picture

The min-max equation

First in words:

Total cost equals (loss on forgotten information plus smoothness penalty) minus (worst-case weight perturbation times forget loss). In words: find weights with low forget-loss and high local flatness, hard to trick to perturbations up to some radius epsilon.

Then the compact form:

min_theta [ L_f(theta) + lambda * SAM(theta) ] subject to hard to trickness against ||delta|| <= epsilon perturbations in weight and representation space

Every symbol:

thetathe model's stored numbers (weights)
L_f(theta)loss on the forget-set (how wrong the model is on data to erase)
SAM(theta)Sharpness-Aware Minimization score (how flat the slopes are in the region around the weights)
lambdahow much to value flatness versus forgetting (the weight on smoothness)
epsilonhow large a perturbation to consider when checking hard to trickness
deltaa small perturbation, added to weights or representations by an attacker
A tiny worked example
Suppose two weights: w1 controls harmful outputs, w2 controls helpful outputs.
After unlearning with PRISM, forget-set loss = 0.1 (model gives mostly wrong answers on the to-erase data).
Slope at w1: how much the forget-set loss changes if you nudge w1 = 2.0 (steep). Slope at w2: 0.05 (gentle, already safe).
Without smoothing, an attacker nudges w1 by 0.01 and recovers harmful loss = 2.0 * 0.01 = 0.02 (bad).
With smoothing, slopes flatten: slope at w1 drops to 0.1 (20 times gentler than before).
Same nudge of 0.01 now recovers only 0.1 * 0.01 = 0.001 in harmful loss (attacker must nudge 20 times further to cause trouble).

The equation says: move weights to a place where the forget loss is low (forgetting works) and all slopes are flat (reattacks fail because every direction is nearly level).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Probe layer choice matters. The adversarial probe is trained on hidden states from a single layer (e.g., layer 28 in one experiment, layer 32 in another). If you pick the wrong layer, the probe cannot see the jailbreak attack. Pick it using layer-wise separability analysis before training.
Decoupling requires careful math. If the retain and forget update directions are not separated correctly, they still fight each other, and you get back the trade-off problem. The paper solves this by projecting the forget objective onto the orthogonal complement of the retain objective — a closed-form solution, but easy to get wrong in implementation.
Flatness is necessary but not sufficient. A calm region helps against relearning, because small training steps make tiny changes. But if the forget loss itself is not actually low (the model still knows the harmful knowledge), a calm region will not hide a tall peak — the attacker can train uphill and recover everything. The method assumes you can make the forget loss small first.
Done right. Start by verifying that gradient ascent alone can reduce forget-set loss to near zero (prove the objective is achievable). Then apply the decoupling and smoothing layers. Test against realistic relearning attacks (a few steps of fine-tuning on the forget-set) and jailbreaks (small adversarial perturbations). If the model still cannot recover the erased knowledge under these attacks, the method is working.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model fine-tuned to remember a specific fact (e.g., a person's private address or a copyrighted passage). Split data into forget-set (the address/passage), retain-set (benign questions about the world), and test-set (new benign questions to check utility). Run PRISM to unlearn the forget-set. Then verify three things: (1) the model gives wrong answers to the forget-set (unlearning works), (2) the model still answers benign questions correctly (utility is preserved), and (3) after a small nudge or a few fine-tuning steps on the forget-set, the model still does not recover the erased knowledge (reattacks fail). Plot the forget-set loss and utility over training steps. Without PRISM, one would rise while the other falls. With PRISM, both stay good.

Unlearning effectiveness = accuracy_benign_before - accuracy_forget_after; Utility preservation = accuracy_benign_after - accuracy_benign_before; Attack survival = accuracy_forget_after_attack - accuracy_forget_after

The demo must show that all three things happen at once: the model forgets, it keeps utility, and attacks cannot bring the erased knowledge back.
