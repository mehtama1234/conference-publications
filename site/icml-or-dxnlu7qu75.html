Active Budget Allocation for Scaling Law Estimation

Instead of training all models equally, train the promising ones more and cut the weak ones early to measure scaling laws with far less compute.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Scaling laws describe how a model's error shrinks as you give it more data and computing power. Finding these laws requires training hundreds of models at different sizes — a huge expense. This paper shows how to spend that budget smarter: train your best models longer, stop your worst models early, and use a helper model to predict which ones deserve more resources.

Zero assumption version

Why scaling laws are expensive to measure

A scaling law is a formula that says: if your model is twice as big, or your data is twice as large, your error will shrink to this smaller value. These laws let engineers design systems that hit specific accuracy targets. To find a scaling law, researchers train hundreds of models — some tiny, some medium, some huge — each trained for different amounts of time, and measure where each one bottoms out (its error on fresh data). This takes weeks or months on expensive computers.

The naive approach: train every model for the same number of steps. But a tiny model reaches its best performance quickly, while a huge model keeps improving for much longer. So you waste computing power on models that have already plateaued and on models that are so bad they will never contribute to the scaling law.

The method in one sentence. Watch each model's learning curve during training, and use a prediction model to detect which ones are promising; pour more compute into the promising ones, and stop the weak ones early.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a model family

You have ten models ranging from tiny to large, and a fixed total compute budget B.

Round 1: train all briefly

Give each model the same small budget, observe their loss numbers after one epoch.

Rank and filter

Choose the top half by current loss (the ones learning best so far), discard the bottom half.

Predict the winners

Fit a helper model to the loss curves seen so far; predict which survivors will stay good.

Round 2: richer training

Give the predicted-good models more compute; train the rest briefly. Repeat until budget is spent.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the compute budget allocated to each model. Instead of an equal slice for every model, it allocates more compute to models that are tracking toward low loss, and pulls back resources from models that are stalling. This is a schedule over rounds: in each round, only the models predicted to contribute to the final scaling law get extra training, while the rest get stopped.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frontier contribution

measured quantity  =  whether a model will lie on the loss-compute frontier

As you train a model, you record a learning curve: the validation loss at each training step. If you train it for a very long time, it settles at a final loss. The scaling law is the curve connecting the final losses of the best model at each size. But you do not need every model — you only need the ones that end up on the frontier (the lowest-loss model at each size). Many models are interior points, worse than the frontier at their size, and contribute nothing to the scaling law.

The paper uses a surrogate model (a small neural network trained on the loss curves observed so far) to predict which model will end up on the frontier if you train it longer. This is the hidden quantity: frontier membership, predicted early.

Why it works

Why cutting bad models and retraining good ones finds the scaling law faster

Here is the concrete reason. Scaling laws come from the lowest-loss model at each size. If a model is going to be interior (not on the frontier), training it any amount is wasted compute — that budget could go to a model that will be on the frontier. The paper's move is to detect frontierhood early and reallocate.

Early in training, loss curves show the direction: a model with a steep downward slope is probably heading toward the frontier, while one already flattening is probably not. The surrogate model learns from these curves and predicts final loss. If a model is predicted to miss the frontier, stop it. If a model is predicted to lie on it, give it more compute in the next round. This triage means your total budget covers more of the frontier than if you split it equally among all models.

The reason early stopping works: small models converge fast (their loss flattens early), so they do not need the full budget. Huge models improve for much longer, because they have more parameters to tune and the optimization surface has more hills and valleys — it takes more steps to reach a good solution. The paper detects this dynamic and moves compute from fast-converging weak models to slow-converging strong ones.

Math after the picture

The budget allocation equation

First in words:

maximize the quality of the loss-compute frontier (G) by choosing budget C for each model m in M, subject to total budget not exceeding B

Then the compact form:

arg max_{C1,...,Cm} G(M) subject to sum_m C_m <= B

Every symbol:

G(M)a measure of how well the models cover the frontier (how close the observed frontier is to the true one)
C_mthe total compute budget allocated to model m
Bthe total compute budget available
Mthe set of models (different sizes and architectures)
mone specific model
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 4 models (tiny, small, medium, large) and 100 units of compute budget.
Naive: give each 25 units. Tiny and small plateau at loss 0.5 and 0.3. Medium and large keep improving but you run out of budget halfway through their convergence.
With the paper's method: Round 1 gives each 10 units. You observe slopes: tiny nearly flat (will plateau soon), small steep downward (promising), medium steep (promising), large steep (promising). Discard tiny.
Round 2: allocate 30 units to small, 30 to medium, 30 to large. Now you see: small reaches 0.3, medium reaches 0.15, large reaches 0.08. The frontier is small-0.3 (size 2), medium-0.15 (size 3), large-0.08 (size 4).
Naive approach would not reach this frontier quality because it split budget equally among a model that was not going to matter.

The goal is to maximize G (quality of frontier coverage) by allocating more compute to models predicted to contribute to it, and less to models predicted to miss it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Early stopping is too aggressive. If you stop a model based on its first epoch, you might kill a model that converges slowly but ends up on the frontier. The paper mitigates this by waiting several rounds (gathering more loss-curve data) before making a final stopping decision.
The surrogate model is wrong. If your helper model (the LMC or deep ensemble) poorly predicts final loss from partial curves, you will reallocate to the wrong models. Noisy or short training curves are especially risky.
Frontier is not smooth. If the true scaling law has a kink or jump (e.g., a regime change at a specific model size), the surrogate model trained on smooth regions may miss it and stop models near the kink.
Done right. Gather enough early data (several rounds) before filtering, use both parametric and non-parametric surrogates (ensemble them), and validate the final frontier against a held-out compute budget to check the method did not overfit to early rounds.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a small experiment: generate 6 synthetic models of increasing size (or download a real scaling-law dataset). Implement three budget-allocation strategies: (1) uniform (all models get equal compute), (2) successive halving (prune each round by loss), and (3) successive halving with a simple surrogate (fit a polynomial to early loss curves, predict final loss, prune by prediction). For each strategy, measure the quality of the resulting loss-compute frontier (how well it matches the ground truth). Time the wall-clock compute for each.

frontier_error = sum of distances from observed loss-compute points to the ground-truth scaling law, normalized by number of points; compute_savings = (uniform_cost - strategy_cost) / uniform_cost

The demo should make it impossible to miss: the surrogate-guided method obtains a frontier of the same or better quality as naive methods, while using a fraction of the compute, because it stops spending resources on models that will not reach the frontier and redirects them to models that will.
