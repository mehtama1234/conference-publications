Efficient and Sharp Off-Policy Learning under Unobserved Confounding

When hidden factors bias treatment decisions, this method finds the safest policy by computing tight worst-case value bounds instead of unstable guessing.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

In healthcare and other fields, doctors make treatment choices, but hidden factors (wealth, genetics, previous health history we never record) secretly influence both who gets treated and whether they recover. Standard policy learning pretends these hidden factors do not exist, which gives wildly biased estimates and can lead to harmful recommendations. This paper finds the safest policy anyway by computing a sharp bound: the true value under the worst-case hidden confounding. Unlike earlier methods, it sidesteps numerical instability and achieves the lowest possible variance among all unbiased approaches.

The hidden confounding trap

Suppose we have historical data of who received a treatment (a drug, a surgery, a job training program) and what happened to them. We want to learn: what is the best treatment policy? That is, for each person, when should we give them the treatment? The naive approach: fit a model of outcomes given treatment, then recommend treatment to whoever the model predicts will benefit most. But this breaks catastrophically if hidden factors affect both who got treated in the past AND their outcomes. For example, doctors may prefer to give an expensive surgery to younger, healthier patients (because they recover faster). So in the historical data, younger people got surgery more often and had better outcomes, because the true hidden factor (health status) is making both outcomes and treatment choice happen together. Our naive model learns: recommend surgery to young people. But this is backwards—surgery was only recommended to young people because they were already healthy. The hidden factor (health status) was doing the work all along.

When such hidden confounding exists, every standard method—direct outcome estimation, inverse propensity weighting, two-way-corrected approaches—yields biased policy recommendations. A beginner might try: just increase sample size, or use more complex models. But that does not help: you cannot remove a systematic error by collecting more data. With a hidden factor really present, the confounding correlation—younger people both get treated more and have better outcomes, because both are driven by true hidden health—persists in every new sample you collect, because the same hidden factor keeps creating the same correlation. More data gives you more measurements of the same biased relationship.

The paper's move in one sentence. Instead of guessing the true policy value, compute a sharp upper bound on what the value could be under the worst-case hidden confounding, then find the policy that optimizes that bound.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes which quantity the policy learner optimizes. Standard methods try to maximize the estimated true policy value—but that estimate is biased when hidden confounding exists, so optimizing it gives a bad policy. This paper maximizes the estimated sharp upper bound on the policy value instead. That bound is never biased (it is always an upper bound, regardless of hidden confounding), so the policy stays safe.

the object it moves  =  the optimized quantity (from estimated value to estimated worst-case bound)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: unobserved confounding strength

measured quantity  =  sensitivity parameter (Gamma)

The paper introduces a single number, called Gamma, that measures how strongly hidden factors can shift outcomes. Specifically, Gamma bounds the odds ratio: if a hidden factor exists, it can change the odds of a good outcome by at most a factor of Gamma. For example, if Gamma is 2, a hidden factor could make an outcome twice as likely or half as likely (that is a 2x swing in odds), because the sensitivity parameter sets a strict ceiling on how much confounding you need to guard against. If Gamma is 10, hidden influence can be even stronger (up to a 10x swing in odds), because the definition of Gamma directly sets that maximum multiplier. The paper never claims to know Gamma exactly. Instead, the user specifies a plausible range (e.g., Gamma between 1 and 5), and the method finds a policy that stays safe under any confounding strength in that range.

The paper estimates the worst-case value by integrating over all possible true outcome-generating processes consistent with the observed data and the Gamma bound. This is done via a one-step bias-corrected estimator: it adjusts the standard estimators (outcome estimates and propensity scores) by one correction term that accounts for worst-case confounding, making the final bound both sharp and efficient.

Paper reading. Formally, the paper places outcome data in a sensitivity model parametrized by Gamma. The true outcome probabilities lie in a set of all patterns consistent with the observed treatments, covariates, and the Gamma bound. The paper computes the worst-case policy value: the minimum expected outcome across all such patterns when a policy is used. The goal is to find the policy that maximizes that minimum value.
Why it works

Why it works: the two-part solution

Previous methods (Kallus & Zhou, 2018) solve a minimax game: maximize the policy value while minimizing over all confounders in the sensitivity set. This minimax loop is numerically unstable—small rounding errors in one step throw off the entire optimization. The paper takes a different path: it avoids the minimax loop entirely by deriving a closed-form sharp upper bound. Here is how.

First, the paper proves a theorem: given the observed data and the sensitivity parameter Gamma, there exists a computable closed-form expression for the worst-case value of a policy. This expression is a linear combination of the estimated outcome model, the propensity weights (inverse of treatment probability), and a correction term that accounts for worst-case confounding. Importantly, this bound is sharp: no policy can guarantee a higher value under the given Gamma, and it is achievable by some pattern in the sensitivity set.

Second, the paper builds a bias-corrected estimator for this bound. The standard approach (naive plugin) plugs in estimates of outcomes and propensity scores, but these estimates carry noise that biases the final bound. The paper applies a one-step bias correction: it adds a term that removes first-order errors from the estimated outcome and propensity models. This single correction ensures that the bound estimator has the lowest variance among all unbiased estimators, even though outcomes and propensity scores are estimated imperfectly.

The payoff: the paper can now optimize the policy by simply maximizing the estimated bound (a standard supervised learning problem), with no minimax loop. And because the bound is sharp and efficiently estimated, the learned policy is both safe (it never makes assumptions that violate the Gamma bound) and optimal (no other policy under the same constraints can do better).

Math after the picture

The value bound equation

First in words:

the worst-case policy value equals the estimated outcome under treatment minus a correction for hidden confounding, weighted by the inverse of propensity and adjusted by the sensitivity parameter

Then the compact form:

V_hat(pi) = mean{ pi(a|x) * [outcome_hat(a,x) - Gamma * (|outcome_hat(a,x) - outcome_mean| / propensity(a,x))] }

Every symbol:

V_hat(pi)estimated worst-case value for policy pi
pi(a|x)policy probability: probability policy chooses action a given context x
outcome_hat(a,x)estimated outcome if we give action a in context x
outcome_meanaverage outcome across the data
propensity(a,x)probability action a was taken given context x in observed data
Gammasensitivity parameter (how much hidden factors can shift outcomes)
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have one person: the treatment gives an estimated outcome of 80, average outcome is 50, propensity for treatment was 0.5, and Gamma = 2.
Rewriting: outcome_hat(a,x) = 80, outcome_mean = 50, propensity = 0.5, Gamma = 2.
Correction term = Gamma * (|80 - 50| / 0.5) = 2 * (30 / 0.5) = 2 * 60 = 120.
Worst-case value = 80 - 120 = -40.
Interpretation: even though the estimated outcome is optimistic (80), once we account for worst-case hidden confounding (Gamma = 2) and the fact that only half the patients got treatment (propensity = 0.5), the value plummets. The correction is large because propensity is small (rare treatments get extra scrutiny under confounding). This ensures the policy learned from such rare signals is pessimistic and safe.

The correction term grows larger when treatments are rare (small propensity) and when estimated outcomes are far from average. Rare, surprising recommendations get penalized hard, making the learned policy conservative.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gamma is set too tight (underestimated confounding). If the true hidden confounding is stronger than the Gamma bound specified, the learned policy can still be biased, because the bound being optimized is not tight enough to cover the real adversarial worst case. The method is only as safe as the user's Gamma choice. Setting Gamma to 1 (no confounding) gives no protection at all.
Outcome model is severely misspecified. The one-step bias correction removes first-order errors in the outcome and propensity estimates, but not higher-order errors. If the outcome model is dramatically wrong (e.g., missing key interactions), the bound can still be misleading.
Propensity scores are too small (treatment is very rare). When a treatment is rarely given (small propensity), the correction term blows up (it divides by propensity). The bound becomes so pessimistic that even the best policy looks bad. This is theoretically sound—rare treatments carry high uncertainty under confounding—but can be frustrating in practice.
The policy class is too rigid. If the policy class (e.g., linear policies) cannot express good policies even under the Gamma bound, the learned policy will be suboptimal within that class. Flexible classes (like neural networks) help because they can better approximate the true optimal structure.
Done right. Set Gamma based on domain knowledge (consult experts on plausible hidden factors), check that propensity scores are not degenerate (no treatment is given to zero people), use flexible policy classes (e.g., neural networks), and validate the bound on held-out data to check if it is believable.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic dataset where treatment assignment depends on both an observed covariate (age) and a hidden covariate (true health). Simulate outcomes such that treatment helps everyone, but doctors historically gave treatment preferentially to younger, healthy people—this creates confounding because health is a hidden shared cause of both treatment and outcomes. Now run three learners: (1) naive two-way-corrected (assumes no confounding), (2) the method from Kallus & Zhou (minimax version), and (3) this paper. Vary the sensitivity parameter Gamma from 1 (no confounding) to 20 (heavy confounding). For each Gamma, measure the regret of the learned policy on a held-out test set where you know the true outcomes. Plot regret versus Gamma for all three methods. The demo should show that this paper stays safe even as Gamma increases, while naive methods and existing baselines degrade.
