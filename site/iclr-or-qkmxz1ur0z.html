Evolution of Concepts in Language Model Pre-Training

By watching what patterns the model learns to recognize during training, we can see that learning happens in two clear stages: first raw stats, then genuine concepts.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When a language model trains on text, its internal wiring changes dramatically. But how? This paper watches the model learn by taking snapshots at different training steps and identifying what patterns (called features) the model recognizes at each moment. The key finding: learning splits into two phases. Early on, the model just memorizes basic statistics about word counts and sequences. Then suddenly, around step 1,000 of training, real concepts emerge—patterns like subject-verb agreement or induction—that directly enable downstream tasks because these patterns are specific, reusable rules that apply reliably to new examples, unlike memorized statistics which capture only aggregate frequencies.

The Black Box Pre-Training

When engineers train a large language model, they feed it billions of words and watch the loss (the error score) drop. But the loss dropping tells you almost nothing about what the model is actually learning inside. Are all the learning steps the same? Do all concepts emerge together? Do early learning steps build toward later ones, or are they wasted effort? Nobody knows—it is a complete black box.

If you try the naive approach—just reading the numbers and weights after training is done—you get a soup. The weights are tangled together, storing thousands of overlapping patterns at once. You cannot see which patterns the model recognizes at step 10 versus step 10,000, or whether an early pattern is later discarded or refined. You are blind to the actual learning journey.

In one sentence. The paper watches the model learn by tracking which interpretable patterns (features) it recognizes at each training checkpoint, revealing a hidden two-stage structure beneath the loss curve.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take snapshots

Save the model's internal activations (lists of numbers flowing through the network) at 32 different training steps, from the start to step 143,000.

Run a decoder

A sparse autoencoder (a small auxiliary network) tries to reconstruct each snapshot's activations using a combination of named sparse features, like a decoder finding the minimum set of concepts needed to explain what the network computed.

Track births

For each named feature, plot when it first becomes active. Most features are born around step 1,000; a few emerge much later (step 100,000).

Measure complexity

Use an LLM to score each feature's learned patterns (simple or rich). Early features are shallow (word order); later features are deep (induction, subject-verb agreement).

Prove causality

Run a circuit test: when you silence one learned feature, does a downstream task (like recognizing a verb matching its subject) break? If yes, the feature caused the performance gain.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the model itself or its training. Instead, it changes what we observe: from a single global loss number to a detailed map of when each interpretable pattern (feature) emerges during training.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: feature emergence step

measured quantity  =  When each pattern first activates

During training, random weights gradually organize themselves into recognizable patterns. Each pattern—such as subject-verb agreement, or detecting the previous token—awakens at a specific moment. The emergence step is the training checkpoint where that pattern first becomes active and stays active (rather than flickering on and off). Some patterns spring to life at step 500; others wait until step 50,000. The paper measures emergence by watching the activation strength of each feature across all 32 snapshots and finding the first checkpoint where that feature consistently activates.

The paper estimates emergence by training a sparse autoencoder (a decoder network) on snapshots side by side. When the decoder is forced to compress each snapshot into the same set of named features, the ones that matter at step 10 are weak; the same features at step 1,000 are strong; and at step 500 they are flickering in between. The paper plots this strength over time and calls the step where strength first rises and sticks the emergence step.

Why it works

Why watching emergence reveals two stages

The learning process has two hidden phases, and emergence timing exposes both. In the first phase (steps 0–1,000), the model is just memorizing basic statistics: what fraction of sentences have a noun, how often nouns come before verbs, what the letter frequencies are. These are not really concepts; they are just aggregate numbers from the data. Importantly, the model can shrink its loss dramatically using only statistics—loss falls from high to low without any real reasoning patterns forming. You can see this because the sparse autoencoder finds almost no features activating until step 1,000. The model is not yet recognizing specific, reusable patterns.

Then around step 1,000, something shifts. New patterns suddenly emerge. The model begins isolating specific recognizable concepts: this word is a verb, this position is the start of a clause, this sequence looks like an induction pattern (if A appears once here and again there, C probably comes next). These are actual concepts, not just stats, because they are built from combinations of input tokens and can be applied to new examples. The paper calls this the feature learning phase. The loss still falls (because loss was already pretty low from memorized stats), but now it is refinement: the model tunes up these concepts over the next 100,000 steps, getting them precise and general. By tracking emergence, you can see this two-stage structure in the data itself.

Math after the picture

The sparse autoencoder loss

First in words:

Total loss is reconstruction error plus a penalty for complexity. Reconstruction error: how far off the decoder's guess is from the real activations. Complexity penalty: how many features did the decoder actually turn on to make that guess.

Then the compact form:

L = sum_snapshots || activations - reconstructed || squared + lambda × sum_snapshots sum_features (feature_activation × feature_norm)

Every symbol:

activationsthe real numbers flowing through the model layer at this snapshot
reconstructedthe decoder's approximation using sparse features
feature_activationhow much this feature is on (0 or a small number)
feature_normthe magnitude (strength) of this feature's decoder weights
lambdathe knob that trades off accuracy versus simplicity (higher pushes sparser)
A tiny worked example
Snapshot at step 100: real activations are [2.3, -1.1, 0.8]. Decoder uses feature A (norm 1.2) and feature C (norm 0.9), producing [2.4, -1.0, 0.7].
Reconstruction error: (2.4-2.3)^2 + (-1.0-(-1.1))^2 + (0.7-0.8)^2 = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.03.
Sparsity: feature A is on (activation = 1), feature C is on (activation = 1), all others off. Cost = lambda * (1*1.2 + 1*0.9 + 0*0 + ...) = lambda * 2.1.
Total loss = 0.03 + lambda * 2.1. If lambda is high, the decoder is forced to use fewer features. If lambda is low, accuracy wins. At each snapshot, the decoder rebalances.

The sparse decoder is forced to pick a small set of features to reconstruct each snapshot; by comparing decoded feature sets across snapshots, we see which features are 'on' early, which turn on at step 1,000, and which wake up later.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Sparse autoencoder hyperparameters chosen badly. If the sparsity knob (lambda) is too high, the decoder is forced to be so sparse it misses real features. If it is too low, the decoder spreads features too thin and cannot detect clean emergence steps. The paper uses ablation (trying many values) to find the right balance, but other work might not.
Cherry-picked snapshots. The paper uses 32 snapshots, chosen to oversample early steps (all 20 before step 10,000) and undersample late steps. If the real emergence happens at step 8,000 but you only have snapshots at 0, 2000, and 15000, you miss it. The choice of snapshots shapes which emergences you see.
Features are tools, not ground truth. The sparse autoencoder finds features that are linear combinations (sums and differences) of the actual neural activations. A 'real' concept in the model might not compress into a linear feature, or might require several features. The emergence step measures when the decoder's chosen features activate, not when the concept is fully learned internally.
Done right. Use a well-tuned sparse autoencoder, oversample early checkpoints to catch phase transitions, and validate emergence steps by checking that silencing those features breaks downstream tasks. This is what the paper does.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Pick a small language model (e.g., GPT-2 or Pythia-70M). Save activations at 10 training steps: 0, 100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, 20000, 50000, 100000. Train a sparse autoencoder on all 10 snapshots at once (using the same set of features for all). Plot the activation strength (decoder norm or mean feature magnitude) for each feature at each step. You should see most feature strengths stay near zero until step 500–1000, then spike and stay high. A few features (initialization artifacts) should spike early then crash. This plot is visual proof of the two-stage process.

emergence_step = first_checkpoint where feature_strength > threshold and stays > threshold thereafter; two_stage_clarity = (step_1000_feature_count - step_500_feature_count) / step_1000_feature_count; should be > 0.6

The demo must make it impossible to miss the phase transition: a dense cluster of features emerging around step 1,000, preceded by silence and followed by refinement.
