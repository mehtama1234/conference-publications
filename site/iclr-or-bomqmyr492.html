RePro: Process-Level Reward for Optimized Reasoning

Score each reasoning step by measuring forward progress and smooth motion, then use those scores to reward efficient thinking patterns during training.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

When an LLM solves a math problem step-by-step, sometimes it overthinks, backtracks, or goes in circles. This paper frames the reasoning sequence as an optimization problem in motion — like rolling downhill toward a solution. It introduces RePro, which watches for two things: whether the reasoning is moving forward (intensity) and whether it is moving smoothly without flip-flopping (stability). These two scores together guide the LLM to reason efficiently.

Zero assumption version

Overthinking and wasted reasoning steps

Long chain-of-thought reasoning in LLMs can be wasteful. The model generates many reasoning steps (often 100-500 words of inner monologue), but not all steps are equally useful. Some steps backtrack: the model makes progress on a problem, then doubts itself and retraces earlier ground. Others are redundant: repeating the same sub-goal multiple times. A naive approach would be to just count the final correct answer and say well done, but that misses the journey. A better LLM should reason efficiently: each step should move toward the answer, because wasted steps consume computation time and confuse the model about which direction to go.

The world before this paper: we had end-result rewards (correct answer gets a score, wrong answer gets zero) and penalties for long responses. But these are blunt instruments. They do not teach the model to think cleanly because the model does not know which of its 100+ intermediate steps actually helped. Length penalties often just rush the model to answer quickly even if the reasoning is sloppy, because the model learns to cut corners rather than reason carefully.

In one sentence. Score the quality of each step in the reasoning chain, measuring both forward progress and smooth motion, then reward steps that move efficiently toward the solution.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Watch the journey

Capture the full sequence of reasoning steps, not just the final answer.

Score intensity

Measure whether the model's internal estimate of solving the problem actually improves at this step, because forward motion means the reasoning is on track.

Score stability

Check whether the improvement is steady and smooth, not oscillating wildly or suddenly reversing.

Combine into one reward

Merge intensity and stability into a single per-step score that the training algorithm can use.

Reinforce efficient thinking

Train the model using standard reinforcement learning, but penalize steps with low scores and reward high-scoring steps.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the model architecture or the final training objective. It introduces a new intermediate signal: a step-level reward that measures reasoning quality in flight. Before, the model only learned from the binary outcome (correct or incorrect). Now, at each step of the chain-of-thought, the model gets a numerical score (between 0 and 1) that says how efficiently that step is behaving.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: process quality score

measured quantity  =  Process-level optimization score

The core quantity is a score that measures how well a reasoning step is doing its job. It has two parts. Intensity: a measure of whether the model's estimated progress on the problem improved at this step. Stability: a measure of whether the improvement was smooth and consistent with the previous step, or chaotic and self-contradicting. The paper calls this a dual score.

The paper estimates this by computing a proxy objective function (a numerical summary of how close the model is to the correct answer at each step) and tracking whether it increases smoothly. If the objective jumps up, the step is good. If it oscillates or drops, the step is bad. The dual score combines these two observations.

Plain reading. Imagine watching a student solve a math problem. You score each intermediate step: did the student make progress, and did that progress feel steady or did the student keep second-guessing themselves. That is the process-level score.
Paper reading. The paper defines a surrogate objective function that tracks progress, then computes intensity (change in that objective) and stability (smoothness of change). These combine into a composite reward aggregated from top-k reasoning segments.
Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Reasoning is an optimization process. When you solve a problem, you move through a space of possible thoughts toward the solution. Each reasoning step is a movement in that space. Some movements are directly toward the goal (efficient), and some are sideways or backward (wasted).

Before this paper, the training signal was binary and delayed: you get one number at the end (correct or incorrect) after all 100+ steps have been generated. The model has to guess which of those 100 steps helped and which hurt. This is like getting a single grade on a homework set but not knowing which problems you did well on.

RePro adds intermediate signals. By scoring intensity (did this step move the objective value forward?) and stability (did this step maintain smooth forward motion?), the training algorithm can now say, mid-reasoning, which steps are actually useful. Steps that make progress and do it smoothly get reinforced. Steps that backtrack or oscillate get suppressed. Over time, the model learns reasoning patterns that avoid wasted motion.

The stability score matters because a step can increase the objective by accident or by oscillation — going up, then down, then up again — which looks like progress but is actually confused. By requiring smooth, steady improvement, the model learns to commit to a reasoning direction and follow through, rather than flip-flopping on the same sub-problem.

Math after the picture

Equation and worked example

First in words:

For each step in the reasoning chain, the proxy objective increases or stays flat. Intensity is the magnitude of that increase. Stability is measured as one minus the oscillation. The process-level reward is a weighted combination of the two: intensity weight times intensity score plus stability weight times stability score.

Then the compact form:

r_step = w_intensity * I_step + w_stability * S_step, where I_step = max(0, J_step - J_prev), S_step = 1 - oscillation_measure_step

Every symbol:

r_stepthe reward score for this reasoning step
w_intensityweight on how much we care about forward progress
w_stabilityweight on how much we care about smooth motion
I_stepintensity score: how much the objective increased at this step
J_stepthe proxy objective value at this step
J_prevthe proxy objective value at the previous step
S_stepstability score: how much the step avoided oscillation
oscillation_measure_stepa measure of how much the objective bounced around
A tiny worked example with three reasoning steps
Step 1: objective goes from 0.3 to 0.5 (increase of 0.2, smooth). Intensity I1 = 0.2, Stability S1 = 1.0 (no oscillation). Reward r1 = 0.5 * 0.2 + 0.5 * 1.0 = 0.6.
Step 2: objective goes from 0.5 to 0.4 (backward step). Intensity I2 = 0 (no progress), Stability S2 = 0.3 (big oscillation). Reward r2 = 0.5 * 0 + 0.5 * 0.3 = 0.15.
Step 3: objective goes from 0.4 to 0.7 (increase of 0.3, steady). Intensity I3 = 0.3, Stability S3 = 0.95 (nearly smooth). Reward r3 = 0.5 * 0.3 + 0.5 * 0.95 = 0.625.
Training learns: Step 1 and 3 are good, Step 2 is penalized. Over many examples, the model's reasoning learns to avoid the backward-step pattern.

The dual score rewards steps that move forward (intensity) and move smoothly (stability); backtracking or oscillation gets penalized, teaching the model to reason linearly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Proxy objective is a proxy. The paper estimates progress using a surrogate function, not the true problem structure. If that proxy is miscalibrated, the scores will misdirect training. A step might look like progress but actually not be.
Tuning intensity and stability weights is tricky. If w_intensity is too high, the model rushes to any forward-looking step and misses nuance. If w_stability is too high, the model is too conservative and avoids creative pivots. The balance depends on the task.
Oscillation measure might miss subtle confusion. A reasoning chain can oscillate in ways that the oscillation score does not catch — for example, semantic contradiction (saying one thing, then denying it) while the objective score stays flat.
Done right. Use process-level rewards on diverse benchmarks (math, code, science) and verify that backtracking decreases and reasoning becomes linear, as the paper shows.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple reasoning task (e.g., a multi-step algebra problem or a logic puzzle). Generate a reasoning chain from an LLM, and manually score each step using the dual metric: (1) did this step move a numerical estimate of progress forward, (2) was the move smooth and consistent. Assign high scores to efficient reasoning and low scores to backtracking or contradiction. Train a small model on a batch of such examples, rewarding high-scored steps and penalizing low-scored ones. Compare trajectories before and after: the after version should show fewer reversals and more linear step sequences.

efficiency = (steps_that_increase_objective + steps_that_maintain_smooth_motion) / total_steps

The demo must show that intermediate per-step rewards (not just final correctness) teach the model to reason without wasted backtracking.
