Rectifying LLM Thought from Lens of Optimization

Treat the steps an LLM takes while reasoning as movement toward a solution, and measure how well each step advances, then reward the best-shaped reasoning processes.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Large language models reason by writing out their thinking step by step (chain-of-thought). But sometimes they get stuck overthinking or write reasoning steps that go nowhere. This paper views the reasoning process as moving through space toward the right answer, and introduces a way to measure whether each step actually advances you closer. During training, the model learns to prefer reasoning that makes steady, stable progress instead of rambling.

Zero assumption version

Overthinking and wandering reasoning

When an LLM reasons, it writes out a chain of thoughts before giving an answer. A naive first attempt might be: just train the model to write a longer chain, assuming more steps means better thinking, because intuition says more deliberation leads to better answers. But the problem appears immediately: the model learns to write longer chains that do not actually move toward the answer. It creates reasoning steps that repeat themselves, explore dead ends, or contradict each other.

The real issue: the model has no signal telling it whether a step is progress or waste. It just learns to predict plausible next words, which can lead to plausible-sounding nonsense. Without a way to measure whether reasoning actually advances, the model cannot learn the shape of good reasoning.

In one sentence. Long chains of reasoning fail when steps do not actually move toward solving the problem.

What it actually does

Treat reasoning as optimization

Each reasoning step moves the model's internal state closer to or farther from the right answer.

Measure intensity and stability

For each step, calculate how much it moves and whether that movement points toward the goal.

Blend the intensity and stability scores into a single number that reflects reasoning quality.

Optimize across models and algorithms

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the model is trained during reinforcement learning: instead of rewarding only the final correct answer, it now rewards the entire reasoning chain based on how well-shaped the steps are.

The hidden quantity

Process-level reasoning quality

measured quantity  =  How purposeful and steady each reasoning step is

The hidden quantity is a measure of whether each step in the chain actually moves toward solving the problem and does so consistently. It has two parts: intensity (how large is the step) and stability (does the step point the same direction as previous steps, or does it change course randomly).

The paper estimates this by looking at the internal state of the model after each reasoning step. It measures how much the model's representation shifted (intensity) and whether that shift aligns with shifts from prior steps (stability). These two scores are combined into one process-level reward that grades the entire reasoning chain.

Why it works

Why well-shaped reasoning gets rewarded

The mechanism hinges on viewing reasoning as movement in an abstract space. When the model writes a reasoning step, its internal state shifts. If that shift is both large and points in a direction consistent with prior steps, the model is making purposeful progress. The paper measures this by computing intensity (how far the state moved) and stability (whether the direction aligns with before). A step that is both intense and stable means the model is confidently advancing toward the answer.

When training uses this process-level reward instead of only final-answer reward, the model learns that good reasoning does not just sound plausible — it advances steadily. Steps that ramble or contradict prior progress get low scores and are gradually pruned out. Over time, the model learns to write reasoning chains where each step builds on the last, moving incrementally toward the solution. This shape of reasoning is more reliable because it reflects genuine problem-solving rather than fluent-but-aimless text generation.

Math after the picture

The process-level reward equation

First in words:

The reward for a reasoning chain is the sum of intensity (step size) weighted by stability (how aligned each step is with the overall direction). A step that is both large and well-aligned earns a high score. A step that is small or goes off-course earns a low score.

Then the compact form:

R_process = sum_t [Intensity_t * Stability_t], where Intensity_t = ||state_t - state_(t-1)||_2 and Stability_t = cosine_similarity(direction_t, mean_direction)

Every symbol:

R_processTotal reward for the reasoning chain
Intensity_tHow far the model state moved at step t
Stability_tHow much step t aligns with the overall direction
state_tModel's internal representation after step t
direction_tDirection of movement at step t
mean_directionAverage direction across all prior steps
A tiny worked example
Step 1: state moves distance 2.0, aligns perfectly with nothing yet. Stability = 1.0. Reward = 2.0 * 1.0 = 2.0
Step 2: state moves distance 3.5, aligns well with step 1 direction. Stability = 0.9. Reward = 3.5 * 0.9 = 3.15
Step 3: state moves distance 0.5, goes sideways (contradicts prior). Stability = 0.2. Reward = 0.5 * 0.2 = 0.10
Total chain reward: 2.0 + 3.15 + 0.10 = 5.25. Step 3 is penalized heavily because it abandoned the direction.

Steps that move far and stay on course earn high rewards; the model learns to write reasoning that builds steadily toward the solution.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

State representation may not capture progress. If the model's internal state does not actually reflect distance to the solution, then intensity and stability measurements become noise. The reward signal then teaches the model nothing useful.
Intensity and stability may not be the right metrics. It is possible that good reasoning has a different shape — maybe it sometimes backtracks, or moves in short bursts. If the paper's metrics do not match the real pattern of good problem-solving, the training will steer the model in the wrong direction.
Stability metric can punish creative exploration. If a problem genuinely requires trying a new direction, the stability penalty will discourage it. The model may learn to stick rigidly to one approach even when switching approaches is smarter.
Done right: intensity and stability are validated on real hard problems. The paper tests on math, science, and code benchmarks where correct reasoning has a clear shape. Success here suggests the metrics capture real problem-solving patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small reasoning task (e.g., solve a multi-step word problem or debug a code snippet). Train two versions of a model: one with only final-answer reward, one with RePro process-level reward. For each version, examine 10 reasoning chains and measure the average number of wasted or contradictory steps. Measure the final accuracy. The demo should show that RePro models write tighter reasoning (fewer wasted steps) while maintaining or improving accuracy.

Reasoning efficiency = (steps taken + contradictions) / final accuracy

A RePro-trained model should produce reasoning chains that rarely wander and rarely contradict themselves, whereas the baseline model wastes steps on dead ends and detours.
