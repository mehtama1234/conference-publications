Understanding what neural networks actually learn

Papers analyze and measure the geometry of learned representations to understand how networks encode information, what patterns they capture, and whether they learn interpretable structure.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A trained neural network is a black box: it takes data in and produces predictions, but what is it really capturing? Are the learned representations organized? Do they encode interpretable ideas? These questions matter because if you understand what a model has learned, you can diagnose failures, design better architectures, and predict when transfer to a new task will work. Papers in this subtheme are detective work: they develop tools and experiments to peer inside the model's learned representations.

The difficulty is that representations are high-dimensional (often hundreds or thousands of numbers per input), which is hard to visualize or analyze directly. Researchers use several techniques: they measure how similar the internal representations are for similar inputs, they project high-dimensional spaces down to 2D or 3D for visualization, they check whether you can linearly separate different categories in the learned space, they measure information content — how much of the input information has the model preserved or discarded. Some papers use adversarial perturbations to understand if small intentional changes break the model. Others analyze what individual neurons or layers are sensitive to. The results often reveal that models learn hierarchically: early layers capture textures and edges, middle layers capture parts, and late layers capture whole objects.

Across 64 papers, researchers explore what self-supervised models have learned, what makes representations good for transfer learning, how different pretraining methods produce different structural organizations, and whether interpretable features emerge naturally. This work is foundational for building intuition about why these methods work, and it often reveals surprising failures: models sometimes learn spurious shortcuts or miss important dimensions entirely. Understanding these patterns helps design better pretraining objectives because researchers can target the proxy tasks and architectures that naturally encourage the kinds of structure that downstream tasks need.

How the work breaks down

Neural networks learn high-dimensional representations that are hard to understand directly. Researchers split their investigation into five directions: finding ways to make representations interpretable and disentangled (breaking them into separate, understandable components), developing tools to measure and probe what networks have actually captured, uncovering the causal structure behind learned features, building specialized representations for specific domains and tasks, and ensuring that learned vector embeddings (numerical encodings) transfer well and continue working when applied to new tasks or when the input data changes.

Making Representations Interpretable

A representation is interpretable when you can understand what each learned dimension means. The challenge is that most learned representations are tangled: many dimensions work together in ways that resist simple explanation. These papers develop methods to separate representations into independent, understandable pieces.

The Bayesian Nonparametric Framework paper tackles a core trade-off: if you force a representation to be disentangled (each dimension captures one isolated concept), you often run out of capacity and can't represent everything. Their approach uses a nonparametric formulation that guarantees you can identify what each dimension learned while having enough total capacity. The Disentangled Representation Learning Through Symmetry Discovery paper solves a different angle: discovering what symmetries the data has (translations, rotations, etc.) and using those symmetries to automatically organize the learned representation. The Domain Expansion paper addresses multi-task learning, where a single representation must serve many different downstream tasks. By constructing the latent space explicitly to prevent collapse (where all tasks map to the same useless representation), they create a space where concepts can be directly manipulated. Mechanistic Independence provides graph-theoretic criteria for when a representation's latent factors are truly identifiable even through nonlinear transformations. Finally, the Spatially Informed Autoencoders paper shows that incorporating spatial structure into the learning objective makes representations far more interpretable because they naturally align with how the input space is actually organized: when you embed this knowledge, the learned numbers correspond to real spatial positions, making predictions like simulating point patterns much easier.

These papers collectively attack the same core problem from different angles: representations are more useful when their learned dimensions are independent, identifiable, and correspond to real, interpretable aspects of the data that you can actually understand and explain.

Measuring What Networks Have Captured

Before you can understand what a network has learned, you need tools to measure it. Most probing methods either fail to capture the complexity of learned features or require so many learned numbers (parameters are the tunable weights) that they just memorize the answer instead of actually measuring what the representation contains. The challenge is designing measurements that are efficient, consistent, and honest about what information actually exists in the representation.

Attention, Please! Revisiting Attentive Probing tackles this head-on: they show that linear probes and attentive probes (small neural networks that try to extract a specific signal from the representation) can be both efficient and effective. Their attentive method uses fewer learned weights than past work but performs better than standard baselines, and the attention weights themselves become interpretable because they show which parts of the representation actually matter for a given task—this is useful because you see exactly where the signal is. Estimating Dimensionality of Neural Representations addresses a different measurement: how many effective dimensions does a high-dimensional representation actually use? They develop an estimator that works on finite samples and stays consistent across different data types (neural recordings from the brain, brain imaging scans, language model internal numerical activations—the outputs each neuron produces), revealing that networks often learn lower-dimensional structures than their weight count suggests because they compress information heavily. Measuring the Intrinsic Dimension of Earth Representations shows that this principle holds even for specialized domains: geographic representations learned by neural networks have intrinsic dimensions of 2-10 even when the space is 256-512 dimensions, and this intrinsic dimension predicts how well the model performs downstream because it reflects whether the network found a truly compressed understanding. Uncovering the Latent Potential of Deep Intermediate Representations asks a complementary question: not just what do final representations contain, but what useful information hides in intermediate layers that we typically throw away? Their method extracts and uses these middle-layer representations, gaining interpretability and enabling cross-lingual and cross-modal analysis because these layers often capture richer, more nuanced signals.

Together, these papers provide increasingly refined tools for measurement, from probing single tasks to quantifying dimensionality to extracting value from every layer of the network.

Understanding Causal Structure

When a network learns to predict well, does it discover the true causal structure of the world, or does it memorize spurious correlations? This question matters because if you understand what causal relationships a representation has captured, you can predict when transfer learning will work and when it will fail because causal patterns generalize but spurious patterns break when the data distribution shifts (changes). The challenge is that learning causality is fundamentally hard—the data itself doesn't reveal which correlations are causal.

Beyond DAGs: Latent Partial Causal Model for Multimodal Learning shows that multimodal learning (using multiple input types like images and text together) naturally encourages representations to discover causal structure because different input types reveal different parts of the true causal mechanism. They demonstrate this by showing that networks trained on multimodal data learn disentangled representations that enable few-shot learning and better generalization to new domains—evidence that they've captured genuine causal factors rather than just surface correlations. Learning Intervention Representations with Delta Embeddings takes a different approach: if you have data from interventions (experiments where you change one variable), you can use this to learn representations that respond predictably to changes because interventions directly reveal causal relationships. They test this on both synthetic and real-world benchmarks, showing that embeddings learned from intervention data generalize much better when you encounter new cases where the underlying data distribution changes because the representation captured how the system actually responds to change. Linear Causal Representation Learning by Topological Ordering, Pruning, and Disentanglement makes these ideas concrete by learning which factors causally influence others, using topological ordering to recover causal hierarchy, and validating the approach on language models where you can inspect learned causal structure directly.

These papers show that representations capturing causal relationships, not just correlations, are more interpretable, transferable, and predictive of real-world out-of-distribution behavior.

Specialized Representations for Domains

Different domains have different structure. A representation that works brilliantly for text might fail for medical images, and an embedding designed for 2D photos may not apply to 3D geometric data. The challenge is designing representation learning methods that respect domain-specific structure and constraints—for example, that medical images have particular visual patterns, or that 3D shapes follow specific mathematical laws—not just applying generic deep learning.

Embedding-Based Context-Aware Reranker (EBCAR) tackles information retrieval, where you need to rank which document snippets are most relevant to a query. By learning embeddings that can infer cross-passage relationships, they capture context that simpler embedding methods miss because they can look across multiple passages to understand the full answer. Learning Unified Representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting addresses 3D graphics: Gaussian Splatting is a modern way to render 3D scenes as collections of blurred points, but these representations were ad-hoc and lacked principled structure. This paper develops a proper representation that preserves both color and 3D spatial structure (the relative positions of points), enabling downstream learning and neural network applications because the representation now respects what matters in 3D. Frequency-Balanced Retinal Representation Learning targets a specific medical domain: retinal imaging in ophthalmology. Standard self-supervised methods learn features skewed by natural image statistics from everyday photos, which doesn't match retinal images. Their method rebalances to learn features that capture the distinctive patterns actually present in retinal data, improving performance compared to generic encoders because it adapts to what matters in that specific domain. MedGMAE: Gaussian Masked Autoencoders for Medical Volumetric Representation Learning extends masked autoencoding to 3D medical scans (CT volumes), where individual voxels are points in 3D space with spatial structure. By using Gaussian masking adapted for volumetric data, they learn representations that transfer better to segmentation, classification, and registration tasks than generic methods because masking respects the 3D structure.

These papers show that generic representation learning works, but tailoring the approach to domain-specific structure—whether retrieval patterns, 3D spatial structure, imaging statistics, or volumetric scans—yields substantially better and more interpretable results because the representation can focus on what actually matters in that domain.

Transfer, Generalization, and Composition

A representation is only useful if it transfers: if you learn it on one task, will it help on a different task? This matters because you want to reuse learned knowledge. Staying consistent also matters—if the input is corrupted or the new distribution is different, does the representation still work? The deeper question is whether representations naturally compose: can you combine multiple learned representations to handle complex scenarios?

From Vicious to Virtuous Cycles: Coordinated Representation Learning for Unsupervised Video Object-Centric Learning breaks the typical encoder-decoder gap in video understanding. Encoders (which compress video data) and decoders (which recreate it) usually learn incompatible representations because they optimize different objectives. By making encoder and decoder work together as a coordinated system, they learn cleaner, more reusable representations of objects that persist across video frames, getting excellent results because object identity now stays consistent. Uncertainty-driven Embedding Convolution (UEC) addresses generalization: standard embeddings treat all dimensions as equally important, but some learned features are more reliable than others. By modeling which dimensions are uncertain and combining embeddings as an ensemble, they improve both performance and consistency across diverse benchmarks—especially important for models deployed in the real world because unreliable features can cause failures on new data. Compositional Generalization Requires Linear, Orthogonal Representations in Vision Embedding Models asks whether transfer works for compositional tasks. They prove that if you want to combine learned representations (use them together to handle new combinations of learned concepts), the representations must be linear and orthogonal—and they show that widely-used models like CLIP and DINO partially satisfy this constraint because their representations already have this structure. Distribution-Aware Set Representation Learning Under Test-Time Element Corruption shows that representations must handle not just distribution shift but element-level corruption (missing or broken data elements), and they provide a framework that maintains high performance even when test-time data is incomplete because it expects some data to be unreliable.

Together, these papers show that the most useful representations are those that transfer across tasks, continue working when data is corrupted or the distribution shifts, and compose linearly so that learned concepts can be combined in new ways.
