AIR-VLA: Vision-Language-Action Systems for Aerial Manipulation

A new benchmark and dataset reveals what vision-language-action models can and cannot do when controlling a flying robot with a robotic arm.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots that follow language instructions by watching video have worked well when sitting on the ground, but flying robots with arms are harder — they move in three dimensions, the arm and drone interact with each other, and tasks take many steps. This paper builds the first test suite designed specifically for these aerial systems, with simulated examples and real measurements, to show which AI models work and which fail.

Zero assumption version

Flying robots are not like ground robots

Today's AI systems that understand images and language and decide what actions to take (called vision-language-action models) work well when a robot sits still or rolls on the ground. But flying robots with arms are different: they must balance themselves in mid-air, the arm's movement shakes the drone, and tasks need many steps. Existing models trained on ground robots do not account for these challenges.

A beginner might try using a model trained on ground robots and hope it transfers to the air. But this fails because the model has never seen floating-base dynamics (how a UAV stays stable), never learned about the tight coupling between the arm and the drone (moving the arm changes the drone's stability), and never practiced long-horizon planning (holding a sequence of steps in mind). The mismatch is fundamental.

In one sentence. AIR-VLA is a test suite with 3000 simulated examples and metrics tailored to aerial manipulation, revealing which models can handle the unique challenges of flying arms.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Builds a simulation world

Creates a physics engine with a flying drone, a robotic arm, and objects to grasp, run through realistic forces.

Collects real demonstrations

A human remotely drives the drone+arm 3000 times, performing pick-and-place and multi-step manipulation tasks.

Designs four kinds of tests

Base manipulation (how well the drone holds position), object understanding (finding things to grasp), semantic reasoning (following language), long-horizon planning (remembering and chaining actions).

Runs existing models on the tests

Tests large pre-trained models, distilled fast versions, and traditional imitation-learning baselines with identical inputs.

Reports what each model can do

Shows success rates per task type, exposes weaknesses (e.g. a fast model fails on complex tasks), and measures the gap between what models do on step one versus steps two through five.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

This paper does not change the models themselves. Instead, it changes what we measure: it introduces a new test suite and four types of evaluation metrics, each focused on challenges unique to aerial systems.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: long-horizon degradation

measured quantity  =  multi-step performance drop

The paper measures something that ground-robot benchmarks miss: how much worse a model's decisions become on the second, third, and later steps of a task. On the first action, a model predicts correctly much of the time. But once the robot moves and the world changes, the model must update its understanding from the new camera view and remember what it is trying to do. This measurement reveals whether models can handle mental burden across time.

The paper calculates this by running models on tasks with multiple steps, measuring success on the first step separately from steps two-through-five, and comparing the drop. A model that maintains 85% accuracy on step one but falls to 20% on step two has a 65-point degradation. This number is invisible in traditional benchmarks that only report final success or failure.

Why it works

Why this matters: the real reason long-horizon fails

Vision-language-action models work by reading the current image, the current task instruction, and predicting the next action. On step one, this works well: the camera shows the original scene clearly, and the model has never been wrong yet. But on step two, everything has changed. The robot may have moved, the target may now be partially hidden, and the model must integrate the instruction (still the original language) with this new visual context.

The challenge is twofold. First, the model must track the state of a flying system across time — the drone's position, the arm's joint angles, whether an object is grasped — by reading the camera alone. Ground robots move slowly and predictably in 2D. Aerial systems fly in 3D and the arm can create chaotic forces. Second, the model must remember the intent ('pick up the red box and place it on the table') and revise its understanding as the world evolves. If the model misses a detail on step two (e.g. the box slipped), all remaining steps will fail. This cascading dependency makes long-horizon tasks exponentially harder than one-shot prediction.

The paper reveals this by running the same model on single-step tasks (usually successful) and multi-step tasks (where performance plummets). Because the model code does not change, only the task length changes, the drop is purely a problem of visual understanding and temporal reasoning at scale.

Math after the picture

The metric: measuring the drop across steps

First in words:

Success rate per step = count of correct actions at step i divided by total attempts at step i. Degradation = success at step 1 minus average success at steps 2 through N.

Then the compact form:

step_success_i = (correct_actions_at_i / total_attempts) * 100, degradation = step_success_1 - mean(step_success_2, ..., step_success_N)

Every symbol:

step_success_ipercentage of correct actions at step i, measured from 100 test runs
correct_actions_at_ihow many times the model chose the right action at step i
total_attemptshow many test runs were executed
degradationthe difference between step 1 success and average success on remaining steps
A tiny worked example
Suppose π0 model runs 10 tasks with 5 steps each.
Step 1: model succeeds on 9 out of 10 tasks. Success rate: 90%.
Step 2: model succeeds on 7 out of 10 tasks. Success rate: 70%.
Step 3: model succeeds on 5 out of 10 tasks. Success rate: 50%.
Step 4: model succeeds on 4 out of 10 tasks. Success rate: 40%.
Step 5: model succeeds on 3 out of 10 tasks. Success rate: 30%.
Average for steps 2-5: (70 + 50 + 40 + 30) / 4 = 47.5%.
Degradation: 90% - 47.5% = 42.5 percentage points.
This large drop signals that the model struggles to maintain performance as the task unfolds.

The degradation metric isolates the problem of long-horizon reasoning: one model can ace the first move but stumble on the sequence.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Distillation destroys aerial reasoning. When large pre-trained models are compressed to run faster (called distillation), the smaller model forgets how to track the world across steps in 3D space. This happens because the compressed model has fewer parameters to store information about what changed and why, so it cannot maintain a mental picture across multiple actions. This trade-off is acceptable for ground robots (which move in 2D and have simpler state) but catastrophic for aerial systems where every step matters because a lost detail in step 3 ruins steps 4 and 5.
Camera dependency hides fragility. The benchmark includes a test with only onboard and wrist cameras (no global view). Many models rely on the global third-person view to understand where the drone is in space. Removing that view causes sharp performance drops, exposing that models have not learned accurate 3D spatial reasoning.
Sim-to-real transfer remains unverified. All examples are in simulation. Real-world air currents, sensor noise, and uneven weight balance may cause additional failures not captured in the test suite. The paper does not test on actual flying robots.
Safety constraints are ignored. The paper notes that some model predictions cause dangerous interactions with the environment (e.g., crashing into a wall, dropping an object unsafely). Current models do not incorporate constraints that prevent unsafe actions, and the benchmark does not penalize them heavily for safety violations.
Done right. A model succeeds by maintaining reasonable performance on steps 2-5 (within 15 points of step 1), passing the no-global-view test with minimal degradation, and predicting actions that respect safety (e.g., never command the arm to rotate beyond joint limits).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulation of a flying drone with a robotic arm. Load a pre-trained vision-language-action model (e.g., a foundation model or OpenVLA). Give the model a language command like 'pick up the blue cylinder and place it in the bin.' Run the model step-by-step for 10 actions. Record whether the model succeeds at each step and compute the step-by-step success rate. Then measure the degradation: how much does performance drop from step 1 to steps 2-10? Run the same task with the fast compressed variant and compare the degradation. A reader should see the foundation model hold up reasonably (85% to 65%), while the compressed variant collapses (70% to 15%).

degradation = success_rate_step_1 - mean(success_rate_step_2 through step_N)

The demo makes it impossible to miss: foundation models trained on broad robot data handle multi-step aerial tasks better than distilled or ground-only models because they have learned patterns of recovery and spatial reasoning across many embodiments, but all models still struggle to sustain performance beyond the first action in long-horizon sequences because even broad training cannot solve the fundamental problem of accumulating errors in 3D space when the world state is observed only through a camera.
