ReCAPA: Hierarchical Predictive Correction to Mitigate Cascading Failures

When a robot makes a small mistake in step 3, the fix predicts that mistake is coming and adjusts what the robot is trying to do at levels 1-2-3 so step 4 does not inherit the error.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Vision-Language-Action agents are robots that read images, understand instructions, and execute step-by-step tasks in real environments. When any single step fails (misidentifying an object, choosing the wrong intermediate goal), that error gets inherited by the next step, then the next, until a whole task collapses. ReCAPA is a training framework that learns to catch and correct these cascading errors before they pile up by adjusting the robot's own sense of what it should be trying to do at every level (individual actions, sub-goals, and full task paths).

Why robots fail on long tasks

A robot reads a picture of a kitchen table and is told to pick up the blue cup and put it in the cabinet. To do that it needs to: (1) find the cup in the image, (2) plan a reach toward it, (3) grip and lift, (4) navigate to the cabinet, (5) place it inside. If step 2 estimates the cup is 5 centimeters to the left of where it actually is, the gripper will miss it. When the robot realizes the grip failed, it has already wasted time and is now confused about where the cup really is. On the next attempt to reach, the navigation system inherits that wrong belief. By step 4, the whole plan has been poisoned by the mistake in step 2.

Humans fix this by constantly re-checking: when the grip fails, the brain immediately questions every earlier assumption and re-plans from the current reality. Robot systems today try to prevent this two ways: either they pre-plan everything rigidly before starting (so one mistake stops the whole plan), or they correct each step in isolation without updating what the robot is trying to achieve overall. Neither approach catches a mistake that appears in the middle of a long task and fixes what comes next.

The method in one sentence. Teach the robot to predict that a mistake is happening at each stage and automatically adjust its goals at all three levels (individual moves, sub-goals, and the full task path) to stay on track.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Robot encodes the current scene.

Takes an image and turns it into a list of numbers that describe what the robot sees.

Three-level predictions are made.

At the action level (should my gripper be here?), subgoal level (is this an intermediate success?), and trajectory level (is the overall plan still sane?).

Compare predictions to reality.

Once the robot acts, check whether what actually happened matches the prediction at each level.

Compute correction signals.

For any prediction that was wrong, compute how far off it was and what needs to change to fix it.

Adjust the robot's sense of its goals.

Update the representation of actions, subgoals, and the full trajectory so the next step starts from the corrected understanding, not the one poisoned by the earlier error.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the internal representation (the numerical description) that the robot maintains of its task at three nested levels. Instead of freezing the goal once the task starts, ReCAPA continuously adjusts what the robot thinks it is trying to achieve — the numerical encoding of the current action, the current sub-goal, and the overall trajectory — in response to prediction errors that emerge during execution.

The hidden quantity

The error propagation signal

measured quantity  =  Prediction error at each level

Prediction error is the gap between what the robot expected to happen and what actually happened, measured separately at each level: does this action (move the gripper 3 cm right) happen as predicted? Does this sub-goal (reach the cup) get achieved as predicted? Does this step move the trajectory closer to success as predicted? The robot computes this error signal after every move by comparing its pre-made prediction to the actual result.

ReCAPA measures this error signal and then uses it not to throw away the step, but to send a correction signal backward to adjust what the robot was trying to do. When the robot's internal goal representation changes, the next step is computed from that revised representation instead of the poisoned one, because the goal is not hard-coded once but computed at runtime from the current state. So later steps inherit the corrected representation instead of propagating the earlier mistake, because they always read the most up-to-date goal definition that has been updated by the correction signal.

Why it works

Why adjusting representations stops cascading errors

The core insight is that an error in step N is only a catastrophe if step N+1 inherits the error. That inheritance happens through the robot's internal representation — the numerical description of what the goal should be. If a step fails, instead of ignoring the failure or trying to fix just that step in isolation, ReCAPA immediately re-computes what the next step should be trying to do, based on the corrected representation of the world after the failure.

This works at three scales. At the finest scale, if an action (gripper position) mispredicts, the robot updates its action representation before the next action. At the medium scale, if a sub-goal (intermediate success state) mispredicts, the robot revises its sub-goal representation, so the next sub-goal is chosen with corrected knowledge. At the largest scale, if the overall trajectory mispredicts, the robot re-thinks the full plan. Because all three levels are corrected in concert, a mistake at any level gets caught before it compounds into a task failure. The corrective signals propagate backward in the representation space, not forward in time, so the robot knows what mistake was made and adjusts its expectations accordingly.

Math after the picture

The correction formula

First in words:

For each level (action, subgoal, trajectory), add a penalty term to the loss that grows with how much the prediction error is and how misaligned the level is to what was intended; stronger semantic alignment gets stronger correction.

Then the compact form:

Loss = L_execution + lambda_action * align_action * err_action + lambda_subgoal * align_subgoal * err_subgoal + lambda_traj * align_trajectory * err_trajectory

Every symbol:

L_executionthe original loss for the action being executed
lambda_actionstrength of action-level correction
align_actionhow well the action aligns to the intended subgoal
err_actionthe prediction error measured for this action
lambda_subgoalstrength of subgoal-level correction
align_subgoalhow well the subgoal aligns to the overall trajectory
err_subgoalthe prediction error measured for this subgoal
lambda_trajstrength of trajectory-level correction
align_trajectoryhow well the trajectory aligns to the task intent
err_trajectorythe prediction error measured for the full trajectory
A tiny worked example
Suppose step 2 predicts gripper will be at coordinate (10, 15). Reality is (10, 12). Prediction error = 3.
Alignment score between action and subgoal = 0.6 (medium alignment, not great).
Subgoal-level correction penalty = lambda_action * 0.6 * 3 = 0.5 * 0.6 * 3 = 0.9.
Now suppose step 3 also mispredicts. Its error = 4, alignment = 0.8, penalty = 0.5 * 0.8 * 4 = 1.6.
Total correction loss = 0.9 + 1.6 = 2.5. This teaches the network that both misalignments need fixing.
When training stops, action and subgoal representations have been adjusted so predictions match reality and levels align with each other.

Larger prediction errors and weaker alignment both trigger stronger correction, because the loss term grows, so the network must adjust its weights more to shrink the loss, which rewrites the representations for action, subgoal, and trajectory.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Over-correction amplifies small mistakes. If the correction strength (lambda values) are set too high, the robot over-trusts the prediction error and revises its goals too aggressively, sometimes moving further from the true solution. This is why alignment scoring (Sinkhorn) is essential—it gates correction to cases where the mismatch is real, not noise.
Misalignment at one level hides errors at another. If action and subgoal are poorly aligned, the robot cannot tell whether the mistake is in the action or the subgoal expectation, so correction signals get muddled. This is why ReCAPA enforces alignment across all three levels, not independently.
Prediction error itself can be wrong. If the robot's prediction model is systematically biased (always underestimates how far the gripper will overshoot), the correction signal will be wrong, and the robot will learn to expect the wrong thing instead of fixing the true mistake.
Done right. When alignment is high, prediction error is accurately measured, and correction strength is tuned to the task, the robot learns to catch its own mistakes mid-task and adjust on the fly, so long-horizon tasks survive early errors.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple simulation where a robot must pick up objects in order and place them in bins. Inject a deliberate error in step 3 (misplace the second object by 2 cm). Measure: (1) how many of the remaining steps fail without correction, (2) how many fail with single-step correction, and (3) how many fail with ReCAPA's three-level correction. The demo should make it obvious that ReCAPA catches the step-3 error and prevents it from infecting steps 4, 5, and 6.

error_propagation_rate = (steps_failed_after_injected_error) / (total_steps_remaining); ReCAPA should reduce this by 50-70% compared to no correction

The key observation: without correction, one bad step breaks the rest of the task. With ReCAPA, the robot catches the bad step and adapts its expectations, so the next step succeeds anyway.
