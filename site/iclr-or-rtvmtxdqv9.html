Math Blind: Why Vision-Language Models Fail on Diagrams

When models see a diagram, they are blind to its shapes and structure; training them to see diagrams as graphs of connected shapes instead of pixels fixes this and makes their reasoning work.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Large vision-language models can read text and photo images, but when you show them a math diagram (a flowchart, a graph, a geometric figure), they fail badly. This paper shows the real problem is not that the model cannot reason. Instead, the model cannot even see the diagram correctly. When perception fails, the model gives up trying to understand the picture and just guesses from any text nearby. The fix: teach the model to see a diagram as a collection of simple shapes (circles, lines, boxes) and how they connect to each other.

Zero assumption version

Models see pixels, not diagrams

A vision-language model (a machine trained on images and text together) can answer questions about a photograph: show it a picture of a cat and ask what it is, and it says cat. But diagrams are different from photos. A diagram is made of simple shapes: circles, boxes, arrows, lines. These shapes are arranged to mean something abstract. A flowchart uses boxes for steps and arrows to show which step comes next. A Venn diagram uses overlapping circles to show which groups share members.

When you ask the model a question about a diagram, it often gives wrong answers. The answers sound confident but they are nonsense. A model might count five dots when there are three. It might say a box is a circle. It might misread which shapes are connected. The natural guess is that the model cannot reason about the diagram's meaning. But this paper shows that is wrong. The real problem is earlier: the model cannot even see the diagram's shapes correctly. The perception is broken before reasoning even starts.

The diagnosis. MLLMs perform poorly on basic perceptual tasks like shape classification, object counting, and spatial relationships on diagrams, with near-zero accuracy on fine-grained grounding. When perception fails, models fall back on text in the image, relying on labels and nearby words instead of actually understanding the picture.

What it actually does

Test what the model can see

Ask the model simple perception questions on diagrams: what shape is this, how many objects, what connects to what.

Find it is blind

The model gets basic questions wrong and near-zero on precise shape grounding.

Measure the fallback

Show the model reads nearby text instead of the picture; it has blind faith in captions.

Collect graph annotations

Humans label diagrams as graphs: mark each shape as a node, mark connections as edges.

Feed the model the diagram plus its graph of shapes; the model learns to see and reason.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes the model's training data. Before, the model learned from images paired with text answers. Now, the model learns from diagrams paired with two things: a text description of the diagram's structure (which shapes are present and how they connect) plus the text answer. This one change teaches the model to see the diagram as a collection of shapes instead of a jumble of pixels.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: diagram perception accuracy

measured quantity  =  Diagram perception score

This is the fraction of basic perceptual tasks the model gets right: counting objects in a diagram, classifying shapes, identifying which shapes connect to each other, and precisely saying where a shape is located. Before the paper's method, this score is near zero on most tasks. This score is hidden because the model's final answer on a math problem can look right even if the model's perception of the diagram is wrong (the model might guess from text labels and get lucky).

The paper measures this score by showing diagrams to models and asking only perception questions, not reasoning questions. This isolates perception from reasoning. The key is that the paper shows perception must come first: if the model cannot see the diagram, its reasoning will fail or be unreliable, because it is really just guessing from text.

Why it works

Why training on structure makes perception work

A diagram is not a random collection of pixels. It is a structured object: shapes arranged in a specific way, connected by lines, positioned to mean something. When you show a model a photo, the model has learned to pick out edges, textures, and objects by seeing thousands of photos. But diagrams are artificial: their shapes are clean, their arrangements are deliberate, and their meaning lives in the structure, not the texture.

The standard training approach shows a model a diagram as a flat image (many pixels) and asks what the answer is. The model tries to find patterns in the pixels, but diagrams do not have natural pixel-level patterns. So the model gives up and looks for text: it reads the labels and the question, and guesses based on the words. The model does this because finding the right answer from pixels alone is too hard, but finding it from nearby text labels is easy, so the model learns the shortcut.

The paper's fix: instead of showing only the image, show the model the image plus a description of its structure (a graph: this shape is a node, these two shapes are connected by an edge). Now the model learns two things at the same time: how to see the image AND how to map what it sees to the abstract structure. The structure acts as a scaffold. As the model practices this mapping, it gets better at recognizing what each shape looks like in the image, because it has a label for each shape to guide it. This repetition strengthens the model's ability to see diagrams. Once the model has reliable perception of what the diagram contains, it can reason correctly: it actually sees what the diagram shows, and so its answers are based on seeing, not guessing from text.

Math after the picture

Scoring diagram understanding

First in words:

perception_score equals the sum of all correct shape counts plus correct classifications plus correct grounding, divided by the total number of questions asked

Then the compact form:

perception_score = (correct_counts + correct_shapes + correct_grounding) / total_questions

Every symbol:

correct_countsnumber of times the model counted shapes right
correct_shapesnumber of times the model classified shape types right
correct_groundingnumber of times the model located a shape correctly
total_questionsall perception questions asked in the test
A tiny worked example
A test has 10 perception questions: 3 counting, 3 shape classification, 4 grounding.
Before training: model gets 0 counts right, 1 shape right, 0 grounding right. Score = (0 + 1 + 0) / 10 = 0.1 or 10%.
After training on graph structure: model gets 3 counts right, 3 shapes right, 3 grounding right. Score = (3 + 3 + 3) / 10 = 0.9 or 90%.
The paper shows gains of +79% on grounding tasks and +3 to 4% transfer on reasoning benchmarks.

This score captures what the model can actually see in a diagram, before reasoning gets a chance to make it wrong.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Graph labels are wrong. If the training data incorrectly labels which shapes are connected, the model learns wrong structure. A true perceptual scaffold must be accurate.
Diagram is too different from training. If the test diagram uses shapes or arrangements the model has never seen, the graph structure helps less, because the model has not practiced recognizing those specific shapes. Without practice, the model's mental picture of what that shape looks like is weak, so it falls back on text labels instead.
Text labels are very strong. If the diagram has very clear text labels that make the answer obvious, the model may rely on text even with graph training and ignore the picture.
Graph structure captures the right abstraction. When diagrams are labeled with accurate graphs, the model learns the abstract structure of the diagram domain. It then transfers this learning to new diagrams.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple test: create or collect 20 diagrams (flowcharts, simple graphs, geometric shapes, Venn diagrams). For each, write down the correct answer to a perception question (e.g., count the triangles, which boxes connect to which). Ask a standard vision-language model (without the paper's training) and measure how many it gets right. Then, retrain a version of the model on just 10 of those diagrams, where you provide the ground-truth graph structure (shapes and connections). Test both models on the remaining 10 diagrams and measure again. The demo must show: (1) the untrained model scores near zero on grounding, (2) the retrained model scores 80 percent or higher, and (3) when you ask a reasoning question that depends on the diagram (e.g., follow the flowchart from start to end), the retrained model gets it right more often than the untrained model.

improvement = (retrained_perception_score - baseline_perception_score) / baseline_perception_score * 100%

The demo shows that the bottleneck is perception, not reasoning. The untrained model has the reasoning ability but cannot see the diagram; the retrained model can see and so its reasoning works. This proves that low perception causes poor reasoning.
