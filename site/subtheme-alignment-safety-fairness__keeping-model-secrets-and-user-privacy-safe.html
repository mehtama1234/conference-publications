Keeping model secrets and user privacy safe

These papers defend against attacks that try to steal what models learned (membership inference, model extraction) and develop privacy-preserving training where individual data points stay protected.

What this is

A trained machine learning model is a trove of valuable information. It has seen training data—potentially millions of documents, conversations, or medical records. It has learned patterns that encode insights about the world. Attackers want to steal this. They might try membership inference: querying the model to figure out whether a specific person's data was in the training set. They might try model extraction: asking the model many queries to reverse-engineer its weights and parameters. Or they might try data extraction: making the model repeat verbatim pieces of its training data. Meanwhile, privacy regulations like GDPR say that people have a right to know what data is held about them. If your data is in a training set, you might want it removed. This creates a tension: how do you train capable models on sensitive data without exposing individuals?

Why is this hard? Because machine learning works by absorbing training data into learned patterns. The model doesn't explicitly store 'this person's medical record,' but it has implicitly learned something from it, and that knowledge is woven through millions of parameters. You can't point to a specific neuron and say 'this one holds the secret.' And even if you could, removing it might break other things the model learned. Furthermore, privacy and utility are in tension: a model trained on more diverse, detailed data works better. A model trained with strict privacy guarantees (like differential privacy, which adds mathematical noise to guarantee privacy) usually works worse because the noise degrades signal. And attackers keep getting smarter: they might combine membership inference with auxiliary information to increase the odds of a successful extraction.

The papers in this area work on defense in multiple ways. Some develop privacy-preserving training algorithms that guarantee that learning from a person's data only slightly increases the chance that an attacker can detect their presence. Others study membership inference attacks and defenses: how can you make a model's behavior insensitive to whether one training example is included or excluded? Others focus on model extraction: can you detect when someone is systematically querying the model to steal it? Still others study data extraction and how to defend against it. A newer direction is unlearning: if a person asks you to remove their data from a trained model, can you surgically modify the model weights without retraining from scratch? These are inherently tricky problems because privacy and capability trade off.

How the work breaks down

Research in this subtheme splits into five major directions: detecting and defending against membership inference attacks (where adversaries try to figure out if someone's data was used for training), building privacy-preserving training algorithms that add mathematical noise without destroying model accuracy, auditing what models memorize and building safeguards, federated learning setups where data stays distributed across devices, and encrypted inference methods that let models work on data they cannot see.

Membership inference attacks and defenses

When you train a machine learning model, it absorbs information from its training data. An attacker can sometimes figure out whether a specific person's data was in the training set by querying the model and observing its behavior. This is called membership inference. The threat is real: if someone wants to check whether their private medical record was used to train a hospital's model, they could potentially do it. Defending against this requires making the model insensitive to whether any single training example was included or excluded.

The InfoRMIA paper goes beyond earlier membership inference methods by working at the token level in language models—instead of just asking 'was this person in the training set,' it pinpoints which specific words from the training data the model has memorized. The Benchmarking paper reveals that privacy risks depend heavily on distribution shifts: when test data looks different from training data (out-of-distribution), some methods like LoRA offer surprisingly strong protection even though they have identical theoretical privacy guarantees on paper. The Membership Privacy Risks paper uncovers a counterintuitive finding: sharpness-aware minimization (SAM), a method that improves test accuracy, actually makes models more vulnerable to membership inference attacks because of how it reorganizes the learned weights in parameter space—showing that better accuracy does not automatically mean better privacy.

Together, these papers show that membership inference is a practical threat that depends on model training details (SAM vs standard training), how data distributions shift, and which part of the model's behavior you probe (token-level vs full output). One pattern emerges: privacy and accuracy do not always move together—a more accurate model can leak more information about specific training examples because higher accuracy often comes from fitting training data more precisely, which necessarily encodes details that let attackers detect which examples were in the training set.

Privacy-preserving training with differential privacy

Training models on sensitive data requires a privacy guarantee—a mathematical promise that learning from one person's data only slightly increases the odds of detecting their presence. Differential privacy provides this guarantee by adding carefully calibrated noise to gradients during training. The challenge is that different scenarios (federated learning, transfer learning, different data types) need different noise schedules and hyperparameters to work well, and the theory behind these choices is often unclear.

The Convergent Differential Privacy paper establishes tight theoretical foundations for privacy in federated learning, proving that results can be converted between different privacy frameworks (epsilon-delta differential privacy) without losing guarantees—this matters because practitioners need to compare apples to apples. The Federated Learning of Quantile Inference paper handles a specific practical problem: when training models on distributed data where each person has a different privacy budget (some willing to share more, others less), it builds confidence intervals directly without needing to estimate nuisance parameters. INO-SGD addresses utility imbalance—when some clients have lots of data and others have little, standard privacy-preserving training treats them the same, hurting those with small datasets. This algorithm adjusts training to balance accuracy across all clients under strict privacy budgets. The Optimal Hyperparameters paper shows that one-size-fits-all privacy settings fail across transfer learning tasks: loose privacy works best for some tasks, tight privacy for others. It explains when and why performance drops.

These four papers together establish that differential privacy training is not a single recipe but a family of algorithms that must be tuned per scenario—federated vs centralized, balanced vs imbalanced data, transfer learning vs from-scratch.

Auditing memorization and privacy extraction

Models memorize their training data—sometimes intentionally when data is rare, sometimes accidentally. This memorization is a privacy risk: an attacker could extract verbatim pieces of training data (a patient's medical record, a trade secret) by asking the model to generate text. Before deploying models, you need to know what they memorized and defend against extraction. The challenge is auditing this without retraining the model, and understanding how different kinds of privacy attacks escalate.

The Curation Leaks paper reveals a gap nobody expected: models trained on curated public data leak information about the private guidance used to curate that data. If a researcher uses private patient data to select which public papers are 'most relevant,' a model trained on the resulting curated set will leak which patients influenced the selection. This requires formal privacy guarantees in curation design itself. Natural Identifiers paper enables post-hoc privacy auditing by identifying which specific patterns in the data are most memorable to the model, allowing dataset inference without retraining—this is important for auditing already-deployed models because you cannot afford to retrain. The Searching for Privacy Risks paper simulates an LLM agent under escalating attacks: adversaries start with direct requests ('tell me the user's email'), escalate to impersonation and consent forgery, and the model's defenses must evolve from simple rules to identity verification that is harder to fool. This shows privacy defense is an arms race that must account for creative attackers.

These three papers show that privacy auditing must look beyond what the model outputs—it must catch how data selection influences training and how adaptive attackers probe agents over time.

Federated learning with privacy and federated adaptation

Federated learning trains models on data that never leaves the client—hospitals keep patient data on-site, users keep their phone data local. But federated learning still leaks information: gradients sent to the server can be inverted to recover training data. The papers in this group tackle two problems: how to add privacy guarantees to federated learning, and how to efficiently adapt large language models on federated data without exposing private information.

RESFL balances three things at once on autonomous driving tasks: privacy (reduces membership inference attack success by 37%), fairness (reduces equality-of-opportunity gap by 17%), and utility (maintains high detection accuracy). It shows these three goals can be designed together rather than traded off one against the other. Rethinking LoRA shows that LoRA—a parameter-efficient method that updates only a small adapter layer—offers strong privacy protection in federated learning, achieving top results on image classification models trained with strict privacy budgets. The reason is that LoRA reduces the amount of sensitive gradient information flowing from clients to server. SHE-LoRA goes further by combining LoRA with selective homomorphic encryption: the server receives encrypted gradients and does not learn the raw updates, yet still computes model updates correctly. This achieves 99.71% reduction in communication overhead and 99.87% reduction in encryption time compared to fully encrypted approaches, meaning privacy does not have to be slow.

Together, these papers show that privacy in federated learning is not just about adding noise—it is about architectural choices (LoRA) and cryptographic tools (encryption) that reduce what needs to be private in the first place.

Cryptographic methods for private computation

Some systems need to perform machine learning inference (answering questions from a model) without the model or server ever seeing the raw data. Cryptography enables this: encrypt data client-side, send encrypted data to the model, model processes it without decryption (via homomorphic encryption or secure computation), and only the client learns the answer. This is harder than differential privacy because the data is never revealed even in principle, but the cost is significant—encrypted computation is slow.

The Pisces paper builds the first practical cryptography-based retrieval-augmented generation system. RAG is a common architecture where the model first retrieves relevant documents from a database, then reads them to answer a question. Pisces preserves privacy for both the retrieval step (which documents are retrieved is hidden) and the reading step (what the document says is hidden from the server). It achieves this with just 1.87% accuracy loss compared to plaintext—which is significant because cryptographic methods usually lose much more accuracy due to the extra computation required. This makes privacy-by-cryptography practical for real systems because the small accuracy cost means organizations can deploy it without sacrificing the quality users expect.
