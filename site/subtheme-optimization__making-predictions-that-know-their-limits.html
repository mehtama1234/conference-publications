Making predictions that know their limits

ML models should admit when they're uncertain. How do you quantify confidence, calibrate predictions, or provide guarantees that hold even on new data?

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model trained on historical data makes a prediction on a new example. Should you trust it? Maybe the new example looks nothing like the training data, which means the model is guessing in unfamiliar territory. Maybe the prediction came from extrapolation far outside the region where the model saw examples, which usually leads to mistakes. Maybe the data has changed since you trained the model, so patterns that used to work no longer hold. A good prediction system should not only give an answer but also admit when it is uncertain. For critical applications like medical diagnosis or autonomous driving, a confident wrong answer is worse than an honest "I do not know" because a doctor can ask for a second opinion or a car can take the safe path.

The challenge is that neural networks are usually overconfident. They give a prediction and a score (called a confidence), but that score does not mean what it seems. A model trained on high-resolution images might give 95% confidence when shown a blurry image it has never encountered, even though it should be unsure because the image is outside its experience. Calibration is the process of making confidence scores match reality—if the model says 95%, it should be right about 95% of the time, not 70%, so users can make decisions based on the score. On top of calibration, you often want guarantees: promises about performance that hold even on data the model has not seen. These guarantees allow predictions to be used in high-stakes settings because someone taking the prediction can trust it will not fail silently.

Papers use several strategies. Some post-process predictions: take the model's output and adjust the confidence scores so they align with observed accuracy. Others add uncertainty layers directly into training: use Bayesian methods that maintain a distribution over possible model settings rather than a single point estimate, so uncertainty propagates through predictions. Conformal prediction is a recent method that provides mathematical guarantees without assuming anything about the data: it builds a set of predictions for each example and guarantees that the true answer is in that set more than 90% of the time (if you set the threshold to 90%). Some papers learn when to abstain: train the model to say "I do not know" on examples it cannot handle, so users know when to ask a human. A few use ensemble methods: combine many models and see if they agree, using disagreement as a signal of uncertainty. The theme is that predictions should come with an honest assessment of doubt.

How the work breaks down

This subtheme splits into two main directions: measuring and fixing overconfident predictions through calibration, and building prediction systems that give real guarantees about what they do not know. Calibration work asks: how do you adjust confidence scores so they match reality? Conformal prediction asks: how do you promise that your answer will include the truth, in a mathematically rigorous way? A third direction adapts predictions when the data shifts, because patterns that worked in the past may fail on new data. Together, these approaches let ML systems admit uncertainty before they guess wrong.

Calibration: making confidence scores honest

Neural networks are overconfident. A model trained on one dataset will give high confidence to predictions on out-of-distribution data (images it has never seen before), even though it should be unsure. Calibration is the task of adjusting those confidence scores so they match reality: if the model says 95% confidence, it should actually be right 95% of the time. The challenge is that this must work even when your new data looks different from the training data, which is called covariate shift.

Measuring Uncertainty Calibration takes the first step: providing methods to measure how miscalibrated a model actually is on real-world datasets. Expectation Consistency Loss works to solve calibration under covariate shift, building a theoretically grounded approach that can be trained on mini-batches and has proven sample efficiency. Sample Margin-Aware Recalibration of Temperature Scaling improves on the most common calibration trick (temperature scaling, which divides the model's scores by a tuning constant called temperature) by making it aware of how close predictions are to decision boundaries, achieving better calibration across different architectures and data distributions. Together these papers move calibration from a post-hoc patch to a principled component of the training process.

Conformal prediction: mathematical guarantees that hold

Conformal prediction is a method that gives mathematical guarantees: for each prediction, it builds a set of answers and promises that the true answer is in that set, say, 95% of the time. This works because it does not depend on any assumptions about where the data comes from. The challenge is making these sets useful—if the set is so large it includes everything, the guarantee is worthless.

COMPASS applies conformal prediction to medical image segmentation, where you need both tight prediction sets and guarantees that hold. Colorful Pinball improves conditional coverage by weighting the prediction sets based on the density of training data, which means you get tighter guarantees when predictions are near dense training regions and looser guarantees in sparse regions. Conformal Prediction for Early Stopping uses conformal guarantees to know when you can stop solving a mixed-integer optimization problem and still be confident in the answer, cutting solve time in half. Singleton-Optimized Conformal Prediction makes the guarantee sets as small as possible (ideally just one answer) without losing the statistical guarantee. These papers show that conformal prediction scales from theory to real medical systems and optimization solvers.

Learning to know when you do not know

Sometimes the right answer is to abstain: say "I do not know" and let a human decide or try a different approach. Training models to abstain is hard because the model must learn both what it can predict confidently and what it should refuse. Another angle is learning from imperfect guidance: a teacher might give hints that are sometimes wrong, so the model needs to figure out what is reliable because following bad guidance hurts performance.

Adaptive Conformal Guidance improves learning under imperfect guidance by combining conformal prediction with adaptive thresholds; it shows dramatic speedups in gridworld navigation because the model learns to trust good guidance and distrust bad guidance. Learning Permutation from Structure Without Supervision trains on sorting and routing tasks without labels, learning to handle routing problems where the model must choose what permutation of elements to use; the approach keeps training stable even on large problems. No-Dependency Tabular Test-time Adaptation lets models adapt to new data at test time without seeing the training data, which matters when your training distribution does not match the real world. The thread is: models should learn which inputs they handle well and adjust behavior when they encounter unfamiliar situations.

Uncertainty in online learning and optimization

When you learn or optimize online—processing data one batch at a time, without storing everything—you cannot just compute statistics over all your data. You need guarantees that hold as you go, and you need to know how sure you are about what you have learned. Thompson Sampling is a classic method that picks actions by sampling from a distribution over what the model might be, using that sampling to balance exploration and exploitation. The challenge is understanding why Thompson Sampling works: what is it really optimizing?

What Does Thompson Sampling Optimize uses an online optimization lens to show that Thompson Sampling is actually solving a well-defined benchmark problem, which means you can improve it by comparing against that benchmark. Inference of Online Newton Methods combines Newton's method (which learns fast but needs expensive computations) with sketching (which cheapens those computations) and provides asymptotic normality guarantees, so you know the uncertainty in your estimates. Depth over Fidelity in Fixed-Budget Noisy Evolution Strategies addresses hyperparameter search and RL policy search with noise and limited budget; the paper shows that exploring many shallow options beats exploring a few deep options, which is counter-intuitive but holds because you can rank shallow options more reliably with limited budget. These papers move uncertainty from a nuisance to a tool for making better decisions, because knowing what you are unsure about lets you decide whether to explore more or trust your current estimate.

Guarantees for high-stakes decisions

In medical diagnosis, loan approval, or autonomous driving, a wrong prediction can have severe consequences. You need not just a good average prediction but a promise that performance will not degrade in the worst case. This means combining uncertainty quantification with worst-case thinking: understanding what could go wrong and building guarantees that you can still make sound decisions even when the worst case happens.

Calibrating Decision Safety via Inverse Conformal Risk Control provides finite-sample guarantees that let practitioners evaluate and calibrate how reliable their system is, accounting for the cost of different kinds of mistakes. Conformalized Decision Risk Assessment builds risk certificates—simple human-readable promises about what will go wrong and how bad it will be—that practitioners can trust. Non-Asymptotic Analysis of Efficiency in Conformalized Regression proves that conformal prediction converges to tight sets at a known rate, revealing phase transitions where performance suddenly improves at certain problem sizes. Distributional Consistency Loss improves inverse problems (like medical image reconstruction) by replacing the standard pixel-level loss with one that matches the full distribution of pixel values, avoiding overfitting to measurement noise. Sequential Experimental Design for A/B Testing bounds the error in treatment effect estimates, which is important when running online experiments and you cannot rerun them. Together these papers build a foundation for predictions that do not just work in the common case but deliver promises that hold even in the rare, hard case.
