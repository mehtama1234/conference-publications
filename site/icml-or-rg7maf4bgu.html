Gaussian Mean Field Variational Inference can Overestimate Predictive Variance

A factorized approximate posterior can make each weight look too certain while still making predictions look too uncertain in the directions where the data actually lives.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Bayesian linear regression separates two questions that people often merge. First: after seeing data, how uncertain are we about the model weights? Second: after seeing data, how uncertain are we about a prediction at a new input? Mean field variational inference (an approximation that forces the updated uncertainty over weights to split into independent pieces) is known for shrinking weight uncertainty too much. This paper shows that the usual lesson stops too early. Once those shrunken pieces are pushed through the input features to form a predictive variance, the result can be too large, not too small, especially along directions where the training inputs are concentrated.

Zero assumption version

Why weight uncertainty and prediction uncertainty are not the same thing

In Bayesian regression, the updated belief after seeing data is the posterior (the distribution of plausible weight values after training). Mean field variational inference replaces that posterior with a simpler one that assumes the weights are independent. That simplification usually makes the weight variances too small, because it cannot represent correlations such as 'if one weight goes up, another can go down and keep the same predictions.'

A common shortcut is to conclude that smaller weight variances must produce smaller predictive variances. That shortcut is wrong because prediction uncertainty depends on how the whole covariance structure interacts with each test input. If the approximation removes negative correlations that would cancel each other in the exact posterior, then the approximate predictive variance can grow along some input directions. The paper studies exactly where that happens and shows that those directions are not random: they align with the parts of input space where the training data puts its mass.

Core point. Forcing independent weight uncertainty can remove cancellation between weights, so the final prediction variance can go up even while each individual weight variance goes down.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Write the exact predictive variance

Start from Bayesian linear regression, where prediction uncertainty is a quadratic form of the input and the posterior covariance. This matters because it makes every source of variance explicit.

2. Replace the posterior with a factorized one

Force the approximate posterior to keep only per-weight variances. This matters because it removes all covariance terms, which is exactly the structural change being tested.

3. Compare directions in input space

Check predictive variance along different input directions. This matters because overestimation cannot be judged from one single summary number; it depends on where you ask the model to predict.

4. Identify data-concentrated directions

Show that the directions with overestimated predictive variance line up with directions emphasized by the training inputs. This matters because those are the directions the model sees most often in practice.

5. Change the temperature

Scale the approximate posterior with a temperature control value and study how the predictive variance shifts. This matters because it connects the theory to the cold posterior effect as a possible correction.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the object people inspect when judging variational approximations. Instead of stopping at weight-space variance, it tracks the predictive variance induced at each input. That shift matters because prediction is the quantity users actually consume. A weight covariance can look too small and still produce prediction intervals that are too wide once the input direction mixes the coordinates.

the object it moves  =  the predictive variance induced by an approximate posterior
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: covariance cancellation

measured quantity  =  how much cross-weight covariance cancels predictive variance

Predictive variance is not only the sum of separate weight variances. It also includes cross terms that say whether two weights tend to move together or in opposite directions after conditioning on the data. When those cross terms are negative, they cancel part of the total predictive variance for certain inputs.

Mean field variational inference deletes those cross terms because it assumes independent weights. The hidden quantity is therefore the amount of cancellation the exact posterior would have produced along a given input direction. If that cancellation was large, deleting it makes the approximate predictive variance too high even though each remaining marginal variance is smaller.

Why it works

Why it works: factorization changes the structure of uncertainty

In linear regression, a prediction at input x depends on a weighted sum of the learned coefficients. The predictive variance is the uncertainty of that sum. The exact posterior tracks how the coefficients co-vary after seeing the training input table. If the data says coefficient one and coefficient two should move in opposite directions, the exact covariance records that relation, and the predictive uncertainty for inputs using both coordinates can shrink because the two changes partly cancel.

Mean field variational inference keeps only one variance per coefficient. It can still fit the average location of the posterior well, but it cannot record those compensating moves. That changes the predictive quadratic form in a structured way: the diagonal pieces may shrink, yet the removed negative cross terms can matter more along certain inputs. The paper proves that if the approximation is too small in some directions, it must be too large in others, because the overall way variance is redistributed is constrained by the algebra of the factorized optimum. The training distribution then matters because data-heavy directions are precisely the directions that get weighted most in the expectation over test points drawn from the same source.

Math after the picture

Predictive variance as a quadratic form

First in words:

Predictive variance at an input equals the input transposed times the posterior covariance times the input, plus observation noise. Mean field changes that value by replacing the full covariance with a diagonal one.

Then the compact form:

Var[y | x, D] = x^T Sigma x + sigma_noise^2, while MFVI uses x^T Diag(s) x + sigma_noise^2

Every symbol:

A tiny worked example
Take one input with two active coordinates, x = [1, 1].
Suppose the exact posterior covariance is [[1.0, -0.8], [-0.8, 1.0]]. Its predictive variance contribution is 1.0 + 1.0 - 1.6 = 0.4 before adding observation noise.
Now force a diagonal approximation with the same marginal variances: [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]. The predictive variance contribution becomes 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0.
The approximate posterior looks simpler, but the prediction is now much more uncertain because the negative cross term that created cancellation is gone.

The sign and size of the off-diagonal covariance terms determine whether factorization shrinks or inflates predictive uncertainty for a given input.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The result is exact for a simple setting. The proofs use conjugate Bayesian linear regression, where posterior moments can be written exactly. Neural networks with non-Gaussian posteriors may show the same pattern, but the paper does not prove that every deep model behaves this way because their covariance structure is harder to characterize.
Average under the training distribution is not universal. The expected overestimation result is tied to test inputs drawn from the same distribution as the training inputs. If deployment inputs emphasize different directions, the balance between underestimation and overestimation can shift because a different part of the quadratic form gets weighted.
Temperature can fix one mismatch and hurt another. Lowering temperature shrinks the approximate posterior, which can reduce predictive overestimation in data-heavy directions. But if some directions were already too confident, extra shrinking can worsen them because the same single temperature value is applied everywhere.
Done right. Use the paper's lesson as a calibration check: inspect predictive variance on representative inputs, not only marginal weight variances, and treat temperature as a targeted correction whose effect should be measured on the directions that matter in deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build Bayesian linear regression on a synthetic two-feature dataset where the exact posterior has strong negative covariance between the two weights. Fit the exact posterior and a mean field variational approximation, then sweep test inputs over a grid of directions. Plot three curves for each direction: exact predictive variance, mean field predictive variance, and mean field predictive variance after applying a colder temperature. The demo should show one region where mean field is too small, another where it is too large, and a data-heavy direction where the average error goes upward because missing covariance cancellation dominates.
