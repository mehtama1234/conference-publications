Detecting deepfakes, forgeries, and anomalies

Spotting manipulated or fake images and videos, plus detecting unusual or out-of-place content that doesn't fit expectations.

What this is

Modern generative models can create synthetic images that look real to the human eye, making it harder to distinguish fakes by inspection. A deepfake is a video of a person saying or doing something they never did, synthesized so convincingly that it fools most viewers. A forged image might have objects added or removed, or stitched together from multiple sources. The ability to create fake media threatens trust in images and video evidence — if you cannot tell real from fake, you cannot trust what you see. But there is a counterforce: research into detecting fakes. When a generative model creates an image, it leaves subtle fingerprints — patterns that do not match how real cameras capture light. The challenge is to find and exploit those patterns before fakes become indistinguishable from reality.

Modern generative models produce images with fewer obvious flaws, which makes detection harder. When deepfakes first appeared, they had obvious artifacts — discontinuities (sharp changes in appearance), blinking at wrong moments, slight spatial distortions. But today's models learn to avoid these tells, producing images that pass casual inspection. This drives an arms race: forensics researchers find new fingerprints, generative models learn to avoid them, researchers find other clues. Detection also requires finding global patterns, not just local artifacts. A single forged face in a video of a thousand frames must be caught — missing one fake ruins the whole purpose. A manipulated object in a complex image must stand out even though the rest looks normal. And the methods must generalize: detect deepfakes made with models the detector has never seen before, which means learning deep principles, not memorizing specific tells.

Approaches to this problem split into several categories. Some are forensic: look for traces left by the generation process, like compression artifacts or frequency patterns that reveal where an image was synthesized. Others are behavioral: face deepfakes often have biological inconsistencies — unusual eye movement, asymmetrical expressions, or lighting inconsistencies. Some use neural detectors trained on real and fake images, learning to spot the difference. Others use frequency-domain analysis: examine the image's Fourier transform for unnatural patterns. A few combine multiple signals: if many weak clues point to fakeness, conclude it is fake even if none alone is conclusive. The underlying principle is that perfect forgery is hard — there is always a trail if you know where to look.

How the work breaks down

Detecting manipulated media splits into a few key challenges. First: catching AI-generated images that look real because they fool neural networks and human eyes alike. Second: spotting anomalies in 3D objects, medical scans, and sensor data where normal examples are plentiful but abnormal ones rare. Third: detecting deepfakes in video — faces swapped or expressions altered across many frames. Fourth: building detectors that generalize to generators they have never seen before. Fifth: making detection work at high resolution or with few training examples. The research splits this way because each challenge demands different tools: image detectors look for generation artifacts, video methods track temporal inconsistencies, and anomaly detectors learn from the norm rather than from collections of labeled fakes.

Detecting AI-Generated Images at Scale

Spotting AI-generated images is hard because synthetic images look more real every month. A detector must identify subtle traces left by the generation process — perhaps unnatural patterns in how colors blend, or frequency artifacts from how the model learned. The challenge is that different generators leave different traces, so a detector trained on one type of fake often fails on another. And high-resolution images are especially tricky: examining every pixel at full resolution is expensive, but downsampling loses the fine details where the clues hide.

The Panoptic Patch Learning paper attacks this by breaking images into patches and ensuring every patch contributes to the detection decision. Rather than letting some patches dominate, this method weights them uniformly so subtle anomalies in quiet regions do not go unnoticed. The HiDA-Net work addresses high-resolution detection directly: it preserves fine details by processing at native scale instead of shrinking the image first, and introduces a benchmark of images up to 64 megapixels so future detectors can be tested on real-world resolution. The PGC approach uses frequency analysis to calibrate which signals matter most, improving detection across multiple datasets by learning what peaks in the frequency spectrum reveal whether an image is fake. The Dissect and Prune method strengthens detection by analyzing where predictions fail — when a generator changes or the image is post-processed after generation — and surgically removing the parts of the detector that do not hold up to these changes. The GenShield system goes further: it not only detects fakes but also corrects them by removing the artifacts, which helps when removal is easier than perfect detection.

These five methods all recognize that detection must be thorough (covering every pixel), general (working across generators), and reliable (surviving when images are modified after generation). They compete on datasets but complement each other: patch-uniform methods find global patterns, high-resolution approaches catch local details, frequency calibration learns what physics reveals, and pruning makes detectors survive when someone shrinks an image or adjusts contrast.

Anomaly Detection Without Labels

Detecting anomalies in the wild is a different problem from spotting deepfakes. Often you have many normal examples — healthy tissue in medical imaging, standard shapes in industrial inspection, typical sensor readings from a robot — but almost no examples of the faults you want to catch. Training a detector on labeled anomalies is expensive or sometimes impossible. So the question becomes: how can a detector learn what normal looks like and flag anything sufficiently different, without seeing examples of the abnormal?

The Foundation Visual Encoders paper shows that large pre-trained image models already encode enough understanding to detect anomalies with just a few examples. This matters because it means you do not need a large dataset of abnormal cases; the model already learned what normal should look like from billions of images. The MRAD approach goes further by using memory and retrieval: it stores representations of normal examples and compares new data to them, working across 16 different domains (factories, medical scans, etc.) without retraining. The CoGeoAD method fuses color and geometric information for 3D anomaly detection, handling objects where both texture and shape matter. For sensor data, the Counterfactual Occlusion-Aware Learning method tackles LiDAR anomaly detection by reasoning about what should be visible versus what is hidden by occlusion, so a missing part does not falsely flag as anomalous. The Crossmodal Feature Replacers approach learns to swap features between modalities, making it possible to detect anomalies with only one or two examples by leveraging information from multiple sources (like RGB and depth together).

All five methods share a principle: they lean on what normal should look like rather than memorizing abnormal patterns. They work across medical imaging, industrial inspection, 3D shapes, and autonomous driving sensors, showing that the core insight — learning normality through retrieval, fusion, or pre-trained understanding — applies broadly.

Detecting Deepfakes and Temporal Anomalies in Video

Deepfakes are manipulated videos, and detecting them raises a new challenge: the manipulation must survive across many frames, and tiny inconsistencies — a blink at the wrong moment, unnatural eye tracking, lighting that does not match the face — can accumulate into patterns that reveal the fake. Unlike image detection, video gives you time sequences to analyze. Conversely, generating convincing fakes across 100 frames is harder than faking one still image, which means detectors can look for temporal inconsistencies that do not exist in natural videos.

The Preserving Forgery Artifacts paper directly tackles AI-generated video by keeping high-frequency details and spatiotemporal inconsistencies intact — it processes at native scale rather than downsampling, so temporal breaks between frames stay visible. For broader video anomaly understanding, the Learning to Watch method uses a small neural network (2B parameters) that learns to focus on which parts of a video matter for detecting anomalies, making it efficient enough for real-time use. The Linguistic Relative Policy Optimization approach brings language into the loop: rather than retraining on new datasets, it uses descriptions of what anomalies look like and refines its reasoning without changing its weights, which means it adapts to violence, theft, or other domain-specific events without expensive retraining. The Steering and Rectifying method adjusts the internal numerical space within a frozen pre-trained language model to highlight anomalies, using only 1% of the training data that typical methods need. The TD-VAD approach breaks away from purely visual clues by incorporating text descriptions of anomalies, moving beyond raw pixel analysis to understanding what is wrong in words.

These methods show that video detection benefits from temporal analysis, efficient architectures, language grounding, and representation steering. They move beyond per-frame inspection toward frame sequences and semantic reasoning.

Learning from Few Examples and Generalization

Real-world detection often starts with almost no training data. Perhaps you have one example of a fault, or only ten AI-generated images from a new generator you have never seen before. Typical deep learning requires thousands of examples, so few-shot detection asks: how can you build detectors that work with minimal training? Equally important is generalization: a detector trained on older generative models should still catch fakes from tomorrow's models. Both of these challenges require learning something deeper than memorizing specific artifacts.

Dual Distillation for Few-Shot Anomaly Detection trains on a curated medical dataset and learns to distill knowledge from a larger model into a smaller one, so it works well even when you only have a handful of examples from a new domain. The Fleet approach shows that ten examples of a new generator's output is enough to adapt a detector from 20% accuracy to 73% by efficiently fine-tuning on those few shots. The Mixture Prototype Flow Matching uses a probabilistic approach: it learns example cases (prototypes) of what anomalies look like and can detect both seen and unseen anomaly types by treating them as points that flow through probability space rather than hard yes-or-no decisions. The OmniAID method tackles the generalization problem by decoupling what the image means semantically from the artifacts that reveal it is fake, so the detector can recognize artifacts from generators it has not seen during training. The Where Detectors Fail work explicitly probes the space of generative models to find hard cases, then uses those cases to strengthen the detector, closing gaps in generalization before they become problems.

These five papers converge on the insight that successful detection requires few-shot adaptation and generalization beyond training data. They use distillation, prototype learning, semantic decoupling, and active probing — all techniques that extract general principles rather than memorize specific fakes.
