SAGE: Steerable Adversarial Scenario Generation

Train two opposing expert policies and mix their weights at test time to steer between adversarial and realistic driving scenarios without retraining.

Testing autonomous cars with fixed trade-offs

Autonomous cars need to be tested against bad scenarios that might cause crashes. But there are two competing goals: make scenarios dangerous enough to find real problems, and keep them realistic enough that the car's new behaviors actually transfer to real roads. Existing methods train a single model that commits to one fixed balance between these two goals.

A naive approach: train one model to maximize danger (make adversarial scenarios). It works for finding problems, but the scenarios become physically impossible — cars driving through walls, instant 90-degree turns. The trained driving policy learns to handle these fake tricks and fails on real roads. Or train for realism only. Then you get dull scenarios that never stress the system, so the car never learns to handle unexpected edge cases.

The core problem. You want to choose at test time whether a scenario should be more dangerous or more realistic, but retraining the generator for each choice is too slow.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decouple hard rules from soft goals.

Separate physical constraints (map boundaries, max acceleration) from competing objectives (danger versus realism).

Train Expert Adversary.

Teach one policy to favor adversarial scenarios, weighted heavily toward the danger goal.

Train Expert Realist.

Teach another policy the opposite preference: favor realistic, feasible scenarios.

Interpolate weights at test time.

Given a user preference dial from pure realism to pure danger, blend the two experts' learned weights in between.

Generate scenario once.

Run the mixed-weight model to produce a scenario with the desired balance, no retraining.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves the learned weights (the numbers that define what the model does) rather than training a new model. Instead of retraining when you want a different balance, it linearly blends the weight values from the adversary expert and the realist expert.

the object it moves  =  model weights
The hidden quantity

The weight interpolation spectrum

measured quantity  =  position on the Pareto front

The hidden quantity is how far along the optimal trade-off curve you are. The Pareto front is the set of all possible balanced scenarios: moving more toward danger means losing realism, moving toward realism means losing danger. No single point dominates all others. This paper measures your position on that curve numerically by the interpolation weight.

The paper estimates this quantity implicitly through the test-time dial. When the user sets the preference weight to 0.3, the model interpolates: 0.3 times Expert Adversary's weights plus 0.7 times Expert Realist's weights. This positions you 30 percent along the spectrum from realism to danger.

Why it works

Why weight interpolation works across the trade-off space

When two expert models are trained from the same starting point on related tasks (both generating driving scenarios, just with different goal weights), their learned weights live in a connected space. Moving in a straight line through weight space from one expert to the other does not hit broken or nonsensical models; instead, it traces a smooth path that represents the full spectrum of trade-offs.

Here is why this works mechanically. During training, Expert Adversary learns which scenario changes and trajectory tweaks lead to crashes, while Expert Realist learns which changes keep things realistic. Both experts learn from the same data, with the same basic model structure. When you linearly interpolate their weights, you are literally mixing their learned intuitions. A weight that both experts set high means both care about that feature — it stays high. A weight where they disagree gets a compromise value. The result is a model that balances the two goals smoothly, without ever retraining. The paper proves this works by showing that when fine-tuned models start from the same initialization on related tasks, there is a smooth, well-behaved path in weight space connecting them — no dead zones where the mixed model breaks.

Math after the picture

The interpolation formula

First in words:

mixed weights equal preference-weighted mix of adversary expert weights and realist expert weights

Then the compact form:

theta_mixed = mu * theta_adv + (1 - mu) * theta_real

Every symbol:

theta_mixedthe model weights to use at test time
muuser preference dial, 0 to 1 (0 = pure realism, 1 = pure danger)
theta_advlearned weights from Expert Adversary model
theta_reallearned weights from Expert Realist model
A tiny worked example
Say Expert Adversary learned weight w1 = 0.8 (leans heavily toward danger).
Expert Realist learned w1 = 0.2 (leans toward realism).
User sets mu = 0.4 (wants 40 percent danger, 60 percent realism).
Mixed weight: 0.4 * 0.8 + (1 - 0.4) * 0.2 = 0.32 + 0.12 = 0.44.
The mixed model uses w1 = 0.44, a middle ground between the two experts.

The formula simply blends the two trained expert models; no new training happens when the user changes the dial.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Interpolation hits a dead zone. If the two experts learned extremely different strategies, a straight line between their weights might pass through regions where the mixed model is broken or incoherent. Linear mode connectivity assumes the models were trained on related tasks from the same start, so this is rare, but it can happen if the experts diverge too much.
Constraints get violated at intermediate mixes. Hard rules like map boundaries and maximum acceleration are enforced during training. But if the experts learned to bend the rules in different ways, mixing their weights might produce scenarios that violate physical constraints. This is why the paper separates hard constraints from soft preferences during training.
User misunderstands the dial. The interpolation weight is not a direct measure of danger or realism; it is a position in learned weight space. Small dial changes can have large effects if the experts learned different importance for certain details in the scenario.
Done right: separate constraints and preferences. The paper enforces hard rules as data filters during training (only use valid scenarios), then trains experts on soft preferences (the weighted trade-off between danger and realism). This ensures interpolation never breaks physics, only the balance between competing goals.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple scenario generator on a tiny driving domain (e.g., a two-lane road with one other car). Train Expert Adversary to maximize collision likelihood while keeping scenarios valid. Train Expert Realist to minimize speed and acceleration changes. Then, at test time, vary the interpolation weight mu from 0 to 1 (pure realism to pure danger) and collect generated scenarios. Measure danger as probability of collision, and realism as how closely the scenario matches natural driving patterns from a logged dataset.

frontier_quality = (adversarial_scenarios_at_mu * realism_score_at_mu) averaged across all mu in [0, 1]

The demo makes impossible to miss: as you dial the preference slider, scenarios smoothly shift from mundane to perilous, all without retraining the model.
