Neural Synchrony Between Socially Interacting Language Models

The paper measures how closely two language models change their internal activity over time during a shared task, because stronger alignment tracks better coordination.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

People who work together often line up in timing: they respond at the right moment, use the same context, and keep the conversation moving toward the same goal. This paper asks whether two language models show a similar kind of coordination inside their computations when they interact. The authors define neural synchrony as temporal alignment between the models' internal states during a social simulation, then test whether that alignment rises when the models coordinate well. The result matters because it gives a measurable way to study multi-model interaction below the level of surface text.

Zero assumption version

Surface dialogue alone does not show whether two models are truly coordinating

Two language models can produce a plausible conversation even when their internal processing is poorly aligned. One model may answer late, ignore the other model's update, or shift to a different subgoal. Text alone hides that mismatch because the final sentences may still look coherent.

If we want to study social behavior in model-to-model interaction, we need a signal that changes during the exchange itself. The paper argues that internal temporal alignment can serve as that signal because a coordinated pair should update in compatible ways while they react to the same evolving context.

Without such a measure, claims about multi-model social behavior stay vague. We can count task success, but we cannot tell whether success came from real coordination or from one model doing most of the work while the other happened to stay out of the way.

The core gap: We need a direct measure of internal coordination, because fluent dialogue alone does not reveal whether two models are aligned while they interact.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Run social simulations

The system places language models into interaction tasks because synchrony only makes sense when the models must react to each other over time.

Record internal states at matched turns

For each turn, the paper extracts hidden state values from both models so that the comparison uses aligned time points from the same dialogue.

Measure temporal alignment

The method computes how strongly those internal states track each other across the interaction because synchronized changes indicate shared adjustment to the same conversation.

Score social performance

The paper also measures task quality, engagement, and timing alignment so that synchrony can be checked against observable interaction outcomes.

Test correlation

If high synchrony and strong performance rise together, the measure is useful because it reflects coordination that affects the task rather than random internal similarity.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces neural synchrony as a representational-level metric for interacting language models. Instead of judging multi-model social behavior only from outputs, it measures how the models' internal states align over time during the exchange.

the object it moves  =  Measurement target for multi-model coordination
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: turn-by-turn alignment

measured quantity  =  Temporal alignment of internal states across interacting models

The paper's key quantity is not the final answer and not the literal overlap between two messages. It is the extent to which the two models change their internal states together as the interaction unfolds. If one model shifts topic, updates its plan, or responds to new evidence, the other model should show a compatible shift when coordination is strong.

This quantity is hidden because users never see those internal state signals. The paper treats them as a trace of each model's processing state at each turn. Strong synchrony means the traces move in step over time, while weak synchrony means the models are reacting to the exchange in unrelated ways.

The authors show that this hidden quantity is informative because it tracks both social engagement and temporal alignment. That makes it useful as a proxy for coordination, since a coordinated pair should process the same evolving context in a compatible temporal pattern.

Why it works

Why synchrony can reveal coordination

A multi-model interaction is a sequence of mutual updates. Each model reads the current exchange, adjusts its internal state, and produces the next response. If the pair is coordinating well, those updates should stay compatible because both models are responding to the same context and adapting to each other's behavior.

That is why synchrony can help. It does not rely on one lucky final answer. Instead, it looks at the full trajectory of internal change. A coordinated pair should repeatedly align in timing and direction, because each turn depends on the same evolving exchange. A poorly coordinated pair should drift apart, because one model will update toward a different local objective or fail to absorb the other model's last move.

The correlation with task quality matters because it shows that synchrony is not merely a static similarity score. High synchrony is useful only if it rises when the pair performs better, because that is the test that separates task-relevant coordination from accidental resemblance. The paper reports that link, which supports the claim that synchrony captures an internal feature tied to social coordination.

Math after the picture

A simple way to write the idea

First in words:

Synchrony is the average alignment between the two models' internal state vectors over matched turns in a dialogue.

Then the compact form:

S = (1 / T) * sum_t align(h_A_t, h_B_t)

Every symbol:

Soverall synchrony score for one interaction
Tnumber of matched dialogue turns
h_A_tinternal state of model A at turn t
h_B_tinternal state of model B at turn t
align( , )a similarity function that scores how closely two states line up
A tiny worked example
Suppose a dialogue has 4 matched turns.
At those turns, the alignment scores are 0.8, 0.7, 0.9, and 0.6.
Then the synchrony score is (0.8 + 0.7 + 0.9 + 0.6) / 4 = 0.75.
If a second dialogue gets 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.2, its synchrony score is 0.275.
The paper then checks whether the 0.75 interaction also performs better, because a useful synchrony measure should rise with coordination quality.

The exact similarity function can vary. The core point is that the score summarizes whether the two models move together over time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Synchrony may rise for the wrong reason. Two models built from the same base system may look aligned even when the interaction is shallow, so the metric needs controls because shared architecture can create similarity without genuine coordination.
Turn matching may be noisy. If the measurement compares states from mismatched points in the dialogue, the synchrony score becomes unreliable because the timing signal is broken.
Correlation is not full explanation. A strong correlation shows that synchrony is informative, but it does not prove that synchrony causes better coordination because both could be driven by another factor such as model quality.
Done right: synchrony becomes a useful probe. When timing is aligned and architecture effects are controlled, the metric helps because it reveals coordination patterns that do not appear in text alone.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small two-model interaction benchmark with negotiation, collaborative planning, or question answering. For each dialogue, log hidden states from both models at each turn, compute a synchrony score, and also score the joint outcome. Then compare high-synchrony dialogues with low-synchrony dialogues. The demo should show that stronger synchrony usually comes with better timing and stronger joint outcomes, because that would confirm that the metric is tracking real coordination.

corr = correlation(synchrony_score, joint_performance_score)
