HVAE: Hyperbolic Geometry for Flexible Knowledge Transfer Across Domains

Instead of arranging user preferences in flat space with uniform distances everywhere, this paper arranges them in hyperbolic space where distances grow exponentially, so rare items naturally sit far from common ones and knowledge transfers even when domains barely overlap.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

When a recommendation system recommends movies, it tries to guess what you like based on movies you have already rated. In the real world, most users rate only a tiny fraction of all movies (data sparsity), and new users have rated nothing at all (cold-start). The trick is to transfer knowledge from other domains (e.g., predict music tastes from movie tastes) to fill in the gaps. But current systems arrange users and items on a flat map, which squashes hierarchies and rare items, making it hard to separate what you like everywhere from what you like only in one domain.

Zero assumption version

Flat maps crush rare items and hierarchies

In a recommendation system, you arrange users and items (movies, songs, products) on a map, where distance means how similar they are. If two movies are close on the map, users who like one tend to like the other. The naive approach uses a flat, Euclidean map (like graph paper): just x and y coordinates, or more dimensions, but all stretching and shrinking the same way everywhere. This works well for evenly distributed data. But real user-item interactions follow a power-law distribution: a few movies get thousands of ratings, while millions get almost none. Rare movies get squashed together in the flat space, and hierarchies (genres inside categories inside moods) get distorted.

When you try to transfer knowledge across domains (e.g., predict a user's taste in music based on their movie ratings), the flat map makes it even worse because all features are treated equally in distance. The system must separate domain-invariant preferences (the user likes all upbeat content everywhere) from domain-specific interests (the user watches only action movies, not action music). On a flat map, shared and domain-specific preferences get tangled—the system must force separation via explicit masking or disentanglement networks that compete with each other. In sparse, low-overlap scenarios (few movies and music rated by the same user), the flat map cannot reliably extract what is truly universal because there is not enough signal to learn which features belong to which category.

In one sentence. The paper uses hyperbolic space—where distances grow exponentially—so hierarchies and rare items fit naturally without distortion, and the system can disentangle universal preferences from domain-specific ones even when domains barely overlap.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode users and items into hyperbolic space

Run a variational autoencoder that maps each user and item (who rated what) into a hyperbolic embedding—a list of numbers living in a space where distance grows exponentially. This mimics how real hierarchies expand.

Disentangle shared and domain-specific features

Split each embedding into two parts: one shared across all domains (e.g., the user is into upbeat content everywhere) and one unique to each domain (e.g., prefers foreign films, not foreign music). Hyperbolic space naturally separates these because it has room.

Align distributions across domains using hyperbolic Wasserstein barycenter

Instead of averaging shared features on flat space, compute their center point in hyperbolic space (the barycenter). This prevents distortion when merging sparse domains because distances stay consistent in hyperbolic space.

Route new users via shared embeddings

When a new user arrives with no history, use the shared embedding distribution to predict their taste, bypassing the domain-specific part. Hyperbolic space ensures rare items sit far apart, so predictions are honest.

Recommend by distance in hyperbolic space

For each user, find items that are closest in hyperbolic distance. Because rare items naturally sit further out, they are recommended less often, matching real-world frequency without ad-hoc balancing.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper moves the embedding space itself—the coordinate system where users and items live—from flat Euclidean space to hyperbolic space. In flat space, distances between points grow the same way everywhere: x and y coordinates expand uniformly. In hyperbolic space, distances grow exponentially as points move away from the center—the space expands like tree rings, with more room further out. This single change cascades: hierarchies fit without distortion, rare items naturally spread out because they sit further from the center, and disentanglement of shared vs. domain-specific features becomes easier.

the object it moves  =  embedding space's distance metric
The hidden quantity

Curvature: how fast space expands

measured quantity  =  curvature (Riemannian)—the bending of space itself

In flat Euclidean space, all distances are uniform: the space around the origin looks the same as space far away. Hyperbolic space curves inward (negative curvature): as you move away from the origin, the space expands exponentially, creating more room. The paper measures this curvature and learns it during training. A curvature close to zero means nearly flat space; a large negative curvature means aggressive exponential expansion. The paper's key move is to let the curvature adapt per domain (mixed-curvature HVAE), so each domain's hierarchy gets the right amount of breathing room.

The paper estimates curvature via the Riemannian distance metric—a formula that grows exponentially as embeddings move away from the origin. During training, the variational autoencoder adjusts curvature to maximize how well user-item pairs fit hyperbolic space while minimizing reconstruction loss. When curvature is too small, hierarchies get squashed; when it is too large, disentanglement becomes noisy. The Wasserstein barycenter mechanism then uses this curvature to align shared user preferences across domains without distorting them.

Why it works

Why hyperbolic space fixes cross-domain transfer

Start with the core problem: real user-item interactions are hierarchical (genres, sub-genres, moods, sub-moods) and follow power-law frequency (a few blockbusters, millions of unknowns). A flat map tries to place all items with uniform spacing, but uniform spacing grows linearly: to fit twice as many items, you need four times as much area. Hierarchies need to multiply: to fit twice as many levels, you need exponential room. Hyperbolic space grows exponentially, so it matches the problem's structure exactly. When items sit at their natural distance in hyperbolic space, rare items end up far apart by design, and hierarchies nest without distortion.

Cross-domain transfer requires extracting shared user preferences (what the user wants everywhere) from domain-specific preferences (what they want only in one domain). On a flat map, these two preferences must be separated by an explicit mechanism: either masked embeddings or competing embedded factors that fight during training. Because flat space is uniformly constrained, the mechanism cannot tell one from the other reliably, especially when domains barely overlap. In hyperbolic space, the exponential room allows the shared preferences to live in one region (near the origin, where space is still relatively uniform and distances grow slowly) and domain-specific preferences to sprawl outward (where space expands rapidly and provides room). This is automatic because shared features do not need much room (they are universal and compact), while domain-specific features need breathing room (they are idiosyncratic and sprawling). The Wasserstein barycenter then aligns these shared regions across domains without distortion, because it computes the center point using hyperbolic distance, not flat-space distance.

The result: when a cold-start user arrives (new, no history), the system uses only the shared embedding region. Because that region is hyperbolic, rare items are already spread far apart, so the system naturally avoids predicting rare items when it has no evidence. Disentanglement is honest because it is built into the space structure, not forced by competing losses. Transfer works even in low-overlap scenarios because the shared region stays compact and stable in hyperbolic space.

The reconstruction and curvature loss

First in words:

The paper minimizes a variational autoencoder loss in hyperbolic space: the reconstruction error (how well the decoded embedding matches the observed ratings) plus a Kullback-Leibler divergence that keeps the curvature-adaptive latent distribution close to a standard hyperbolic normal. A Wasserstein term aligns shared embeddings across domains. All distances are measured in hyperbolic Riemannian metric, not Euclidean.

Then the compact form:

Loss = Recon(X, X_hat) + beta * KL_hyp(q_hyp || p_hyp) + lambda * Wasserstein_hyp(Z_shared_domain1, Z_shared_domain2)

Every symbol:

Recon(X, X_hat)reconstruction error: difference between observed ratings and predicted ratings from the decoded embedding
betaweight controlling how much to enforce the latent distribution (higher beta = more regularization, lower curvature adaptation)
KL_hyp(q_hyp || p_hyp)Kullback-Leibler divergence in hyperbolic space: distance between the learned latent distribution and the standard hyperbolic Gaussian (penalizes strange curvatures)
lambdaweight on domain alignment (higher lambda = stronger transfer across domains, lower lambda = domains stay independent)
Wasserstein_hyp(...)hyperbolic Wasserstein distance between shared embeddings of two domains: measures how far apart their shared user preferences sit in hyperbolic geometry
A tiny worked example with three ratings
User Alice in Domain 1 (movies): rated 10 movies, 3 ratings observed, curvature c1 = 0.5
User Alice in Domain 2 (music): rated 5 songs, 2 ratings observed, curvature c2 = 0.3
Shared embedding for Alice: hyperbolic distance from origin = 2.1 (in the stable region)
Domain 1 offset: hyperbolic distance 0.8 (movie-specific taste, expanded outward)
Domain 2 offset: hyperbolic distance 0.4 (music-specific taste, smaller expansion)
Reconstruction loss: ||[10 ratings predicted] - [3 observed]||_hyp = 1.2
KL loss: KL(learned hyperbolic Gaussian || standard hyperbolic Gaussian) = 0.15
Wasserstein alignment loss (shared embeddings across domains): = 0.08 in hyperbolic distance
Total loss: 1.2 + 0.5 * 0.15 + 1.0 * 0.08 = 1.335 (example; real training minimizes this)

The three loss terms work together: reconstruction fits the data, KL keeps curvature stable, and Wasserstein aligns shared preferences across domains in hyperbolic space so transfer works even when domains barely overlap.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Curvature collapse. If the learned curvature approaches zero (flat), the method reduces to ordinary Euclidean VAE and loses its geometric advantage. Cure: regularize curvature during training, or use domain-specific curvature bounds.
Wasserstein distance is expensive. Computing the hyperbolic Wasserstein barycenter requires solving an optimization problem, which can be slow on large item sets. Cure: use approximations (e.g., adding a small noise penalty to optimal transport) or pre-compute barycenters for frequent domain pairs.
Disentanglement can still fail if shared region is not compact. If the shared embedding region is allowed to sprawl (high variance in latent space), domain-specific offsets and shared embeddings blur together even in hyperbolic space. Cure: use tighter KL regularization or supervised losses that explicitly separate shared and domain-specific features.
Power-law mismatch: many domains do not follow strict hierarchies. If a domain's structure is truly random (no hierarchy), hyperbolic space buys less benefit than hierarchical domains. The method works best on tree-like or moderately hierarchical data.
Done right. When curvature is learned stably, domains overlap in real ways (users rate items in multiple domains), and shared user preferences actually transfer across domains, HVAE achieves both rare-item accuracy and transfer that works on power-law-distributed recommendation data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small cross-domain recommendation system with three domains: books, movies, and podcasts. Simulate a power-law distribution: 10 popular items in each domain get 80 percent of the ratings; 90 rare items share the remaining 20 percent. Create 50 users with cross-domain histories: most users rate items in one or two domains, a few rate all three. Train two systems: one with ordinary Euclidean embeddings (variational autoencoder baseline), one with HVAE (hyperbolic embeddings, mixed-curvature, Wasserstein barycenter). Test on cold-start users (new arrivals with zero history) and measure recommendation accuracy on rare items separately from popular items.

Cold-start transfer accuracy = (rare item accuracy with shared embedding only) / (rare item accuracy with domain-specific embeddings). Disentanglement quality = correlation between shared embedding and user preference vectors that span multiple domains minus correlation with domain-specific offsets. Rare item recall = (number of rare items in top-10 recommendations) / (total rare items rated by user). Target: rare-item recall should be 50 percent or higher (honest frequency), while cold-start accuracy should exceed 70 percent.

The demo makes impossible to miss that rare items spread far apart in hyperbolic space and stay far apart when predicting for new users, while baseline Euclidean embeddings squash them and recommend only blockbusters. And when domains barely overlap, HVAE's shared embedding still transfers knowledge accurately because the space structure naturally separates shared from domain-specific preferences, not forced by competing losses.
