Gradient Descent with Large Step Sizes: Chaos and Fractal Convergence Region

When you move weight numbers (parameters) too fast during training, they jump into a chaotic zone where where you land is almost impossible to predict.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Gradient descent (the standard training method: measure the error, find which direction points downhill, nudge the weights that way) is how we teach a model by moving its number values downhill on the error curve. Usually, a gentle nudge (small step size) works fine. But this paper reveals something surprising: if you nudge too hard, the numbers bounce around chaotically, and tiny changes to where you start lead to wildly different endings. The convergence region splits into a fractal pattern—like a coastline where no matter how close you zoom in, you see more jagged edges.

Zero assumption version

When size of each nudge goes too large

Normally, training a model works like rolling a ball downhill. You measure how far from the goal you are (the error), compute which direction is downhill (the gradient), and take a small step that way. Each step nudges the weight numbers a little closer to good values.

But what if you take a really big step? A naive thought: bigger steps get you downhill faster, so you should converge quicker—because each update moves you closer to the goal. The problem is that downhill only points the right way locally, near where you are now. Take a giant leap and you might overshoot so far that you land on an uphill slope, then bounce to another slope, and keep ricocheting chaotically instead of settling down, because the error surface curves in unexpected ways far from your starting point.

The question. What is the maximum step size before gradient descent stops converging reliably?

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes: step size relative to the critical threshold

The paper identifies a precise boundary: the critical step size. Below it, the training converges. Above it, it diverges. Near that boundary, the behavior becomes extremely sensitive to starting conditions. The paper's move is to compute this boundary exactly for a problem where you rebuild a table by multiplying two arrays of numbers (matrix factorization) and show that once you cross it, the convergence zone develops a fractal structure where initialization sensitivity amplifies.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: initialization sensitivity

measured quantity  =  sensitivity of minimizer to starting conditions

This is the central hidden quantity the paper measures. It answers: if I start at two points that are nearly identical, do I end up at nearly identical solutions? Below the critical step size, the answer is yes—small changes in initialization cause small changes in the result. But as you approach and exceed the critical step size, this breaks. Tiny differences in where you start (a hair's breadth) lead to wildly different outcomes.

The paper estimates this by plotting the convergence region—which starting points land at which minimizers—and measuring how finely divided and jagged that region becomes. This jaggedness is a signature of chaos.

Why it works

Why large step sizes cause chaos: overshooting breaks the local-descent assumption

Gradient descent assumes you are always descending because you take a small step in the downhill direction. The gradient points downhill locally, but only within a small neighborhood. A large step breaks this assumption. You compute which direction is downhill from where you are now, but then you leap so far that you land in a completely different region of the error surface where the local curvature is different—maybe even uphill. This causes you to bounce to a different spot and repeat.

As the step size approaches the critical threshold, these bounces become more violent and more sensitive to the exact error surface. The paper shows that the error surface in matrix factorization has a special structure: near the minimizer, adding a penalty term (regularization) makes the error surface narrower and pointier. This tighter constraint amplifies how much a slightly-different starting point changes the outcome. The result is a fractal convergence boundary—a boundary so jagged that zooming in reveals more jags infinitely, just like a coastline.

The mechanism works like this: (1) large step size causes overshooting, (2) overshooting creates sensitive dependence, (3) adding a penalty term tightens the constraint surface, making it even more sensitive, (4) sensitive dependence with a complex error surface produces chaos, (5) chaos means no predictable bias toward simple solutions like balanced weights or minimal norms.

Math after the picture

The critical step size formula

First in words:

The penalty grows from the product of how far the weights moved, how important each weight is, and the regularization strength. At the critical step size, the largest penalty exactly balances the geometry of the loss surface, preventing further growth of the parameter norm. Beyond it, parameters spiral outward chaotically.

Then the compact form:

eta_star = 8 / (4 * lambda + Q(u_0, v_0))

Every symbol:

eta_starthe critical step size (maximum safe step before chaos)
lambdaregularization strength (how hard we penalize large weights)
Q(u_0, v_0)a geometric measure of the loss surface shape at initialization
etathe step size we choose for training
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model has regularization lambda = 1.0 (moderate penalty on weight size).
At initialization, the loss surface curvature measure Q = 4.0 (determined by the data).
Critical step size: eta_star = 8 / (4*1.0 + 4.0) = 8 / 8 = 1.0.
If we choose eta = 0.5 (below 1.0), gradient descent converges predictably.
If we choose eta = 1.5 (above 1.0), weights bounce chaotically and diverge.
If we choose eta = 1.0 exactly (at the boundary), sensitivity to initialization explodes: one start leads to solution A, a start 0.00001 away leads to solution Z.

The formula shows that regularization increases the critical step size denominator, shrinking eta_star and pushing you closer to chaos—the opposite of the naive intuition that regularization makes training more stable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Chaos erases implicit bias. Below the critical step size, gradient descent naturally biases toward certain kinds of solutions (balanced weights, small norms, flat minima). The paper shows that in the chaotic regime, these biases vanish—you can land anywhere.
Regularization amplifies chaos. Intuitively, adding a weight penalty should stabilize training. But here it does the opposite: it tightens the loss surface around minimizers, making the convergence zone even more fractal and sensitivity even more extreme near criticality.
Fractal boundaries are not just theoretical. On a real neural network with two hidden layers, the paper observes that plotting which initializations converge reveals actual fractal structure—zooming in shows new roughness indefinitely, with boundaries that split infinitely into smaller copies of themselves.
The analysis applies broadly. The core mechanism—sensitivity explosion at critical step size—appears in simple two-array problems, full table reconstruction, and two-layer neural networks, suggesting it is a general phenomenon whenever you train networks with large step sizes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

The demo trains a matrix factorization model at different step sizes, starting from multiple nearby initializations (starting points that differ by 1e-5). For each step size, record whether training converges and which minimizer it finds. Plot the starting points on one axis and the outcome on another; color-code by whether convergence succeeded. Below the critical step size, nearby starts cluster together (same outcome). At the critical step size, a checkerboard pattern emerges: points a pixel apart have opposite outcomes. Zoom into the checkerboard and observe the same fractal pattern repeating at finer scales.

fractal_dimension = log(N(epsilon)) / log(1/epsilon), where N(epsilon) is the number of boxes of size epsilon needed to cover all boundary points in the convergence region

The demo must make it impossible to miss this: at the critical step size, initialization sensitivity becomes infinite. A single pixel change in your starting weights can flip the outcome from convergence to divergence.
