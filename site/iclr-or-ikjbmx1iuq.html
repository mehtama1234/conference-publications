Contractive Diffusion Policies

Make action diffusion resistant to learned-model errors by adding a penalty that keeps nearby noisy actions flowing toward similar clean actions.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

When a robot learns from past experience to pick actions, diffusion models work by gradually cleaning up noise-covered actions. But tiny errors in that cleaning process pile up, making the robot confused about which action to pick. This paper adds a penalty that pulls nearby noisy actions toward the same destination, like a funnel, so errors do not throw the action choice wildly off course.

Zero assumption version

The problem: noisy paths lead the robot astray

Diffusion policies work by starting with a fully noisy action (random garbage) and gradually removing noise step by step until a clean action emerges. Each step uses a learned model to predict how much noise to strip away. This is like a very long, wobbly path through a fog.

In image generation, small stumbles along the path do not matter—a blurry spot in the corner goes unnoticed. But for robot actions, a tiny mistake compounds. If the denoising at step 50 is wrong, then step 51 starts from the wrong place, making step 52 even worse. By step 200, the robot is doing something completely different from what was intended.

A beginner might just use more training data to get the denoising model perfect. But that is expensive, and it does not solve the real problem: even a small error in the learned model is inevitable, and stacking 200 steps of small errors breaks the action choice.

In one sentence. Contractive Diffusion Policies add a penalty during training that makes the denoising path less sensitive to mistakes, so small errors do not throw the final action off course.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn to denoise actions

Train a model to predict how much noise to remove at each step, guided by offline data the robot collected.

Compute the Jacobian

For each batch of training data, calculate how the denoising prediction changes when you tweak the input slightly—this is the Jacobian.

Penalize large Jacobians

If the Jacobian is too large, nearby inputs would diverge—add a penalty to the training loss to shrink it.

Train the whole loss together

Combine the denoising error loss with the contraction penalty, weighted by a tuning knob, and optimize.

Sample actions reliably

At test time, follow the denoising path step by step; the trained contraction keeps nearby flows funneled together, so small errors stay small.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the denoising sensitivity

The paper modifies one specific part of the learned denoising model: how much the output changes when the input changes slightly. In normal diffusion, the model is trained only to predict noise removal accurately—it can be very sensitive to tiny input tweaks. Contractive Diffusion Policies adds a second objective: make the denoising output insensitive to small input changes. This is called making the function 'contractive.'

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: contraction rate

measured quantity  =  contraction magnitude

Contraction measures how much the denoising model's output would change if you nudge the input slightly. Mathematically, it is the largest eigenvalue (the strongest 'stretch') of the Jacobian—the matrix that tells you how sensitive the output is to each input direction. A low contraction means nearby inputs stay close; a high contraction means they diverge. The paper estimates contraction by computing this largest eigenvalue during training.

Why measure it? Because contraction directly controls error sensitivity. If contraction is high, a small mistake at one denoising step gets amplified at the next step, and errors compound. If contraction is low, errors stay local and do not blow up over 200 steps.

Why it works

Why it works: funneling error into safety

The core insight is that denoising is a dynamical system. At each step, the learned model takes in a noisy action and outputs a slightly cleaner one. If the model is contractive, then two paths that start close together will stay close—like two rivers flowing into a funnel. This means errors in the learned model get damped out rather than amplified.

Mathematically, contraction is defined by the largest eigenvalue of the Jacobian being less than 1. When this holds, feeding the output of one step as input to the next step shrinks distances exponentially. So even if step 50 makes a mistake and outputs an action that is 10 percent wrong, that mistake gets halved at step 51, quartered at step 52, and vanishes by step 100. The penalty during training enforces this exponential shrinkage.

In contrast, a non-contractive denoising model might have an eigenvalue of 1.01. Then a 0.1 percent error becomes 0.101 percent error at the next step, then 0.102 percent, and by step 100 that tiny mistake has become a 2 percent error, throwing off the final action. The contraction penalty prevents this.

Math after the picture

The equation: score matching plus contraction penalty

First in words:

loss equals diffusion-error loss plus gamma times contraction-loss, where diffusion-error measures how wrong the predicted noise removal is, and contraction-loss penalizes the largest eigenvalue of the score Jacobian if it exceeds 1

Then the compact form:

L(theta) = E[(norm(epsilon_theta + sigma_t * grad log p_t(a_t|s))^2)] + gamma * max(0, lambda_max(J_epsilon_theta) - 1)^2

Every symbol:

epsilon_thetathe learned denoising model's prediction of noise to remove
sigma_tthe known standard deviation of noise at step t
grad log p_tthe true direction toward cleaner actions at step t
J_epsilon_thetathe Jacobian matrix: how the model's output changes per input change
lambda_maxthe largest eigenvalue of that matrix
gammathe tuning knob: how much to prioritize contraction over denoising accuracy
A tiny worked example
Suppose at step 50, the learned model predicts noise-to-remove = 2.5, but true noise = 2.3. Error loss = (2.5 - 2.3)^2 = 0.04.
Also suppose the Jacobian has largest eigenvalue lambda_max = 1.2 (the model is over-sensitive). Contraction penalty = (1.2 - 1.0)^2 = 0.04.
With gamma = 1, total loss for this step = 0.04 + 1 * 0.04 = 0.08.
The optimizer will reduce both: improve the noise prediction AND shrink the Jacobian (make the model less sensitive).
If lambda_max falls to 1.02, contraction penalty drops to (1.02 - 1)^2 = 0.0004, so most pressure shifts to improving denoising accuracy.

The contraction penalty forces the denoising model to be insensitive to input changes, so errors from imperfect learning do not compound across 200 steps.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too much contraction kills flexibility. If gamma is too large, the penalty dominates, and the model becomes so insensitive it forgets how to adjust to different states. The robot learns a one-action-fits-all policy.
Contraction does not fix bad data. If the offline data contains only bad actions (e.g., a robot trained only on clumsy pickups), contraction will reliably produce a clumsy pick every time. Resistance to errors does not improve the quality of the underlying actions in the data, because contraction prevents errors from the learned model, not errors in the original data itself.
Eigenvalue computation is expensive. Computing the Jacobian and its largest eigenvalue at every training step adds overhead. For very large action spaces or long denoising chains, this can slow training.
Done right. Tune gamma carefully via validation data; use a recent contraction-aware diffusion solver at test time; validate on both high-data and low-data regimes. The contraction penalty is most valuable when data is scarce or the learned model is approximate.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple offline robot-learning task, like picking and placing a cube on a table using a short trajectory (20 steps). Collect only 50 past episodes of successful pickups, making the data scarce and noisy. Train both a standard diffusion policy and a contractive one on this scarce data. At test time, run 100 episodes with each policy and measure how often the robot successfully places the cube near the target location.

success_rate = (number of successful placements) / (total episodes); standard_variance = variance of final placement positions; robustness = mean placement distance when adding 0.1-unit sensor noise to observations during inference

Contractive Diffusion Policy should have higher success rate and lower variance, especially when sensor noise is added, proving that contraction makes the denoising path stable under error.
