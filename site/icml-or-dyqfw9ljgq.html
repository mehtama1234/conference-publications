Zero assumption version

Flat space distorts hierarchies when merging two image types

Infrared cameras see heat: a person glows bright, cold ground stays dark. Visible cameras see color: that person wears a blue shirt, the ground is brown grass. Each tells a different story about the same scene, so merging them together captures both viewpoints at once—heat information tells you where the warm things are, and color tells you what they look like. But current methods use flat (ruler-straight) space, which treats all similarities the same distance apart. This fails for hierarchical concepts: the idea person is a special case of warm-blooded-animal is a special case of living-thing. In flat space, you can't encode that nesting without metric saturation: there is no room left to place finer distinctions once you've placed the broad ones.

A beginner would say: just average or concatenate the infrared and visible features (the learned number-lists each camera produces). This breaks because the methods don't understand that when you fuse infrared heat with visible color, you need to respect that some concepts matter more at coarse scales and others at fine scales. Flat space cannot naturally hold this tree structure, so you get texture distortion and confused alignments.

The move. Place the images in a curved mathematical space (shaped like a disk that feels infinite at its edges), guided by text descriptions that act like anchors. This space has exponentially expanding room as you move toward the edges, which naturally holds the tree of concepts without distortion, because each hierarchical level has its own distance range.

What the paper changes

The mathematical space where image features live

The paper changes where and how image features live. Instead of placing them in ordinary flat space, it maps them into a curved mathematical space (shaped like a disk where space expands exponentially as you move toward the edge). This single change lets hierarchical relationships fit naturally.

The hidden quantity

Hierarchical nesting capacity

measured quantity  =  Tree-fit without saturation

The hidden quantity is: how much room a space has to hold a hierarchy without compressing fine details. In flat space, if you place a coarse concept far from the origin, finer sub-concepts have nowhere left to go without overlapping. Curved space has exponentially expanding room away from the center, so a tree can branch indefinitely without squashing.

The paper exploits this by extracting text descriptions (which naturally form hierarchies: warm-blooded-animal contains person contains person-with-blue-shirt) and placing them as anchors in curved space. The image features are then pulled toward these anchors, so the learned alignment respects the tree structure.

Why it works

Why curved space avoids distortion

The core reason: curved space expands exponentially as you move away from the center, like a tree with infinite branching room. When you encode a hierarchy (person is-a living thing, person is-a warm-blooded, person is-a two-legged), each level can spread out. In flat space, those branches collide and get squashed. Text descriptions provide the labels for these levels, and they act as anchors, pulling the image features into alignment. This avoids the bottleneck that flat space creates, where there is not enough distance room for all the levels.

When infrared and visible features are both embedded in this curved space, guided by the same text anchors, they naturally align on the hierarchical structure. Fine details (the exact texture of the blue shirt, the exact heat signature) have their own space to live, separate from coarse distinctions (person versus background). This prevents the texture distortion that happens when flat-space methods try to force both scales into the same limited set of distances.

Math after the picture

Placing features in curved space with text guidance

First in words:

alignment loss = sum over images and text anchors of (distance in curved space between image features and text anchors, plus a term that spreads out fine details to prevent overlap)

Then the compact form:

L = sum_i d_H(x_i, t_i) + lambda * sum_j d_H(f_j, f_k) where d_H is distance in curved space and lambda encourages separation

Every symbol:

x_ithe feature vector for image i, placed in curved space
t_ithe text anchor for image i, placed in curved space
d_H(a,b)distance between points a and b in curved space (expands faster at the edges)
f_j, f_kfine-detail features that are kept separate by the lambda term
lambdaa weight controlling how strongly details are spread out
A tiny worked example
Say we have one infrared feature and its text anchor person. In curved space, they are distance 2.5 apart. The loss from this pair is 2.5.
We also have a visible feature for the blue shirt (a fine detail). We want it separated from a feature for the brown background. In curved space, we place them distance 1.8 apart. With lambda = 0.1, the spreading penalty is 0.1 times 1.8 = 0.18.
Total loss = 2.5 + 0.18 = 2.68. During training, the model nudges features closer to their text anchors and farther from competing details, minimizing this loss.

The curved-space distance naturally gives coarse and fine features their own room, so the single loss term prevents both saturation and texture distortion.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Text descriptions are shallow. If BLIP only generates labels like person, building instead of deeper attribute hierarchies like person wearing formal jacket, the anchors are too coarse. Fine details have less structure to attach to, and texture distortion returns. Use a richer text model or caption generator.
Placement in curved space collapses if not careful. If image features and text anchors are placed in curved space without careful initialization, features can cluster at the center (where the space is nearly flat and has no hierarchical room). Then you lose the benefit. Requires proper mathematical optimization that respects the curved shape.
Test-time adaptation fails on very different scenes. The method learns to drop text input at test time and predict alignment from images alone. If a new scene has very different content from training (e.g., a scene with no visible people when training saw mostly people), the learned patterns may not generalize. The space was tuned for the training data's semantic hierarchy.
Done right: handles mismatched image qualities well. When infrared and visible have very different appearances (thermal blur versus sharp color), curved space and text guidance keep them aligned because they anchor to the same abstract concept hierarchy, not raw pixel similarity. The space naturally tolerates the difference.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small fusion demo: take 10 pairs of infrared-visible images from a dataset (thermal and RGB aligned). Train the model with text descriptions extracted by BLIP. At test time, drop the text and run fusion on new image pairs. Measure two things: (1) how well fine textures survive in the fused output (compare edges and details to a reference), and (2) how faithfully the coarse scene structure (person location, background) is preserved (measure alignment with ground truth masks). A good curved-space method should win on both metrics without trading one off for the other.

texture_score = edge_similarity(fused, reference); structure_score = intersection_over_union(foreground_mask); total = 0.5 * texture_score + 0.5 * structure_score

The key proof: fine details and coarse structure both improve together, not separately. This happens because curved space naturally gives each hierarchical level its own distance range, preventing the bottleneck that flat space creates. Flat methods always sacrifice one for the other because distances are limited.
