EVEREST: Transformer for Rare-Event Anomaly Detection

A neural model that forecasts rare dangerous events by separately modeling normal uncertainty, extreme tail risks, and early warning signs.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Predicting rare catastrophic events in time-series data is hard: there are almost never examples of the bad thing happening, making it almost impossible for a model to learn what it looks like. EVEREST tackles this by splitting the prediction job into three independent parts: one head estimates how much we should doubt our forecast (measured two ways), another specifically learns the behavior of extreme events, and a third watches for early warning signs. This lets the model learn from both the everyday data and rare historical disasters.

Zero assumption version

Why rare-event forecasting breaks

A naive approach: train a standard model on all your historical data, then ask it to predict whether a rare event happens next. The problem is that rare events appear in your data almost never—maybe 0.1% of the time. The model sees the normal state 999 times for every 1 time it sees the rare state, so it learns to predict 'normal' almost always. This is cheap: it gets 99.9% accuracy by just guessing 'normal' every time. But when the rare event actually comes, the model misses it completely. Even worse, when a model is trained on imbalanced data like this, it often gives overconfident predictions—it says 'definitely normal' with 99% confidence when it should say 'I'm not sure.'

The second problem is distributional uncertainty: the extreme behaviors are so rare and different from everyday data that the model has no good way to estimate how confident it should be about whether an extreme event is coming. This means predictions lack calibration—they do not honestly reflect how sure the model is.

In one sentence. Rare events are so underrepresented in training data that standard models either miss them or give false confidence, especially in forecasting extreme tail behaviors.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Temporal aggregation

A learnable bottleneck compresses time-series history into key events, not naive averaging.

Uncertainty quantification

Outputs a Bayesian distribution (Normal-Inverse-Gamma) to capture both measurement noise and model doubt.

Extreme-value modeling

A separate head learns the tail—how rare events actually behave—using Generalized Pareto Distribution.

Early-warning detection

A lightweight precursor head watches for subtle pre-disaster patterns in the signal.

Composite training loss

Four losses (focal, evidential, tail-sensitive, precursor) train each head jointly, but only one head runs when answering a new question.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of one prediction head that outputs a single number (or class), EVEREST splits the job: one head learns uncertainty (capturing doubt and randomness), another learns tail risk (how extreme events really behave), and a third learns precursor signals (early hints). Each head specializes, so each can be trained on its own signal and loss function. At test time, only the single-best head runs, so there is no extra cost when answering a new question.

The hidden quantity

Aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty—two kinds of doubt

measured quantity  =  Aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty

Aleatoric uncertainty is randomness inherent to the world: even if the sun rises predictably, its exact brightness fluctuates. Epistemic uncertainty is doubt from incomplete knowledge: if you have only seen 10 days of weather, you are less sure about month-50 than month-50 forecast based on 10 years. The paper separately models both using a Normal-Inverse-Gamma distribution (a mathematical form that produces both a mean prediction and a pair of uncertainty estimates). This matters for rare events because the few disasters in your historical data leave you genuinely uncertain—that epistemic doubt must be surfaced.

The paper estimates these by training the evidential head to output the four numbers that define a Normal-Inverse-Gamma distribution, not just point predictions. When answering a new question, the model outputs not just 'disaster happens at 10%' but 'based on the data, the true probability is somewhere between 5% and 20%, and there is also inherent randomness of ±2%'—honest uncertainty that reflects the rarity of historical examples.

Why it works

Why splitting the heads works

Standard training on imbalanced data pulls the model toward predicting 'normal' because that is the safe choice. But rare events have their own structure: they follow specific patterns that only appear at extreme tail values. A single head cannot learn both 'normal behavior' and 'tail behavior' at once—it gets confused and downweights the rare signals. By giving the tail-risk head its own training signal (Generalized Pareto Distribution fitted to extremes), the model learns the mechanics of disasters independently from everyday data. The uncertainty head is trained with evidential loss (a Bayesian technique), which penalizes overconfidence and rewards models that output high doubt when examples are rare. The precursor head watches for time-series anomalies that precede events—subtle signal changes that the main heads might miss. When answering new questions, the model runs all three heads and combines them, but during training each learns its own specialization. This separation means each head gets a clear, unambiguous learning signal instead of three heads competing for the same resources.

The focal loss (used in the main training) upweights hard, easy-to-miss examples—exactly the rare events. Combined with tail-aware penalties, the model is forced to allocate capacity to both everyday accuracy and extreme-event detection. The result is a model that does not ignore rare events to chase overall accuracy.

The composite training objective

First in words:

Total loss = focal loss (handles imbalance) + evidential negative log-likelihood (measures aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty) + tail-sensitive extreme-value penalty (captures Pareto tail) + precursor loss (early-detection signal). Each term pulls the model toward a different goal; jointly they create a model honest about rare events.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_focal + L_evidence + lambda_tail * L_EVT + L_precursor

Every symbol:

L_focalLoss that upweights hard-to-predict rare events
L_evidenceBayesian loss: penalizes overconfidence when data is scarce
L_EVTExtreme-value penalty: enforces Pareto tail structure on rare outcomes
lambda_tailWeight controlling how much to emphasize tail risk
L_precursorLoss for early-warning signal detection
A tiny worked example
Suppose on day 1 we predict normal (easy case). Focal loss: near-zero, model was right.
On day 100 (a rare disaster), we predict disaster with 30% confidence. Focal loss: large penalty because the model struggled (hard example gets upweighted).
Evidential loss: if we have only 5 historical disasters to learn from, evidence loss is large (high uncertainty penalty) unless the model output high doubt, like 'probability 50% ± 30%'.
Tail loss: if our Pareto-fit says tail events follow a specific shape and our prediction violates that shape, lambda_tail * L_EVT penalizes it.
Precursor loss: on day 99 (one day before disaster), if the early-warning head outputs high alert and focal loss is not zero, precursor loss flows backward through the neural network layers, teaching the head to recognize pre-disaster signals.

The sum of four different losses forces the model to be simultaneously accurate on common days, honest about uncertainty on rare days, truthful about extreme behavior, and attentive to early hints.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fixed input length. EVEREST requires fixed-length time windows; real-time streaming (where the window slides dynamically) requires architectural changes the paper does not address.
No image modalities. The model only handles multivariate numerical time-series; if your rare-event data includes images (e.g., satellite photos before a storm), EVEREST cannot use them directly.
Extreme-value fitting requires tail examples. The Generalized Pareto Distribution is fitted to historical tail observations; if you have only one or two rare events, the tail model may be unreliable. The uncertainty head becomes even more important in that case.
Four losses means four hyperparameters. Tuning focal loss focus, evidential loss temperature, lambda_tail, and precursor weight is non-trivial; mistuning can cause one head to dominate and others to atrophy.
Done right. When tail data is present, loss weights are balanced, and the bottleneck is trained end-to-end, EVEREST produces calibrated, tail-aware predictions that catch rare events without false alarms.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic time-series dataset: 1000 days of normal weather (temperature, humidity, pressure) plus 5 disaster days (extreme values with a specific pattern). Train a standard neural model with one head and EVEREST side by side on the first 900 days. On the held-out 100 days, log the predictions. Run three tests: (1) On days 1–95 (normal), does EVEREST's confidence calibrate correctly (i.e., when it predicts 97% confidence, is it right 97% of the time)? Standard model will be overconfident (says 99% confidence when it is right 97% of the time). (2) On days 96–100 (the 5 disasters in the test set), does EVEREST flag them as high-risk with its tail head, or does it miss them? Standard model should miss them or flag them with false high confidence. (3) Does the precursor head raise an alert on day 99.5 (one half-day before the first disaster), giving you time to act?

Calibration error = sum of |predicted_confidence - actual_accuracy|; True Skill Statistic = (true_positives * true_negatives - false_positives * false_negatives) / (all_positives * all_negatives); precursor alert lag (hours before actual rare event).

The demo must show that EVEREST either catches rare events that the naive model misses OR gives honest uncertainty ('I am not sure') instead of false confidence, on data the model has never seen before.
