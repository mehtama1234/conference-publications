Cultivating Pluralism In Algorithmic Monoculture: The Community Alignment Dataset

When all language models give the same boring answers, you cannot teach them to respect the messy diversity of human values—so generate wildly different candidate responses before asking humans to choose.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models today produce responses that sound too similar to each other. While humans around the world care about wildly different things—family, tradition, individual freedom, daily survival—the models trained on preference data learn only to mimic the narrow slice of human values already baked into the training set. The paper shows that generating diverse candidate responses (using a technique called negatively-correlated sampling) before asking humans to rank them lets alignment methods finally capture the full spectrum of what people actually want.

Zero assumption version

Models collapse into monoculture

Today's language models are trained to predict the next token (word or subword piece)—a single task that makes them converge. Twenty different top-performing models, when asked the same question, often pick the same top answer because they have learned similar patterns from similar data. Meanwhile, humans across the world genuinely disagree. In one study of people from five countries, humans split sharply on questions about tradition versus change, or individual freedom versus collective survival. But the models? All 21 tested only matched 41% of what real people actually wanted.

A naive attempt to fix this: collect human preference data the old way—show people a few candidate answers and ask which they prefer. But if all the candidates sound the same (because they all came from the same model or models that think alike), then the humans cannot express their real disagreement. You might as well ask someone to choose between cola and cola. Even with fancy alignment techniques like DPO (direct preference optimization), you cannot learn preferences that are not visible in the data.

The method in one sentence. Generate wildly different candidate responses before asking humans to rank them, so alignment methods can see and learn the full diversity of human preferences.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how candidate responses are generated before humans see them. Instead of asking a single model or similar models for multiple tries at the same question, the paper uses negatively-correlated sampling: a technique that deliberately pushes each new candidate to be different from the ones already made. The effect is that where the old method might generate four versions of 'the answer is yes because tradition matters', the new method generates 'yes because tradition', 'no because change is good', 'yes because duty to family', and 'it depends on context'—four genuinely different takes that let humans actually disagree.

the object it moves  =  candidate response generation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Candidate diversity

measured quantity  =  Variance in candidate responses

The hidden quantity is how different the candidate responses are from each other. In the old system, if you generate four candidate answers to the same question, they might all cluster around the same mode (the most likely answer the model learned). Diversity is low; it is as if the model is a ball rolling downhill and getting stuck at the bottom. Candidate diversity measures whether the candidates explore different parts of the response space.

The paper does not explicitly measure diversity as a single number, but it infers it from alignment performance: if the method works well—that is, if humans' ranked preferences now match their stated values—then the candidates must have been diverse enough. The paper estimates this implicitly by showing that four standard alignment methods (prompt-steering, SFT, DPO, GRPO) all perform better with negatively-correlated candidates than with standard temperature-sampled candidates. Better downstream performance signals that the candidates explored the space of possible preferences more thoroughly, because when an alignment method (like DPO) trains on diverse preference examples, it sees patterns linking different response styles to different annotators, so it learns to respect multiple viewpoints instead of converging to one.

Plain reading. Candidate diversity is the amount of different viewpoints in the menu a human sees. Low diversity means all options converge to similar opinions. High diversity means the options disagree—some value tradition, others value change; some emphasize duty, others emphasize freedom.
Paper reading. The paper operationalizes diversity through the success of downstream alignment: if the preference-learning method (DPO, GRPO, etc.) achieves higher accuracy on held-out human preferences after training on negatively-correlated candidates versus standard temperature-sampled ones, then the candidates must have had higher diversity. This is inferred diversity, not directly measured, but it is the right quantity because diversity only matters insofar as it lets humans express and methods learn heterogeneous preferences.
Why it works

Why it works: The causal chain

Here is the mechanical reason the method works. Standard language model sampling (temperature sampling) draws tokens based on how likely they are according to the model's learned probabilities. If the model has learned that response A is very likely and response B is unlikely, repeated sampling stays close to A. This is the monoculture: all models converge because they have learned similar probabilities from similar data. Negatively-correlated sampling breaks this by actively penalizing candidates that already exist in the set. When the algorithm generates candidate 1, it records what it generated. When it tries to generate candidate 2, it does not just sample from the model; it also applies a penalty that says avoid making candidate 2 look like candidate 1. This penalty forces candidate 2 to explore a different region of the response space. Candidate 3 is penalized against both 1 and 2, and so on.

Now when a human annotator sees these four candidates—each pushed to be different—they can actually express disagreement. The annotator might pick candidate 3 over candidate 1 because 3 aligns with their values, while someone else picks candidate 1. This visible preference is the signal. Standard alignment methods (DPO, GRPO) then learn a reward function: which features of the preferred candidate explain why it won? If the candidate set was diverse, the method can learn 'oh, when the response emphasizes family duty, this annotator prefers it' and 'when it emphasizes individual freedom, that annotator prefers it'. The diversity in the candidate set acts like a control experiment: by varying the response style, you isolate which aspects humans actually care about.

Math after the picture

The equation: Negatively-correlated sampling penalty

First in words:

Each new candidate incurs a penalty proportional to how similar it is to candidates already generated in this batch.

Then the compact form:

penalty_i = sum_j similarity(candidate_i, candidate_j) for all j < i

Every symbol:

candidate_ithe i-th response being generated
candidate_ja response already in the set (j less than i)
similarity(A, B)a score measuring how alike two responses are (e.g., cosine distance between embeddings)
penalty_ithe cost incurred if candidate_i is too similar to earlier candidates
A tiny worked example
Suppose we measure similarity as a number from 0 (completely different) to 1 (identical).
Candidate 1: 'Family bonds matter most' (generated first, no penalty because there are no other candidates yet).
Candidate 2: 'Individual freedom matters most' (similarity to Candidate 1 = 0.2, low penalty because they disagree).
Candidate 3: 'Both matter, but family comes first' (similarity to Candidate 1 = 0.6, similarity to Candidate 2 = 0.4; total penalty = 0.6 + 0.4 = 1.0). This is higher, but the candidate is still allowed because it explores middle ground.
Candidate 4: 'Family bonds matter most' (similarity to Candidate 1 = 0.95, similarity to Candidate 2 = 0.2, similarity to Candidate 3 = 0.7; total penalty = 0.95 + 0.2 + 0.7 = 1.85). Sampling algorithm heavily discourages this because it is too close to Candidate 1.
Result: Candidates 1–3 are kept (diverse). Candidate 4 is rejected or re-sampled because it is a copy.

The penalty does not prevent sampling a similar candidate outright, but it makes the model explore other parts of what it learned, discovering responses it otherwise would not have generated.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forcing diversity too hard creates nonsense. If the penalty for similarity is too strong, the algorithm might generate candidates that are valid language but do not answer the question—e.g., 'Bananas are purple' when asked about family values. The diversity must be in the space of legitimate, coherent responses, not random noise.
Similarity metric matters. Negatively-correlated sampling relies on a similarity function to detect when two candidates are too alike. If the metric is broken (e.g., it treats 'I agree with A' and 'I disagree with A' as equally different), then the penalty does not work, and you fall back into monoculture.
Humans may not explore the menu. If the diverse candidates are presented poorly (e.g., all at once, overwhelming the annotator), humans might still default to their favorite and ignore the others. The diversity only matters if humans actually engage with it.
Done right. With careful choice of similarity metric, a reasonable penalty weight, and clear presentation of candidates, negatively-correlated sampling exposes the true diversity of human preferences, and standard alignment methods learn to respect it.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A runnable demo should take a real question (e.g., 'Should companies choose profit or employee welfare?'), generate four candidate answers using both standard temperature sampling and negatively-correlated sampling, show a human the two sets of candidates, and measure how much the human's preference varies across the sets. With standard sampling, the candidates cluster around one view (e.g., all lean pro-profit). With negatively-correlated sampling, the candidates span the space (pro-profit, pro-employee, balanced, context-dependent). The demo should then fine-tune an alignment method on preference data collected with each set and measure how many different human values the trained model learns to respect. This proves that diverse candidates let methods learn pluralism.

pluralism_score = (fraction of held-out human preferences the trained model respects)

The critical observation: with standard candidates, pluralism_score will be low because the alignment method never saw minority preferences. With negatively-correlated candidates, pluralism_score will be high because the method had diverse data to learn from. The gap between these two scores is the entire point of the paper.
