Learning to Recall with Transformers Beyond Orthogonal Embeddings

Transformers can store and retrieve facts from real random embeddings, and the amount it can remember depends multiplicatively on data size, embedding width, and sequence length.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

When a language model learns a fact (like 'Paris is the capital of France'), it must store that mapping inside its weights and then pull it back out when asked. We usually assume in theory that these weights live in perfect, perfectly-spaced locations, but real models use jumbled random placements. This paper shows how many examples a transformer (a type of neural network that uses a learned focusing mechanism to spotlight the most relevant parts of the input) needs to learn reliable recall from messy, real embeddings, and reveals that memory capacity grows multiplicatively with three factors: how many training examples you have, the width of the embedding space, and how long the sequence can be.

Zero assumption version

Why transformers struggle to learn recall under real conditions

A language model learns to answer questions by encoding facts into its weight matrices during training, then using a learned focusing mechanism (which picks out the important words in the input) to retrieve the right fact when needed. Theoretical work on this has always made a simplifying assumption: the embedding space (the numerical space where words live as lists of numbers) is perfectly organized, like a grid with equal spacing in all directions. Real transformers, however, start with random embeddings, where this perfect spacing does not exist. The theoretical analysis breaks down.

A naive approach would guess: 'Just throw more examples at it, and the model will figure it out.' But how many is enough? And does the answer depend on embedding width, sequence length, and training set size in some complex way? Without theory, we cannot predict when recall learning will actually work.

The question the paper answers: What is the minimum model size and number of training examples needed for a transformer to reliably learn and recall facts when embeddings are random, not perfectly spaced?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts the theoretical lens from 'perfect orthogonal embeddings' (unrealistic) to 'random non-orthogonal embeddings' (what real models use). This means the trainable numbers in the focusing and memory layers must now learn without the luxury of perfectly-spaced directions to move in. The method stays the same (transformer with a learned focusing mechanism plus a feedforward memory layer), but the analysis of what model size guarantees success is entirely different.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Storage capacity

measured quantity  =  Storage capacity (mappings the model can reliably memorize)

Storage capacity is the maximum number of distinct word-to-label mappings a transformer can reliably memorize. Think of it like the number of phone numbers you can remember by heart: if you can hold 50 numbers reliably, that is your storage capacity. For a transformer, capacity is determined by how many examples it sees (sample size N), the width of the embedding space (dimension d), and the maximum sequence length (length L). The paper derives an explicit formula showing that capacity grows as a product of these three factors—triple one of them, triple the capacity.

The paper estimates this hidden quantity through a theoretical analysis of training (how the model updates its weights to reduce errors). Starting from random embeddings, the paper tracks how the focusing and memory layer weights move over the first few training steps, deriving the exact conditions under which the model can learn a one-to-one word-to-label mapping perfectly.

Why it works

Why it works: The two-stage retrieval-and-recall dance

A transformer solves recall in two linked steps. First, the focusing mechanism learns to zero in on the informative word even though embeddings are random. Normally, random embeddings are useless for this—every direction looks equally random. But training updates, over multiple steps, align the query and key matrices so that the dot products between query and the informative key become much larger than the dot products with noise keys. This focusing-sharpening succeeds because the signal is repetitive: every example has the answer at the same position, so the pattern is learnable.

Second, the MLP layer builds an associative memory: it learns to map the focused-on word to the correct output. The MLP's hidden layer can encode a lookup table in its weights. But here is the catch: the embedding space is random, so the MLP must route through random directions. This requires more capacity than routing through orthogonal directions. The paper shows that training can solve this, but only if the model has enough parameters. Below a critical size, the noise from random embeddings drowns out the signal; above it, the sharpness of focusing and the expressiveness of the MLP combine to encode the memory reliably. The phase transition is sharp: just-barely-too-small fails completely, just-barely-big-enough succeeds perfectly.

The capacity formula and what every part means

First in words:

For learning with Attention-MLP, the model width m must satisfy: vocab_size≤model_signal_strength divided by (gradient_noise_per_step plus mean_bias_error plus MLP_encoding_cost).

Then the compact form:

V ≤ (signal) / (gradient_noise + mean_bias + MLP_noise) where signal ≡ d, gradient_noise ≡ sqrt(L*d) / (N*sqrt(d)), mean_bias ≡ sqrt(V*d) / (N*d), MLP_noise ≡ sqrt(V) / (N*sqrt(m))

Concrete example: can a 10-token vocabulary be learned?
Suppose d=16 (embedding width), L=8 (sequence length), N=40 (training examples), m=64 (MLP width).
Signal = 16.
Gradient noise ≈ sqrt(8*16) / (40*sqrt(16)) = sqrt(128)/160 ≈ 0.09.
Mean bias ≈ sqrt(10*16) / (40*16) = sqrt(160)/640 ≈ 0.06.
MLP noise ≈ sqrt(10) / (40*sqrt(64)) ≈ 0.04.
Total denominator ≈ 0.09 + 0.06 + 0.04 = 0.19.
Capacity ≈ 16/0.19 ≈ 84 distinct labels. Vocabulary of 10 fits easily; 100 would just fail.

The formula shows storage capacity grows multiplicatively in N, d, and L: triple the embedding width and you triple how many labels the model can remember.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Vocabulary too large relative to model. If the vocabulary size V grows faster than the model width m, the MLP cannot encode enough distinct mappings, because a small MLP has limited capacity in its hidden layers to store a lookup table—think of it as a filing cabinet that can only hold so many distinct labels before it runs out of drawers. The formulas show that m must grow at least as fast as d*sqrt(d*L/N) to keep pace with V.
Too few training examples. Training needs enough examples to average out the noise in random embeddings. If N is too small, learning updates remain noisy and the focusing mechanism cannot reliably narrow in on the answer.
Embeddings that are biased, not just random. The analysis assumes truly random embeddings. If embeddings have hidden structure (e.g., some tokens cluster together), the theoretical bounds may not hold.
Done right: all three factors align. When N is large enough, m and d are balanced, and L is reasonable (not exponentially long), training on even messy random embeddings can reliably learn factual recall.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a single-layer transformer with random embeddings trained on a synthetic token-retrieval task: given a sequence of random tokens, one token is marked as the answer; the model must identify it and map it to one of several labels. Train the transformer for a few gradient steps on N training examples, varying N, d, L, and m. Plot the final accuracy (on held-out examples) as a function of these four parameters. The core claim is that below a threshold in m, accuracy is near-random (~1/V); above it, accuracy jumps to near-perfect (~95%+). Verify that the threshold scales exactly as predicted: multiply N by 10 and the required m shrinks; multiply L by 4 and the required m grows by roughly 2.
