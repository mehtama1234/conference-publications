Taming Polysemanticity via Neuron Resonance

Neurons learn single features when you tune their activation frequency to match how often that feature appears in the data.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models hide their internal logic. To peek inside, researchers use Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) — machines that find individual features (like the concept of "dog" or "verb") buried in the model's activations. But SAE training is brittle: hyperparameters are finicky, and neurons often learn sloppy features that mix multiple meanings. This paper fixes that by discovering that neurons work best when their firing rate exactly matches the feature's occurrence rate in data.

Zero assumption version

Neurons learn messy features when trained naively

Large language models are black boxes. Inside, they store patterns (a neuron fires when it sees "doctor" or "sick"; another fires for "verb endings"). Finding these patterns is how we decode what the model learned.

A Sparse Autoencoder is a helper machine that reads the model's internal numbers and says: I found 256 features. It compresses the model's hidden state (say, 4096 numbers) into a sparse code (256 numbers, most zero), then reconstructs the original. When working well, each of those 256 features is pure: one neuron fires only for "dog concepts", another only for "negative sentiment".

But existing training methods fail. The standard approach: turn a dial (the sparsity penalty) and hope neurons spread out. But turn it wrong and neurons either all sleep (learn nothing) or all wake up (learn garbage). The neural network's bias parameters — tiny adjustments that nudge neurons to fire — are never tuned with purpose. So neurons fire erratically, mixing 5 different meanings into one neuron.

The problem. Sparse Autoencoders find features but train poorly and unreliably because nobody was tuning the neurons' baseline firing rates.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure activation frequency

Count: how often does each neuron fire across the training data?

Decide target frequencies

Rare features fire 1% of the time; common ones fire 15%. Group neurons by what they should learn.

Adjust the bias

The bias is a number added before the neuron fires (like a threshold). Nudge it to bring the neuron's actual firing rate toward its target.

Train the encoder-decoder

Let the main weights learn the feature patterns while bias is periodically tweaked.

Verify single features

Check: each neuron's top activations are one topic, not a mix.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper tunes the neural network's bias parameters — the tiny numbers added before each neuron decides to fire. Normally these are random or ignored. This paper actively adjusts them to control how often neurons fire.

the object it moves  =  neuron bias values
The hidden quantity

Neuron activation frequency

measured quantity  =  how often a neuron fires

The activation frequency is a percentage: out of all the training examples, on how many does this neuron actually turn on? A neuron with activation frequency 5% fires for 1 out of every 20 examples.

The paper discovered something striking: if a neuron's activation frequency matches the frequency of a feature in the data, the neuron learns that feature cleanly. A rare feature (like "Shakespeare reference") appears 2% of the time; tune the bias so the neuron fires 2% of the time, and it learns to detect exactly that. Swap the frequencies and the neuron becomes confused, mixing in unrelated patterns.

Plain reading. Activation frequency is just a count: out of N examples, how many times did this neuron turn on? If it's 5, then frequency is 5/N.
Paper reading. The paper defines activation frequency as the fraction of input examples in a data buffer where the neuron's pre-activation (the sum before the neuron fires) exceeds the bias. The key finding (neuron resonance) is that when this frequency matches the feature's ground-truth occurrence frequency in the data, the neuron recovers that feature exactly.
Why it works

Why matching firing rates works

Here's the concrete reason. A feature (say, "question mark at sentence end") appears in some examples but not others. If you train a neuron to fire whenever it detects this feature, its firing rate should match the feature's rate. If the feature appears in 10% of sentences, the neuron should fire 10% of the time.

The bias is the threshold. Too high and the neuron rarely fires (misses the feature). Too low and it fires all the time (false alarms). The paper's insight: watch the actual firing rate, and slowly adjust the bias toward the target. When they match, the neuron's learned pattern now cleanly splits feature-present from feature-absent. The neuron has resonated with the feature.

Why does this beat traditional regularization? Sparsity penalties (like the L1 norm, a cost for firing) are indirect — they squish all neurons equally. Bias adaptation targets each neuron individually by its actual firing rate. A neuron that should fire 1% gets pulled harder than one that should fire 20%. This precision beats a one-size-fits-all penalty.

The bias adaptation rule

First in words:

For each neuron, compute its actual firing rate (fraction of examples where pre-activation exceeds bias). If it is below target, lower the bias (make it easier to fire). If above, raise the bias (make it harder). Repeat periodically until the neuron's firing rate matches its target.

Then the compact form:

b_m := b_m - step_size * (actual_freq_m - target_freq_m)

Every symbol:

b_mthe bias (threshold) for neuron m
step_sizehow much to nudge the bias per update
actual_freq_mfraction of examples where pre-activation > b_m
target_freq_mdesired firing rate for neuron m (e.g., 0.05)
A tiny worked example
Neuron learns dog-concepts; should fire 10% of the time. Bias starts at 2.5.
After a buffer of 1000 examples, neuron fired 240 times -> actual frequency 24%.
Target is 10%, actual is 24%. Difference: +14%.
step_size = 0.5. Update: b_new = 2.5 - 0.5 * 0.14 = 2.5 - 0.07 = 2.43.
Higher bias makes firing harder. Next buffer: 120 fires out of 1000 -> 12% (closer).
Bias continues adjusting until frequency is stable around 10%.

The bias slides left or right to trap each neuron at the right firing rate, where it learns its true feature.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong target frequencies. If you set target frequencies poorly (guessing instead of measuring features), neurons converge to the wrong rate and learn useless patterns.
Bias stuck at extremes. If a feature is truly absent from the data, no bias can make the neuron learn it. The algorithm stops trying; the neuron sleeps.
Oscillation if step size is too large. Nudge the bias too aggressively, and the neuron overshoots its target rate, then swings the other way. Smaller step sizes fix this but slow convergence.
Done right: reliable feature discovery. When target frequencies are set from actual data and step sizes are tuned to be gentle, neurons converge to their targets and learn clean, interpretable features consistently.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a Sparse Autoencoder on a small language model (e.g., GPT-2 small, 12 million parameters) with and without Group Bias Adaptation. Log each neuron's actual firing rate during training. Measure how often neurons' top-10 activations share a common theme (e.g., all about animals, all about syntax). Compare monosemanticity (purity of single features) between the method and baseline.

monosemanticity = (neurons with >90% top-activations from one theme) / total neurons

The demo succeeds when GBA neurons reach 70%+ monosemanticity while existing methods plateau below 50%.
