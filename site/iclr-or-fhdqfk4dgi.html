Micro-Macro Coupled Koopman Modeling on Graph for Traffic Flow Prediction

A unified model that predicts both how individual cars move and how traffic density changes by lifting the real nonlinear dynamics into a simpler linear space.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Traffic has two sides that fight each other: what individual cars do (swerving, accelerating, changing lanes) and how the overall flow moves (congestion waves, smooth-flowing highways). Traditional models pick one side or the other. This paper shows how to model both at once by using a mathematical trick called Koopman lifting: translate the messy real-world motion into a high-dimensional space where the dynamics become linear and predictable, then translate predictions back.

Zero assumption version

Two models, two failures

Microscopic models (focus on one car at a time) do a good job predicting what an individual driver will do next—turn left, speed up, stay put—because they learn from detailed car-by-car sequences. But they miss the forest: when lots of cars bunch together, they move as a wave, and that wave has rules the car-level model never learned, because the model sees each car in isolation and never learns how density and flow feedback into individual motion.

Macroscopic models (focus on the highway as a whole) capture the wave: they see traffic density (how crowded a stretch of road is) and predict how fast it will clear. But they flatten the picture—they lose the fact that a solo car is still a solo car, and a truck behaves differently than a motorcycle.

The worst part: older methods that try both require you to feed them long histories of past car positions. In real traffic, you often don't have that—you just have now.

In one sentence. Koopman lifts microscopic and macroscopic dynamics into a shared linear space so a single model predicts both vehicle motion and flow evolution from the current state alone.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of separate models for cars and flow, MMCKM builds one operator that acts on a joint representation of both. The operator is linear in Koopman space, so it avoids the nonlinear prediction collapse that derails simpler models.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Koopman linearity

measured quantity  =  how much the dynamics become linear

Real traffic motion is nonlinear: a car's next speed depends on the squared distance to the car ahead, densities interact in waves, and accidents create sudden jumps. A Koopman operator is a hidden transformation that maps this nonlinear world into a high-dimensional space where motion follows a simple linear rule (apply the same operator again and again). The paper measures this by checking whether predictions in Koopman space stay on track: if the lifted dynamics are truly linear, repeated applications of the operator should forecast accurately.

The paper estimates Koopman linearity by learning scenario-adaptive operators—different evolvers for different driving intents—so that each piece of the dynamics (aggressive driving, cautious driving, flow blockage) has its own linear rule. Better linearity means fewer steps where the prediction drifts, because a true linear rule can be applied over and over without accumulating error from nonlinear terms that were not captured in the model.

Why it works

Why it works: Lifting to simplicity

Traffic prediction fails at nonlinearity. A car moving 5 meters per second in bumper-to-bumper traffic responds differently than the same car moving 5 meters per second on an empty highway. The collision risk is not linear; it follows a curve. Densities don't add linearly either—a highway with half density does not move at half speed.

Koopman's trick: instead of modeling the real state space directly, map into a much higher-dimensional space where the observed dynamics are linear. Think of it like rotating your view: a spiraling tornado looks like a straight line if you rotate your viewpoint to face it head-on. Once in that space, a single linear operator (a set of rules that say: multiply each dimension by a specific number and add a specific offset) predicts the next step correctly. Apply it again for step two. This works because the high dimensions absorb the nonlinearity into the encoding itself.

The paper adds a second insight: traffic intent matters. A car that is about to brake is in a different regime than one about to accelerate. The Intent Discriminator (a small classifier that sees position and guesses intent) switches which Koopman operator applies. So instead of one global linear rule, there are several scenario-specific linear rules, and the right one is chosen in real time. This keeps the operator simple and keeps errors from compounding.

The coupling is explicit: each car's dynamics are influenced by overall flow density (a macro quantity pushed down to micro level), and density evolution is influenced by individual car motions (micro pushed up). In Koopman space, both feedback paths are embedded in the operator, so a single forward step captures both.

Math after the picture

The equation: scenario-specific linear dynamics

First in words:

For each intent i, the next state in Koopman space is the operator applied to the current state. The operator is a matrix that encodes both micro and macro evolution and their coupling.

Then the compact form:

z_{t+1}^i = A_i z_t^i

Every symbol:

z_t^istate in Koopman space at time t under intent i (high-dimensional vector)
A_ilinear operator matrix for intent class i (micro-macro coupled evolution)
z_{t+1}^ipredicted next state, also in Koopman space
A tiny worked example
Suppose at time 0 in Koopman space, a 3-element state is z_0 = [2, 1, 3] (an encoded view of two cars and flow density).
Intent detected: aggressive (A_aggressive chosen).
Apply operator: z_1 = A_aggressive * z_0 = [0.8, 0.1, 0.2] * 2 + [0.1, 0.7, 0.3] * 1 + [0.1, 0.2, 0.5] * 3 = [1.6 + 0.1 + 0.3, 0.2 + 0.7 + 0.6, 0.4 + 0.3 + 1.5] = [2.0, 1.5, 2.2].
Next step: apply the same A_aggressive again to z_1 to forecast further.
Back in real space: decode z_1 to get actual car positions and flow density.

The operator is learned so that when applied repeatedly, it forecasts both car trajectories and traffic density accurately without requiring past history.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Sudden accidents break linearity. If a car crashes or a lane closes, the nonlinear shock does not fit in any Koopman operator learned from normal traffic. The model predicts the continuation of the pre-crash trend, not the actual congestion wave.
Intent guessing is fragile. If the intent discriminator misfires—guessing brake when the car is actually accelerating—it applies the wrong operator, and the forecast drifts immediately.
Graph connectivity matters. If nearby cars are not connected in the dynamic graph, their influence is not captured. A distant car in a separate cluster will be forecasted as if alone.
Done right. With good intent classification and a well-learned scenario-specific operators, the model forecasts many steps ahead without diverging, handling both car-level detail and flow-level patterns at once.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulator with 50–100 cars on a circular track. Record their starting positions and intended accelerations (fast, slow, medium). Now hide the intents and give the model only current positions and densities. Have it predict 20 steps ahead. Compare predicted positions to ground-truth positions. Separately, measure predicted density error (compare predicted number of cars per section to actual count). The demo must show: (1) prediction accuracy stays stable across all 20 steps (Koopman linearity holds), (2) density predictions track the actual flow waves (macro coupling works), and (3) no historical data was needed—only the snapshot at step 0.

avg_position_error = mean(|predicted_car_i_x - actual_car_i_x| over all cars and all 20 steps); density_wave_mae = mean(|predicted_density_per_section - actual_density_per_section|); history_used = 0 frames.

A good demo must show that one model predicts both individual car positions and overall flow density accurately from only the current snapshot—something older methods could not do.
