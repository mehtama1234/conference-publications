Distributionally Robust Linear Regression with Block Lewis Weights

Find a prediction rule that works fairly well on every subgroup simultaneously, not just on average, by reweighting groups before solving.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When you train a predictor on mixed data (say, hospital records from different regions), a method that minimizes error on average might fail badly on small or unusual groups. This paper solves a harder problem: find one predictor that keeps the worst-group error as low as possible. The trick is a clever reweighting called block Lewis weights that turns this fairness problem into a standard least-squares setup, solvable with accelerated methods that converge fast.

Zero assumption version

The unfairness trap in regression

Suppose you have medical records from five hospitals. You train a linear model (a simple weighted-sum predictor) to predict patient recovery time. Standard training minimizes the average prediction error across all hospitals. But what if Hospital C is smaller? Its errors might be much larger, and no one notices, because averaging hides the worst performers.

A naive fix: just add a penalty that hits big errors harder (e.g., an ell-infinity rule: minimize the single worst error). This works mathematically, but solving it is slow—a standard method called interior point methods must invert a large rectangular table of numbers (a matrix) many times, and the cost grows with the number of groups. For 100 hospitals, solving becomes expensive.

The problem: Standard training hides unfair performance on small groups; solving fairness the obvious way is computationally expensive.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Identify the worst group

Check which hospital (or group) currently has the largest error.

Step 2: Compute block Lewis weights

Assign a reweight to each group based on its error size and how its data points are spread out, favoring groups that are hard to fit.

Step 3: Reweight the least-squares problem

Form a new weighted least-squares objective using the Lewis weights, now a standard convex problem.

Step 4: Apply accelerated optimization

Use mirror descent, a fast iterative method, to solve the reweighted problem efficiently.

Step 5: Iterate

Repeat until all groups have similar errors and the solution is within epsilon of optimal fairness.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the optimization method, not the predictor itself. Instead of solving the worst-group problem directly (which is slow), it reformulates it as a reweighted least-squares problem solvable in fewer steps.

the object it moves  =  optimization algorithm (the path to the solution)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Group importance

measured quantity  =  Block Lewis weight for each group

The paper measures and uses a quantity called a Lewis weight: a number for each group that says how much it should influence the training. A large Lewis weight means that group is hard to fit well, so training should pay close attention to it. The paper computes these weights by examining the data shape—specifically, how spread out each group's data points are and how badly a naive solution fits them.

Lewis weights are not new, but applying them to grouped regression is. The weights emerge from a mathematical construction called John's ellipsoid: imagine shrink-wrapping the tightest oval around your data cloud in each direction; the ellipsoid's shape tells you which directions matter most for balancing all groups simultaneously.

Paper reading. Block Lewis weights emerge from solving a geometric problem: find the minimum-volume ellipsoid that contains a certain polytope defined by the groups' data. The ellipsoid's axes tell you which groups are most constrained.
Why it works

Why it works: The reweighting trick

The core insight is a conversion: the worst-group problem (minimize the single largest group error) is mathematically equivalent to a reweighted least-squares problem, where groups with high Lewis weights contribute more to the total penalty. This conversion is the magic. Once you have the reweighted problem, you can use any fast convex optimizer; the paper uses mirror descent (a close cousin of gradient descent) accelerated by Nesterov's trick, which converges very quickly when you choose the right step sizes for the data shape.

Why does reweighting solve the fairness problem? Because in the reweighted problem, any group with a large error automatically contributes more to the total loss. Training thus naturally adjusts the predictor to shrink the biggest errors first. Groups that are easy to fit get low weight, so training can ignore them safely. Groups on the edge (high Lewis weight) are protected by becoming the dominant term in the objective. The result: all groups end up with similar, balanced errors.

Math after the picture

The reweighting equation

First in words:

minimize sum over all groups i of (Lewis weight for group i) times (squared prediction error for group i), where prediction error for group i is computed as the norm of residuals for that group scaled by group size

Then the compact form:

minimize_x sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i * (1/sqrt(n_i)) * ||A_i * x - b_i||_2^2

Every symbol:

xthe predictor weights we are learning
w_ithe Lewis weight for group i (computed from data geometry)
n_ithe size (number of samples) in group i
A_ithe data matrix for group i
b_ithe true values (targets) for group i
||...||_2the Euclidean length (norm) of the residual vector
A tiny worked example
Say we have 2 hospitals. Hospital 1: 10 patients, Lewis weight 1.0, residual norm 5. Hospital 2: 20 patients, Lewis weight 4.0, residual norm 3.
Hospital 1 penalty: 1.0 * (1/sqrt(10)) * 5^2 = 1.0 * 0.316 * 25 = 7.9
Hospital 2 penalty: 4.0 * (1/sqrt(20)) * 3^2 = 4.0 * 0.224 * 9 = 8.0
Penalties are nearly equal, even though Hospital 2 has lower raw error. Lewis weights enforced balance by giving Hotel 1 a lower weight (since it is harder to fit well) and Hospital 2 a higher weight (since it is easier). Training adjusts the predictor to keep both penalties in line.

The Lewis weights emerge from analyzing the data shape; they are computed once as a preprocessing step using existing efficient algorithms. Then the reweighted problem is solved with standard accelerated convex optimization.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Underspecified Lewis weights. If the data-shape computation is done incorrectly or on approximate data, Lewis weights can be inaccurate, and the reweighting does not balance fairly. The paper requires a correct data-shape analysis; mistakes there propagate.
Very imbalanced groups. If one group has 10 samples and another has 100,000, scaling by group size helps, but extreme imbalance can still cause numerical instability during optimization, because the matrix being inverted in each solver step becomes very ill-conditioned: some directions are much more sensitive than others, and rounding errors accumulate.
Nonlinear data. The method solves linear regression. If the true relationship is highly nonlinear, fair linear predictions may simply be impossible; the method can only balance error among bad fits.
Done right. Compute Lewis weights carefully, use double-precision arithmetic, and let the accelerated solver run until convergence. The result is a provably fair and near-optimal predictor.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic regression problem with multiple groups (say, 5 groups, 50 samples each, with different noise levels per group). Train using average least squares, then train using the block Lewis weight reweighting with accelerated mirror descent. Compare the largest error across groups before and after.

fairness_gain = (worst_group_error_average) - (worst_group_error_fair)

The demo must make it impossible to miss that Lewis-weighted training brings all groups to similar error levels, while average training leaves one group in the dust.
