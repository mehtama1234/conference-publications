Discovering Symmetry Groups with Flow Matching

LieFlow discovers what symmetries (rotations, reflections, repetitions) exist in data by learning which transformations leave the data unchanged.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Physical systems and real-world data often have hidden symmetries—patterns that stay the same under certain transformations like rotation or reflection. Finding these symmetries automatically is hard, yet knowing them can make machine learning models faster and more accurate. LieFlow solves this by treating symmetry discovery as a learning problem: instead of searching for the symmetry generators, it learns a probability model (assigning confidence scores to each candidate symmetry), and the candidate symmetries with the highest scores reveal which ones actually exist in the data.

Zero assumption version

Finding symmetries the naive way

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves something unchanged. A square looks the same after a 90-degree rotation, or flipped across a diagonal. Real data—point clouds from 3D scanners, molecular structures, images—often has symmetries baked in. A naive approach searches through a list of candidate symmetries and checks which ones work. But the list is huge, and discrete symmetries (like reflection) are especially hard to spot—you can easily miss them.

The bottleneck: symmetry search requires testing millions of possible transformations, and testing is expensive. Moreover, if your hypothesis space is incomplete or biased toward certain symmetry types, you will miss the real ones.

In one sentence. LieFlow learns to score candidate symmetries (assigning high scores to real ones, low scores to false ones); the high-scoring candidates are the actual symmetries.

What the paper changes

What changes: scoring function for symmetry candidates

The paper does not search for individual symmetries one by one. Instead, it learns a single object: a probability distribution defined over the entire space of symmetries (a Lie group or other group structure). The support of that distribution—the region where probability is high—automatically reveals which symmetries exist in the data. This is a shift from generate-and-test to learn-and-read-off.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: symmetry likelihood

measured quantity  =  per-transformation probability

The paper's core hidden quantity is the probability assigned to each possible transformation. For a rotation by angle theta, or a reflection across plane X, what is the model's confidence that this transformation is a real symmetry of the data? In flow matching, this is learned implicitly by training a velocity field (a continuous map) that guides samples through group space toward high-density regions.

The paper estimates this via the learned flow model: sample elements from the group, evaluate them under the trained model, and rank by score. Transformations that score highest (the modes) are the actual symmetries.

Why it works

Why it works: flow matching learns where symmetries concentrate

Flow matching is a technique that trains a model to smoothly move points from a simple starting state (random) toward target states where data is invariant. Here, the target is implicitly defined: transformations that leave data unchanged (symmetries) should score high, and random transformations should score low.

The method defines a velocity field on group space (a continuous map that points in the direction of higher scores). During training, the model learns to nudge samples toward score peaks where data is invariant under those transformations. Unlike testing each symmetry one by one, this is a single optimization where the model learns one velocity function that steers all samples toward the true symmetry peaks at once. Once trained, you can sample or optimize in this learned direction, and high-scoring clusters appear automatically. Because flow matching handles both continuous symmetries (rotations) and discrete symmetries (reflections) with the same machinery, it avoids building separate pipelines for each type.

Math after the picture

The flow matching equation

First in words:

Minimize the difference between a learned velocity field and a target velocity that transitions from noise to invariant transformations.

Then the compact form:

L = E[ ||v(g_t, t) - u_t(g)||^2 ] where g_t is a path in group space parameterized by time t, v is the learned velocity, u_t is the target velocity.

Every symbol:

g_ttransformation at time t (element of group space)
v(g_t, t)learned velocity field predicting direction in group space
u_t(g)target velocity field derived from data invariance
Ltraining loss to minimize
E[ ]average over all training examples and time steps
Tiny numeric example
Consider a rotation group: angles from 0 to 360 degrees.
Sample paths: start at random angles (50°, 180°, 290°), end at true symmetry (90°).
Target velocity u_t: angle should drift smoothly from start toward 90°.
Learned velocity v: model predicts how fast angle should change at each step.
Loss at step 1: v predicts +5°/step, target is +8°/step, squared error 9.
Loss at step 2: v predicts +7°/step, target is +6°/step, squared error 1.
Average: training reduces error by learning v to match u_t, guiding samples to symmetry peaks.

The flow model acts as a compass in group space, pointing samples toward regions where transformations leave data unchanged.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incomplete hypothesis group. If the group G does not contain the true symmetries, the method cannot find them, because no score can favor what is not in the search space. Choosing too small a group (e.g., only rotations when the data also has reflections) means the learned model scores reflections as low even though they are real.
Noisy or asymmetric data. Real data is never perfectly symmetric. If noise is high relative to the true invariances, the learned distribution may spread across many plausible transformations rather than concentrating at true peaks.
Ambiguous symmetries. If multiple distinct symmetries explain the data equally well, the learned distribution may have multiple high-probability modes. You must decide how many to keep.
Done right. Choose a hypothesis group that is plausible for the domain. Collect enough clean data. Use the learned distribution's confidence (entropy or peak sharpness) to judge whether symmetries are genuinely there or are artifacts.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment on synthetic data: generate a point cloud with a known symmetry (e.g., a square rotated by 90 degrees), train LieFlow to learn the distribution over rotations and reflections, and check that the model assigns high probability to the true symmetry (90-degree rotation) and low probability to random transformations. Then test on data where discrete symmetries exist (a vertically flipped rectangle) and confirm the model finds the reflection. Finally, compare discovery accuracy (fraction of true symmetries correctly ranked in the top k) and wall-clock time versus a naive baseline that tests all hypotheses one by one.

accuracy = (true symmetries correctly in top-k) / (total true symmetries); speedup = baseline_time / lieflow_time

The demo is successful if LieFlow finds both continuous and discrete symmetries faster than exhaustive search (because one model replaces many individual tests) and with fewer false positives (because the learned score concentrates at true symmetries, not noise).
