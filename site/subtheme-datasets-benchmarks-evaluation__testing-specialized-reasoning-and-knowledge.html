Testing Specialized Reasoning and Knowledge

Papers create benchmarks for narrow domains like medical diagnosis, chemistry, law, theorem proving, or biological science — where background knowledge and careful reasoning both matter.

What this is

Some domains require not just general intelligence but deep background knowledge and precise reasoning. You cannot diagnose a rare disease without understanding medicine. You cannot prove a theorem without logic. You cannot synthesize a stable drug without chemistry. This subtheme is about building benchmarks for these specialized domains where models have to show they actually understand the field, not just pattern-match.

The challenge is that general-purpose benchmarks miss domain-specific nuance. A medical benchmark cannot just ask "which drug treats this disease?" because context matters enormously: the patient's age, allergies, other medications, kidney function, pregnancy status. A chemistry benchmark cannot just ask "does this molecule exist?" because stability and synthesis routes matter. A law benchmark cannot just ask "is this legal?" because precedent and jurisdiction and interpretation matter. Building benchmarks for these fields requires collaboration with experts who understand what real reasoning looks like in that domain.

The papers here tackle narrow domains: medical diagnosis, chemistry and molecular design, law and legal reasoning, theorem proving in formal mathematics, biological sciences. Each team builds tests that capture what it means to truly understand that field. Some use real examples from practice: actual medical cases, actual legal documents, actual chemistry papers. Others create synthetic tests carefully designed by domain experts to probe specific understanding. The goal is to move past generic benchmarks toward specialized evaluation that honors the actual difficulty and knowledge required in each field.

How the work breaks down

This subtheme splits into five natural directions based on specialized domains: protein structure and biological function, formal mathematical reasoning and theorem proving, professional multi-domain knowledge, chemistry and molecular design, and clinical medicine. Each direction requires different types of deep understanding—not just pattern matching but real knowledge of how proteins fold, how formal proofs work, how professionals reason across domains, how molecules behave, and how diseases present. The papers here build benchmarks to test these different kinds of specialized understanding.

Protein Structure and Biological Function

Proteins are molecules with extraordinary complexity. Their shape determines what they do—a small change in how they fold can mean the difference between function and failure. Evaluating models on proteins is hard because you need to understand not just the sequence of amino acids but the 3D structure, how it relates to biological function, and whether generated designs will actually work in real cells. These papers build benchmarks that capture these different aspects of protein understanding.

CAPSUL tests whether models can predict where proteins go inside cells by understanding structural features, particularly the alpha-helix patterns that act as localization signals—it found that learned weights in the attention layers (the parts of a neural network that focus on important features) reveal these patterns. VenusX evaluates protein language models on fine-grained functional questions, testing both cases the model has seen before and harder new cases outside its training to reveal gaps between different model types. GeomMotif benchmarks protein generation by asking: when you design a new protein, can you preserve specific geometric motifs? It reveals that sequence-based and structure-based models struggle with different parts of this task. ProtDBench addresses a practical problem in protein design: structure prediction tools introduce systematic biases that downstream steps don't account for, so the benchmark includes consistent evaluation methods and fairness checks. TadA-Bench uses a million variants of a real protein (from wet-lab experiments) to test whether models can rank mutations for future experimental rounds—it finds that models interpolate well on data they have seen but struggle to predict what new mutations will work, and that evolutionary diversity matters more than local density.

Together these papers reveal a consistent pattern: protein understanding requires integrating sequence, structure, and real-world function in ways most models still get partly wrong.

Formal Reasoning and Theorem Proving

Proving theorems is one of the purest forms of reasoning. It requires finding the right premises, applying logical steps in the correct order, and detecting when you have reached a genuine proof. Unlike natural language where approximate answers can work, theorem proving demands precision: either the proof is valid or it is not. Building benchmarks for theorem proving is hard because you need real mathematical theorems at research difficulty—not toy problems but actual mathematics that experts spend time on.

FormalML benchmarks the ability to complete proofs in machine learning theory by combining premise retrieval (finding the right previous results to build on) with complex research-level proof contexts. It shows that the best existing provers still have major accuracy and efficiency problems. SorryDB provides a continuously updated stream of over 1000 real Lean theorems (formal proofs written in a computer-verifiable language) so that the benchmark stays fresh—this prevents models from just memorizing examples during training—and reveals which approaches are stronger at which types of tasks. Both papers show that formal reasoning remains a frontier—what works for natural language does not automatically work for precise logical structures.

Professional Knowledge Across Domains

Real professionals—doctors, lawyers, engineers—solve problems that require deep domain knowledge combined with reasoning about specific cases. A lawyer cannot just know general legal principles; they must understand precedent, jurisdiction, and interpretation of statutes. A doctor cannot just recognize diseases; they must handle edge cases, conflicting symptoms, and personalized treatment decisions. Building benchmarks for professional reasoning requires creating tests that experts would face, not simplified versions.

ProfBench creates multi-domain rubrics in law, medicine, science, and business where both the questions and the judgment of correctness require professional knowledge. It reveals that even the most advanced models (GPT-5-high) achieve only 65.9% overall performance, and there are large gaps between proprietary models and open-weight models. SEDRAS provides 600 diverse evaluation scenarios across research and science with different demands—symbolic reasoning (pure logic), file handling (accessing documents), and varying complexity—so that evaluators can see which models are strongest where. It finds that different models excel at different types of work because they have different training and architecture: Gemini 3.0 Pro scores best overall, but GPT 5.2 is stronger on symbolic data (reasoning through pure logic), Claude Opus 4.5 handles files better (extracting information from documents), and all models show declining performance as complexity increases. These differences matter because a user choosing a model for a specific professional task needs to know which model actually works for that work. These papers demonstrate that professional-domain reasoning is not yet solved and requires diverse evaluation methods to reveal which models are actually useful for which real-world tasks.

ProfBench: Multi-Domain Rubrics requiring Professional Knowledge to Answer and Judge

Chemistry and Molecular Design

Chemistry deals with molecules—their shapes, how they react, which ones are stable, which ones can be synthesized. Understanding chemistry means knowing that atoms bond in specific ways, that 3D shape affects behavior, and that stability depends on the energy required to hold them together. Building benchmarks for chemistry models requires testing whether they actually understand these principles or just recognize patterns.

3DCS benchmarks how well modern molecular representations handle 3D shape and orientation. It finds that data-driven representations handle basic shape decently but are inconsistent with chirality (left-handed versus right-handed versions of the same molecule, which often have very different properties) and poorly predict which physical orientations are actually stable according to the energy required to hold them. MolLangBench tests whether language models can understand chemistry when given text descriptions—recognition, editing, and generation of molecules from language prompts. The strongest models (GPT-5) achieve 86.2% on recognition and 85.5% on editing but only 43% on generation, showing a steep drop-off when you ask models to create new molecules that satisfy constraints. Together these papers show that chemistry understanding—particularly the connection between molecular structure and real-world properties—remains a significant challenge.

3DCS: Datasets and Benchmark for Evaluating Conformational Sensitivity in Molecular Representations
Reveals that modern data-driven representations handle geometry well but inconsistently handle chirality and poorly align with energy landscapes, providing the first rigorous benchmark for 3D molecular representations.
MolLangBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Language-Prompted Molecular Structure Recognition, Editing, and Generation
Shows strongest model (GPT-5) achieves only 86.2% and 85.5% on recognition and editing, and 43.0% on generation, revealing significant limitations in molecular manipulation.

Clinical Medicine and Diagnostics

Medicine requires integrating multiple types of information: anatomy, physiology, patient history, and clinical evidence. A patient's symptoms might point to dozens of possible diseases, and the doctor must reason through which is most likely given the full context. Building benchmarks for medical reasoning requires grounding in real cases and real diagnostic difficulty.

AbdCTBench uses medical imaging data—the surface contours of the abdomen from imaging scans—to test whether models can predict patient outcomes and disease status. The benchmark shows that surface shape is informative for age prediction, mortality risk, and diabetes detection, establishing that physical characteristics visible in imaging contain clinical signal. Can You Hear Me Now? takes a different angle, benchmarking whether graph neural networks can propagate information across long distances—a capability needed in many systems including medical data networks where information must flow between distant nodes. It reveals clear performance gaps in long-range propagation, establishing standards for evaluating this capability. Together these papers ground medical AI benchmarking in real clinical tasks and real system requirements.

AbdCTBench: Learning Clinical Biomarker Representations from Abdominal Surface Geometry
Demonstrates surface geometry is predictive of internal tissue composition; best models achieve age prediction MAE 6.22 years (R²=0.757), mortality AUROC 0.839, diabetes detection AUROC 0.801.
