Catching when models hallucinate or go wrong

Models sometimes confidently say false things. These papers detect when a model is uncertain, lying, or relying on spurious patterns rather than true reasoning.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Language models sometimes generate text that is false, nonsensical, or based on patterns that don't generalize. When asked 'What is 2 plus 2?', a model might answer '5' with high confidence. This is called hallucination. The model isn't reasoning; it's matching patterns in its training data. These papers try to detect when a model is uncertain, contradicting itself, or leaning on spurious correlations — surface-level patterns that happen to be true in the training set but don't reflect real reasoning.

Detection methods look for red flags inside the model. High uncertainty in the internal states might signal doubt. Incoherence — when different parts of the model disagree — might mean the model is guessing. Some papers check whether the model's explanation of a prediction actually holds up under scrutiny: if you ask the model to justify its answer and the justification doesn't logically follow, it's probably hallucinating. Others measure whether the model is truly using the features it claims to use or just pattern-matching shallow correlations. The aim is to catch failures before they reach the user, so the system can fall back to a human, ask for clarification, or refuse to answer.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five directions based on where and how the detection happens. Some papers read the model's internal states—the hidden activations that flow through the network—to spot uncertainty or confusion. Others build explicit uncertainty scores using probability distributions or gradient analysis. A third group creates counterfactual examples (small changes to the input) to ask: which parts of the input actually drive the model's decision? A fourth group focuses on structured reasoning like chain-of-thought, checking if the model's step-by-step justifications actually hold together logically. Finally, some papers focus specifically on multimodal models (which handle images and text together) where hallucinations take a different form—the model might see an object that isn't there in the image.

Reading uncertainty from internal activations

These papers attack hallucination detection by looking inside the model at the hidden numbers—called activations—that flow between layers as the model generates text. The core insight is that when a model is uncertain or hallucinating, those internal states show telltale signs: they might be noisy, contradictory, or fail to align with what the model actually outputs. The challenge is that these internal signals are high-dimensional and hard to interpret; you can't just look at a single number and know what it means.

From Flat Facts to Sharp Hallucinations distinguishes between knowledge the model truly understands (stored smoothly across many similar inputs) and brittle memorization (sharp, sensitive to small input changes) by measuring how activations respond to gradient perturbations. LatentQA trains a small decoder to directly translate internal activations (the hidden numbers flowing through layers) into natural language, so you can ask the model 'what do your hidden layers know?' and get a readable answer about what it's actually encoding. Hessian-Enhanced Token Attribution (HETA) improves attribution by using second-order derivatives (a calculus tool for measuring curvature) to identify which tokens actually shaped the model's decision, going beyond just looking at attention weights. Attention Sinks as Internal Signals shows that position-0 attention (which models develop as a dumping ground for unwanted information) acts as a thermometer for hallucination: high values at that position correlate with false outputs. These papers converge on a simple strategy: hallucinating models leave traces in their internal states that a careful observer can spot.

These approaches are complementary—some target specific layers, others measure how activations change under perturbation, and one uses attention as a proxy—but they all assume that genuine reasoning looks different from pattern-matching in the hidden space, and that difference is detectable if you know where to look.

Quantifying uncertainty with probability and statistics

These papers step back and ask: can we measure how confident the model actually is about its answer? When a model outputs 'The capital of France is London' with 95% confidence, that's a hallucination signal. But language models don't naturally output confidence scores—they generate one token at a time. Building a reliable uncertainty estimate requires either extracting it from the model's intermediate representations or running the model multiple times and looking at how much its answers vary.

Semantic Uncertainty Quantification of Hallucinations uses a quantum tensor network (a way to compress high-dimensional data) to build a single uncertainty score that works across different sequence lengths and even when the model's weights are quantized (made smaller for faster speed). GAUSS adds structure by building a graph of how sentences relate to each other and tracks whether the semantic meaning stays coherent as the model keeps generating—if later sentences contradict earlier ones, that's a sign of hallucination. Information Flow Reveals When to Trust Language Models analyzes how information propagates from layer to layer, asking whether the model is drawing on real knowledge or just recycling its own previous outputs. Spilled Energy measures 'wasted' energy in the attention mechanism (parts of the attention that don't help drive the output) because when the model is uncertain or hallucinating, it spreads attention across many irrelevant tokens rather than focusing on true signals. The Layerwise Trajectory Evolution paper tracks how the internal representations move as the model reasons through a problem: if the path becomes erratic or lands in a place unlike genuine reasoning traces, it's probably hallucinating.

These methods all try to turn fuzzy, implicit uncertainty into a number between 0 and 1 that a downstream system can act on: if the score is too low, refuse the answer; if it's high, probably trustworthy. They differ in whether they derive confidence from a single forward pass or require multiple runs.

Counterfactual explanations and input perturbations

Rather than asking 'is the model confident?', these papers ask a different question: 'which parts of the input actually drove this decision?' The strategy is to generate counterfactual examples—small changes to the input—and see how the model's output changes. If removing a word has almost no effect, the model probably wasn't using it. If changing a pixel in an image flips the prediction, that pixel was critical.

Optimal Transport Group Counterfactual Explanations generates diverse alternative examples that would produce different outcomes, while preserving the statistical structure of similar examples, using mathematics to find paths through input space that respect realistic input distributions. Synthesising Counterfactual Explanations via Gaussian Mixture VAE creates multiple recourse actions—concrete ways to change your input to get a different prediction—by learning what makes examples good vs. bad and finding the shortest realistic path between them. ATEX-CF integrates adversarial attacks (how to deliberately fool the model) into counterfactual reasoning, so the explanations still work even if the model tries to exploit spurious patterns that differ from real data. Perceptual Geodesics finds these counterfactuals along paths that humans would recognize as valid changes (like smoothly morphing a face rather than random pixel noise), which reveals failure modes that standard accuracy metrics miss and also continues to work when inputs are slightly changed. Density-Guided Counterfactual Explanations handles the harder case where multiple different models trained on the same task disagree on what matters—it finds explanations that work even when the models conflict.

These papers are asking: can we reverse-engineer what the model is actually using by seeing what happens when you change things? This ties directly to hallucination: if the model's prediction doesn't change when you remove the 'grounding' information (the fact it should be using), it's probably hallucinating.

Verifying structured reasoning and explanations

Some of the most dangerous hallucinations happen in chain-of-thought reasoning—the model writes out step-by-step logic but the steps don't actually follow. These papers ask: are the model's intermediate reasoning steps actually valid, or is the model just generating plausible-sounding words that happen to reach a correct-looking conclusion? The challenge is that validating reasoning requires understanding the domain deeply.

Grounding or Guessing distinguishes between tokens the model truly grounds in its input (like sign language tokens that correspond to actual hand positions in a video) versus tokens it hallucinates by measuring visual attention: if the model attended to the actual visual evidence, it was grounded; if it ignored it, it was guessing. Thought Branches reveals that inserting made-up reasoning sentences into the middle of a chain-of-thought often has surprisingly little effect on the final answer, which means the model is not actually using those steps—it's the initial setup and final output that matter, and the middle is scaffolding the model ignores. Hallucination Detection from Structural Reasoning reads the structure of the reasoning trace like a tree or graph, checking whether the logical relationships hold: if branch A depends on branch B but branch B is contradictory, there's a hallucination. Outcome Rewards Do Not Guarantee Verifiable Reasoning shows that training models with reinforcement learning on answer correctness alone doesn't guarantee the reasoning is real—two different reward functions can both drive high accuracy but one produces reasoning that actually generalizes to new domains and one produces shortcuts. VeriTrail creates a traceability chain: when hallucination is detected, it points backward to which input token led to the error, so you can see exactly where the model went wrong.

These papers share the insight that you can't just check the final answer; you have to check the path, and the path reveals whether the model is actually thinking or just imitating thought.

Hallucinations in vision-language models

Multimodal models that combine images and text create a new hallucination risk: the model might see objects in an image that aren't there, or miss objects that are. A vision-language model trained on descriptions often learns shortcut patterns—for instance, if horses almost always appear in rural settings, the model might hallucinate a horse whenever it sees fields, even if the image shows something else. These papers focus specifically on detecting when the vision component has failed.

DOUBT decouples the object-level understanding (what objects are in the image) from the bridging to language (connecting those objects to words), measuring trustworthiness separately for each component so hallucinations from vision vs. language confusion can be distinguished. Evaluating and Steering Modality Preferences reveals that all tested multimodal models show clear bias toward either vision or text—some trust images more, others trust text more—and this bias directly predicts whether the model will hallucinate; a model that over-trusts the image modality might invent objects it thinks should be there. Instruction Lens Score notes that the instruction the user provides shapes what objects the model looks for, so hallucinations often reflect over-eagerness to satisfy the instruction rather than actual visual understanding; the score works by checking whether the model's internal attention to instruction elements matches where it actually looked in the image. Interpreting and Enhancing Emotional Circuits shows that when multimodal models hallucinate emotional content, there are discoverable neural circuits responsible; amplifying the right information flow reduces those hallucinations.

These papers recognize that vision-language hallucinations are not just abstract 'uncertainty' problems—they're failures of alignment between the visual input and the language generation, and they can be attacked by looking at the cross-modal signals inside the model.
