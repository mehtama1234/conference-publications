Geometric Flow Grounding: Manifold-Constrained Dynamics

Keep predicted motion on the learned data surface to stop predictions from wandering into impossible territory.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

When a neural network learns to predict how a system will move (a ball rolling, genes turning on/off, a video frame changing), it often makes predictions that break the invisible rules governing that system. Geometric Flow Grounding adds a guard rail: instead of letting predictions go anywhere, it forces them to stay on the learned shape of real data. This prevents physically impossible trajectories and makes predictions more stable over long time horizons.

Zero assumption version

Predictions that violate the rules

When you train a neural network to predict the next frame of a video or the next state of a gene-regulatory system, the network learns two separate things at once: a snapshot (what the system looks like right now) and a direction of motion (how it is changing). These two pieces are tangled together during training.

The beginner's approach is to ask the network to predict the next state directly, giving it the current state as input. The network learns, but its predictions gradually drift. After ten steps, it predicts something that does not match the true data. After a hundred steps, the prediction might be completely impossible—a gene expression level of negative 50, or a video frame with objects in spatially impossible positions.

This happens because the network is free to predict any point in space. It has no constraint keeping it on the learned manifold—the surface that real data actually occupies. Predictions accumulate errors and off-manifold hallucinations.

In one sentence. The paper decouples state shape from motion direction by projecting all predictions back onto the learned data surface, making long predictions stable and rule-respecting.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn the shape

Neural network learns the low-dimensional surface that real data occupies.

Learn the motion

Separate network learns the direction and speed of change along that surface.

Predict the next state

Motion network generates the raw next prediction anywhere in space.

Project onto the surface

Projection layer finds the nearest legal point on the learned surface.

Repeat with the projected state

Loop: use the snapped-back state to predict the next one.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces a differentiable Neural Tangent Projection Layer—a new piece that runs after each motion prediction. It takes the raw predicted next state and projects it orthogonally onto the tangent bundle of the learned manifold. In plain words: it snaps the prediction down to the nearest legal point on the learned surface. This constraint is enforced at every step, not just at the end.

the object it moves  =  the projection step
The hidden quantity

The projection residual: how far off-surface the prediction went

measured quantity  =  Projection residual (distance off the learned surface)

The hidden quantity is the projection residual: how far the raw prediction drifted away from the learned data surface before being snapped back. It measures in numbers: for each prediction step, compute the distance between the unconstrained prediction and the nearest point on the surface. A large residual means the prediction tried to go somewhere impossible.

The paper uses this residual in two ways. First, it is part of the training loss—the network learns that producing large residuals is bad, so it learns to predict closer to the surface from the start. Second, for deepfake detection, a large residual signals that a frame does not match the learned pattern—a zero-shot way to spot synthetic content without any deepfake-specific training.

Why it works

Why constraining to the surface fixes the problem

The core issue is that a standard network is free to predict anywhere in the full high-dimensional space. Over many steps, this freedom compounds: a small error in step 1 gets fed into step 2, which introduces a new error, which goes into step 3, and so on. After 100 steps, the compounded drift is huge and the prediction is completely off-manifold (physically impossible).

The paper's fix: by forcing every intermediate prediction back onto the learned surface, it eliminates the accumulation. Even if the motion prediction is slightly wrong, the projection corrects it. The result is a tight feedback loop: predict a motion direction, snap back to the surface, repeat. The surface acts as a guardrail—it prevents the drift from building up.

For dynamics with interpretable structure (like gene-regulatory networks), this constraint also helps because the learned surface often captures the true low-dimensional shape of how the system evolves. When the network is forced to stay on this learned shape, it cannot invent biologically impossible patterns—it must discover the real regulatory rules hiding in the data. For detecting deepfakes, a video frame that does not match the learned pattern of real video will have a large residual—the projection layer shows you the inconsistency by measuring how far the frame is from the learned surface.

Math after the picture

The constraint at each step

First in words:

Total loss is the prediction error plus the projection residual. Projection residual is the squared distance from the unconstrained prediction to the nearest point on the learned surface.

Then the compact form:

loss = prediction_error + lambda * residual^2, where residual = ||p_raw - p_proj||

Every symbol:

p_rawthe unconstrained prediction from the motion network
p_projthe nearest point on the learned manifold
residualthe Euclidean distance between them
lambdathe weight controlling how strongly we enforce the constraint
lossthe total training loss to be minimized
A tiny worked example
Suppose the motion network predicts state [5.2, 3.8, 1.9].
The nearest point on the learned surface is [5.0, 4.0, 2.0].
Residual = sqrt((5.2-5.0)^2 + (3.8-4.0)^2 + (1.9-2.0)^2) = sqrt(0.04 + 0.04 + 0.01) = sqrt(0.09) = 0.3.
If lambda = 10, the residual term adds 10 * 0.3^2 = 0.9 to the loss.
Large lambda makes the network learn to predict closer to the surface from the start.

The residual term steers predictions toward the learned surface during training, and its value signals off-manifold drift during both regular prediction and deepfake detection.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The learned surface itself is wrong. If the manifold-learning stage (the first network) fails to capture the true shape of the data, the projection will snap predictions onto an incorrect surface. Garbage in, garbage out.
The system is truly high-dimensional. Some dynamical systems do not compress into a low-dimensional surface. If the real dynamics require many degrees of freedom, forcing predictions onto a low-d surface will eliminate necessary information and hurt accuracy.
Projection is expensive. Computing the nearest point on the learned manifold at each step adds computational cost. In real-time applications (like video), this overhead may matter.
Done right on interpretable systems. For gene-regulatory networks and sparse dynamical systems, the paper shows that the learned surface often captures true biological structure, and staying on it both stabilizes predictions and recovers interpretable patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy system: a ball rolling on a frictionless curve in 2D, or a simple gene-regulatory network with 3 genes. Train a neural network to predict the next state. Compare two versions: one that projects predictions back onto the learned manifold at each step, and one that does not. Run both for 500 steps, starting from a real data point. Measure how far the prediction drifts from real trajectories and how often it predicts impossible states (negative values for gene expression, positions outside the curve for the ball).

Stability = (prediction error at step 500) / (prediction error at step 1). Lower is better. Constraint violation count = number of times the unconstrained network predicted outside the learned surface bounds.

The demo must make visible that the unconstrained network's predictions gradually diverge into impossible territory, while the projection-constrained version stays on the learned surface and accurate 10x longer.
