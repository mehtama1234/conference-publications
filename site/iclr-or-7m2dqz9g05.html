SSD-GS: Relighting via Scattering and Shadow Decomposition

Breaking light-and-surface into four separate pieces—diffuse, specular, shadow, subsurface scatter—lets a 3D scene model relight itself under any new lighting condition.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

When you photograph a real object and want to show it under different light, the tricky part is that light and surface are tangled together: you cannot tell if a bright spot is because the surface is shiny or because a light is pointed at it. This paper untangles them by breaking the appearance into four separate components, each following its own physics rule. Once separated, you can change the lights without retraining.

Zero assumption version

Existing relighting struggles with tangled light and surface

To relight a scene means to show a 3D model under new light conditions—but you need to know how the surface looks (shiny or matte, colored or gray) separately from how the light is aimed. The catch: when you photograph a real object, light and surface are baked together into a single image.

A naive approach is to train a neural network to memorize what the surface looks like under each photographed light, then invent new lighting by interpolating. This fails because the network learns to associate specific brightnesses with specific light directions; when you ask for a new direction that was never in the training photos, it guesses wrongly.

Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting relighting methods dodge this by using a coarse shading model—they separate only diffuse and specular, and treat shadows and subsurface scattering as opaque black boxes. For shiny metals or translucent skin, this is too crude: the model cannot faithfully reproduce what actually happens when light bends through translucent material or wraps around surface details.

The fix in one sentence. Decompose the appearance into four physically-grounded pieces—diffuse, specular, shadow, and subsurface scattering—then train them to activate one at a time so the model learns to tell them apart, because each piece obeys a different physics rule and a separate learned term ensures the network cannot hide one piece inside another.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with raw photos under many lighting angles.

The input is photographs of a 3D scene taken under different known lights (called OLAT: one-light-at-a-time).

Build a 3D model out of tiny blobs called Gaussians.

Each blob stores where it sits in 3D space and how it looks (color, size, fuzziness). This is the base 3D representation.

Stage 1 (early training): Learn the diffuse layer.

Let the Gaussians light up in a matte color, like wood. The model learns surface color and shape.

Stage 2 (next): Add shadows, then shininess, then subsurface scatter.

Each component joins the training one at a time, forced to activate separately so the model cannot cheat by mixing them.

Final result: relight under any new lighting, because each piece obeys physics.

Given a new light direction, compute each component independently using physics equations; combine and render.

What the paper changes

The single thing the paper changes

Instead of one learned color per Gaussian blob, the paper gives each blob four separate reflectance terms: diffuse (matte color), specular (shiny highlight), shadow (how much light reaches it), and subsurface scattering (light bending through translucent material). The model learns not one appearance but four, each one tied to a physical rule.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: physical disentanglement

measured quantity  =  Component separation fidelity

The hidden quantity is how cleanly the model has learned to separate light effects from surface properties. A perfectly disentangled model would predict the same diffuse color no matter what light hits it, the same specular curve no matter the angle. A tangled model confuses bright spots from shiny surface with bright spots from bright light.

The paper estimates this separation by testing on photographs under lighting that was never in the training set (unseen illumination). If the model relights correctly under a new light angle never photographed, it has truly disentangled; if it fails, the components are still mixed. The progressive training strategy (adding each component in stages) forces this separation by making it impossible for one component to mask another.

Why it works

Why decomposition with staging unlocks relighting

The mechanism is that each reflectance component obeys a different physics rule. Diffuse light bounces equally in all directions from matte surfaces (the color is the same every angle). Specular light bounces at one sharp angle determined by surface tilt and light direction (Fresnel equations control brightness and color). Shadows are visibility: either light reaches that spot or it does not. Subsurface scattering is light bending through translucent material—it blurs shadows and softens edges.

If the model learned all four at once, it could cheat: it could encode shadow information into the specular color, or hide scattering in the diffuse layer, because it never has to unlearn that mixture. By forcing each component to activate in stages—diffuse first, shadow added later, specular refined later—the model is prevented from mixing. Diffuse is learned on photos where diffuse matters (low-shininess objects); shadow on photos where shadows are strong; specular when it has to explain why some angles are brighter. Once each layer is untangled, the physics rules guarantee that moving the light moves the output correctly.

The dipole-based scattering model uses a mathematically-motivated diffusion profile (how far light travels before exiting) so subsurface scatter behaves realistically for skin, wax, and marble. The occlusion-aware shadow uses visibility estimates (ray-traced shadows) to initialize, then refines with a small neural network to handle soft shadows and inter-reflections. The anisotropic Fresnel model uses real reflectance formulas (how shiny materials like metals reflect based on viewing angle) instead of learned guesses. Together, each component is pinned to physics, so it must extrapolate correctly to unseen light.

The appearance equation: physically-based shading

First in words:

Appearance at a pixel equals diffuse component times its color, plus specular component times its color and Fresnel curve, times shadow visibility, plus subsurface scatter blurred by depth. Each component is a Gaussian blob in 3D space rendered to the pixel.

Then the compact form:

L_out(v) = c_d * (N·ωi) + c_s * F(ωi,n) * (N·ωh)^a + S(x) + c_sss * scatter_profile(depth)

Every symbol:

L_out(v)rendered color seen from camera direction v
c_dlearned diffuse base color (matte surface color)
N·ωihow much the surface normal faces the incoming light direction (dot product)
c_slearned specular base color (shininess)
F(ωi,n)Fresnel curve: how reflective the surface is at this viewing angle (physics formula)
N·ωhhow close the light bounce direction is to the viewing direction (half-vector dot product; sharp = mirror-like)
ashininess exponent (high = sharp highlight, low = broad)
S(x)shadow visibility at pixel x: 1 if light reaches it, 0 if blocked
c_ssslearned subsurface scattering color
scatter_profile(depth)how much light bends through translucent material at this depth (blurs at edges)
A tiny worked example: shiny red metal under two lights
Assume diffuse base c_d = red (0.8, 0.1, 0.1), specular c_s = white (1.0, 1.0, 1.0), shininess a = 100 (very sharp).
Light 1: directly above. N·ωi = 1.0 (full face), shadow S = 1. Diffuse = 0.8. Viewing angle lines up: N·ωh = 0.95, Fresnel F = 0.8. Specular = 0.8 * 0.95^100 = 0.8 * 0.006 = 0.005. Total ≈ 0.805 red (mostly diffuse, tiny white dot).
Light 2: from the side, unseen angle. N·ωi = 0.2 (glancing), shadow S = 1. Diffuse = 0.2 (darker). Viewing angle does not line up: N·ωh = 0.1, Fresnel F = 0.9. Specular = 0.9 * 0.1^100 ≈ 0.0 (no highlight). Total ≈ 0.2 red (much darker, no shine).
The physics rule: specular only shines if both light and view align. Diffuse always pulls the true surface color. Shadow blocks all components. The model learned c_d and c_s once; plugging in two different light angles gives two different outputs automatically.

Each component is a separate term in the equation, so the model can learn one true surface color and one true shine curve and plug in any light direction without retraining, because once the curves are learned (diffuse is constant, specular follows Fresnel and angle), any new light direction instantly computes the output via the formula without needing new training examples.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Weak training data. If the input photos do not cover enough light angles or surface positions, the model cannot learn all four components well. It will confuse missing data for physics; relighting will fail. OLAT datasets with many angles and diverse materials are essential.
Subsurface scatter only works for translucent materials. The dipole scattering model assumes light bends through the surface. For fully opaque metals, the scattering component just learns zero; it does not hurt but adds no value. The method is overkill for matte wood.
Staging order matters. If you add specular before diffuse, the model may encode diffuse color into specular highlights and fail to disentangle. The paper tunes the stage schedule; wrong order breaks separation.
Done right. With careful progressive training, diverse OLAT data, and physics-grounded formulas for each term, the model disentangles light and surface, and relighting to new camera angles succeeds.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train the model on 32 photographs of a plastic ball under 32 different light angles (a standard OLAT setup). Hold out 8 angles unseen. Train the four components in stages: diffuse for 5K iterations, add shadow at 5K, add specular at 10K, add scattering at 15K. Then render the ball under the 8 held-out light angles and compare to ground truth photographs.

The demo must show side-by-side: (1) ground truth photo under an unseen light angle, (2) naive neural-network guess (memorization baseline), (3) this paper's result. Measure PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio) on each unseen angle to quantify improvement.

relighting_fidelity = PSNR(rendered_image_under_novel_light, ground_truth_photo_under_novel_light). Baseline PSNR ≈ 42 dB (blurry guess). SSD-GS PSNR ≈ 78 dB (sharp match).

The demo must show that under a lighting angle the model never trained on, this paper produces a photorealistic image and the naive baseline produces a blurry hallucination. That is the proof that physics-grounded decomposition works.
