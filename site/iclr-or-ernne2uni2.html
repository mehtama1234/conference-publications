Minor First, Major Last: A Depth-Induced Implicit Bias of Sharpness-Aware Minimization

Training deep networks with SAM (a trick that looks for flat valleys) accidentally learns to use weak signals first, then strong ones, in a predictable order.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When you train a deep network, sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) — a tweak that nudges away from sharp valleys in how wrong the network is — acts differently than plain gradient descent. This paper discovers SAM has a hidden bias: it makes the network learn from weak patterns before strong patterns, in a specific sequence. This matters because it reveals an unexpected side effect of SAM in depth.

Zero assumption version

SAM is a mystery in deep networks

Gradient descent (the standard way to train a network) is well understood: it nudges the weights toward lower error. But sharpness-aware minimization, or SAM, is trickier. SAM adds an extra step — it looks around the current weights to find a worse spot, then nudges away from it. This makes the network prefer to land in flat valleys where small changes do not blow up the error, because by training away from high-error peaks, the network ends up in regions where tiny weight adjustments keep the error low.

Nobody fully knows how SAM behaves when you stack multiple layers. A beginner might guess: SAM should behave like gradient descent, just with a flatness penalty. That guess turns out to be wildly wrong. On simple test problems, SAM does strange things that gradient descent never does, especially when using the L-infinity variant (which looks at the single largest weight change, not the sum of all changes).

The problem in one sentence. SAM's hidden bias changes drastically with network depth, causing it to process features in a surprising order that depends on initialization.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Normal training step

Compute the error on the data, nudge weights to shrink it.

SAM adds a perturbation

Push weights in a direction that makes the error worse, to find a harder spot.

Gradient on the perturbed weights

Compute the error at this worse spot. Minor signals amplify more under this perturbation.

Normalize and pull back

Apply a normalization that emphasizes minor coordinates early. Nudge to move away from the worse spot.

Over training, minor fades into major

As the network improves, SAM's normalization effect changes because the weight sizes grow, and the relative amplification of small coordinates fades. Major signals gradually become dominant.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

SAM changes how gradients are scaled when looking for a flat valley. Instead of treating all coordinates equally, SAM's normalization (the denominator in the perturbation step) amplifies minor signals early in training. This asymmetry is the core difference from gradient descent.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Feature order

measured quantity  =  sequential feature discovery

The paper uncovers a new phenomenon: the order in which the network learns to use signals, ranked by strength. In early training, the network relies on weak signals (minor coordinates in the data). As training progresses, the network gradually shifts to using strong signals (major coordinates).

The paper estimates this by tracking which coordinates have large weights at different points in training. They measure when each signal starts to dominate the predictor. This is not done via a direct metric; instead, they track the evolving lists of numbers (the weights) and see the shift from minor to major coordinates over training time.

Why it works

Why SAM learns minor-first, major-last

SAM works by perturbing the weights to find a flat spot. The perturbation is normalized by dividing by the norm (the overall size of the change). This normalization has an asymmetric effect on coordinates with different scales.

When data has coordinates of different magnitudes — say, some vary from 0 to 1, others from 0 to 1000 — the normalization amplifies small coordinates and dampens large ones relative to their natural size. Early in training, this amplification makes minor signals respond more strongly to the perturbation. The network then learns to use these minor signals because they provide a clearer gradient signal under SAM's perturbation.

As training proceeds and the network's weights grow, the relative effect of this normalization changes. Major signals, being naturally larger, eventually dominate. The network gradually shifts its reliance to major signals because they become more stable. This creates the observed sequential feature discovery: minor patterns are learned first, then abandoned as major patterns take over.

Math after the picture

The equation behind the order

First in words:

The SAM perturbation grows proportionally to the gradient, but is capped by its norm. This norm acts as a divisor for each coordinate, creating an amplification of small signals and dampening of large signals relative to their data magnitude.

Then the compact form:

gradient_SAM_step = gradient(error_at_perturbed_weights) / norm(perturbation)

Every symbol:

gradient_SAM_stepthe direction to nudge weights under SAM
error_at_perturbed_weightserror measured after pushing weights to a worse spot
perturbationthe push away from current weights
norm(perturbation)the size of the push, used as a scaling factor
A tiny worked example
Suppose the perturbation moves: minor signal by 0.1, major signal by 10.
Norm of perturbation = sqrt(0.1^2 + 10^2) = sqrt(0.01 + 100) = sqrt(100.01) ≈ 10.0.
After normalization: minor signal gradient / 10.0 ≈ 0.01 (amplified relative to data scale)
major signal gradient / 10.0 ≈ 1.0 (dampened relative to data scale).
Minor coordinates are emphasized (stronger effect on the gradient). Early training uses them. As weights grow, major signals' natural size overcomes this effect.

The normalization factor treats all coordinates the same in absolute terms, but relative to the data's natural scales, it favors minor signals early and major signals later.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

L-infinity SAM is unpredictable. When using L-infinity (tracking only the single largest weight change), the limit direction depends critically on initialization. The network might converge to using any coordinate direction, not a predictable order. This variant does not exhibit sequential feature discovery.
Depth matters critically. These phenomena appear when depth is at least 2. On shallow networks (depth 1), SAM behaves like gradient descent. The implicit bias only emerges with depth.
Linear separability required. The analysis applies to linearly separable data. On non-separable data, the dynamics may differ or the sequential discovery may not hold.
On linearly separable binary classification. When data can be perfectly split by a line, L2-SAM reliably exhibits sequential feature discovery. The order is predictable and matches the coordinate importance in the data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a 2-layer linear network on a simple linearly separable binary classification task. Track the network's weights over training under SAM (using L2 normalization). Plot which coordinates have the largest weights at each training step. Measure the fraction of the prediction coming from minor vs major coordinates at each point in training.

fraction_from_minor = sum(abs(weights_for_minor_signals)) / sum(abs(all_weights))

The demo should show this fraction starting high (near 1.0), then dropping smoothly as major signals take over. This proves the sequential feature discovery: SAM forces the network to learn minor patterns first, then switch to major patterns.
