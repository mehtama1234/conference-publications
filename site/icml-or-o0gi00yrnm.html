Lower-Tail Calibration for Gaussian-Process Bayesian Optimization

Instead of calibrating a Gaussian process everywhere, calibrate the part of the predictive uncertainty below the current target threshold, because that is the region expected improvement uses when searching for minima.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Bayesian optimization chooses expensive experiments by fitting a Gaussian process, meaning a probabilistic model that predicts both an average value and an uncertainty at every candidate point. For minimization, the acquisition rule often cares most about whether the function could fall below the current best value. If that lower tail of the predictive uncertainty is badly calibrated, the optimizer makes the wrong exploration decision even when the rest of the predictive curve looks acceptable. This paper studies exactly that failure mode and proposes a post-hoc calibration method aimed only at the lower tail relevant to optimization.

Zero assumption version

Optimization decisions depend on the wrong part of the uncertainty

A Gaussian process predicts a full distribution at each candidate point, not just a single number. Standard model selection tries to make that distribution fit the observed data overall. But expected improvement for minimization does not use the whole distribution equally. It asks how much probability mass and how much expected gain lie below the current incumbent value or another low threshold.

That creates a mismatch. A model can look reasonable in average calibration while still misrepresenting the lower tail. If it is too optimistic there, the optimizer chases points that only appear promising. If it is too pessimistic there, the optimizer misses genuine improvements. The paper isolates this optimization-specific error instead of treating calibration as one global property.

Core issue. For minimization, the useful question is not whether the whole predictive distribution is calibrated, but whether the low-value tail that drives acquisition is calibrated.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Fit a standard Gaussian process

Use the usual kernel and maximum-likelihood hyperparameter fitting to get predictive means and uncertainties.

Choose a low threshold

Set a target threshold such as the current best observed value, because improvement is defined relative to that level.

Measure lower-tail reliability

Check whether predictive probabilities below the threshold match what actually happens across the design space.

Apply post-hoc tail calibration

Correct the predictive uncertainty only in the below-threshold region so tail probabilities become more reliable, because the correction is fitted to the mismatch between predicted and observed improvement events there.

Run expected improvement on the corrected tail

Use the calibrated predictive distribution to rank new evaluations while keeping the usual Bayesian-optimization loop.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the target of calibration. Standard approaches ask for one predictive distribution that is trustworthy everywhere. This work asks for trustworthiness where the optimizer actually uses it: below a low threshold. That is a narrower but more relevant requirement, because expected improvement only rewards outcomes that beat that threshold.

the object it moves  =  the part of the predictive distribution that is calibrated
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: lower-tail reliability

measured quantity  =  lower-tail reliability

Lower-tail reliability asks whether the model's claimed chance of achieving values below a threshold matches reality across candidate points. If the model says ten points each have a 20 percent chance of beating the threshold, about two of them should actually do so. If only zero or one do, the model is over-optimistic in the tail.

The paper studies this reliability with spatial notions of calibration. One view checks occurrence calibration over the design space: when the model predicts a given sub-threshold probability, how often does the event occur at points receiving that probability? Another checks calibration on low-function-value regions themselves. These measurements isolate the exact mismatch that expected improvement turns into poor search behavior.

Plain reading. The question is simple: when the model says a point has a certain chance of being better than the current best, is that chance honest? Bayesian optimization depends heavily on the answer because that chance drives where it spends experiments.
Paper reading. The method defines calibration specifically below a threshold and uses that goal-oriented notion to post-process Gaussian-process predictions for optimization rather than for general uncertainty reporting.
Why it works

Why tail-focused calibration can improve expected improvement

Expected improvement integrates only the part of the predictive distribution that falls below the threshold. That means two models with similar global likelihood can still produce very different acquisition values if one allocates too much probability mass to rare good outcomes. Tail-focused calibration corrects that mismatch directly.

The method is post-hoc, which matters operationally. You can keep a standard Gaussian process fitted by maximum likelihood and then adjust how its predictive distribution behaves in the relevant region. This avoids redesigning the surrogate from scratch while still fixing the search decision that matters most.

The density result in the paper matters conceptually. Calibrating the tail could have made the optimizer overly conservative and caused it to stop exploring parts of the design space. The paper shows that the expected-improvement search built on the calibrated model still remains dense in the space, so the correction does not destroy the long-run ability to reach any region.

Math after the picture

Probability of improvement below a threshold

First in words:

For minimization, the acquisition cares about the predictive mass below a threshold. Tail calibration adjusts that mass so the probability of beating the threshold matches measured outcomes more closely.

Then the compact form:

improvement_probability(x) = P( f(x) <= t ); calibrated_probability(x) = Calibrate( improvement_probability(x) )

Every symbol:

xa candidate point to evaluate next
f(x)the unknown objective value at x
tthe threshold, often the current best observed value
P( f(x) <= t )the model's predicted chance that x beats the threshold
Calibrate(...)the post-hoc map that corrects the lower-tail probability
A tiny worked example with three candidates
Suppose the current best value is 0.5. The Gaussian process predicts candidate A has a 40 percent chance of falling below 0.5, candidate B has 35 percent, and candidate C has 20 percent.
Historical checking shows that points predicted at 40 percent only succeed about 15 percent of the time, while points predicted at 35 percent succeed about 30 percent of the time.
Tail calibration therefore pushes A downward and leaves B closer to its original value. After correction, B can outrank A even though the raw surrogate preferred A.
That ranking change matters because expected improvement is supposed to spend experiments where true low outcomes are plausible, not where the model is only overconfident.

The calibration map is useful only if it changes the acquisition ranking for the right reason: it should remove false optimism and preserve genuine low-value opportunities.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Threshold chosen badly. If the threshold is unrelated to the optimization goal, the method calibrates the wrong tail region. Then the correction can leave expected improvement unchanged or even distort it.
Too little data in the lower tail. Calibration is hard if very few observations fall near or below the threshold because the data-based check of predicted probabilities becomes noisy.
Severe model misspecification. If the kernel or noise assumptions are badly wrong, a post-hoc tail map cannot fully repair the surrogate because the model may be misranking whole regions, not just mis-scaling their probabilities.
Done right. Use a threshold tied directly to the current optimization target, apply calibration only when there is enough evidence to estimate tail reliability, and retain exploration guarantees so the corrected acquisition still visits the design space broadly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a one-dimensional black-box minimization problem with a few local minima. Fit a Gaussian process after a small number of initial samples and compare two acquisition loops: standard expected improvement and expected improvement after lower-tail calibration relative to the current best value. For each loop, record simple regret after each evaluation and measure how often predicted probabilities of improvement match actual outcomes on held-out candidate points.

simple_regret = best_found_value - true_global_minimum; tail_calibration_error = mean( absolute difference between predicted improvement probability and observed improvement frequency )

The demo should show that tail calibration can lower tail-calibration error and reduce simple regret because the acquisition spends more trials on genuinely promising regions instead of on points whose downside was overstated.
