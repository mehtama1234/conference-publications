In plain terms

Imagine you're trying to guess what's broken in your car. Before you know anything, you might think it's equally likely to be the battery, the alternator, or the fuel pump. But then you notice the headlights are also dim. That's new evidence. The dimness makes the battery much more likely than the other two. Bayes' rule is the recipe for turning 'new evidence' into 'updated belief.' It says: look at how often the evidence shows up when your guess is right, multiply by how plausible your guess was to start with, then divide by how often you'd see that evidence no matter what. That gives you the updated probability that your guess is correct.

Here's why this matters: whenever you learn something, you're doing this backward reasoning. You observe the effect (dim lights), and you work backward to the cause (which system broke). Bayes' rule formalizes that backward step so machines can do it too. It's not guessing — it's taking your initial uncertainty (the guess you started with), mixing it with the new data, and producing a sharper belief that respects both.

The equation
p(\theta|x)=p(x|\theta)p(\theta)/p(x)
p(θ|x)The probability you believe in some explanation after seeing the data — called the posterior.
p(x|θ)The probability you'd see this data if that explanation were true — called the likelihood.
p(θ)Your initial belief in that explanation before seeing any data — called the prior.
p(x)The overall probability of seeing this data from any source — called the evidence or normalizer.
Worked example
Guessing a coin flip from tosses
You find a coin. Is it fair (50-50) or rigged (90% heads)?
Prior belief: 50% fair, 50% rigged.
You toss it 3 times: all heads. Evidence strength: fair gives 1/8, rigged gives 0.9³ = 0.73.
Posterior: (0.73 × 0.5) / (0.73 × 0.5 + 0.125 × 0.5) ≈ 0.85. Now you're 85% sure it's rigged.
Why it shows up

Bayes' rule appears across machine learning because almost every modern method must update its beliefs: inference engines guess hidden causes from observations, optimization adjusts the knobs inside a system based on how wrong it was, reinforcement learning updates value estimates from collected rewards, and language models adapt their prediction patterns based on context. Whenever a system sees new data and must say 'I was partly wrong, here's my better guess,' it's applying this recipe — sometimes explicitly as Bayesian inference, sometimes implicitly baked into the learning algorithm (the process that tunes the model) or sampling. This rule also connects to entropy (a measure of uncertainty about which outcome will happen), because updating beliefs means reducing entropy: you started uncertain, now you're sharper.
