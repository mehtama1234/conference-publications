Getting models to follow detailed instructions better

These papers improve how well models follow specific instructions and learn from feedback using preference optimization, reinforcement learning, and fine-tuning techniques.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Imagine you ask a model to write a poem in a specific style, with specific constraints, and the model mostly ignores your constraints and does its own thing. This happens because models trained on internet text learn many different writing styles but have not learned to rank your particular instructions as more important than their default patterns. Papers in this area focus on making models respect and follow detailed instructions precisely. The core idea is fine-tuning: after a model learns basic language patterns, you show it many examples of what good instruction-following looks like and train it to match that behavior.

One effective technique is preference optimization, where instead of just showing the model correct outputs, you show it pairs of outputs—one good and one bad—and train the model to prefer the good one. This works because the model already understands language well, so it just needs to learn to steer toward human preferences. Another approach uses reinforcement learning with a reward signal that scores how well the model followed the instructions. The model learns which behaviors get rewarded and leans into them. The challenge is that feedback has to be consistent and signal real information, so creating good training signals becomes a central problem.

The result is models that actually do what you ask instead of doing something similar-ish. This matters for practical applications because users become frustrated when models do not listen, and it matters for safety because a model that does not follow instructions is unpredictable. Papers show that careful fine-tuning can make a model follow instructions much more reliably, and this skill does not require training on trillions more examples—just thousands of well-chosen examples with clear feedback.

How the work breaks down

Making models follow detailed instructions splits into a few natural directions. First, showing models pairs of good versus bad outputs and training them to prefer good ones—this is called preference optimization, which works because the model already understands language, so it just needs to learn to steer toward your preferences. Second, building better reward signals that accurately score instruction-following, so models learn what good looks like instead of gaming a noisy reward. Third, finding smarter ways to fine-tuning that avoid breaking the model's general abilities, because models often forget what they learned during pre-training when taught new instructions. And fourth, handling specialized domains where generic training fails because the model lacks domain knowledge or was trained on different data.

Preference Optimization & Direct Alignment

When a model understands language well but does not consistently follow your instructions, showing it pairs of responses—one following instructions well, one not—and training it to prefer the good one often works better than just showing good examples because comparisons are easier than absolute judgment. This approach, called preference optimization, avoids retraining the entire model from scratch, which saves computation. But it only works if the preference signal (the score of which response is better) is accurate and consistent, because if the reward is noisy or backwards, the model learns the wrong behavior.

Anchored Supervised Fine-Tuning directly improves preference-based learning by anchoring training to what the model is already good at, achieving better results than standard fine-tuning across math, medical, and code tasks while staying computationally cheap. Chasing the Tail tackles a real problem: when you train on preferences, the model can learn to game the reward instead of actually following instructions better, so rubric-based rewards—scoring actual behaviors like "checks work" not just "final answer right"—fix this over-optimization because they measure the actual behavior you want, not just a proxy. From Verifiable Dot to Reward Chain trains the model on reasoning steps with rewards at each step, not just the end result, which improves reasoning and open-ended generation on many benchmarks because step-level feedback guides the model through its thinking process. SoLoPO enhances preference optimization to work on very long contexts, which is a practical need because many detailed instructions are long. When Weak LLMs Speak with Confidence shows that models need not be confident to help train better models—training with only 20% of human annotations still beats standard methods when you use confidence-weighted preference optimization, which means less human work for the same result because the model itself scores its confidence. Capability Traps in DPO warns that the preference margin (how much the model prefers good over bad) does not always mean the model got better at the actual task, so you should measure the actual capability, not just the preference score, because sometimes the preference and capability diverge.

These papers agree that preference signals are central but disagree on how to get good signals: rubric-based rewards measure behavior, step-level rewards measure reasoning, confidence weighting uses model uncertainty, and direct capability measurement avoids proxy metrics. Together they show preference optimization only works with honest feedback.

Fine-Tuning Without Forgetting General Skills

A core problem is that teaching a model to follow your specific instructions often makes it worse at other tasks—fine-tuning can erase general knowledge the model learned during pre-training. This happens because the model has to redistribute its learned patterns to focus on the new instructions. Smart fine-tuning picks which parts of the model to update and which to leave alone, or picks the right training examples to avoid erasing useful knowledge.

Alignment-Enhanced Integration shows that you can efficiently train sparse versions of large models and keep instruction-following ability, which matters because sparse models save memory and compute. Differential Fine-Tuning trains the model on many different reasoning styles at once instead of one at a time, which stops the model from forgetting earlier reasoning styles as you teach it new ones because multi-task training spreads learning across all tasks equally. CPQS-Tuning uses a self-assessment trick: the model itself predicts which training examples will help it improve, so you train on only the hardest or most informative examples instead of wasting time on easy ones, which means you need less data to reach the same performance—with just 10% of data, it beats models trained on everything. Comparing the Learning Dynamics shows that fine-tuning works differently than in-context learning (showing examples in the prompt): fine-tuning changes what the model knows, while in-context learning just changes how the model applies what it knows, suggesting that the best approach depends on whether you want the model to learn new knowledge or just apply old knowledge differently. Getting Your LLMs Ready for Reinforcement Learning shows that to prepare a model for reinforcement learning afterward, early stopping based on diversity (does the model produce varied outputs?) beats stopping based on performance (is the final answer right?), which hints that overfitting to one instruction style blocks later learning because it locks the model into narrow behavior. SFT Does Not Always Hurt General Capabilities addresses the worry directly: with a careful weight-protection method called TALR, teaching a model domain-specific instructions does not harm its general abilities because the method shields learned knowledge, and it actually outperforms simpler weight-protection methods, which solves the classic tradeoff between specialization and generalization.

These papers agree that you can fine-tune without forgetting, but disagree on the mechanism: sparse training reduces model size, multi-task training prevents focus loss, data filtering avoids harmful examples, diversity-based stopping avoids overfitting to one style, and weight protection preserves general knowledge. All show that the right approach depends on what you want—domain skill, general retention, or preparation for later RL training.

Building & Using Reward Models

A reward model is code or a neural network that scores model outputs—good instruction-following gets a high score, bad gets a low score. This score trains the model or guides choice among outputs. But reward models often fail: they can be tricked, they can score answers right even when the reasoning is wrong, and they can disagree with humans about what matters, which breaks the entire training process since the model learns to optimize a bad signal. Building better reward models is a hard, central problem because every improvement in reward quality lifts every downstream model that uses it.

Foundational Automatic Evaluators trains a single large model to score outputs across many different domains and tasks using natural language rubrics (like "is the answer correct and well-explained?"), and this single model beats specialized scorers on reasoning tasks because general-purpose models learn transferable scoring patterns, and it works as a verifier in reinforcement learning to improve downstream models by up to 14%. Chasing the Tail (already mentioned above) shows that rewards should score behaviors, not just final answers, because behavior-level rewards detect when the model is using the right reasoning even if the answer is slightly wrong. Distributional Alignment Games treats reward modeling as an optimization problem (a mathematical setup where you minimize a function) with a tractable solution, unifying different approaches to reward design and making it work with popular reinforcement learning algorithms because it gives a single framework. Factored Causal Representation Learning learns reward models that understand which parts of an output affect the reward (e.g., correctness vs. style), making them resistant to adversarial attempts to fool the reward because they ground rewards in causal structure, and this helps reinforcement learning avoid reward hacking because the model cannot game one factor without breaking others. Reward Model Routing avoids building one reward model—instead, it routes each input to the best reward model for that type of task, which beats having one-size-fits-all reward or just averaging multiple reward models because different tasks reward different behaviors. Why DPO is a Misspecified Estimator warns that preference optimization assumes reward models are accurate, but they are not, and proposes AuxDPO which adds auxiliary information to fix this misunderstanding because the math breaks when rewards have bias.

These papers agree that reward models are central but imperfect because they are the bottleneck in instruction-following. Some build better single models (Foundational Automatic Evaluators) because general-purpose models scale, some make rewards measure behavior not just outcomes (Chasing the Tail) because behavior is what you actually care about, some understand causal structure in rewards (Factored Causal) because that prevents gaming, some route to the right reward for the task (Reward Model Routing) because different tasks need different scoring, and some fix the math when rewards are noisy (AuxDPO) because theory must match practice. Together they show that the quality of the reward signal limits how well the model can follow instructions.

Instruction-Following for Specialized Domains & Multimodal

Generic instruction-following training often fails on specialized domains like cybersecurity, medicine, or vision tasks—the model lacks background knowledge or has been trained on different data distributions. Multimodal models (that see images and text) also face unique challenges: visual information can be lost or misused when fine-tuning. Domain-specific fine-tuning requires curated data or careful architectural changes.

RedSage trains a model specifically for cybersecurity questions by fine-tuning on curated examples, achieving clear wins over baseline models on both cybersecurity and general benchmarks, showing that domain focus does not require sacrificing general ability because domain data teaches specialization not narrowing. The Unseen Bias in Pre-Norm MLLMs identifies a real architectural problem in vision-language models: the visual and text pathways are unbalanced, causing the model to lose visual information during fine-tuning because the text pathway overpowers it, and fixing this imbalance improves performance across multimodal tasks and text-only tasks like MMLU because the model now preserves both signals. We-Math 2.0 builds a dataset and fine-tuning approach for visual math reasoning, where the model must understand images of math equations and diagrams, achieving competitive results and strong generalization across all 491 math knowledge points because visual math requires reasoning about spatial and symbolic structure together. pFedMMA tackles federated fine-tuning for vision-language models—where models are trained on-device across many users without sharing raw data—using multi-modal adapters, and achieves top personalization-generalization tradeoffs on domain-shift and label-shift scenarios because multi-modal adapters adjust both modalities independently. Deep Pre-Alignment for VLMs aligns the visual and language encoders before fine-tuning on downstream tasks, preventing vision capability forgetting during fine-tuning with 32.9% less vision knowledge loss because alignment happens before task-specific pressure distorts the visual encoder. Seeing is Understanding improves multimodal understanding by changing how visual and text pathways interact during fine-tuning, achieving 6.2% average improvement across 12 multimodal benchmarks without adding model weights because it improves how existing weights compute together.

These papers agree that domain and modality matter: cybersecurity instruction-following needs domain data, visual understanding needs architectural fixes to prevent information loss, math needs specialized datasets, federated training needs privacy-aware methods, and multimodal alignment should happen before downstream fine-tuning. Together they show that off-the-shelf instruction-following methods fail in specialized settings and must be adapted.

Reinforcement Learning & Test-Time Methods

Beyond supervised fine-tuning, you can train models using reinforcement learning—the model generates outputs and gets rewards, then learns to generate higher-reward outputs. You can also improve outputs at test time without retraining, which avoids the cost of model updates. These methods are impactful and complex: reward signals must be reliable because they guide all learning, and the model can learn to fake good rewards instead of actually improving because the reward is what the model optimizes.

From Verifiable Dot to Reward Chain (already mentioned) uses step-level rewards in reinforcement learning, substantially beating supervised fine-tuning even with ten times more data because step-by-step guidance teaches reasoning, and generalizing better while keeping output diversity. Getting Your LLMs Ready for Reinforcement Learning shows that preparing a model before reinforcement learning matters: using diversity-based early stopping gives better RL results than performance-based stopping because diversity prevents overfitting to early solutions. Good SFT Optimizes for SFT, Better SFT Prepares for Reinforcement Learning confirms that standard supervised fine-tuning does not prepare the model for RL because it optimizes for likelihood not for reward, and better preparation leads to large gains—up to 14.6% on reasoning benchmarks after RL training. Implicit Turn-Wise Policy Optimization improves reinforcement learning by giving feedback at each conversation turn instead of just at the end, converging faster with PPO, GRPO, or RLOO algorithms because turn-level rewards shape behavior throughout the interaction. REAR enables test-time preference realignment by decomposing rewards after the model has generated output, letting you improve outputs at test time without retraining because it re-scores without updating weights, and it scales to math and vision tasks. Temporal Self-Rewarding Language Models uses past and future context—the model looks at what it said earlier and generates a preference judgment, which is more stable than external reward models because internal consistency matters, reaching 29.44 win-rate on AlpacaEval for an 8B model.

These papers agree that reward design is central to reinforcement learning because the reward is what the model optimizes: step-level rewards guide reasoning better because they provide dense feedback, turn-level feedback converges faster because it reshapes behavior throughout interaction, test-time decomposition avoids retraining because it re-scores without updating, and self-generated rewards from context are more stable because internal consistency grounds them. But they disagree on how to make rewards: some use external models, some decompose after generation, some use the model's own past context. Together they show that reinforcement learning for instruction-following is possible but requires careful reward engineering.
