Negative Pre-activations Differentiate Syntax

A small set of neurons use negative values as a dedicated channel for syntax, separate from semantic meaning.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Modern language models use smooth functions like GELU that let neurons output negative numbers, not just positive ones. Older interpretability work assumed positive outputs carried all the information. This paper shows that negative outputs actually do real work: a tiny fraction of neurons send a specific signal through their negative values, and that signal is what the model uses to track grammar and syntax.

Zero assumption version

Why did interpretability overlook negative signals?

For decades, the most common neuron activation function was ReLU, which forced all outputs to zero or positive. Since negative values simply did not exist, researchers naturally focused on positive activations (large ones meant the neuron was active, small meant it was not). That habit stuck around even after models switched to GELU and SiLU, which do allow negative values, because interpretability researchers did not yet have a reason to look for negative-value patterns.

A naive approach: assume negative outputs are just noise, either leftover artifacts from the learning process or unused channels. If that were true, you could ignore them or zero them out without losing anything important. But this paper asks: what if they are actually being used for something specific?

The shift: negative values might carry real signal, not noise. The paper finds that certain neurons deliberately use their negative outputs to differentiate grammatical structures, separate from semantic meaning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies how negative activations are treated in a handful of neurons. Specifically, it zeros out only the negative part of the output for these special Wasserstein neurons, while leaving their positive parts and all other neurons untouched.

the object it moves  =  Negative pre-activations in Wasserstein neurons
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: what negative channels are doing

measured quantity  =  Negative-channel syntax load

This paper measures how much of the grammar-detection work flows through the negative output channel of a small, special population of neurons. It is not about overall neuron importance; it is specifically about whether the negative values carry task-critical signal (as opposed to positive values doing all the work).

The paper estimates this quantity by comparing two ablations: zeroing only negative outputs vs. zeroing only positive outputs. If grammar degrades more when negatives are zeroed, that negative channel was carrying syntax information. They also use Wasserstein distance (a mathematical measure of how far a distribution sits from a simple normal curve) to identify which neurons have this special property.

Why it works

Why negative outputs became a syntax channel

During training, the model gradually discovered that it could separate concerns: use one output channel (positive) for what a word means semantically, and a different channel (negative) for how it fits into the sentence structure. This separation is efficient because grammar and semantics are partly independent — a word like 'run' can be a verb or noun depending on context, but the model handles that split in different neurons.

When you zero out the negative values of these Wasserstein neurons, the model loses its dedicated syntax channel. It must now try to pack both grammar and meaning into positive activations alone. The other neurons cannot easily reroute that syntax information because the specialized neurons have already learned to rely on their negative channel; changing them suddenly breaks the downstream reading machinery.

The paper shows this is a real causal effect by comparing to random ablations of other neurons. If you zero out random positive activations across many non-special neurons (damaging them equally badly), grammar performance holds up better, because the model can work around generic damage by rerouting through many paths, but cannot reroute syntax without those specialized negative channels. This proves the syntax channel is not just any part of the network, but specifically the negative values in these Wasserstein neurons.

Measuring the syntax signal in negative activations

First in words:

A tiny worked example
Wasserstein neuron negatives ablated:
Grammar accuracy: 92 (before) -> 64 (after) = 28 point drop
Semantics accuracy: 85 (before) -> 83 (after) = 2 point drop
Double dissociation = 28 - 2 = 26 (large, syntax-specific damage)
Random neuron negatives ablated:
Grammar accuracy: 92 (before) -> 85 (after) = 7 point drop
Semantics accuracy: 85 (before) -> 78 (after) = 7 point drop
Double dissociation = 7 - 7 = 0 (balanced damage, no specialization)

A large dissociation shows Wasserstein neurons use negatives specifically for syntax, not as a side effect.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Negative activations are not always used. This signal only exists in a tiny population of Wasserstein neurons. Most neurons do not split syntax and semantics into different polarity channels. The discovery is specific, not universal.
Only works with smooth activations. Models with ReLU do not allow negative outputs, so this syntax channel cannot exist, because the model has no way to send signals through negative values. Smooth activations like GELU and SiLU are required for this division of labor to emerge.
Damage accumulates layer-by-layer. Small ablations of negatives in one layer only slightly degrade grammar; but the same ablation in every layer adds up to major failure. The effect is distributed across depth, not localized.
This mechanism is real. The paper controls for perplexity (total model confusion) and shows random ablations have different effects. The sign-specific targeting of Wasserstein negative activations is causal, not coincidental.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model (a few million parameters) with a smooth activation and train it on a task with grammar + semantics (e.g., predicting the next word in grammatically varied sentences). Measure Wasserstein distance for each neuron. Identify the high-Wasserstein ones. For those neurons only, zero out negative pre-activations and measure grammar accuracy on a held-out grammatical benchmark (BLiMP-style) and a semantic benchmark (word-sense or factual). Compare to zeroing positive pre-activations (or random neuron negatives) to confirm the double dissociation.

Double_Dissociation = (Grammar_accuracy_drop) / (Semantics_accuracy_drop)

If the dissociation is large (>5x) for Wasserstein neurons and near 1.0 for random neurons, the demo proves syntax and semantics are routed through different polarity channels in these special neurons.
