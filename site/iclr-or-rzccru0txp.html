Negative Pre-activations Differentiate Syntax

Neurons in language models use negative numbers to tell grammar apart from meaning.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Modern language models use smooth activation functions (rules that turn raw scores into outputs, like GELU or SiLU) that let negative numbers carry real information, not just dead weight. But interpretability studies have ignored negative numbers since the ReLU era, treating them as noise. This paper shows they are not. In a language model, a small set of special neurons actively uses negative pre-activations to track grammar rules, and breaking just that negative region breaks grammatical knowledge while leaving other abilities intact.

Zero assumption version

Why interpretability studies miss negative numbers

For decades, neural networks used ReLU, an activation function (a simple rule that turns raw scores into outputs) that outputs zero for any negative input. So negative inputs were indeed dead — nothing to interpret there.

Modern models switched to smooth activation functions like GELU and SiLU, which do not clamp negatives to zero. Negative pre-activations (the raw scores before the activation function processes them) can now carry real signal. But neuron interpretability studies have not updated. They still focus only on neurons that fire big positive values, treating negative regions as invisible or noise. This leaves half the story untold.

In one sentence. The paper finds that a sparse subpopulation of neurons, called Wasserstein neurons, use negative pre-activations as an active mechanism to track grammar.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Find Wasserstein neurons

Search the model for neurons whose output patterns (the collection of all outputs they produce) deviate strongly from a Gaussian, a normal bell curve.

Step 2: Measure the negative region

For these neurons, look at pairs of inputs and check: when do their negative pre-activations differ?

Step 3: Sign-specific ablation

Zero out only the negative pre-activations of these Wasserstein neurons, leaving positives alone.

Step 4: Test grammatical understanding

Run the perturbed model on grammar benchmarks like BLiMP and TSE that test syntax knowledge.

Step 5: Compare to controls

Ablate other neurons' negatives at matching perplexity; they barely hurt grammar, confirming Wasserstein negatives are special.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper targets the negative pre-activations (the raw negative scores before activation) in a small subpopulation of neurons. It does not change the positive side or the neuron weights themselves. It only zeros the negative region, asking what role it plays.

the object it moves  =  negative pre-activations in Wasserstein neurons
The hidden quantity

Wasserstein distance: how different is this neuron's output?

measured quantity  =  Wasserstein distance (WD)

Wasserstein distance is a number that measures how far an output distribution (the pattern of all outputs this neuron produces) is from a standard bell curve (Gaussian). The paper measures it by taking random pairs of inputs, computing how far apart their outputs are in space, and comparing that to how the outputs would spread if they were normally distributed. A high WD means the neuron's outputs are clumpy or bimodal (two hills instead of one smooth mound), a strong signal that it is doing something special.

The paper estimates WD during training and across models, and finds that the same neurons with the highest WD at the end of training are the ones that use negative pre-activations to differentiate syntax. These neurons emerge within the first 50 billion tokens (words, including repetitions, that the model sees during training) of training, exactly when grammatical abilities crystallize.

Paper reading. The paper identifies Wasserstein neurons as the top 1 percent by WD among pre-activation layers immediately before the MLP nonlinearity (the gate projection in modern Llama models, the up projection in Pythia). These neurons are sparse and emerge sharply during training; their structural specialization correlates with grammatical competence.
Why it works

Why negative pre-activations crack the syntax code

Smooth activation functions amplify small differences in negative pre-activations. When two inputs produce similar positive scores, their outputs after GELU stay close. But when they produce different negative scores, the negative region of the activation curve is steep, so small input differences become big output differences. This creates a natural region for doing fine-grained comparison work, exactly what syntax demands.

Wasserstein neurons in early layers (layers 1–4) build a syntactic scaffold — a layer-by-layer encoding of grammar rules like subject-verb agreement and part-of-speech tags. Because layer outputs feed into the next layer's inputs, errors in early syntax scaffolding compound; later layers build on broken foundations. Syntactic scaffolding tokens (the words that reveal grammar structure) produce the worst surprisal (prediction error) when their Wasserstein neurons' negative regions are ablated. Non-grammatical benchmarks, which rely on semantic understanding rather than rule structure, remain largely intact because semantics is encoded elsewhere.

Math after the picture

The math behind Wasserstein distance

First in words:

Wasserstein distance between actual outputs and Gaussian is the average distance between random pairs of outputs, compared to the expected distance under a Gaussian model.

Then the compact form:

WD = E[||y_i - y_j||_2] - E_gaussian[||y_i - y_j||_2]

Every symbol:

y_i, y_joutputs of the neuron on two different inputs
||y_i - y_j||_2the Euclidean distance (straight-line length) between the two outputs
E[·]average value computed over many random pairs
E_gaussian[·]average distance you would expect if outputs followed a normal bell curve
A tiny worked example
Suppose a Wasserstein neuron processes four test inputs and produces outputs: y1=0.2, y2=0.9, y3=−0.5, y4=−0.8.
Pair 1: ||0.2 − 0.9||=0.7. Pair 2: ||0.2 − (−0.5)||=0.7. Pair 3: ||0.9 − (−0.8)||=1.7.
Average distance of actual pairs: (0.7+0.7+1.7)/3≈1.03.
For a standard Gaussian outputting similar-magnitude numbers, expected average pair distance ≈ 0.8.
WD ≈ 1.03 − 0.8 = 0.23: this neuron's outputs spread wider than normal, signature of a high-WD neuron.

High Wasserstein distance signals that the neuron is not averaging signals passively but actively segregating inputs into distinct categories — exactly the fine-grained comparison needed for syntax.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gaussian assumption breaks down. Wasserstein distance only reveals neurons that deviate from Gaussian. If a neuron uses negatives for syntax but maintains a Gaussian-like shape overall, the metric might miss it. The paper finds the top 1 percent by WD, so some edge cases are not caught.
Syntactic effects compound across layers, hiding local damage. A single ablated negative region might cause small local error, but when this error propagates through eight layers of transformer blocks (each block processes and passes information to the next), it compounds into dramatic failures. Isolating the true single-layer contribution is hard.
Non-syntactic models might not show the effect. The paper studies models trained primarily on next-token prediction, which incentivizes syntax early. A model trained on a different objective or a smaller model might distribute grammar knowledge differently.
Done right: layer-wise ablation + perplexity-matched controls. The paper ablates layers one group at a time and compares to a baseline of ablating random neurons at the same perplexity cost. This isolates the Wasserstein effect and proves it is not just damage.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model (or use a pre-trained mini model) and implement a sign-specific ablation: a function that zeros the negative pre-activations of a given set of neurons while preserving the positive side. Run the model on a simple grammar test: a dataset of grammatical versus scrambled sentences, or a benchmark like subject-verb agreement in English. Compute accuracy on both before and after ablation. Then ablate the same number of random neurons' negatives at the same average perplexity cost. The demo succeeds if Wasserstein neuron ablation drops grammar accuracy much more than random ablation does.

grammar_drop = accuracy_before_wasserstein_ablation - accuracy_after_wasserstein_ablation; randomness_drop = accuracy_before_random_ablation - accuracy_after_random_ablation; ratio = grammar_drop / randomness_drop (should be > 2x for small Wasserstein sets).

The irreplaceable role of negative pre-activations in Wasserstein neurons becomes impossible to miss: a small surgical strike on one region breaks grammar, while brute-force damage to ten times as many random neurons leaves syntax largely standing.
