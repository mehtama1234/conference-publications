scDFM: Distributional Flow Matching for Single-Cell Perturbation Prediction

Instead of predicting one cell at a time, predict the whole population shift when a drug or gene edit hits cells.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When a drug hits a cell, it does not change just that one cell in isolation—it shifts the entire population. Cells respond differently based on what cell type they are and what other genes are already active. This paper shows how to predict the full pattern of what happens to thousands of cells at once, not just individual cell-by-cell guesses. The solution: use a flow (a smooth path through expression space) to learn the population-level transformation, and add a population-matching score to make sure the predicted cells look like a real crowd, not a jumble.

Zero assumption version

Cell response prediction is stuck on an old assumption

In biology labs, scientists use drugs or genetic tools to perturb cells—trigger a change—and watch what happens to the genes that turn on or off. The goal: predict how cells respond without running the expensive experiment. But measurements are noisy and sparse. Each cell's response is also different, even starting from the same type.

Current methods try to predict one cell at a time: given a control cell state and a perturbation label, guess what that cell will become. The problem: this ignores the fact that perturbations push populations, not individuals. When a drug hits a dish of 10,000 cells, not all of them shift the same way. Some cells may not respond at all; others shift far. A good prediction method needs to capture the entire crowd—the distribution of where cells end up—not just match individual pairs.

The real challenge: Predict where an entire population of perturbed cells lands, not where one cell goes.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build a smooth path

Learn a flow: a curve from control cells to perturbed cells. The flow is time-conditioned, so it can curve and bend.

Inject perturbation signal

At every step, tell the model which drug or genetic edit was applied and let it adjust the path differently per perturbation.

Use gene knowledge

Build a mask from real co-expression (genes that often appear together in nature). Use it to focus the model on gene pairs that naturally go together.

Amplify the real shift, cancel the noise

Use differential attention (subtract one attention pattern from another) to boost signals that differ between control and perturbed, and suppress gene correlations that are just noise, because what matters for predicting the drug response is which genes *change* together, not which genes happen to be correlated in both control and perturbed states.

Measure population fit

After training, check if the predicted crowd has the same statistical shape as the real one, using a population-distance score. This catches when cell-by-cell accuracy hides a wrong overall distribution.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes what the model is trained to predict: not individual cell trajectories, but a population-level transformation (a flow). The move: add a population-matching signal (the MMD score) alongside the usual cell-by-cell loss. This forces the model to care about global shape, not just local accuracy.

the object it moves  =  the training objective—from individual cell pairs to population distributions
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: distribution shift

measured quantity  =  Population-level discrepancy (MMD)

The paper measures something new: how different the predicted crowd is from the real crowd, treating them as statistical distributions, not sets of individual cells. This quantity is called MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy). It is a number: how far apart are two crowds in terms of their overall shape and spread?

The paper estimates it by comparing summary statistics of the predicted and real populations—like comparing group averages, group variance, and higher moments (measures of how lopsided or fat-tailed each group is). Unlike metrics that compare cell by cell, MMD asks: if I mixed the predicted and real cells into one pile, how obviously separate would they still look? A large MMD means the crowds are visibly different. A small MMD means they could be the same population.

Paper reading. MMD loss is computed as the squared distance between feature-space representations of the true and generated cell distributions, using multiple kernel functions (mathematical recipes for measuring similarity) to capture different aspects of how the distributions differ.
Why it works

Why population-level matching improves predictions

Here is the mechanistic chain. When you train a model cell-by-cell, each prediction is compared to one ground truth: if your model predicts cell output A and the real output is B, you measure the difference and nudge the model to make A closer to B. The problem: this only forces local accuracy. You could get every cell right but the group as a whole wrong—maybe all predicted cells cluster in one tight spot while the real ones spread out, or vice versa. The crowd might have the wrong amount of variance, the wrong shape, the wrong extremes.

By adding a population-level score (MMD) to the training objective, the model is rewarded not just for getting cells individually close, but for making the predicted crowd's overall statistics match the real crowd. Why this works: it acts as a second, global supervisor. The cell-by-cell loss says 'make each cell match its pair.' The MMD loss says 'make the whole group have the same shape.' Together they guide the model toward a transformation that is both locally accurate and globally faithful. This matters in biology because cells are inherently noisy—some cells in the real data are just random variation—and the model should not try to match every quirk, only reproduce the true population mean and spread.

The paper further adds differential attention—a mechanism that learns two separate attention patterns (one for control, one for perturbed) and then computes their difference. Why: it separates the stable baseline correlations (which genes usually go together) from the perturbation-specific changes (which genes change together when the drug hits). By suppressing the baseline and amplifying only the differential signal, the model focuses learning on the causal response, not the background noise.

Math after the picture

The training equation

First in words:

loss equals flow-matching loss plus MMD penalty weight times population discrepancy

Then the compact form:

total_loss = flow_matching_loss + lambda * MMD(generated_distribution, real_distribution)

Every symbol:

flow_matching_lossthe standard point-by-point error: how far is each predicted cell from its intended target along the flow
MMD(...)Maximum Mean Discrepancy: a number measuring how different two crowds are in their overall statistics
lambdaa weight (a tuning number) that controls how much to care about crowd shape vs. individual accuracy
generated_distributionthe set of all predicted perturbed cells
real_distributionthe actual perturbed cells from the experiment
A tiny worked example
Suppose 100 predicted cells have mean gene activity 5.2 and spread (variance) 1.8.
Real 100 cells have mean 5.0 and spread 1.9.
Flow-matching loss (cell-by-cell) might be 0.03 (cells are close to their targets).
MMD measures the gap between (mean, spread) of predictions vs. real: roughly 0.1 (small, they are similar).
If lambda = 10, the MMD penalty is 10 * 0.1 = 1.0.
Total loss = 0.03 + 1.0 = 1.03.
If the model had made cells individually accurate (loss 0.02) but populations mismatched (MMD = 0.5), total would be 0.02 + 10 * 0.5 = 5.02—much worse, so the model learns to fix the distribution shape.

The equation says: get each cell right, AND make the predicted crowd look like the real crowd. Balancing the two stops the model from overfitting to noise in individual cell pairs.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If the co-expression graph is wrong or stale. The model uses a static gene co-expression graph as a mask to focus attention on gene pairs that go together. If this graph is outdated or built from cells very different from the test cells, it becomes a constraint that hurts rather than helps, because the mask prevents the model from learning the true perturbation-induced gene dependencies (which may differ from baseline co-expression). The model gets locked into spurious gene pairs and cannot learn the actual response.
If lambda (the MMD weight) is set too high. The model will ignore individual cell accuracy and only chase population statistics. Predicted cells might cluster far from their true positions as long as the group shape is right. This sounds good but is deceptive—a cell with the wrong exact state is useless in a wet-lab follow-up.
If training data has batch effects or hidden structure. Biological data often has technical noise—cells from one batch of experiments have slightly different baseline activity than cells from another batch. If the training set is skewed (too many cells from one condition, not enough from another), the model learns the wrong idea of what the 'population' should look like. MMD will match a wrong population.
Done right. The model learns both local accuracy (individual cells close to truth) and global fidelity (the crowd has the right shape, spread, and tails). It generalizes to unseen perturbations by relying on the underlying biology, not overfitting to the training set.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy dataset: 500 control cells (each is a list of 20 gene activity levels) and their responses to a known drug. Split into training and test. Train a model with and without the MMD objective. For each, compute two metrics: (1) mean error (how far predicted cells sit from real cells, cell by cell) and (2) population-shape error (how far apart are the summary statistics—mean, variance, third moment—of predicted vs. real populations).

The old baseline will show: low cell-by-cell error but higher population-shape error. The new method (with MMD) should show: similar or better cell-by-cell error, and much lower population-shape error, because the MMD loss acts as a second judge that forces the model to care about whether the predicted crowd *as a whole* looks like the real crowd, not just whether each cell is close to one true cell. The demo clinches that adding MMD pushes the model to match not just individual cells but the entire crowd.

population_error = sqrt((mean_pred - mean_real)^2 + (var_pred - var_real)^2 + (skew_pred - skew_real)^2)

The key win: predicted populations look like real ones, making the model useful for discovery, not just individual-cell benchmarking.
