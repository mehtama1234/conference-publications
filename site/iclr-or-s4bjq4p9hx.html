Enhancing Molecular Property Predictions by Learning from Bond Modelling and Interactions

Most molecule models only look at atoms, but DeMol treats bonds as first-class citizens and learns how atoms and bonds talk to each other.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When you predict what a molecule will do, you describe it by listing atoms and which atoms are stuck together. But this misses something real: the glue itself has properties. Bonds are not just pairwise atom connections—resonance and stereoselectivity are bond-level facts. DeMol fixes this by running two parallel descriptions (atoms and bonds) and weaving them together, so the model learns how atoms and bonds interact.

Zero assumption version

Atoms alone are not enough

To predict what a molecule does—how fast it reacts, what color it is, how stable it stays—you start with its structure. A structure is a list of atoms and which atoms are bonded to which other atoms.

A naive atom-only model just reads the atoms and the bonding pattern, then passes this through a graph neural network (a network that treats atoms as nodes and bonds as edges connecting them). The model learns to predict the output from atom properties and which atom is next to which. It never explicitly asks: what are the properties of the bond itself?

This breaks for subtle chemistry. A bond is not interchangeable—the same two atoms connected by different bond orders (single, double, triple) are different. Resonance (when a bond flips between two positions in a molecule) and stereoselectivity (when the 3D shape of a bond matters) are real bond-level facts that atom-only models skip over. The model predicts worse on these tricky cases.

The move. Run two separate graph models in parallel—one sees atoms, one sees bonds—then weave their outputs together so they learn how atoms and bonds interact.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Read the molecule two ways

One graph model processes atoms; another processes bonds. Each has its own embeddings.

Weave at multiple scales

Double-Helix Blocks at different depths fuse atom embeddings with bond embeddings, letting each channel see the other.

Learn cross-interactions

The model learns patterns like how a bond order affects nearby atom updates, and how atom changes propagate to bond importance.

Enforce chemical reality

A regularization term (based on covalent bond lengths) gently steers learned representations toward geometrically sensible structures.

Output the prediction

The fused embeddings produce the final molecular property estimate—resonance, stability, reactivity, whatever the task asks for.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of running a single graph neural network that treats bonds as featureless edges between atoms, DeMol runs two parallel graph networks—one atom-centric, one bond-centric—and weaves together their learned number-lists (embeddings, which are just lists of numbers that encode what the model has learned about each atom or bond) at multiple layers. This forces the model to maintain two separate, complementary descriptions and learn how they interact.

The hidden quantity

Bond-atom interaction strength

measured quantity  =  cross-channel fusion depth

The hidden quantity is: how tightly do the atom and bond channels exchange information? Each Double-Helix Block at a given layer can fuse atom embeddings with bond embeddings—the model learns how much of each channel to mix in. A high fusion score means atom features drive bond updates strongly, and vice versa.

The paper measures this implicitly through the learned weights in the fusion blocks. At test time, you can inspect a fusion block and see how much each atom-side feature contributes to bond-side updates. Chemistry intuition says: bonds near aromatic rings should fuse strongly (resonance), and bonds in chiral centers should fuse strongly (stereoselectivity). If the model learns this, it has found the right hidden quantity.

Why it works

Why two channels learn chemistry better

A single atom-only model compresses all chemistry into atom states. When an atom has four neighbors, its embedding tries to encode both the identities of those neighbors AND the nature of the bonds. This is cramped—the embedding has one job and it cannot split focus.

DeMol gives bond-ness its own channel. A bond embedding can specialize in tracking order (single, double, triple), conjugation (part of a resonant system), and stereochemistry (cis/trans, or chiral direction). The atom channel tracks valence, electronegativity, and local environment. They exchange information through fusion blocks—atom updates can say to the bond channel what it has learned, and vice versa. This is more expressive because each channel has room to be an expert in its own domain.

Mathematically, the fusion blocks act as learned bridges. A block might learn: when an atom has high aromatic character (detected from its embedding), boost the resonant-bond weight in the bond channel, because aromatic electrons are delocalized across multiple bonds and the model must flag this to the bond channel. When a bond shows high conjugation (from the bond channel), adjust neighboring atom embeddings to track delocalized electrons, because the atom channel then knows not to treat those atoms as isolated. Over thousands of training examples, the model tunes these bridges to capture chemistry's real dependencies.

Math after the picture

The learning objective

First in words:

final loss = task loss plus regularization loss; regularization loss penalizes distances between atoms that violate covalent bond lengths

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_task + lambda * sum_bonds (d_predicted - d_covalent)^2

Every symbol:

L_taskerror on the molecular property (what you are trying to predict)
L_regularizationpenalty for unrealistic bond lengths
lambdastrength of the chemical-reality constraint
d_predictedthe bond length learned by the dual-channel model
d_covalentthe standard covalent radius sum for that atom pair
A tiny worked example
Suppose we are predicting reaction energy. The model outputs a guess of 42 kJ/mol, but the true value is 40 kJ/mol. Task loss = (42-40)^2 = 4.
The model also produces internal embeddings that imply a C-C bond of length 1.54 Angstroms. Standard C-C covalent length is 1.54 A. Bond regularization penalty = (1.54-1.54)^2 = 0.
But an N-O bond in the molecule is learned as 1.25 A when covalent N-O is typically 1.40 A. Penalty = (1.25-1.40)^2 = 0.0225.
With lambda=0.1, regularization loss = 0.0225. Total = 4 + 0.1*0.0225 = 4.00225. The constraint is light, but it nudges the model toward real chemistry.

The regularization term is optional but essential: it tells the dual-channel model to respect known bond lengths, so atom-bond fusion learns bond structures that match actual chemistry.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too little fusion. If the Double-Helix Blocks do not weight fusion heavily enough, the two channels remain isolated and you get back the atom-only model.
Regularization too weak. If lambda is too small, the model learns geometrically implausible structures. The embeddings do not respect chemistry, and you overfit to training data.
Missing bond features. If bond input features (order, stereochemistry, conjugation) are poorly encoded, the bond channel starts blind and fusion cannot help, because the atom channel has nothing to fuse with—without good bond signals, the fusion blocks cannot learn which atom-side patterns should trigger bond-side updates.
Done right. The model learns to fuse strongly on bonds where chemistry is subtle (resonance, chirality) and lightly on simple bonds (alkanes). Regularization is tuned to guide without dominating. Bond features include all chemically relevant properties.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a set of small molecules with known properties (e.g., heats of formation, reaction barriers, or binding affinities from QM9 or PCQM4Mv2). Split them into training and test sets. Train DeMol and an atom-only baseline on the training set. On test molecules with subtle features (high aromatic character, stereoselectivity, resonance), the dual-channel model should predict within 5-10 percent of true value while the baseline is off by 20-30 percent. The demo should measure per-bond-type accuracy: aromatic and chiral bonds show the largest gap between DeMol and baseline.
