Zero assumption version

Flow Matching Generates Paths That Break Robot Rules

A flow matcher is a neural network that learns to generate robot movement plans (sequences of positions and motor commands over time) by training on good trajectory examples. At test time, you feed it a random starting point and it nudges it step by step toward realistic, useful motion.

The catch: there are three rules these trajectories must obey in the real world. First, safety constraints—the robot must not hit walls or obstacles. Second, action constraints—the motor commands must stay within safe power and torque limits. Third, dynamic consistency—the predicted motion must follow the actual laws of physics that govern the robot, so the plan is actually executable when sent to the real machine.

Naive flow matching has no way to guarantee any of these three rules at test time. It can violate collision bounds, exceed motor limits, and produce motion that contradicts physics. Rejection sampling—generate many trajectories and discard the broken ones—wastes huge computation and often fails on tight constraints.

In one sentence. Wrap the flow matcher in a virtual control layer that resets the trajectory to obey safety, motor, and physics rules on every step.

What the paper changes

The One Thing Changed

The flow matcher itself is unchanged. Instead, SAD-Flower adds a control layer that rewrites the trajectory in real time at test time. It takes the raw flow output and feeds it through a constraint-satisfaction optimizer before handing it to the robot. This virtual control input acts like an invisible hand that gently pushes the trajectory toward legality without breaking the learned motion patterns.

the object it moves  =  the trajectory at test time
The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Constraint Violation Margin

measured quantity  =  Constraint violation margin at each step

SAD-Flower measures how close the trajectory is to breaking each rule at each time step. For safety: the distance to the nearest obstacle. For motors: how much headroom remains before torque or power limits. For physics: whether the predicted motion matches what the laws of motion dictate—if the arm says it will swing down with a certain acceleration, does that match gravity and the arm's mass? If not, the trajectory is dynamically inconsistent and will fail on the real robot. Each of these is a number—how much illegal we are right now.

The paper uses control-theoretic tools (Barrier Functions for state and action constraints, Lyapunov Functions for physics) to track these margins. When a margin goes negative (rule broken), the optimizer pulls it back to zero. The control layer continuously estimates and enforces these margins from the learned flow model without ever retraining it.

Why it works

Why It Works: Hijack the Flow As a Controllable System

The core insight: instead of asking the flow matcher to learn constraint satisfaction (which requires retraining), treat the flow model as an existing dynamical system with a secret control input baked in. The flow model predicts: given where I am now, here is my next move. SAD-Flower adds a second question: given where I am now, here is my next move from the learned flow, but what adjustment do I need to apply so that adjustment respects all three rules?

Here is the real mechanism. The flow matcher has already learned realistic motion from data. At test time, the control layer reformulates the optimization as: minimize how much you change the flow's suggestion, subject to three hard constraints. This is a quadratic program—fast to solve. The constraints themselves come from control theory: barrier functions keep you away from bad states (collisions, motor overload), and Lyapunov functions ensure the dynamics obey physics. The solution is the smallest tweak to the flow that passes all three tests, applied every single step.

Why this works without retraining: the control layer does not retrain the flow matcher. It only solves an optimization at each step. The flow model's learned patterns are preserved. The constraints are enforced formally, not learned approximately. And because control theory provides a direct mechanism—not a classifier or a learned guidance—the method carries formal guarantees. Unseen constraints at test time are handled the same way: the barrier functions adapt to new bounds without any retraining.

Math after the picture

The Equation: Minimize Change, Enforce Constraints

First in words:

At each time step, find the smallest adjustment to the flow's prediction such that the safety margin stays non-negative, the motor margin stays non-negative, and the physics margin stays non-negative.

Then the compact form:

min_{delta} ||delta||^2 subject to h_s(x + delta) >= 0, h_a(u + delta_u) >= 0, h_p(dynamics) >= 0

Every symbol:

deltaadjustment to the position or command predicted by flow
||delta||^2squared size of that adjustment—keep it small
h_sbarrier function measuring distance to collision (safety)
h_abarrier function measuring distance to motor limits (admissibility)
h_pfunction measuring alignment with physics laws (dynamic consistency)
xcurrent predicted position from flow matcher
ucurrent predicted motor command from flow matcher
>= 0constraint must be satisfied (not violated)
A tiny worked example
Flow matcher predicts: next position = 5.0, next motor command = 12.0.
Safety check: distance to nearest wall = 0.3 (positive, safe).
Motor check: available torque headroom = 2.0 (positive, in limit).
Physics check: predicted acceleration matches laws = 0.1 (positive, consistent).
All three margins are non-negative. Adjustment delta = 0. Return flow prediction unchanged.
---
Different example: flow predicts next motor command = 15.0, but limit is 14.5.
Motor check: headroom = 14.5 - 15.0 = -0.5 (negative, violates limit).
Optimizer finds: delta_u = -0.6 reduces command to 14.4.
New motor margin: 14.5 - 14.4 = 0.1 (now positive, safe).
Safety and physics margins still non-negative. Return adjusted trajectory.

The optimizer finds the tiniest push to the flow's output that makes all three constraint barriers non-negative, preserving the learned motion while enforcing formal safety.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Conflicting constraints. If the task goal and a safety constraint truly conflict (e.g., goal requires entering a no-go zone), the optimizer cannot satisfy both. The method will obey the constraint and sacrifice the goal. This is correct behavior but may reduce task reward.
Numerical jitter from integration. The control layer uses numerical integration to solve the dynamics. Too few integration steps introduce small violations and jerky motion. The paper shows this: with few steps the trajectory wiggles, violating smoothness. Enough steps fix it.
Tight unseen constraints slow down. When test-time constraints become much stricter than training data (e.g., 70% taller constraint reduced to 50%), the optimizer must work harder to fit both safety and the task. Motion quality degrades, though constraint satisfaction holds.
Done right. When constraints are reasonable and independent, SAD-Flower consistently achieves perfect constraint satisfaction while maintaining plan quality. The method scales across different robot morphologies and tasks without retraining.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robot task (e.g., a 2D maze or an arm reaching a target) where you can toggle hard constraints: an obstacle the arm must not hit, a motor torque limit, and a gravity law the arm obeys. Train a flow matcher on good trajectories from that task (unconstrained). At test time, try three methods: (1) raw flow matching (no constraint checking), (2) rejection sampling (generate 100 trajectories, keep only legal ones), (3) SAD-Flower (apply the control layer). Measure: percentage of trajectories that satisfy all three constraints, and average task success rate for the surviving plans.

constraint_satisfaction_rate = (safe_plans + motor_valid_plans + physics_consistent_plans) / total_plans

The demo should make it impossible to miss: SAD-Flower returns a safe, in-limit, executable plan on the first try, while naive flow often breaks rules and rejection sampling wastes huge samples to fix a problem that control theory solves in one step.
