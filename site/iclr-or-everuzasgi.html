Bias Similarity Measurement: Comparing Fairness Across LLMs

Instead of judging each model alone, compare how biases look BETWEEN models in a shared space.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models carry social biases from their training data. Normally we score each model separately: "This model is 72% fair." But that misses the real story: how do biases persist across different model families? This paper treats fairness as relational—measuring how similar two models' biases are—and discovers that instruction tuning often just makes models refuse to answer rather than actually changing their inner bias.

Zero assumption version

Why isolated fairness scores miss the pattern

Today, we audit language models one at a time: run 100,000 prompts on Model A, compute a fairness score, move on to Model B. Each score sits alone. The problem: this hides what is really happening across model families. Does instruction tuning actually fix bias, or just hide it? Are different brands of models converging toward the same bias, or drifting apart? The isolated score tells you nothing.

A beginner would try: add up the demographic groups where the model is unfair, sum them, report a single number. This breaks because it ignores HOW the model is unfair. One model might refuse to answer (safe but unhelpful). Another might answer with a stereotypical bias. A third might answer correctly but inconsistently. They all get different fairness numbers, but you cannot see that the first and third are actually siblings in the same family.

The core move. Stop asking "Is this model fair?" and start asking "How is this model's bias similar to that model's bias?" Put both models in the same measurement space.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure trait by trait

For each pair of models, compute six different fairness signals: raw accuracy, distribution over answers, abstention (refusal) rate, bias direction, flip rate under instruction tuning, and representational similarity (do their inner layers think alike).

Map into a shared space

Convert each signal to a similarity score (ranging from 0 to 1: 0 means completely different, 1 means identical). Now every model pair lives in the same six-dimensional space.

Look for clusters

Compute which model pairs sit close in this space. Models from the same family (e.g., Gemma 2, Gemma 3) cluster. Different families (Gemma vs. LLaMA) drift apart.

Trace instruction-tuning changes

For controlled experiments (base model -> instruction-tuned version of the same base), see which of the six signals changed and which stayed the same. Does tuning re-wire the layers (representational change)? Or does it just add a surface refusal filter?

Audit procurement and regression

Use the similarity map to pick models fairly: which candidate model is most similar to your trusted baseline? Which deviates when new versions arrive (regression testing)?

What the paper changes

What changes: from single scores to relational comparisons

The object is the way we measure fairness: instead of computing one isolated score per model, we measure how similar the bias is BETWEEN pairs of models across six different traits.

The hidden quantity

The bias similarity signature: six traits at once

measured quantity  =  Bias similarity signature (six numbers in a list)

The paper introduces a hidden quantity called a 'bias similarity signature.' For every pair of models, it is a list of six numbers, each between 0 and 1, capturing: (1) accuracy similarity: do both models get the right answer the same way? (2) abstention similarity: do both models refuse to answer at similar rates? (3) bias direction similarity: do they stereotype in the same direction (e.g., both favor the same demographic group)? (4) output distribution similarity: do their full answer distributions look alike? (5) flip rate under tuning: when instruction-tuning modifies one model, does it modify the other the same way? (6) representational similarity: do their internal layers encode prompts in linearly related ways (computed via CKA, a technique for comparing hidden layer structure)?

The paper measures each trait by computing a similarity score (cosine distance for some, unknown-flip rate for others, canonical correlation analysis for representations) and packages all six into one signature list. High values mean the two models handle bias similarly; low values mean they diverge.

Why it works

Why comparing in shared space exposes hidden patterns

Here is the causal chain. When you measure each model in isolation, you get a single number: "72% fair." That number is useless for spotting family patterns because it flattens away the internal machinery. Does the model refuse answers? Lean toward stereotypes? Inconsistent across demographics? You cannot tell from a single score.

Now measure BOTH models on six traits in the same space. Suddenly you can see: Model A and Model B both got a 72 fair score, but Model A reached it by refusing half the questions (abstention high) while Model B answered questions but stereotypically (bias direction high). They look identical from outside, but the internal wiring is completely different.

Next, track the effect of instruction tuning on these signatures. If tuning changes Gemma's abstention rate from 20% to 60% but leaves the internal layer structure untouched (representational similarity stays high), you know tuning just added a refusal filter on top. It did not rewire the bias. For LLaMA, tuning might leave abstention low but rebalance the bias direction (representational similarity also shifts), meaning the tuning went deeper. By comparing WITHIN a family (same base, different tuning), you isolate the instruction-tuning effect.

Finally, look across families. If Gemma models cluster (all high abstention) and LLaMA models cluster (all neutral-seeking) and they stay far apart from each other, you know the architecture or pretraining data created two divergent bias strategies. A procurement team can now pick a model not by isolated score but by relational proximity: which candidate is nearest to your trusted baseline?

Math after the picture

The similarity signature equation

First in words:

For each model pair (M_i, M_j) and dataset X, compute a six-element signature: each element is the similarity between the models on one trait (accuracy, abstention, bias direction, output distribution, flip rate, representational). Similarity is 1 when models agree, 0 when they differ completely.

Then the compact form:

S(M_i, M_j) = [sim_accuracy, sim_abstention, sim_bias, sim_distribution, sim_flip, sim_representation]

Every symbol:

sim_accuracycosine similarity of accuracy vectors across demographic groups (higher = both models succeed the same way)
sim_abstentioncorrelation of refusal rates: do both models say Unknown at similar rates? (higher = both refuse equally)
sim_biascosine similarity of bias direction (e.g., both favor the same demographic group; higher = aligned stereotypes)
sim_distributionJensen-Shannon divergence of output probability distributions across answers (lower divergence = higher similarity)
sim_flipflip rate: for base and instruction-tuned pair, do they change the same way? low = stable, high = tuning shifted behavior differently
sim_representationCKA (canonical correlation analysis) of hidden layer activations: are internal features linearly related? (higher = isomorphic internal structure)
A tiny worked example: comparing Gemma-base to Gemma-instruct
Gemma-base on gender question: 40% refuse, 35% stereotypical, 25% neutral.
Gemma-instruct on gender question: 65% refuse, 20% stereotypical, 15% neutral.
Abstention similarity: correlation of [40,35,25] and [65,20,15] = 0.15 (low: tuning shifted refusal behavior a lot).
Representational similarity (CKA on layer 5): 0.92 (high: internal layer structure barely changed even though outputs shifted dramatically).
Conclusion: instruction tuning added a surface refusal filter but did not rewire the bias at the layer level. Compare this to LLaMA-base -> LLaMA-instruct, which might show lower CKA (layers reorganized) and balanced refusal change (tuning went deeper).

The six-element signature lets you compare models relationally without collapsing everything into a single fairness number. Each element tells you something different about how bias manifests.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Cross-vendor comparisons are observational only. When comparing Gemma (Google) to LLaMA (Meta), their architecture, training data, and tuning pipelines all differ. A low similarity score might be due to any of these, not the tuning strategy. Within-family comparisons (Gemma 2 -> Gemma 3, same base, different instruction tuning) are causally interpretive; cross-vendor are ecosystem mapping only.
Small models often become less fair under instruction tuning. Counterintuitively, tuning a 3B or 4B model can strengthen stereotypical bias (low representational similarity, high flip rate). The paper shows LLaMA 3.2 3B and Gemma 3 4B both went backward. Similarity measurement exposes this cost.
Instruction tuning often enforces abstention, not internal debiasing. In Gemma and many other models, tuning raises the abstention rate dramatically but leaves the internal layer structure nearly unchanged (high representational similarity). This is a surface patch: the model still encodes bias at the layer level, it just refuses to use it. When forced to choose (no abstention option), the bias emerges.
Done right: measure the signature, not the score. Use the six-dimensional signature to audit. Track which dimension changed under tuning (abstention vs representation). Compare within-family to isolate tuning effects. Use cross-family comparisons to map the ecosystem, not to infer causality. This is a relational audit tool, not an absolute fairness meter.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small fairness audit: take two pretrained language models (e.g., GPT-2 small, a fine-tuned variant of the same base). Run 10,000 prompts asking demographic questions (gender, nationality, race) and collect responses. For each model, compute: accuracy by demographic group, refusal rate, and output distribution. Then compute the six similarity metrics between the two models. Plot the six numbers as a bar chart or radar plot. Show that the two models have high similarity on some traits (e.g., abstention) and low on others (e.g., bias direction). Finally, instruction-tune one model and recompute the signature. The changes will show you where tuning modified behavior (which numbers in the signature changed).

Signature_fidelity = average_similarity(trained_model, reference_model) across all six dimensions; higher means the new model stays aligned with the reference.

The demo must make obvious that isolated fairness numbers hide relational patterns. Two models with the same fairness score should have visibly different signatures, revealing how they diverge in the ways they handle bias.
