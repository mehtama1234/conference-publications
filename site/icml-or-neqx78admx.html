EnsembleVLA: Combining Robot Policies via Energy Models

Combine multiple robot-control policies into one stronger policy by treating each as an energy scoring system and summing their scores for each action.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a robot needs to learn a new task, a single trained model can make mistakes. Researchers have trained many different vision-language-action models (models that look at an image, understand the task in English, and output the motor commands to move the robot). The natural idea is to combine several of these models to get a better result, but standard ensemble methods do not work for policies because they produce continuous actions with many possible choices (standard methods average discrete outputs; averaging two probability patterns over infinite actions gives noise, not a useful policy). This paper shows how to combine them using energy-based models: each policy scores each possible action (lower score means better), and the ensemble adds up these scores. When an action gets a low total score from the addition, it means every model independently assigned it a low score, which is far more reliable evidence than any single model's vote because it is harder for all models to be wrong in the same way.

Zero assumption version

Why do single policies fail?

A robot learns to pick up objects or place them by watching human videos and using a language model to convert a task description into motor commands. One such model might be trained on 100,000 images; another on a different dataset of 50,000 images. Each model has learned something useful, but each also has blind spots—actions it has not seen and will guess poorly on.

The naive idea is to average the predicted actions: if model A predicts move right 2 cm and model B predicts move right 1.5 cm, average them to 1.75 cm. But robot policies are not simple number outputs; they produce entire probability distributions over possible next actions (each action is a high-dimensional list of motor commands, one for each joint of the robot arm). Averaging two probability distributions does not give you a sensible combined policy—it gives you a blurry mess.

The method in one sentence. Instead of averaging predictions (which blurs the decision), each policy scores each action (lower score is better), and the ensemble adds all the scores; actions all models give low scores to are chosen, because they pass multiple inspectors.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Reframe each policy

Convert each learned policy into an energy-based model where lower energy means the model recommends that action more strongly.

Step 2: Sum the energies

At any moment, evaluate both policies' energies and add them; the total energy guides which action the ensemble prefers.

Step 3: Learn composition weights

Assign a weight to each policy (e.g., policy 1 counts as 1.2x, policy 2 counts as 0.8x) so one voice does not drown out the other.

Step 4: Add a confidence gate

If the combined policy is uncertain (energies are close), dampen the final action to make only safe small movements.

Step 5: Execute the blended action

Send the motor commands to the robot; the action balances all models and respects when no consensus exists.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper modifies the way we combine policies. Instead of a single model predicting an action, the ensemble asks multiple models to vote via their energy contributions. Learnable weights determine how much each model influences the final action, and a gating mechanism can reduce the robot's motion when the ensemble is uncertain. The object being changed is the policy combination rule: from naive averaging to principled energy summation with learned balancing.

The hidden quantity

Energy: how the model ranks actions

measured quantity  =  Energy score (lower means better)

Energy is an abstract score assigned to each possible action by the model. Lower energy means the model thinks that action is good for this task; higher energy means the model is skeptical. For example, if the task is to pick up a coffee mug, moving the arm toward the mug gets low energy (good idea), while moving away gets high energy (bad idea).

The paper estimates energy implicitly: every learned policy outputs a probability for each action (the diffusion model generates a distribution step by step; the flow model traces a path). The paper then extracts a corresponding energy: if a model assigns high probability to an action, we assign it low energy. By combining multiple models' energies, we find actions that all models agree are safe, because a low total score means every model recommended it.

Plain reading. Think of energy as a badness score: models predict which actions are good (low badness) or risky (high badness). Combining models combines their badness scores to find actions all agree are safe.
Paper reading. The paper formalizes this by stating that diffusion-based and flow-based policies can each be expressed as energy-based models. The ensemble combines them additively: the total score for an action is the sum of each model's score, with learned weights that let one model have more influence than another if needed.
Why it works

Why combining scores makes a stronger policy

Each trained policy has learned different patterns from its training data. Policy 1 has seen grasping strategies from one dataset and learned to grip tightly; Policy 2 saw different camera angles and learned to place objects carefully. Neither has seen everything.

When we combine the energy scores, we ask: which actions do both models agree are safe? An action that both models rank highly (low score) gets a very low combined score and becomes the ensemble's top choice. An action that only one model likes gets pulled down by the other's skepticism—disagreement reduces its standing. An action neither model has seen well (both assign high scores) gets a high combined score and is avoided. This works because it forces consensus: any action needs to pass multiple inspectors to be chosen.

The learned composition weights ensure that one model does not simply drown out the other. A weight close to 1.0 means the model's vote counts normally; a weight above 1.0 means it contributes more (perhaps because it was trained on more data or performed better on a validation task). The confidence gate is a safety layer: if the combined scores show no clear winner (all actions have similar scores), the robot is told to move slowly and carefully, reducing the risk of a wrong move when the ensemble cannot agree.

Math after the picture

The energy combination equation

First in words:

The combined energy for an action is the weighted sum of each policy's energy plus a confidence-gated residual correction.

Then the compact form:

E_ensemble(a) = w1 * E1(a) + w2 * E2(a) + gate(C) * R(a)

Every symbol:

E_ensemble(a)total energy assigned to action a by the ensemble
E1(a)energy for action a according to policy 1
E2(a)energy for action a according to policy 2
w1, w2learned composition weights (how much each policy contributes)
gate(C)a safety multiplier (between 0 and 1) that shrinks if confidence C is low
R(a)a small residual correction learned during ensemble training
aa possible action (a list of motor commands)
A tiny worked example
Suppose action 'grip and lift' has: Energy from Policy 1 = 0.3 (likes it), Energy from Policy 2 = 0.4 (also likes it).
Learned weights: w1 = 1.0, w2 = 0.9 (Policy 1 gets slightly more vote).
Confidence is high (both policies agree), so gate(C) = 1.0.
Residual R = 0.05 (a tiny learned tweak).
E_ensemble = 1.0 * 0.3 + 0.9 * 0.4 + 1.0 * 0.05 = 0.3 + 0.36 + 0.05 = 0.71.
Now consider action 'release and move away': Energy from Policy 1 = 2.0 (dislikes it), Energy from Policy 2 = 2.2 (dislikes it).
E_ensemble = 1.0 * 2.0 + 0.9 * 2.2 + 1.0 * 0.05 = 2.0 + 1.98 + 0.05 = 4.03.
The ensemble strongly prefers the first action (0.71 vs 4.03), because both policies agree. This is how disagreement is handled: if one model liked it and one disliked it, the energy would land in between, and confidence would be low, triggering the gate.

The equation sums up how much each policy dislikes an action, then multiplies by how much we trust each policy, with a safety gate that slows the robot when the ensemble is unsure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incompatible policies. If one policy has learned a completely different convention (e.g., one model interprets the camera as mounted on the robot's head, another assumes it is mounted on the ceiling), combining their scores does not help because their disagreement is not productive. The ensemble must have policies trained on compatible task definitions. When they disagree on compatible tasks, that is valuable; when they disagree because they speak different languages, it is noise.
Over-confident gates. If the gating mechanism is too aggressive and suppresses actions whenever confidence is not perfect, the robot becomes paralyzed and does nothing. The gate must be tuned so that even when the ensemble is moderately uncertain (the models disagree somewhat), the robot still moves, but does so more slowly and carefully than when all models agree strongly.
Imbalanced weights. If one policy is much better trained and its learned weight becomes 10.0 while the other is 0.1, the ensemble collapses to nearly a single policy again (the better model's scores dominate, drowning out the weaker model's votes). The training process must gently steer the weights to stay within a reasonable range (e.g., not more extreme than 2.0 to 0.5) so that both models' scores remain in the same ballpark. Here is why: suppose action A has score 0.5 from policy 1 and score 3.0 from policy 2. With balanced weights (1.0, 1.0), multiply each weight by its score: 1.0 times 0.5 plus 1.0 times 3.0, which is 0.5 plus 3.0, giving total 3.5. With imbalanced weights (10.0, 0.1), multiply: 10.0 times 0.5 plus 0.1 times 3.0, which is 5.0 plus 0.3, giving total 5.3. The first total (3.5) shows a clear disagreement between the models; the second (5.3) is dominated by policy 1's opinion. Balanced weights preserve the signal from both models.
Done right. With policies trained on related tasks, reasonable composition weights, and a well-tuned gate, the ensemble follows this rule: act boldly when all models agree (low total score), act carefully when they partially agree (medium total score), and do not act when they strongly disagree (no clear winner). This handles new situations better than any single model because the robot only commits when multiple trained minds say the same thing.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train two simulated robot policies on slightly different versions of a grasping task (for example, one policy sees light from the left, another from the right). Evaluate each policy alone on a new set of test tasks with varied lighting. Then create the ensemble using the energy-based combination and evaluate it on the same test tasks. The demo should measure the success rate (percentage of tasks completed without dropping the object or colliding with the environment) for each single policy and for the ensemble.

Improvement = (Ensemble_success_rate - max(Policy_A_rate, Policy_B_rate)) / max(Policy_A_rate, Policy_B_rate) * 100%

The demo should make it impossible to miss that the ensemble succeeds on tasks where both individual policies would fail, proving that combining energies extracts complementary strengths.
