Learning with strategic or adversarial elements

Analyzing how learning algorithms perform when facing adversarial actors, noisy conditions, or game-theoretic interactions where agents have competing goals.

What this is

A spam filter learns to block emails that look like spam. Spammers, knowing this, modify their emails to evade detection. The filter learns again, adapting to new spam tactics. This cycle continues: the learner and the adversary are both adapting, trying to outmaneuver each other. In game theory, two chess players study each other's strategies, each assuming the opponent is optimal. In mechanism design, a platform must set rules knowing that users will strategically respond to those rules. This subtheme examines learning in adversarial and strategic settings: how do you learn when the environment or an opponent is actively working against you?

The difficulty is that in adversarial settings, the standard assumptions of learning theory break down. You assume training data reflects the environment you will encounter, but an adversary deliberately sends data designed to fool your model. You cannot simply retrain when the adversary adapts because you must decide quickly. In game-theoretic scenarios, you must reason about what the opponent will do, but the opponent is reasoning about what you will do, leading to circular predictions. The learner also faces a tradeoff: should you commit to a predictable strategy (which an opponent can exploit) or use randomness (which is less efficient but harder to exploit)? Finding stable strategies where no player wants to deviate requires different tools than standard learning.

Researchers study adversarial reliability — training models that maintain good performance even when inputs are slightly perturbed by an attacker, so the model does not fail catastrophically. They analyze reliable algorithms that work even in the worst case (rather than the average case), providing guarantees that an adversary cannot break by exploiting worst-case scenarios. They study no-regret learning — algorithms where cumulative performance degrades gracefully as the environment changes or an adversary attacks, such that the algorithm did not have a much better strategy in hindsight, which guarantees that even faced with an optimal opponent, the algorithm accumulates reward at a reasonable rate over time because no single strategy could do vastly better. They examine game-theoretic learning where multiple agents simultaneously learn strategies, analyzing convergence to equilibria — stable outcomes where no agent can do better by unilaterally changing strategy, which is important because such stability means learning stops and decisions can be relied upon. They also tackle strategic classification, where data points (people or systems) may misrepresent themselves to influence the model's decision. In all cases, the goal is learning algorithms that remain effective against clever, intentional opposition.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme branches into four directions. First, researchers build models that stay reliable even when attacked—training against adversarial examples so the model doesn't fall apart when inputs are slightly changed, because attackers will find and exploit any weakness in a deployed system. Second, they analyze games where multiple agents learn simultaneously and must reach stable agreements where nobody wants to change strategy, because such stable outcomes are the only ones that last in practice. Third, they study learning when training data itself is poisoned or corrupted by an attacker, because real-world data is often unreliable or sabotaged. Fourth, they develop theoretical guarantees for these hard settings, proving what algorithms can and cannot achieve when facing opposition, so practitioners know which approaches have fundamental limits.

Training models to withstand attacks

When a model is deployed, attackers often send inputs slightly different from normal data, trying to trick the model into wrong predictions. The goal is training methods that keep the model accurate even against these tiny, crafted perturbations—so an adversary cannot reliably fool it. This is hard because standard training ignores adversarial cases, and retraining after each attack is too slow.

The "Adversarially Pretrained Transformers" paper shows that if you train a Transformer (a type of neural network that tracks relationships across many inputs) on adversarial examples once, that training carries forward to new tasks without extra cost—the model learns to stay accurate even against adversarial attacks as a general skill. The "Flatness" paper reveals why adversarial training can fail: the loss surface must curve sharply away from the data manifold to provide true protection; if the curve is flat (smooth) in a direction, the model can make confident wrong predictions there. The "Feature Compression" paper identifies the mechanism: neural networks squeeze information into narrow channels during training, which makes them vulnerable—when attackers perturb inputs in the compressed space, they cause big errors because there is no backup information.

Multi-agent learning and equilibrium strategies

In markets, auction systems, and recommendation platforms, many agents interact at once—each with different goals and each adapting their strategy based on what others do. The challenge is understanding when these learning dynamics settle into stable outcomes where no single agent wants to change their strategy, because stability is required for decisions to be relied upon.

The "Anytime Detection" paper monitors ongoing games to catch when agents deviate from the agreed strategy in real time, giving early warning before the system breaks, so corrective action can happen quickly. The "Bertrand Paradox" paper studies pricing: when learners use simple no-regret algorithms (which guarantee cumulative performance), they can drive prices high because they focus only on not doing worse than hindsight; but stronger algorithms that prevent even swapping strategies push them toward competition and low prices because they eliminate more escape routes. The "Computing Equilibrium" paper proves that finding these stable equilibria is computationally hard—not every algorithm can find them quickly—which means some game designs require exponential time to reach stability. The "Learning a Game by Paying" paper takes a different angle: if a designer observes agents' behavior while paying them, the designer can learn what outcomes the agents prefer and then steer the system toward desired stable points, which is useful when direct control is not possible.

Learning under poisoned and corrupted data

Sometimes the training data itself is attacked—poisoned by an adversary who modifies examples or injects false ones. Other times, natural corruption (noise, errors in labeling, environmental drift) damages the data. The problem is learning algorithms that still converge to good solutions even when a fraction of training data is unreliable or deliberately sabotaged.

The "Bandit Learning in Matching Markets" paper tackles adversarial corruptions in a matching problem (like job markets where workers and employers must pair up)—it gives algorithms with guarantees on stable matching quality even when corruption is present. The "Continual Learning Against Data Poisoning" paper proves a hard truth: if an attacker poisons a fixed fraction of each task's data, no defense can work across all tasks—but it identifies specific scenarios where defense is possible and provides verified guarantees for those cases. The "Linear Dueling Bandits" paper studies preference learning (which option do users prefer?) under adversarial delays and corruptions—it achieves regret that gracefully degrades with corruption amount, meaning cumulative loss grows only as much as the corruption warrants.

Foundational theory and geometric structure

To understand learning in adversarial settings deeply, researchers must sometimes prove what is theoretically possible and impossible. This requires careful analysis of the mathematical structure of the problem—revealing hidden constraints or properties that determine what any algorithm can achieve.

The "Value Function" paper studies decision-making under partial observability (you do not see the full state of the world). It reveals that uncertainty induces fundamentally nonlinear mathematical structure—the set of possible value functions forms a richer shape with isolated peaks depending on your beliefs at the start. This structure matters because it constrains what learning algorithms can do. The "Transformers as Associative Memory" paper analyzes how attention-based models generalize: treating them as statistical memory systems, it derives optimal convergence rates and proves these models achieve those rates, providing theoretical foundation for why attention-based models work well even in noisy, long-context settings where adversarial scenarios are common.
