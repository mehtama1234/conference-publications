Dynamic Structural Distillation for Time Series

Instead of copying the teacher's exact predictions, align the learned patterns at multiple levels to build better lightweight forecasters.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting (predicting stock prices or weather weeks ahead) needs huge models. A lightweight student model trained to copy a big teacher's predictions often fails because the teacher itself is uncertain. This paper says: stop copying predictions. Instead, align the learned patterns—the shapes and structures the teacher discovered—at three different levels of abstraction.

Zero assumption version

The distillation trap: copying bad guesses

A big transformer model (a neural network that reads sequences) is great at forecasting time series—it sees patterns across long histories. But it is slow and eats memory. A simple linear model is fast and tiny, but it misses complex patterns.

The natural fix: train the small model to copy the big model's predictions. This is called knowledge distillation. But it has a fatal flaw: the big model's predictions are uncertain and sometimes wrong. When the student blindly learns to mimic those wrong guesses, it absorbs the teacher's mistakes, not just its knowledge. This is negative transfer—the student ends up worse than if it had just learned from the raw data.

The core problem. Matching point predictions spreads the teacher's errors and ignores the mismatch between how the teacher and student learn differently.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Expand the student's hidden space

Student projects features into a higher-dimensional space so it can hold more complex learned patterns.

Step 2: Measure topological alignment

Check if the patterns the student learned preserve the same nearest-neighbor relationships as the teacher.

Step 3: Measure geometric alignment

Check if the distances between patterns in the student's learned space match the teacher's distances.

Step 4: Learn regime confidence

The student learns which data regimes (seasons, noise levels, price ranges) the teacher handles confidently.

Step 5: Gate copying intelligently

Only copy the teacher's signal when the model is confident the teacher knows this regime well.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of forcing the student to match the teacher's exact output predictions, the paper aligns the learned feature patterns (the internal representations) at multiple abstract levels.

the object it moves  =  Alignment target (patterns, not predictions)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: pattern reliability

measured quantity  =  Regime-aware confidence score

The student learns a confidence score for each regime—a data regime is a distinct operating condition, like winter weather or high-volatility stock markets. A high score means the teacher is trustworthy on this kind of data; a low score means the teacher often gets it wrong here.

The paper estimates this by training a confidence gate that observes how often the teacher's predictions were right in each regime during training. At inference (when the student makes new forecasts), the gate checks which regime the incoming data belongs to, and only lets the student copy the teacher's signal if the score is high.

Why it works

Why it works: three layers of alignment prevent error copying

The core insight is that a teacher's uncertain predictions can poison a student, but the teacher's learned patterns (the internal structures it discovered) survive even when predictions are wrong. So instead of copying outputs, copy the patterns.

First, the student learns in an expanded high-dimensional hidden space (a learned layer inside the model that holds intermediate patterns). This extra room lets the student represent the complex temporal patterns the teacher found, without trying to squeeze them into a tiny space. More dimensions in that hidden layer means fewer forced errors.

Second, topological alignment checks if the student's learned nearest neighbors match the teacher's. This preserves the teacher's discovered relationships—if two time sequences are similar to the teacher, they should stay similar in the student. Geometric alignment (via Optimal Transport, a mathematical technique for matching distributions) goes deeper: it checks if the distances between all learned patterns match. Two different structural alignments ensure the student builds the same shape of knowledge.

Third, the confidence gate blocks misguidance. The model learns which regimes it can trust the teacher on. In a regime where the teacher was wrong during training, the student ignores the teacher's signal and relies on its own learning. This stops negative transfer before it starts.

Math after the picture

The alignment penalty balances three terms

First in words:

Total loss equals the student's forecasting error, plus a penalty for misaligned topology, plus a penalty for misaligned geometry, plus a regime-aware adaptive weight that scales these based on confidence.

Then the compact form:

loss_total = loss_forecast + lambda_topo * D_topology(S, T) + lambda_geo * D_geometry(S, T) + lambda_regime * w_regime * D_distill

Every symbol:

loss_forecasthow wrong the student's prediction was
D_topologyhow much the nearest neighbors diverge between student and teacher
D_geometryhow much the distances between patterns diverge (Optimal Transport distance)
D_distillthe mismatch in the teacher's signal (standard distillation loss)
lambda_topoweight controlling how strongly to enforce topological alignment
lambda_geoweight controlling how strongly to enforce geometric alignment
lambda_regimebase weight for distillation
w_regimeadaptive gate (close to 0 for untrusted regimes, close to 1 for trusted regimes)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the student predicts a stock price tomorrow. The forecast error is 5 (units: dollars lost).
The student's learned patterns are topologically misaligned from the teacher's by 12 (many neighbors swapped). Penalty: lambda_topo=0.5 × 12 = 6.
Geometric distance mismatch: 8 (Optimal Transport sees 8 units of pattern drift). Penalty: lambda_geo=0.3 × 8 = 2.4.
The teacher's predicted signal on this data point: mismatch = 3. But the regime confidence w_regime=0.2 (untrusted). Penalty: lambda_regime=1.0 × 0.2 × 3 = 0.6.
Total loss for this example: 5 + 6 + 2.4 + 0.6 = 14. The topological alignment is the biggest cost, pulling the student's patterns into the teacher's shape.

The regime weight w_regime acts as a valve: trusted regimes let the distillation penalty through, untrusted regimes block it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Not enough training data to learn regimes reliably. If the student never sees some regimes during training, the confidence gate has no signal. The gate then defaults to low confidence, and the student stops copying the teacher in new regimes where it could have learned.
Mismatched architectures can break alignment. If the teacher is a transformer and the student is linear, their learned patterns may live in fundamentally different spaces. Topological and geometric alignment try to bridge this, but some mismatches are too large. The paper shows it works across diverse architectures, but very distant ones may still fail.
Teacher uncertainty not captured by regimes. If the teacher is wrong for reasons that do not align with regimes (e.g. the teacher is bad at a specific pattern that appears across all regimes), the regime gate will not catch it. The method assumes errors cluster by regime, which is not always true.
Done right. Regimes are learned from data (not hand-coded). Topological and geometric alignment are computed from the actual learned patterns. The gate is trained end-to-end with the student. The method is general: the paper tests it on five benchmarks with different teacher and student architectures.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small transformer teacher and a linear student on a synthetic time series dataset with clear regimes (e.g. seasonal + trend + noise, where one regime is noisier than others). Train the naive copy-the-predictions baseline and the Dynamic Structural Distillation version side by side. Then test on held-out data that includes a regime the teacher has not seen before (a new noise level or seasonal pattern). Measure forecast accuracy on both the seen and unseen regimes.

robustness = accuracy_on_new_regime / accuracy_on_training_regime. Naive approach: should drop sharply (e.g. 0.6). DSD: should stay close to 1.0.

The demo makes impossible to miss: regime-aware alignment lets the student forecast well on new conditions the teacher never saw, while naive distillation collapses.
