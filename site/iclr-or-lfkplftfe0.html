VLM-Guided Motion Policy via Relative Movement Dynamics

Use a language-aware image reader to automatically write the reward rules for teaching a robot natural hand-object motions, without manual tuning.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot to pick up a cup or open a door requires either expensive human motion capture videos or hours spent hand-writing reward rules to guide the learning. This paper eliminates the hand-writing step by letting a Vision-Language Model (a system that understands images and text together) automatically describe what the hand and object should do at each step. The robot then uses those descriptions to learn natural, human-like motions on its own.

Zero assumption version

Teaching robots natural motions is slow and manual

Right now, to teach a robot to interact with objects—picking things up, pushing, opening doors—engineers either record humans doing it (expensive and limited to a few recorded poses) or hand-write reward rules for each task. A reward rule is a checklist: if the hand moves here and the object moves there, give the robot a point; if not, penalize it. This is tedious and brittle: change the object shape slightly or the task scenario, and the hand-written rules break.

A beginner's fix: "Just tell the robot what to do in plain English." The problem: text alone has no idea that hands have fingers with joints, or that cups have rims and bases that move differently. The instruction sits floating in air, disconnected from the actual shapes and structure of bodies and objects.

The paper's move: Use a vision-language model to automatically design both goal positions and reward rules by describing the spatial relationships between human parts and object parts at each step.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

VLM reads the scene

Take an image and a high-level instruction; the language model describes what hand and object should do.

Extract spatial step-by-step plan

VLM breaks the interaction into sub-steps: reach, grasp, lift, move, place.

Build RMD representation

For each step, measure relative positions and velocities between human body parts and object parts.

Encode goals and rewards

Use RMD measurements to automatically generate reward rules: reward the robot when hand-to-object distances match the VLM's description.

Train physics-based policy

Reinforcement learning uses these auto-generated rewards to teach the robot natural, realistic motions without manual tuning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the reward function—the rulebook that tells the robot whether its motion is good or bad. Instead of an engineer writing these rules by hand for each task, the VLM writes them automatically based on its understanding of the scene and the instruction.

The hidden quantity

Relative Movement Dynamics: distances that matter

measured quantity  =  Relative position and velocity lists between body parts and object parts

RMD is a named measurement that captures, for each moment during an interaction, where each human body part sits relative to each object part and how fast that distance is changing. Instead of absolute coordinates (hand at pixel 512, 256), RMD measures relative quantities: hand is 15 centimeters above cup rim, hand velocity is 2 centimeters per second toward the rim. These relative quantities naturally describe what hands and objects are doing together.

The paper estimates RMD from the VLM's planned step descriptions and the scene image. It computes position relative to object (p_hand minus p_object) and velocity relative to object (v_hand minus v_object) in the robot's body-centric coordinate frame. These measurements feed into the reward function: the robot earns points when its actual position and velocity match the VLM's description of what should happen at that step.

Plain reading. RMD is a table of measurements: each row is a pair (hand part, object part), each column is time or a coordinate axis, and each cell holds the distance or velocity between them. Brighter cells mean the VLM is trying to control that measurement.
Paper reading. RMD is a bipartite graph representation. Human body parts and object parts are nodes. Edges connect them with relative position (p_obj minus p_human), relative velocity (v_obj minus v_human), and an edge weight encoding interaction type (approach, contact, push, separate). Features are combined into a list of numbers per edge per timestep.
Why it works

Why it works: VLM ground truth replaces manual rules

The mechanism is straightforward: vision-language models have learned (from training on massive image and text pairs) what natural human-object interactions look like and how they unfold step by step. When you show a VLM an image of a hand and a cup and ask 'what should the hand do next?', it outputs a geometrically plausible description based on its learned understanding of physics and human motion. This description, phrased as relative positions and velocities, becomes the reward signal. The reinforcement learning algorithm then trains the robot policy to match those descriptions.

Without RMD, the VLM's output would be abstract (e.g., 'bring hand closer') and hard to turn into a learnable reward. RMD bridges the gap: it translates the VLM's spatial intuitions into concrete, measurable body-part-to-object-part distances. The policy learns to move its joints so that these distances match what the VLM said should happen. Because RMD captures the detailed structure of the interaction—which fingers contact which part of the object, how the wrist rotates relative to the handle—the learned motions end up natural and physically sound, not jerky or unphysical.

Math after the picture

The reward equation

First in words:

Task reward equals VLM-guided relative movement reward plus hand position reward plus object position reward, each weighted by a strength tuning factor and bounded between zero and one.

Then the compact form:

r_G(s_t, g_t, s_{t+1}) = lambda_RMD * r_RMD + lambda_h * r_h_d + lambda_o * r_o_d

Every symbol:

r_Gtotal task reward at timestep t
s_tcurrent state (positions and velocities of hand and object)
g_tcurrent goal state from VLM plan
s_{t+1}next state
r_RMDreward for matching VLM-planned body-part-to-object-part distances
r_h_dreward for hand reaching its planned position
r_o_dreward for object reaching its planned position
lambda_RMD, lambda_h, lambda_ostrength weights; balance the three reward terms
A tiny worked example
Step 1: VLM says hand should be 5 cm from cup rim. Current distance is 8 cm. Difference: 3 cm.
RMD reward: 1.0 minus (3 / max_distance) = 1.0 - 0.3 = 0.7
Hand target was x=20cm, current x=18cm. Hand position reward: 0.9
Cup target was y=50cm, current y=51cm. Object position reward: 0.8
Total: r_G = 0.8 * 0.7 + 0.1 * 0.9 + 0.1 * 0.8 = 0.56 + 0.09 + 0.08 = 0.73
Policy gets partial credit for partially meeting the plan; full reward (0.9+) triggers step transition.

The reward is capped between zero (no progress) and one (perfect step), so the policy gets nudged toward staying aligned with the VLM's plan.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

VLM misunderstands the scene. If the vision-language model misreads the image (e.g., confuses a cup handle for a cup rim), it generates a plan that contradicts physics. The robot then tries to reach an impossible configuration. The paper does not fully address this; it relies on the VLM being accurate.
Mismatch between VLM's high-level plan and low-level physics. The VLM might generate a geometrically correct plan (hand 5cm from rim) that is physically infeasible given the robot's arm length or joint limits. The policy struggles and either gets stuck or fails to reach the reward threshold, blocking progress to the next step.
Contact forces not captured by position alone. RMD measures positions and velocities, not forces. For tasks requiring precise grip strength (e.g., holding an egg without crushing it), position-based rewards are insufficient. The robot may learn to move correctly but apply the wrong force.
Done right. When the VLM plan is physically feasible and the RMD measurements capture the task semantics (relative distances between functional parts—rim, handle, base—not just arbitrary points), the method learns natural motions that work reliably across object instances and task variants.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo should train a robot policy on two different object shapes (e.g., a cylinder and a rectangular box) using the same VLM-guided RMD reward, then test generalization to a third unseen object shape and size variant. Measure whether the learned policy completes the interaction (e.g., lifts or pushes the object successfully) without re-training on the new object.

Success rate = (number of generalization trials where object is moved correctly) / (total trials on unseen object)

The demo succeeds if VLM-guided RMD rewards enable the policy to reach 75%+ success on new object shapes, proving that encoding relative body-part-to-object-part distances captures what the task actually requires. This works better than hand-written rules because relative distances stay true regardless of object size and shape—a hand-to-rim distance works whether the cup is tall or short, ceramic or glass.
