ForceForget: Reinforcement Concept Removal for Enhancing Safety in Text-to-Image Models

A text-to-image model can selectively erase unsafe concepts while keeping the ability to understand safe words in the same prompts, using reinforcement learning to balance what to remove.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Today's text-to-image models can generate unsafe images when prompted with harmful words. One approach is to erase those dangerous concepts entirely, but old methods tend to erase too much — they accidentally destroy the model's ability to understand safe meanings hidden in the same unsafe prompts. This paper finds the middle ground using reinforcement learning to optimize exactly how much erasure is right.

Zero assumption version

The erasure-vs-utility trap

Text-to-image models store knowledge in their weights — the numbers that make them work. When a model learns from internet images and captions, it picks up concepts for things you want (a sunset, a happy face) alongside concepts you do not (violence, explicit content). The weights get tangled together: the information about beautiful sunsets and the information about unsafe scenes both live in overlapping network paths.

A naive approach: surgically remove the unsafe concepts by zeroing or changing the relevant weights. The problem is that a weight can contribute to multiple concepts at once. Erasing the unsafe one often accidentally silences safe concepts that lived in the same region of the network. If you try to erase a dangerous concept that appears in a harmful prompt with words like 'red' or 'person', you risk breaking the model's ability to draw red things or people altogether.

In one sentence. The paper uses reinforcement learning to find which weights to adjust so that harmful concepts disappear but safe interpretations stay intact.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Define the tradeoff

Set a reward for erasing unsafe concepts minus a penalty for damaging safe ones.

2. Identify where to adjust

Safe Adapter finds which embedding weights contribute to the unsafe concepts without touching word meanings.

3. Nudge weights toward the reward

Reinforcement learning gradually changes those weights to maximize the reward over many trials.

4. Test erasure vs. safety

For each adjustment, measure how much unsafe content dies and how much safe image quality survives.

5. Lock in the best adjustment

Stop when the reward is high, meaning unsafe concepts are gone and safe meanings still work.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adjusts which weights in the image-generation pathway get modified during the safety process. Instead of erasing weights across the whole network, it targets only the text-embedding projections in the cross-attention layers — the parts that connect language understanding to image generation — and only the parts that encode the unsafe concepts, leaving other parts of the embedding untouched.

the object it moves  =  text-embedding projections in cross-attention
The hidden quantity

The concept-erasure quality

measured quantity  =  concept erasure reward

The hidden quantity is a single score that says how well the safety method is working. It combines two measurements: (1) how completely the unsafe concept is gone from the model's outputs, and (2) how much safe image quality and semantic understanding have been preserved. On its own, erasing might be total but useless (the model makes no images at all). Preserving utility might be complete but unsafe (the model generates harmful content). The reward is a balance: erase unsafe, keep safe.

The paper estimates this by running the model on test prompts (with unsafe words, with safe words, with mixed prompts) and measuring the generated images against safety classifiers and human evaluation. The reinforcement learning algorithm uses this reward signal to decide whether each adjustment to the weights is good or bad.

Why it works

Why selective weight adjustment works

The mechanism hinges on a insight about how transformers (the architecture behind image models) route information through text embeddings. An embedding is a list of numbers representing a word. When a text-to-image model generates an image, it reads your prompt word by word and uses the embeddings to guide the image generation. Each embedding feeds into cross-attention layers — these are the parts that decide which regions of the generated image should attend to which words from your prompt.

The unsafe concept and safe concepts share the embedding space, but not every part of every embedding is equally important. The Safe Adapter learns to project — to pick out and modify — only the components of the embedding that are strongly tied to generating unsafe content. By adjusting those specific components via reinforcement learning, the method erases the unsafe signal while leaving the rest of the embedding intact. This is like removing a single thread from a fabric that holds a pattern you want to keep: the fabric stays, the unwanted pattern is gone.

Reinforcement learning steers these adjustments by trial and error. Each version of the adjusted embeddings is tested: Does it still generate unsafe images? Does it still make good safe images? The reward signal is the judge. Weights that move the reward higher stay; adjustments that harm utility are discouraged. Over many iterations, the algorithm converges to embeddings that have erased the unsafe concept without collateral damage to safe meanings.

The reinforcement learning objective

First in words:

Total reward = (measure of unsafe erasure) minus (penalty for safe-concept loss) plus (influence of adjusted weights)

Then the compact form:

R = E_unsafe[1 - P(unsafe|e_adj)] - 0.3 * E_safe[|f(x,e_adj) - f(x,e_orig)|] + lambda * sum_i ||e_adj_i - e_orig_i||^2

Every symbol:

Rthe total reward (higher is better)
E_unsafeexpectation over test prompts with unsafe words
P(unsafe|e_adj)probability that adjusted embeddings still generate unsafe content
e_adjthe adjusted embedding (what Safe Adapter produces)
e_origthe original embedding (before adjustment)
E_safeexpectation over test prompts with safe words
f(x,e)quality metric for generated image x given embedding e
lambdahow much to penalize large changes to embeddings
A tiny worked example
Suppose P(unsafe|adjusted_embedding) drops from 0.8 to 0.1: erasure gain = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9 (good).
Suppose safe image quality drops from 0.85 to 0.78 (difference = 0.07): safe penalty = 0.3 * 0.07 = 0.021 (modest cost).
Suppose embedding change size is 0.05: regularization = 0.01 * 0.05^2 = 0.000025 (negligible).
Total R = 0.9 - 0.021 - 0.000025 ≈ 0.879 (high reward, method is winning).

The reward grows when unsafe content vanishes and shrinks when safe images are damaged, guiding the algorithm to the right balance.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Insufficient erasure from weak adaptation. If the Safe Adapter does not adjust enough of the embedding space, the unsafe concept will leak through. If embeddings are only partially modified, the model still generates unsafe content because the unadjusted components carry the signal. This is why reinforcement learning is necessary: it keeps nudging until erasure is complete.
Overfitting to the reward during training. The RL algorithm might learn to game the reward function instead of truly erasing concepts. For example, it could make all images slightly blurry to lower the unsafe metric without actually removing the concept. This is why the paper includes human evaluation and adversarial testing (probing with varied unsafe prompts to see if the concept can still slip through).
Cross-domain gaps in image-to-image scenarios. The method is trained on text-to-image but then applied to image-to-image (where you start with a picture, not text). The unsafe concept may be encoded differently in image space than in text space, because the input representation is different. When the Safe Adapter's learned modifications (designed for text embeddings) are applied to image embeddings, they still suppress some unsafe concept leakage because the underlying model weights are shared, but not as precisely. The paper tests this and shows ForceForget still outperforms coarse erasure in image-to-image because even partial suppression is better than careless erasure, but some unsafe content leaks through because the method was not tuned for image inputs.
Done right. When the Safe Adapter targets the right embedding components, when the reward correctly measures both erasure and utility, and when reinforcement learning runs long enough to converge, the method achieves the goal: no unsafe content and full preservation of safe semantics.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo that loads a pre-trained text-to-image model and a set of test prompts split into three groups: (1) prompts with only unsafe words (e.g., explicit or violent concepts), (2) prompts with only safe words (e.g., animals, nature scenes, colors), and (3) mixed prompts with unsafe words alongside safe ones (e.g., 'explicit image of a red ball'). Apply the old coarse-erasure baseline and the ForceForget method to both versions of the model. For each generated image, run a safety classifier to measure unsafe content and a quality metric to measure how well safe concepts appear. The demo should visually show side-by-side generated images (baseline vs. ForceForget) on all three prompt types. On group 1, unsafe content should drop dramatically in ForceForget. On group 2, image quality should stay high in both. On group 3 — the hard case — ForceForget should keep the red ball sharp and recognizable while erasing the explicit concept.

Safety_improvement = (unsafe_baseline - unsafe_forceforget) / unsafe_baseline. Utility_preservation = safe_quality_forceforget / safe_quality_baseline. Goal: Safety_improvement > 80%, Utility_preservation > 95%.

The demo is won when you can show a mixed prompt (unsafe + safe word) generating an image where the safe concept is fully preserved but the unsafe concept is gone — proof that the method erases with precision, not with a sledgehammer.
