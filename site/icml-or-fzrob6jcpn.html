VideoLoom: Joint Spatial-Temporal Video Understanding

Train a video model on a dataset of precise character locations and timing labels so it learns to reason about what happens where and when at the same time.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Video LLMs (models trained to answer questions about video) usually stumble when you ask them to find something in both space and time together. Most approaches excel at tracking objects moving through the scene or understanding a sequence of events, but not both at once. VideoLoom fixes this by training on a carefully labeled dataset where every caption pins down exactly where in the frame and when in the video each character appears.

Zero assumption version

Video models split the work and lose the connection

When you watch a video, you naturally see where things happen and when they happen as one picture. Your brain says: that person entered from the left at the 3-second mark and walked to the right by 5 seconds.

Video models today usually treat these two questions separately. One part of the model asks: what is moving across the frame (space)? Another part asks: what changes from frame to frame (time)? When you ask the finished model to find a person in a specific spot during a specific moment, it stumbles because the parts never practiced thinking jointly.

A beginner might try to just train on a bigger video dataset and hope the model learns both skills. That fails because videos in the wild do not come with precise location and time labels. The model has no clear signal that says: this caption describes something that happens here and there, not everywhere or just in one frame.

In one sentence. Teach a video model to find characters by training on a dataset where every caption says exactly where and when in the video that character appears.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Curate a grounded dataset

Label videos with captions that name exact frame ranges and pixel regions where characters act.

Train on spatial and temporal anchors

Show the model a caption like "Alice walks right, frames 12–24, pixels 100–200 horizontally" so both location markers and timing markers (the individual pieces of the caption) are tied to the same event.

Learn joint localization

The model learns that locations and timings are not separate—a character's presence ties location to time.

Answer composite questions

When asked "where is Alice at 15 seconds," the model reasons across both dimensions at once.

Benchmark on compositional queries

Test on questions that require both spatial and temporal understanding, e.g. "which character moves fastest from left to right after 10 seconds."

What the paper changes

The single object the paper changes

The object is the training dataset's caption structure. Instead of loose descriptions like "a character walks across the room," each caption now anchors to concrete time intervals and pixel regions: "Frame 12–24, bounding box (x, y, w, h): person walks right."

the object it moves  =  caption annotation precision
The hidden quantity

Joint localization accuracy: a named hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Joint spatial-temporal alignment score

The paper measures a quantity that did not exist before: how well the model pinpoints both where and when in the same answer. This is not spatial accuracy alone (did you find the right pixel?) and not temporal accuracy alone (did you find the right frame?), but the fraction of queries where the model names a location AND a time interval that both match the ground truth.

The paper estimates this by testing on queries designed to require joint reasoning, like "At what frames does Alice sit down?" and "In frame 5, where is Bob?" The metric reports whether the model gives a correct location for the correct time, with no partial credit for getting one right and one wrong.

Why it works

Why it works: grounding teaches binding

The mechanism is surprisingly simple: the model learns only what the data teaches it. In the old regime, a caption like "someone talks" has no timing or location label, so the model has no signal that ties the word "talks" to a specific moment or position—because the data never says "this word goes with this place and time." It treats space and time as independent questions.

When you show the model a caption grounded in both dimensions—"Frame 12–24, box (100, 50, 250, 150): Alice talks"—the model learns that this action happens in a bounded region during a bounded interval. Every word in the caption now has a spatial and temporal anchor. The model gradually internalizes that to answer any question about this action, both dimensions must be checked together. Spatial and temporal information become linked in the model's learned representation.

This changes the model's internal weights (the numbers that encode knowledge). When queried on a new joint question, the weights activate both spatial reasoning and temporal reasoning in parallel, and the model merges the two streams before producing an answer. The binding happens in training, not inference—data with clear joint labels simply shapes the model differently.

Math after the picture

The loss function for joint grounding

First in words:

For each query, the penalty is the sum of spatial mismatch and temporal mismatch. Spatial mismatch is the distance from the predicted box to the true box. Temporal mismatch is the distance from the predicted frame range to the true frame range. The model adjusts its weights to shrink both mismatches at once.

Then the compact form:

Loss = (1 - IoU_spatial) + (1 - IoU_temporal)

Every symbol:

Losstotal penalty the model tries to minimize
IoU_spatialIntersection over Union: how much the predicted box overlaps the true box, divided by how much space they cover together
IoU_temporalIntersection over Union for time: how much the predicted frame range overlaps the true range, divided by their combined span
A tiny worked example
True location: bounding box (100, 50, 250, 150), frames 12–24.
Model predicts: box (110, 55, 240, 145), frames 13–23.
Spatial overlap: pixels in both boxes ≈ 12,000. Total area covered ≈ 20,000. IoU_spatial = 12,000 / 20,000 = 0.6.
Temporal overlap: 11 frames in both ranges. Total range ≈ 14 frames. IoU_temporal = 11 / 14 ≈ 0.79.
Loss = (1 - 0.6) + (1 - 0.79) = 0.4 + 0.21 = 0.61. Model nudges weights to raise both overlaps, lowering the loss.

The model lowers spatial mismatch and temporal mismatch together during training because both contribute equally to the penalty. If one dimension were weighted more heavily, the model would ignore the lighter dimension in favor of the heavier one.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Annotation noise breaks the binding. If captions mislabel where or when (e.g., box is off by 50 pixels or timestamp is wrong by 5 frames), the model learns the wrong joint relationship. The pairing of space and time becomes noisy.
Characters that move fast or go off-screen confuse the model. If a character rapidly changes position, or leaves and re-enters the frame, the model must distinguish separate time periods with the same location. Ambiguous motion patterns weaken the joint signal.
Long videos dilute the signal. In very long videos, a single bounding box might not cover all instances of a character's action. The joint label becomes approximate, and the model learns a softer binding.
Done right: precise curation. Carefully curate the dataset so each caption's spatial and temporal bounds are tight and unambiguous. Use human review or multi-annotator agreement to catch errors. The tighter the pairing, the stronger the joint learning—because a vague label teaches the model that space and time are loosely related, whereas a precise label teaches tight binding.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple benchmark: take a short video (5–10 seconds) with a single actor. Write 10 queries that require joint reasoning, e.g. 'Where is the actor at frame 5?' and 'When does the actor wave?' and 'During frames 3–7, which hand does the actor raise first?' Train a Video LLM on the script with loose captions (no spatial or temporal grounding), then retrain on captions with precise frame ranges and bounding boxes. Measure how often each version answers the composite queries correctly.

Joint accuracy = (fraction of queries where predicted box AND predicted frame range both match ground truth) × 100

The demo must show a sharp accuracy gain on composite queries when the model trains on jointly grounded captions, even though it stays roughly level on single-dimension queries. That is VideoLoom's proof.
