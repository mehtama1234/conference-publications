EduMirror: Modeling Educational Social Dynamics with Value-driven Multi-agent Simulation

Simulate school social interactions with psychologically grounded agents whose values shape behavior, then measure both visible actions and internal states to study interventions that would be hard or unethical to test directly.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Educational researchers want to understand bullying, cooperation, peer influence, and classroom climate, but real-world experiments are often ethically constrained and observational data rarely supports strong causal claims. EduMirror offers a simulation environment in which many agents interact under configurable educational settings. Each agent is grounded in explicit psychological needs and social values, and the simulator measures both outward behavior and internal state. That makes the environment useful for controlled what-if studies of school dynamics.

Zero assumption version

Why educational social dynamics are hard to study

School social behavior unfolds through many interacting factors: peer groups, status, values, unmet needs, classroom norms, and institutional rules. Researchers can observe these processes, but observation alone is weak for causal claims because many influences move together. Controlled experiments would help, but many interventions around bullying or exclusion are ethically unacceptable to run on real students because researchers cannot deliberately impose harmful social conditions on children.

A simulator could help, but only if the agents inside it behave in psychologically grounded ways because the simulator must connect internal motives to later behavior. If agent behavior is arbitrary or shallow, the simulation may produce vivid stories without scientific value. The challenge is to build agents whose actions and internal drives can be measured and manipulated in a structured way.

The paper's move. Ground each simulated agent in explicit needs and social values, then track both observable behavior and internal state so interventions can be studied with clearer causal control.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the level of grounding in educational multi-agent simulation. Instead of agents that only produce outward dialogue or actions, it adds explicit internal drives and a paired measurement protocol for visible and hidden social dynamics.

the object it moves  =  Psychological grounding and dual-track measurement
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: internal social state

measured quantity  =  Unobserved psychological condition of each agent during interaction

In real schools, researchers can observe teasing, helping, cooperation, silence, or withdrawal, but they often cannot directly observe internal conditions such as threat, belonging, frustration, or perceived fairness. Those internal conditions matter because they shape future behavior. EduMirror treats them as a first-class object of study.

The simulator estimates internal state from the agent design itself. Because each agent is built with explicit needs and values, the system can track how an interaction changes those hidden variables across time. This is useful because a policy may reduce visible conflict while increasing internal distress, or it may improve belonging before it changes outward behavior.

Why it works

Why value-driven agents matter

Social dynamics are not random exchanges of text. They are shaped by internal motives and constraints. A student who strongly values belonging may avoid open conflict even when treated unfairly. A student under high status pressure may join exclusionary behavior to preserve group position. If those drivers are absent, a simulator cannot directly study how interventions change group trajectories.

Value-driven agents help because they create structured links between inner state and outward action. When an intervention changes classroom norms, support access, or peer incentives, those changes alter internal needs and values, which in turn alter behavior across later rounds. The simulator becomes more scientifically useful because it can connect cause, internal response, and observed action in one controlled environment.

The paired measurement protocol is equally important. Researchers need to know not only whether bullying incidents fell, but why they fell and what side effects appeared internally. By tracking both behavior and hidden state, EduMirror can compare interventions more carefully because it distinguishes surface calm from genuine improvement in social conditions.

Math after the picture

Behavior from values and state

First in words:

An agent's action tendency depends on its current internal state and its value profile. Interventions matter because they change those quantities over time, which then shifts future behavior probabilities.

Then the compact form:

action_score(a) = sum_j w_j * state_j(a)

Every symbol:

action_score(a)preference score for one candidate action by agent a
state_j(a)current level of internal factor j for agent a, such as belonging or threat
w_jimportance of factor j in the agent's value profile
A tiny worked example
A student is choosing between support-a-peer and stay-silent.
Suppose belonging state = 0.8 and fairness state = 0.7.
If the agent values fairness strongly, the support action may score 0.6*0.8 + 0.7*0.7 = 0.97.
If the same agent also feels strong threat, a silence action may gain score from self-protection terms.
Behavior changes when interventions shift these internal states, so the simulator can study why the visible outcome moved.

The paper's full agent design is richer, but the central idea is that internal state and value weights jointly shape action choice across the simulation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Psychological model is oversimplified. If the chosen needs and values leave out an important driver, the simulation may miss key dynamics because the internal state space is too coarse.
Evaluation focuses only on realism of dialogue. A simulation can sound plausible while still misrepresenting social causality because fluent surface behavior does not guarantee that the internal mechanisms are well specified.
Counterfactual results are overinterpreted. Even a grounded simulation is still a model, so policy conclusions must be qualified because unmodeled school factors may alter what happens in reality.
Done right. The method is most useful when researchers treat the simulator as a structured hypothesis-testing tool and validate both outward behavior and internal-state trends against educational theory and case evidence.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a school-group simulation with roles such as target student, peer, bystander, and teacher. Define internal state variables such as belonging, threat, and trust. Run two scenarios such as baseline classroom practice and an intervention that increases teacher support and peer reporting. Measure visible incidents, cooperation, withdrawal, and the internal-state trends of each role over time.
