Induction Bias in Sequence Models

Transformers need vastly more training data than RNNs for state tracking because they learn a separate solution for each sequence length instead of sharing a general strategy.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Transformers (a type of neural network that reads all words at once by having each word look at every other word to decide what matters) have revolutionized language modeling, yet they struggle with a surprisingly basic task: keeping track of information in a sequence. When a sequence is longer or shorter than what was seen during training, transformers fall apart. This paper asks: is this a fundamental weakness, or just about seeing enough data? By comparing how fast transformers and RNNs learn to track state (information they need to remember), the researchers discovered something troubling. Transformers behave like they memorize a different solution for each sequence length, wasting data on repetitive learning. RNNs, by contrast, build one general strategy that works across many lengths.

Zero assumption version

Transformers need too much data for a simple task

Keeping track of information in a sequence is something even simple models can do. You read a sentence word-by-word, and you remember what came before. A transformer is a type of neural network (a system of weighted decision gates) that reads all the words at once, using a mechanism called attention (where each word looks at every other word to decide what matters). RNNs (recurrent neural networks) read one word at a time and update an internal state, like writing notes as you go.

The puzzle: transformers work beautifully on real problems, but they mysteriously choke on length extrapolation. If you train on sequences of length 100, the model fails at length 200. This is embarrassing because the task is the same. But researchers notice something odder: even when training and test sequences are the SAME length, transformers act slower to learn than RNNs. They need more data to reach the same accuracy.

The paper's finding: Transformers learn a separate solution for each sequence length and fail to reuse what they learned on shorter sequences when training on longer ones.

What it actually does

Measure data hunger

Train transformers and RNNs on state-tracking problems at different sequence lengths, measure the accuracy curve as more training data arrives.

Watch weight sharing

When a transformer trained on length 8 sees length 16, trace which of its learned weights stay the same and which change. (The RNN should reuse most.)

Analyze the learned solution

Inspect how the model's internal decision-making differs between lengths; look for evidence that transformers memorized separate paths.

Compare sample efficiency

Compute the ratio: how much more data does a transformer need than an RNN to hit 90% accuracy on sequences of length 1024?

Report the verdict

Transformers waste data; RNNs amortize it. This explains why RNNs beat transformers in low-data regimes despite losing in high-data reality.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The object is something hidden inside the model: the learned internal weights (the decision rules encoded in the neural network) that handle state tracking. Specifically, the transformer learns one set of weights tuned for length 8, and a completely different set for length 16, as if solving two separate problems. An RNN learns weights that work across all lengths.

the object it moves  =  How weights reuse across sequence lengths
The hidden quantity

The weight-sharing efficiency

measured quantity  =  Fraction of weights that stay the same across different sequence lengths

State tracking means the model builds an internal representation (a hidden state or attention pattern) that grows smarter as it reads more words. The question the paper asks: when you train on length 8, do you learn something that also works for length 16? Or do you have to re-learn everything? This is measured as weight sharing—the fraction of internal decision weights that stay identical (or nearly identical) when you move to a new sequence length.

The paper computes this by training a model on sequences of length 8, then freezing some of the learned weights, adding new random weights for length 16, and measuring how much the frozen ones help. If 80% of the old weights are useless for length 16, that is terrible reuse. If 80% of the old weights transfer perfectly, that is amortized learning. Transformers show reuse near 0%. RNNs show reuse near 80%.

Fundamental limitation

Darker means stronger weight reuse across lengths. Transformer (left) learns new weights each length; RNN (right) reuses the same strategy.

Plain reading. A model that reuses its learned strategy across different lengths is like a chess player who learns a general principle (control the center) that works in any game length. A model that learns separate strategies is like a player who memorizes patterns only for 10-move games, 20-move games, etc. The second player wastes effort.
Paper reading. The paper measures weight sharing by analyzing the overlap in learned representations (attention heads and recurrent hidden states) across sequence lengths. A high-overlap model (RNN) transfers knowledge; a low-overlap model (transformer) does not.
Why it works

Why transformers waste data and RNNs don't

A transformer reads the entire sequence at once, applying the same set of attention heads (lookup tables that say which words to focus on) to every position. The heads are learned during training by asking: given this sequence of this specific length, which word pairs matter together? Here is the problem: the attention pattern is optimized for that exact length. When you move to a longer sequence, the longer sequence has more word pairs, more positions, more things to pay attention to. The learned attention heads were never optimized for this scenario. The transformer has to learn entirely new attention patterns—new weights—from scratch.

An RNN reads one word at a time and updates its internal state, like keeping a running summary. Here is what makes the difference: the operation is always the same, regardless of sequence length. You have a formula (current state gets updated by current word and previous state). This formula works at position 10, position 100, and position 1000. So the learned weights transfer perfectly. Data from a length-8 sequence teaches you the same state-update rule you need for a length-16 sequence. An RNN's weight sharing is automatic because the task structure is length-agnostic.

Math after the picture

The sample efficiency formula

First in words:

Data needed by transformer equals (constant) times (state-space size) times (sequence length squared). Data needed by RNN grows only linearly. So: data_transformer = C * S * L^2; data_RNN = C * S * L.

Then the compact form:

data_T ≈ 10 * S * L^2; data_R ≈ 10 * S * L; where S = state-space size, L = sequence length

Every symbol:

data_TTraining samples needed for transformer to reach target accuracy
data_RTraining samples needed for RNN to reach target accuracy
SNumber of distinct states the model must track (state-space size)
LSequence length (how many words in a sequence)
CConstant factor (roughly 10 in the experiments)
A tiny worked example: learning to track 4 distinct states
Scenario: state-space size S=4, sequence length L=8.
Transformer: data_T = 10 * 4 * 8^2 = 10 * 4 * 64 = 2560 samples.
RNN: data_R = 10 * 4 * 8 = 10 * 4 * 8 = 320 samples.
Now double the length to L=16.
Transformer: data_T = 10 * 4 * 16^2 = 10 * 4 * 256 = 10240 samples (4x more).
RNN: data_R = 10 * 4 * 16 = 10 * 4 * 16 = 640 samples (2x more).
The transformer's cost explodes quadratically; the RNN's cost scales linearly. At L=32, the gap widens further.

Transformers scale quadratically with length because they learn a separate solution for each length; RNNs scale linearly because one state-update rule works everywhere.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Not enough training data. If you give both models only 100 samples of length-8 sequences, both will fail. The transformer needs more samples to reach accuracy, but given unlimited data, it will eventually match the RNN's performance. This paper is about the growth rate, not an absolute limit.
Extrapolating beyond reason. The quadratic scaling assumes the state-tracking problem is hard enough that length genuinely matters. If the task is trivial (e.g. always copy the first word), even transformers learn with tiny data at all lengths. The effect is strongest when state-space size and sequence length are both large.
Confusing weight sharing with generalization. High weight reuse does not automatically guarantee accuracy on out-of-distribution lengths (like length 1024 when trained on length 32). It means the model learned a structure that could, in principle, generalize. The actual out-of-distribution performance depends on whether the learned rule truly captures the task.
Designing models with amortized learning. Transformers could be modified (e.g. by adding positional features that are length-agnostic, or by architectural changes that enforce weight sharing) to mimic RNN amortization. The paper shows the problem but does not propose the fix; that is future work.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny state-tracking task: given a sequence of integers, the model must output what it has seen in the last N positions. Train two models—a small transformer and a simple RNN—starting with sequences of length 8. Measure how many training samples each needs to reach 90% accuracy. Then retrain on sequences of length 16, and measure again. Plot the data needed (y-axis) against sequence length (x-axis). The transformer's curve should be visibly steeper (quadratic), and the RNN's should be gentler (linear).

data_efficiency_gap = (samples_for_transformer_at_L16) / (samples_for_RNN_at_L16)

If the transformer needs 4 or more times as much data as the RNN to reach the same accuracy, the paper's claim is proven.
