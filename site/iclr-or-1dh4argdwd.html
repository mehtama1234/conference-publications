Scaling Up Memory for Robotic Control via Experience Retrieval

A robot policy picks key past moments from memory to help solve long-horizon tasks without the cost of storing every video frame.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots need memory to complete multi-step tasks like 'find three objects in order'. The obvious approach—storing all camera frames and feeding them to a policy—is too slow and unreliable: the robot sees different scenes from the expert, so it gets confused. MemER solves this by using two linked policies: a high-level policy that picks which past moments matter most, and a low-level policy that executes the instructions those moments inspire.

Zero assumption version

Long histories break robot policies

A robot policy is a trained system that decides which action to take next based on what it sees. To complete a task that spans minutes, like finding three hidden objects in sequence, the policy needs to remember what happened earlier. A beginner tries the obvious approach: feed the policy the entire history of camera frames from the start of the task. In theory, this seems sound: if the policy has seen every past moment, it should know what happened and what to do next.

In practice, this fails for two reasons. First, storing and processing minutes of video is computationally brutal; the system becomes too slow to run in real time on the robot. Second, and worse: the robot's camera sees different scenes than the expert's camera did. The frames are slightly different—objects are in different places, lighting shifts, the background changes. So when the policy is trained on expert video with wide context, it learns to rely on visual details that will never match when the robot acts. The longer the history, the bigger the mismatch. The policy becomes fragile.

The paper's move in one sentence. Instead of storing all frames, train a high-level policy to pick which past frames are task-relevant, then use only those frames plus recent ones when giving instructions to a low-level policy.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

High-level policy scans past

A vision-language model reads the most recent frames (roughly 10 frames, a snapshot) and looks back through the entire episode history to spot moments that matter for the current task.

High-level policy nominates keyframes

It selects and marks key past frames—say, when it spotted object A, when it completed step 1, when the context shifted. No theoretical limit; it can pick frames from anywhere in the past thousand+ frames.

High-level policy writes a subtask instruction

It generates a natural-language instruction to the low-level policy, like 'pick up the blue cube you saw earlier.' The instruction is conditioned on the keyframes it selected and the current scene.

Low-level policy executes

A separate fine-tuned vision-language-action model (like Qwen VL) reads the instruction and current frames, then outputs the robot's next action sequence—gripper angles, arm motions, grasp forces.

Repeat until task done

The high-level policy continues picking relevant moments and issuing instructions as the robot progresses, building a chain of short memory windows instead of one long unstable history.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the robot accesses its past. Instead of conditioning every decision on the full video history, it conditions the high-level policy on a selected subset of past frames—the keyframes the high-level policy has learned to pick. This dramatically shrinks the input, reduces computational cost, and avoids forcing the policy to memorize visual details that will shift when the robot acts.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Task-relevance ranking

measured quantity  =  which frames matter for this task

The paper does not explicitly measure 'importance' or 'relevance' with a number. Instead, it trains the high-level policy to learn a hidden ranking: which moments in the past matter most for the current task. This ranking is implicit in the policy's keyframe selections.

The high-level policy estimates this ranking by reading the recent context and the current task state, then deciding which frames from history would help predict the next subtask. The paper teaches this ranking through demonstration: during training, it annotates which past moments were task-relevant for expert trajectories, then the policy learns to predict those same moments from future new episodes.

Why it works

Why it works: Decoupling memory from execution

The core insight is that memory and action execution are two separate jobs, each with different constraints. A policy that picks which past moments matter does not have to execute the action itself, so it can afford to sit back, think, and reason over the full episode. A policy that executes (the low-level one) can afford to see less history, because it is only responsible for the next few steps; it trusts the high-level policy's memory picks.

This decoupling solves both problems. Computationally: the low-level policy only processes a few frames per decision, so inference stays fast. Against scene shift: the low-level policy sees mostly current frames plus a curated set of keyframes with landmarks it needs. When the robot acts, its camera is in the present, and the keyframes were moments of task importance (like 'this is where we saw the blue object'), not specific visual states. So the policy is less tripped up by covariate shift. The high-level policy, which sees more context, acts as a stable filter: it has seen enough history to spot what matters, and it distills that into a few concrete keyframes and a natural-language instruction the low-level policy can follow reliably.

Selecting keyframes: the scoring rule

First in words:

For each candidate frame in the past, score it as: relevance = how well this frame's visual features predict the next subtask. Select the top K frames by relevance score.

Then the compact form:

keyframes = argmax_K [ s(frame_i, current_state, task) ]

Every symbol:

keyframesthe set of selected past frames to remember
s(frame_i, current_state, task)relevance score: how much frame i helps predict what comes next
argmax_Kpick the K frames with the highest scores
frame_ia past frame from the episode history
current_statethe recent frames (last 10) the robot sees now
taskthe task label or goal description
Worked example: picking keyframes from a 12-frame history
Suppose the task is 'find the blue cube.' The high-level policy scores each past frame:
frame_12 (now, current): score = 9 (always relevant)
frame_11: score = 7 (recent, moderate relevance)
frame_10: score = 2 (old, not about blue cube)
frame_8: score = 8 (shows blue cube position! high relevance)
frame_5: score = 1 (old background, irrelevant)
frame_2: score = 6 (shows where we started, moderate relevance)
If we pick K=3 keyframes, we select: frame_12 (score 9), frame_8 (score 8), frame_2 (score 6).
The low-level policy then sees: current snapshot + frame_8 (landmark) + frame_2 (context) + the instruction 'pick the blue cube.' It avoids the clutter of frames 10, 5, etc. That selectivity is what keeps the policy from getting confused when scenes shift.

The paper does not reveal the exact scoring function s(); it emerges from training the high-level policy on annotated expert demos.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor keyframe annotation during training. If the expert demonstrations are not carefully annotated with which moments are task-relevant, the high-level policy learns a noisy ranking and picks useless frames. The system degrades back toward the naive approach.
Scene shift so severe the keyframes become unrecognizable. If the robot's camera view is very different from the expert's at the same location (e.g. different lighting, different angle), even a carefully picked keyframe from 5 minutes ago may be visually unrecognizable. The low-level policy can fail to ground itself.
Low-level policy execution errors cascade. If the low-level policy makes a mistake (e.g. fails to grasp an object), the high-level policy's keyframe strategy cannot recover; the episode is corrupted. Reliable, accurate low-level execution is a prerequisite.
Done right. Train the high-level policy on diverse, well-annotated expert trajectories. Keep the low-level policy on a stable foundation model (such as Qwen2.5-VL or Pi-0.5). Test on objects and scenes similar to training to avoid extreme scene variation. The paper achieves over 90% success on tasks with minutes of required memory.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robot task: place three objects in a row (A, then B, then C). The robot starts 30 seconds into the episode (after A has been moved). It must remember where A ended up, find B on a shelf 15 seconds later, and finally place C. A typical robotics simulation or real robot arm with a gripper can test this. Use a pre-trained vision-language model (Qwen2.5-VL or similar) as the high-level policy and a smaller fine-tuned model as the low-level policy.

success_rate = (tasks_completed_successfully) / (total_trials)

The critical number to watch: if the success rate drops more than 5% when you increase task duration from 30 seconds to 120 seconds, memory is your bottleneck. If it stays near 90%, keyframe selection is working.
