Inductive Reasoning for Temporal Knowledge Graphs with Emerging Entities

When new entities arrive with no history, borrow the reasoning patterns from similar old entities to predict their future interactions.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Temporal knowledge graphs are like a giant filing cabinet: who talked to whom, and when. When a new person joins, they have no history yet. Existing methods fail because they have never seen this person before. But here is the insight: if the new person is similar to someone already in the cabinet (perhaps they work in the same field, or share the same interests), then they probably behave in similar ways. TransFIR uses this similarity to copy reasoning patterns from known entities to new ones, predicting their interactions even on day one.

Zero assumption version

The arrival problem

A temporal knowledge graph is a recording of interactions over time: which entities (people, companies, events) interacted with which others, and when. The system learns to predict missing or future interactions by studying past patterns.

The problem: new entities arrive all the time, and they have zero history. A person joins a social network today. A business starts up today. The existing reasoning method has nothing to learn from, so it fails catastrophically—accuracy plummets because there are no past interactions to analyze.

In one sentence. New entities with no history can borrow reasoning patterns from similar old entities.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure similarity

Compute how similar the new entity is to each known entity using their semantic properties (what field they work in, what they like, who they are).

Find the closest matches

Rank known entities by similarity. The top few are the candidates to copy from.

Extract the pattern

For each closest match, retrieve their temporal interaction patterns: who they usually interact with, in what order, at what times.

Combine patterns

Blend the patterns from the top similar entities (give more weight to the closer ones) into a composite reasoning rule for the new entity.

Predict interactions

Apply this blended pattern to predict who the new entity will interact with next.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard temporal knowledge graph reasoning takes one sequence: the interaction history of one entity, and learns patterns from it. When a new entity arrives, there is no sequence to learn from, so the system has no input.

TransFIR changes which sequences the reasoning algorithm uses. Instead of only the new entity's own (empty) history, it also uses the histories of similar known entities. This gives the reasoner actual data to work with.

the object it moves  =  The input sequences
The hidden quantity

Semantic similarity: which entities are cousins

measured quantity  =  Semantic similarity score

The hidden quantity is a number that measures how much two entities are alike in their inherent properties (not in who they interact with, but in what they are). If both entities are lawyers, or both work in finance, or both are universities in the same region, their similarity score is high.

The paper computes this similarity using a semantic clustering method: a codebook (a short list of standard templates or semantic archetypes) classifies each entity into a latent cluster (a group of 'types'). Entities in the same cluster have high similarity. This clustering is trained once on the known entities, then applied to new arrivals. The paper then uses the cluster assignment to decide which known entities are most similar to the newcomer.

Why it works

Why borrowing patterns works

Here is the causal chain: If a new entity is semantically similar to a known entity, they share the same underlying behavioral rules. Lawyers interact with judges and clients in a predictable order and timing. Accountants interact with their clients in a different, but equally stable, pattern. Similarity in nature means similarity in behavior.

When we give the reasoner the known entity's history instead of just the new entity's empty history, the reasoner has real data to learn from. It extracts the temporal patterns (the sequence of interactions, the timing, the types of partners). The new entity can then use those same patterns, because it is the same type.

Why 28.6 percent better? Because with no history, the old method is guessing blindly. With borrowed history, the new method has a solid starting point. The new entity's reasoning becomes as reliable as an established entity's from day one, instead of starting from zero.

Math after the picture

The scoring equation

First in words:

For a new entity, compute a weighted average of known entities' interaction patterns, weighted by their semantic similarity to the new entity.

Then the compact form:

pattern(new) = sum_i ( similarity(new, entity_i) * pattern(entity_i) ) / sum_i similarity(new, entity_i)

Every symbol:

pattern(new)the reasoning rules for the new entity
similarity(new, entity_i)how much the new entity resembles entity i (a number between 0 and 1)
pattern(entity_i)the learned temporal interaction sequence of known entity i
sum_iadd up over all known entities
A tiny worked example
Three known entities (Alice, Bob, Charlie) with similarity scores to new entity Frank: Alice=0.3, Bob=0.5, Charlie=0.9.
Alice's pattern: interact-with-lawyer, then-judge, then-repeat.
Bob's pattern: interact-with-accountant, then-client, then-repeat.
Charlie's pattern: interact-with-lawyer, then-colleague, then-repeat. (Charlie is a lawyer, like Frank.)
Weighted pattern for Frank: 0.3*Alice + 0.5*Bob + 0.9*Charlie, divided by (0.3+0.5+0.9=1.7) = 0.18*Alice + 0.29*Bob + 0.53*Charlie.
Charlie dominates (0.53), so Frank's predicted pattern is heavily biased toward Charlie's lawyer behavior.

The equation weights each known entity's pattern by how similar it is to the newcomer, then blends them. The most similar entities have the loudest voice.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong similarity measure If the similarity score is computed badly (e.g., measuring interactions instead of inherent type), then similar entities may not share behavioral patterns, and predictions fail.
New entity is truly unique If the new entity is so different from all known entities that no similarity cluster matches, there is no suitable pattern to borrow. The method defaults to random prediction.
Patterns change over time If the temporal patterns of similar entities drift or change (because their field or role shifted), then copying an old pattern leads to stale predictions.
Codebook is tuned correctly If the semantic clustering is trained well (so that true similar entities end up in the same cluster), then the pattern transfer works reliably, even for very new entities.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny temporal knowledge graph: five known entities, each with a short interaction history (a sequence of 5 to 10 interactions over 5 time steps). Train the similarity measure (the codebook classifier) on these five entities. Then introduce two new entities (with no history). For each new entity, measure how similar it is to each of the five known entities using the trained classifier. Retrieve the pattern from the most similar known entity. Use that pattern to predict the new entity's next interaction. Measure accuracy: did the prediction match ground truth?

accuracy = (number of correct predictions on new entities) / (number of new entities) * 100 percent

The demo should make it impossible to miss: new entities, which have zero history and would normally fail, suddenly have reasonable predictions because they borrowed patterns from their closest neighbors.
