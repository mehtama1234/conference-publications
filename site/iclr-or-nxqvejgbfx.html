CompMarkGS: Watermarking 3D Graphics Against Compression

Embed a hidden ownership mark into 3D graphics models so it survives even after the model is shrunk for storage.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

When 3D graphics models are shared online, owners need a way to prove the model is theirs. This paper shows how to hide a tiny message inside a 3D graphics model as proof of ownership, called a watermark. The trick: teach the watermark to survive when the model gets compressed (shrunk down for faster sharing and storage) by training it with simulated compression noise so it learns to stay readable even when numbers are rounded, something earlier methods failed to do.

Zero assumption version

A watermark that vanishes after compression

3D graphics models (built from millions of tiny colored dots) are huge and slow to transmit. In practice, people shrink them using compression, which is a process that throws away information to make them smaller. The problem: if you watermark a model by changing some of its dot attributes, those changes often get wiped out during compression.

A naive approach would be to just mark some dots before sharing. But compressors like quantization (which rounds numbers to fewer digits) alter those marked dots, erasing the evidence of ownership. The watermark disappears.

The challenge: Hide a watermark inside 3D graphics so it stays readable after the model is compressed.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes where and how the watermark is embedded. Instead of stamping it on individual dots (which get disrupted by compression), it embeds the watermark into a learned feature that generates all the dots. Think of it like signing the blueprint instead of signing each page.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: How many watermark bits survive compression

measured quantity  =  Bit recovery accuracy

The paper measures a hidden quantity: how many bits of the watermark message are still readable after compression. The watermark is a short binary message (a sequence of 0s and 1s). After a model is compressed, a small neural decoder tries to extract this message from the rendered image. Survival rate is the percentage of bits recovered correctly.

The paper estimates this by training a watermark encoder (which hides the message) together with a quantization distortion layer that simulates compression noise. During training, the system learns how to hide the message so it stays readable even after the noise happens.

Why it works

Why it works: Training against compression noise

The key is to teach the watermark during training to stay readable when compression distortions happen. Here is how: (1) Instead of marking individual dots (which compression alters), mark the learned feature that generates all dots. This is like signing the seed of the tree instead of every leaf. (2) During training, simulate compression by injecting quantization noise into this feature. This noise mimics what a real compressor will do later. (3) The training process learns to encode the watermark message in a way that stays readable even through this noise, because the noise happens every training step. (4) A pre-trained small decoder network is kept frozen and trained to read watermark signals from the rendered output even when they are noisy.

Two additional losses improve the result: An HSV loss reduces color artifacts that appear when the watermark is hidden (HSV is a color model that tracks hue, saturation, and value; this loss keeps colors from looking wrong because it penalizes any mismatches between the watermarked render and the true image in the color space where human eyes detect oddities most). A frequency-aware loss grows more dots in complex regions of the image, improving rendering quality because detailed areas like edges and textures need more dots to render crisply. The total training loss combines message recovery loss, rendering quality loss, and watermark survival loss.

The training equation

First in words:

Total loss = weight for rendering times (dot prediction loss + color loss weight times color loss + frequency loss weight times frequency loss) + watermark message weight times message loss.

Then the compact form:

L_total = lambda_img * (L_scaffold + lambda_hsv * L_hsv + lambda_freq * L_freq) + lambda_msg * L_msg

Every symbol:

L_totalthe sum of all training costs
lambda_imghow much to prioritize rendering quality
L_scaffoldreconstruction loss (how well does the model render the scene)
lambda_hsvweight for color quality (how natural do the colors look)
L_hsvcolor loss (difference between rendered and real colors)
lambda_freqweight for detail preservation
L_freqfrequency loss (difference in high-frequency details like edges)
lambda_msghow much to prioritize watermark recovery
L_msgmessage loss (how many watermark bits were decoded wrong)
A tiny worked example
Suppose rendering quality loss is 0.5, color loss is 0.2, frequency loss is 0.1, and message loss is 0.3.
With lambda_img = 10.0, lambda_hsv = 5.0, lambda_freq = 3.0, lambda_msg = 100.0:
L_total = 10.0 * (0.5 + 5.0 * 0.2 + 3.0 * 0.1) + 100.0 * 0.3
L_total = 10.0 * (0.5 + 1.0 + 0.3) + 30.0 = 10.0 * 1.8 + 30.0 = 18.0 + 30.0 = 48.0
The watermark message loss (30.0) is largest, so training focuses on getting the message right.

The equation balances three goals: making the 3D model render correctly, keeping colors natural, and ensuring the watermark survives compression.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Compression is very aggressive. If the compressor uses extreme settings (very low precision, extreme pruning), the watermark may still vanish. The method is trained on typical compression levels, not worst-case extremes.
Color artifacts make the watermark visible. If the HSV loss weight is too low, hiding the watermark creates visible color oddities. An attacker could spot and remove the watermark manually.
Adversarial edits destroy the message. An attacker can crop, flip, or apply style transfer to the rendered output. This method is measured against compression and image distortion, but not complex attacks like style-transfer or AI-generated fakes.
Done right. With proper tuning of loss weights, the watermark stays invisible to the eye, survives compression, and remains decodable by the owner's decoder.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A runnable demo should (1) train a 3D model with the CompMarkGS watermark, (2) compress it using quantization (round the numbers to fewer digits), (3) render the compressed model to an image, and (4) run the decoder on the image to extract the watermark bits. Measure how many bits match the original watermark message before and after compression.

Bit accuracy = (number of correctly recovered bits) / (total bits in message)

The demo must show that without the distortion layer, compression destroys the watermark (accuracy near zero). With distortion during training, the watermark is fully readable (accuracy near 100 percent) even after quantization.
