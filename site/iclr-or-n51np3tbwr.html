Stochastic Optimal Control for Brain Representation Learning

When brain scans arrive at random times, skip discretizing them — instead learn by steering latent dynamics through random control.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Brain scans (fMRI) measure activity at irregular intervals with lots of noise. Existing self-supervised methods chop them into fixed time bins or average them, throwing away the real timing information. This paper reframes learning brain representations as a control problem: imagine steering the brain's hidden dynamics like a boat pilot, and learn the most useful representation by finding steering policies that work across messy, irregular timing.

Zero assumption version

Timing wreckage kills what you learn

fMRI measures brain activity by scanning a person's head every few seconds. But the scans do not arrive at the same intervals every time — sometimes 2 seconds, sometimes 3, sometimes with gaps. This is temporal irregularity, and it is real.

Standard self-supervised learning (learning from unlabeled data by comparing examples to each other) handles this by chop-chop-chopping the signal into equal time bins, or just averaging everything into a single number. Both moves discard the actual timing structure — the real rhythm of the brain's activity. A beginner would say: just average everything and train a classifier. But averaging wipes out the temporal pattern, which often tells you the most about what the brain is doing.

The method in one sentence. Learn a brain representation by treating it as a random control problem: steer latent (hidden) brain dynamics through time, and extract the representation from the steering policy itself.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most self-supervised methods learn by comparing two views of the data (e.g., comparing a full sentence to a masked version). This paper replaces that comparison with a control problem: given irregular fMRI signals, find a steering policy that explains them. The policy itself IS the representation.

the object it moves  =  steering policy from continuous control
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: control flow alignment

measured quantity  =  control flow alignment

The paper measures a hidden quantity called control flow alignment — how well a steering policy (control) explains the observed brain dynamics under uncertainty. This is not the brain activity itself; it is a measure of how tightly the learned control policy matches what the brain does when you steer it through hidden state space.

The paper estimates this using a loss function that rewards steering commands that reconstruct the messy, irregular fMRI observations. The better the control policy, the lower the loss, because a policy that truly captures the brain's dynamics will predict each observation more accurately, shrinking the error. Unlike classifying brain activity (which requires labeled data), this method only needs the raw fMRI signal.

Why it works

Why it works: steering catches patterns that averages miss

Averaging throws away timing. But timing is the pattern. When a brain scan arrives late (say 3 seconds instead of 2), an averaging method treats it the same as an on-time scan — it just adds to the pile. A steering policy, by contrast, adapts: if the brain's hidden state moves smoothly and continuously through an abstract high-dimensional space, then a delayed scan should be predictable from where the state was going. The policy learns that prediction.

Here is the causal chain: (1) Model the brain as continuous hidden dynamics (like a ball rolling smoothly through valleys and hills). (2) Ask the steering policy: given those dynamics, what commands would you send to stay on track with the observations? (3) The policy that answers best IS the representation — it encodes the most important features of the brain's dynamics. (4) Because the dynamics are continuous, irregular timing does not break the learning; the policy adapts automatically. (5) This unifies both masked prediction (hide some brain activity, reconstruct it) and contrastive learning (compare different views), because both can be framed as control: one policy steers to reconstruct, another steers to match learned feature lists.

Math after the picture

The steering equation

First in words:

Total loss equals the prediction error (how much the steered brain state misses the real observations) plus a penalty term that discourages wild steering commands.

Then the compact form:

loss = sum_t (observation_t - prediction_t(control_t, dynamics))^2 + lambda * sum_t (control_t)^2

Every symbol:

observation_tthe fMRI reading at time t
prediction_twhat the continuous dynamics predict at time t
control_tthe steering command (policy output) at time t
dynamicsthe hidden continuous-time brain model
lambdahow much we penalize large steering commands
A tiny worked example
Suppose a fMRI signal is observed at times [1s, 2.5s, 4s] with readings [50, 55, 48].
The continuous dynamics predict [49, 54, 50] given steering control [0.8, 0.9, 0.7].
Prediction error = (50-49)^2 + (55-54)^2 + (48-50)^2 = 1 + 1 + 4 = 6.
Control penalty (lambda=0.1) = 0.1 * (0.8^2 + 0.9^2 + 0.7^2) = 0.1 * (0.64 + 0.81 + 0.49) = 0.194.
Total loss = 6 + 0.194 = 6.194. Training adjusts the steering policy to minimize this.

The steering cost penalizes wild commands, so the policy learns efficient, smooth strategies — these become the compact brain representation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Dynamics are not actually smooth. If the brain has truly discontinuous jumps in its hidden state (a sudden switch), the continuous model will miss them and the steering policy will waste effort trying to smooth them out.
Very large timing gaps. If scans are missing for many seconds, the policy has to blindly extrapolate. The model can hallucinate what happened in the gap, degrading the learned representation.
Noise is not random. If fMRI noise has structure (e.g., related to head motion or scanner drift), the control policy will try to explain it as if it were dynamics, polluting the representation.
Done right. Align the continuous dynamics assumption with the real fMRI imaging model, handle large gaps by masking them, and preprocess for known noise sources (head motion regression).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny synthetic fMRI dataset: simulate a continuous brain system (e.g., a low-dimensional dynamical system like a damped oscillator), sample it at irregular times with noise, then train the control-based representation learner. Plot the learned steering policy and verify it recovers the true underlying dynamics better than an averaging baseline, because the steering policy learned where each delayed scan should land given the smooth motion of the hidden state, whereas averaging just piles them up without tracking the motion.
