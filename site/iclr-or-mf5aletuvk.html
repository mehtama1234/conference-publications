AgentFlow: Training Agentic Systems in the Live Loop

Instead of one model doing everything, split the job across specialized modules and train the planner while it actually works.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Large language models (LLMs, the smart language tools that answer questions) can learn to use external tools — like a calculator or search engine — but when trained as a single black box, they scale poorly and break on new scenarios. AgentFlow solves this by splitting the work: a planner decides what to do, an executor runs tools, a verifier checks results, and a generator cleans up answers. It then trains only the planner, directly inside the live loop of multi-turn work, using a method called Flow-GRPO that unravels the hard problem of credit assignment (figuring out which decisions caused success) into manageable single-step updates.

Zero assumption version

The Scaling Trap of Monolithic Models

Today's approach: train one giant model to do everything — think, decide, call a tool, check the result, and give an answer — all in one long sequence. This works for simple tasks, but it breaks in three ways. First, as the task gets longer (more steps needed), training becomes unstable because each small error early on cascades. Second, when you add more tools to the toolkit, the model has to memorize which tool fits which job, and that doesn't transfer to new tools. Third, if you change the environment (a search engine gives different results today), the model fails because it was trained on the old data.

A beginner's fix: just split the job into separate pieces (planner, executor, verifier). But these pieces are usually frozen — they don't learn. You glue them together with prompts or handwritten rules, and those rules are brittle. They break on new tasks because no one wrote a rule for that specific scenario.

The real problem: You either train one monolithic model that scales poorly, or you split the job but freeze the pieces so they never learn to work together.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper trains the planner's decision policy (the weights that choose subgoals and tools) instead of leaving it frozen. Everything else stays frozen — executor, verifier, and generator don't change. This surgical focus means the learning problem shrinks: instead of a single model learning everything at once, one small network learns one clean job: how to plan in this exact multi-turn environment.

the object it moves  =  planner weights
The hidden quantity

The credit-assignment bridge

measured quantity  =  per-turn advantage

In a long agentic chain (query, then plan step 1, then execute, then plan step 2, then execute again, repeatedly until final answer), every planner decision contributes to the final success, but it's hard to know how much. The hidden quantity is the per-turn advantage: how much better was step t's choice compared to typical? This matters because training needs to know which decisions were good and which were bad. In the old approach, people try to grade each step with intermediate rewards (a heuristic like 'Did the tool output look useful?'), but those heuristics are fragile and wrong on new scenarios. AgentFlow's real move: it takes the final outcome reward (pass or fail), broadcasts it to every step, and then uses group-normalized advantages to fairly estimate each step's contribution relative to other attempts at that same step.

The paper estimates this by sampling a group of G parallel rollouts from the planner, scoring them all with the final outcome, then normalizing advantages within the group so high-reward trajectories get higher advantage estimates. This method works because it relies only on the true final outcome, not brittle heuristics, making the credit assignment problem tractable — a problem that is otherwise stuck because you have only one final score to explain many steps.

Plain reading. Imagine grading a student's work. Instead of points for each sentence, you only know the final test score. To figure out which sentences were good, you compare this student to other students who solved the same problem. You say: 'This student did better than the median at step 2, worse at step 3.' By comparing within the group, you deduce which steps were strong — and this deduction works because the group took the same steps with different results. That's a group-normalized advantage.
Paper reading. Flow-GRPO samples G parallel trajectories from the planner policy, scores each with the final outcome, and computes advantages by subtracting the group average from each trajectory's score and dividing by the group's spread (standard deviation). This normalization works because it makes advantages stable: a group where all trajectories fail will show negative advantages, and one where all succeed will show positive — the relative ranking within the group is what matters. It then applies a clipped update rule (a small bounded nudge in the direction of high-advantage choices) to push the planner's weights toward better decisions without overreacting. The clipping bound prevents one lucky success or catastrophic failure from causing a huge weight change, which keeps training stable because wild jumps in weights cause the planner to forget what it learned.
Why it works

Why splitting and training works

The core insight: monolithic models fail because they optimize everything at once — think, tool-pick, verify, format — tangled together. When you split those jobs, each module can specialize. The planner only learns to plan, not to verify or format. The signal is cleaner. Training a specialist is easier than training a generalist, because the planner only has to learn one narrow task instead of five.

The second insight: long-horizon credit assignment is hard when tools take multiple steps and produce messy intermediate outputs. Old methods write brittle heuristics (Is this output likely useful? Did the tool error?) that break on new tools because those heuristics are guesses, not derived from real data. Flow-GRPO does something smarter: it broadcasts the final outcome reward to every step, then uses group-normalized advantages to ask 'how much better was this step's choice than its peer group at that exact same step?' This works because the group saw the same decision point but made different choices, so comparing their results is a fair way to grade each choice — no guessing needed. This removes the need for handcrafted intermediate rewards. The advantage estimate is derived purely from outcome data, using the group as an anchor. Because you're only training the planner, not tool-calling or verification, the trainable part stays small and stable, even over many steps — fewer moving pieces means training is less likely to diverge.

The optimization objective

First in words:

For each planner decision, the loss is clipped to prevent wild updates, scaled by the group-normalized advantage. Advantages are estimated as (actual return minus group average) divided by group standard deviation.

Then the compact form:

L = (1/T) * sum_t [ -eta * clip( (A_t / sigma_group), -delta, delta ) * log( pi_theta(a_t | s_t) ) ]

Every symbol:

A_tgroup-normalized advantage at step t (how much better than peer group average)
sigma_groupstandard deviation of returns within the group of G rollouts
pi_thetathe planner's probability of choosing action a_t in state s_t
deltaclipping bound (prevents huge updates), typically 0.2
etalearning rate multiplier
Tnumber of steps in the trajectory
A tiny worked example
Suppose three rollouts at the same planning step attempt to pick a tool:
Rollout 1: picked search, final outcome = success (return = 1.0)
Rollout 2: picked calculator, final outcome = failure (return = 0.0)
Rollout 3: picked search, final outcome = success (return = 1.0)
Group mean return = (1.0 + 0.0 + 1.0) / 3 = 0.67
Group std = sqrt( ((1-0.67)^2 + (0-0.67)^2 + (1-0.67)^2) / 3 ) ≈ 0.47
Rollout 1 advantage: (1.0 - 0.67) / 0.47 ≈ 0.70 (above average, push this choice up)
Rollout 2 advantage: (0.0 - 0.67) / 0.47 ≈ -1.43 (below average, push this choice down)
Clipped advantages prevent any single mistake from dominating; if two rollouts succeed, the success signal is strong but bounded.

Clipping prevents the planner from making extreme jumps when one group happens to succeed or fail, stabilizing training over many turns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Group size too small. If you only sample 2 or 3 parallel rollouts per step, the group-normalized advantage is noisy. You need enough parallel samples (the paper uses groups of several rollouts) for the group mean and std to be stable.
Frozen modules are weakly coordinated. The executor, verifier, and generator are not trained. If they are poorly designed prompts or heuristics, they become bottlenecks. AgentFlow assumes those three modules already work reasonably well.
Sparse rewards delay learning. If the environment only provides feedback at the very end (after 10 steps), the planner has to infer which of the 10 steps went wrong. Flow-GRPO handles this by using group comparisons, but if the task is extremely long, credit assignment remains hard.
Done right: planner trains on its exact real-world states. Because training happens on-policy (the planner generates its own rollouts and learns from them immediately, while working), the planner sees states it will actually face when deployed. This closes a gap between training and real-world use that haunts methods trained on old data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple multi-step task: a query that needs three sequential decisions (choose tool, then execute, then check result, then choose the next tool). Hardcode the executor, verifier, and generator to be simple rule-based modules. Initialize the planner with random weights. Run Flow-GRPO for 100 training iterations, sampling a group of 4 rollouts per iteration. Track the planner's success rate on new queries it has never seen during training.

success_rate = (number of queries that reach goal) / (total queries)

The demo must show success rate rising from near zero to >80% within 50 iterations, and staying high on a held-out test set. This proves that the planner learns to coordinate with frozen modules and generalizes to new queries.
