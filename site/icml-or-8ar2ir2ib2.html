LIMMT: Less Is More for Motion Tracking

Training humanoid robots on just the best 3% of motion data outperforms using the entire messy dataset because high-quality targets steer the policy toward stable solutions early.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When robots learn to imitate human motion, they train on large motion-capture datasets. But many clips in these datasets are physically impossible for the robot to do, or are nearly identical copies. Feeding the policy (the algorithm that decides what the robot does) all this noisy data wastes training time and pulls learning toward the wrong answers. This paper shows that picking just the best 3% of the motion clips beats training on everything.

Zero assumption version

The noise trap in motion datasets

Motion-capture datasets like AMASS contain thousands of clips of real human movement. But a robot is not a human: many of these clips are physically impossible for the robot to execute (the human bent their spine in ways the rigid robot cannot). Other clips are near-copies of each other, adding nothing new. When a policy trains on this full, messy dataset, it spends training time chasing impossible targets and learning redundant patterns.

An untrained policy learns by trying motions and adjusting based on the error signal (how far it missed the target). If many targets are broken or repetitive, the error signals point in wrong directions, pulling the policy into a bad local solution early in training. Once stuck in that wrong valley, the policy struggles to escape, even with more training steps.

In one sentence. The paper filters motion data by physics feasibility, diversity, and complexity, then trains the policy on only the highest-quality motions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Physics feasibility check

Replay each motion in a simulator; keep only motions where the robot obeys physics constraints (joints don't bend backward, feet don't slide through floors).

Step 2: Embed motion into a learned space

Train a special neural network (periodic autoencoder) that compresses each motion into a short list of numbers capturing its shape. Similar motions are now close together in this space.

Step 3: Score complexity

Measure how much energy each motion uses (kinetic energy, acceleration). Motions that move fast and change speed are marked complex; static poses are marked simple.

Step 4: Diversity-weighted sampling

Pick motions that are spread far apart in the learned motion space and have high complexity scores, skipping clusters of identical or simple motions.

Step 5: Train the policy

Use only the selected 3% of motions to train the robot controller. The clear, diverse, physically-sound targets steer learning toward a stable solution.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper picks which motion clips the policy trains on. Instead of feeding the entire dataset, it curates a tiny, high-quality subset and trains the policy only on those selected motions. Everything else (the policy architecture, the reward function, the training algorithm) stays the same.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: training trajectory quality

measured quantity  =  training trajectory quality

A training trajectory is the path the policy's learning takes over time: where it lands after 100 steps, 1000 steps, 1 million steps, etc. A good trajectory climbs steeply early (the policy learns fast) and settles at a high performance level (it does well). A bad trajectory gets stuck in a shallow local optimum (a wrong answer) and cannot escape.

The paper measures trajectory quality by tracking the error (how far the robot misses each target motion) as training progresses. With full, noisy data, the error curve is jagged and plateaus low (the policy gets trapped). With curated data, the error curve drops steeply early and stays low (the policy finds a good solution quickly). The paper shows that data quality steers the trajectory because better targets (physically feasible, diverse, energetic) deliver clearer error signals: a clear signal tells the policy exactly which direction to nudge, whereas a weak or broken signal points randomly and pulls the policy into the wrong place.

Why it works

Why it works: clear learning signals early

When a policy trains, it adjusts its actions by taking learning steps (nudges) in the direction that shrinks the error. If targets are noisy or impossible, these steps point in wrong directions. Early in training, the policy is equally unstable in all directions, so it takes these wrong steps and lands in a bad local solution. Once stuck, later training cannot escape because the error signals from the noisy data keep pulling it back.

High-quality, diverse motions deliver consistent, clear learning signals early. Physically feasible targets make sure no steps point toward impossible actions. Diverse motions spread learning across the state space (the set of all possible robot configurations), so the policy explores broadly instead of getting trapped in a narrow wrong valley. Complex motions with high energy create stronger learning signals because a big, obvious mismatch between the robot's attempt and a fast, energetic target generates a large, unambiguous learning step, whereas a tiny mismatch is drowned out by random noise. Together, these steer the policy into a good local solution from the start, and remaining training solidifies that solution instead of fighting to escape a bad one.

Redundant or simple motions do the opposite: they waste training steps on repetitive, weak signals that do not push the policy in any clear direction. Removing them speeds up learning because the policy wastes fewer steps on ambiguous information and settles into a good solution faster.

Math after the picture

Scoring motions: feasibility, diversity, and complexity

First in words:

For each motion, compute three scores and filter/select based on them. Feasibility score (from physics simulation) must be high. Diversity is measured as distance in embedding space. Complexity is kinetic energy or acceleration. Select motions that are physically valid, spread far apart, and have high complexity.

Then the compact form:

selected = FPS( embeddings[ S_phy >= threshold ], weights=complexity_scores )

Every symbol:

S_phyphysics feasibility score (0 to 100, from simulator); keep only if >= 90
embeddingscompressed motion representation from the periodic autoencoder (one short vector per motion)
FPSFurthest-Point Sampling: pick the first motion, then iteratively pick the motion farthest from all selected so far
complexity_scoreskinetic energy plus acceleration magnitude for each motion; high = richer learning signal
weights=complexity_scoresin FPS, tie-break geometric distance by preferring high-complexity motions when two are equally far apart
A tiny worked example
Say we have 1000 raw motions. Physics filter removes 200 broken ones (head rotation over 90 degrees, foot sliding). Remaining: 800 valid.
The embedder maps these 800 to points in a 32-dimensional space (32 numbers each). We compute complexity: motion 1 (walking) scores 8 (moderate), motion 2 (jumping) scores 15 (high), motion 3 (standing) scores 2 (low).
FPS picks motion 2 first (arbitrary start). Then it finds the motion farthest from motion 2 in embedding space. Say that is motion 150. Then it finds the farthest from both motion 2 and motion 150. It continues until we have 30 motions (3% of 1000).
Tie-breaking: if motions 200 and 201 are equally far from the selected set, FPS picks motion 200 because its complexity score is 12 vs 5. The result is a tight, diverse, complex training set.

The feasibility filter removes targets that point the learning steps toward impossible robot configurations. Embedding-space diversity ensures the policy sees varied state-action pairs. Complexity weighting focuses on high-energy motions that generate strong, unambiguous error signals.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong feasibility threshold. If the physics filter is too strict (rejecting physically marginal but trainable motions) or too loose (keeping broken motions), the curated set loses signal value. The paper uses a fixed threshold (90 out of 100) but may need tuning for different robots.
Embedding space does not capture what matters. The periodic autoencoder learns a compressed representation. If the embedding does not capture motion semantics that matter to the robot (e.g., contact patterns, joint constraints specific to the robot), diversity in that space does not guarantee behavioral diversity during training.
Complexity score correlates with dataset quirks. Using only kinetic energy and acceleration as complexity measures biases selection toward fast, jerky motions. If the dataset has quirks (e.g., a subset of high-energy outliers), those dominate the selection and the policy may overfit to unrepresentative motions.
Done right: order matters. The paper emphasizes a three-stage hierarchy: filter first (remove infeasible), then measure diversity in a learned space (to avoid broken motions dominating coverage), then weight by complexity (to focus on high-energy motions that teach more). Reordering these steps breaks the approach because broken motions can dominate diversity selection if not removed first.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a motion dataset (even a small synthetic one: 100 random joint trajectories). Use a simulator to assign a feasibility score to each (does a humanoid robot execute it without violating constraints?). Train a simple periodic autoencoder on the feasible ones to embed them. Compute complexity as joint speed magnitude. Run Furthest-Point Sampling with complexity weighting to select the best 10%. Train two copies of a simple reinforcement-learning policy: one on the full 100 motions, one on the selected 10%. Track the mean tracking error over 50 million training steps for each.

tracking_error = mean( |robot_state - target_state| ) at each timestep, averaged over episodes

The demo should show that the policy trained on the curated 10% tracks target motions more accurately than the one trained on all 100 noisy motions, and reaches that accuracy faster (in fewer training steps), because clear, physically-sound targets generate unambiguous learning signals that point the policy toward the right answer from the start, instead of wasting steps on broken or redundant motions.
