Generative Bayesian Optimization: Generative Models as Acquisition Functions

Train a sampler to draw good design candidates directly from observed utility values, so batch Bayesian optimization becomes candidate generation instead of repeated inner-loop search.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Bayesian optimization is used when each experiment is expensive, such as testing a molecule, a chip setting, or a materials recipe. The usual workflow builds a surrogate of the unknown objective, turns that surrogate into an acquisition function, and then solves another optimization problem to find the next candidates. This becomes awkward in large batches, in high dimension, and in discrete search spaces because the inner search can be harder than the original design problem. The paper proposes a different route: train a generative sampler directly from observed utility values so that it places more mass on promising regions. Then drawing a batch is just sampling from that learned proposal. This helps because candidate generation can absorb feasibility structure and large-batch needs without repeatedly solving a difficult search problem from scratch.

Zero assumption version

Why batch Bayesian optimization becomes awkward in hard design spaces

Bayesian optimization usually chooses new experiments by maximizing an acquisition function, which scores how promising each candidate looks under a surrogate model of the unknown objective.

That extra maximization step is manageable in small continuous spaces, but it becomes expensive in high dimension, in combinatorial design spaces, and when one wants a large batch. The optimizer must keep solving a hard inner problem even though the final goal is only to propose many strong candidates. This slows the loop because every batch depends on another search over the design space.

The paper's move. Learn a sampler whose probability mass tracks candidate quality, because then a good batch can be drawn directly instead of found by repeated inner-loop search.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  probability mass assigned to high-utility regions

The hidden quantity is how much of the sampler's probability mass falls on useful parts of the design space. If most mass sits on weak regions, the batch wastes evaluations. If mass concentrates on strong yet diverse regions, the batch is productive.

This quantity matters because the paper replaces explicit acquisition maximization with sampling. The sampler succeeds only when its mass tracks expected utility closely enough that random draws are already good proposals.

Why it works

Why a learned sampler can replace explicit acquisition search

An acquisition function is useful only because its peaks indicate where evaluations should go next. If one can learn a sampler that already places most of its draws near those peaks, then a separate maximization step becomes unnecessary.

That is the core mechanism here. The generative sampler is trained from observed utilities so that high-utility candidates receive higher probability. This is valuable in discrete and structured spaces because the sampler can respect feasibility by construction, whereas a generic inner optimizer may keep proposing awkward or invalid points.

The approach also scales well to large batches because drawing many samples is easy once the sampler is trained. Instead of solving dozens of linked acquisition optimizations, the method samples a batch directly. Theory in the paper supports this direction by showing that, under suitable conditions, the learned sequence of proposal rules concentrates near global optima over rounds.

Math after the picture

Probability mass should track utility

First in words:

Train the sampler so candidates with larger expected utility get larger probability, because then sampling acts like drawing from the acquisition rule itself.

Then the compact form:

proposal(candidate) proportional to exp(utility(candidate) / temperature)

Every symbol:

candidateone design point that could be evaluated next
utility(candidate)the value the optimizer assigns to that candidate from current observations
temperaturea scaling term that controls how sharply the sampler concentrates on high-utility points
proposal(candidate)the sampler's probability for drawing that candidate
A tiny worked example
Suppose three feasible designs have utility values 9, 6, and 1.
Set temperature to 3, so their unnormalized proposal scores are exp(3), exp(2), and exp(0.33).
Those scores are about 20.1, 7.4, and 1.4.
After normalization, the first design receives most of the probability mass, while the weak design receives very little.
A batch drawn from this sampler will contain strong candidates often because high utility points are sampled more frequently by construction.

The exact training rule can vary, but the central requirement is the same: better utility must translate into more proposal mass because the sampler is supposed to draw strong candidates more often than weak ones.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The utility signal can be noisy or sparse. If the observed utilities are too noisy, the sampler may overconcentrate on lucky candidates because it mistakes noise for genuine promise.
The sampler can collapse too early. If proposal mass narrows too quickly, the search may miss alternative good regions because exploration was cut off before enough evidence arrived.
Poor support means poor recovery. If the sampler never places enough probability near a useful region, later training cannot learn that region well because the optimizer does not gather data there.
Done right. With enough exploration and a sampler that can express feasible structure, the method produces strong batches because high-utility regions are sampled directly rather than searched for repeatedly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a hard design benchmark with discrete constraints and a batch budget, such as selecting molecular strings or combinatorial recipes. Compare a standard batch Bayesian optimization loop against the generative sampler approach under the same number of black-box evaluations.

The demo should report best utility found, average utility in each batch, and batch construction time. A convincing result shows stronger or comparable batch quality with easier large-batch handling because the sampler draws candidates directly from promising feasible regions.
