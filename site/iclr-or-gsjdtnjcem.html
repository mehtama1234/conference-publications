Modal Aphasia: When Multimodal Models Hide Concepts in Images

Unified multimodal models memorize concepts visually but cannot describe them in text, creating a hidden safety gap.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

A single model trained on images and text should understand concepts the same way in both modalities—images and words should carry the same meaning. But they do not. This paper shows that leading multimodal models can faithfully recreate iconic artwork or generate photo-realistic images from concepts they cannot name in words, and vice versa. This dissociation is not a training accident; it is a structural feature of how these models learn. The gap creates a serious problem: safeguards applied only to text leave unsafe concepts accessible through the image side.

Zero assumption version

Why modal misalignment happens

A multimodal model learns from both images and text at the same time. A reasonable person would expect this to make the model understand each concept the same way in both languages. After all, the model sees the word car paired with car images, the word sunset with sunset photos, and so on. But in practice, a single model can be exposed to the very same concept through two different channels—seeing it and reading about it—yet end up with two different internal representations, one trained on images and one trained on text.

A naive hope: if you train a model to align image and text in the same space, they will merge into one unified representation. In practice they do not. The image-trained pathway develops its own weights and shortcuts, and the text-trained pathway does the same. When you ask the model to generate text about an image, it can fail entirely to recognize concepts it can paint perfectly. Conversely, a model that refuses to generate unsafe images via text input may happily produce them if you ask it to 'transform this description into an image.' The safeguard only locked one door.

In one sentence. The model builds two separate internal memories—one for images, one for text—even when trained on both simultaneously, so knowing a concept in one modality does not mean the other modality knows it.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not propose a fix. Instead, it systematically reveals what current multimodal architectures actually do: they build separate internal memories (called representations) for images and text, even when trained together.

The hidden quantity

The dissociation: when one modality loses access to a concept

measured quantity  =  Cross-modal recognition gap

The paper measures a hidden quantity: the gap in accuracy when a model is asked to recognize or articulate the same concept in two different modalities. If a model achieves 95% accuracy recognizing a face in images but 10% accuracy when asked to identify the same face from text description, that gap is 85 percentage points. This gap is the dissociation—the proof that the model holds two separate internal models of 'face,' one for images and one for text.

The paper estimates this gap through controlled experiments on synthetic datasets (where ground truth is exact) and on iconic artwork (where human raters verify the model's output). High gaps reliably appear across all tested architectures, proving modal aphasia is not a quirk but a fundamental structural property.

Why it works

Why modalities drift apart during training

The root cause is optimization pressure. When you train a model on images, the image decoder (the part that outputs pictures) receives direct feedback: the loss function compares its output to the real image and tells it whether to turn pixels up or down. Every pixel error updates the image pathway. But the text pathway is trained separately: its losses come only from language-modeling objectives, like predicting the next word. Neither pathway has a reason to check whether the other modality truly understands the concept, because the loss functions never measure that.

Picture two students studying the same subject with different textbooks. The image textbook rewards visual pattern recognition—the model learns to paint faces, render outdoor scenes, and match shadows. The text textbook rewards word prediction—the model learns to recognize word co-occurrence, complete sentences, and follow grammar. They read at different speeds, in different orders, and their tests never overlap. Over time, each builds its own detailed internal model of the subject in the language of its textbook, but the two models may not translate. The model has memorized the visual essence of a face without knowing the word Marilyn or the description blonde hair blue eyes iconic. Conversely, it may know how to spell Oscar and describe his filmography but never see his actual face in the image pathway.

Measuring the dissociation

First in words:

gap = accuracy_when_concept_presented_in_modality_A - accuracy_when_same_concept_presented_in_modality_B

Then the compact form:

gap_concept_i = acc_image_i - acc_text_i

Every symbol:

gap_concept_ithe dissociation size for concept i (ranges from 0 to 100 percentage points)
acc_image_iaccuracy when asked to recognize or produce concept i given an image input
acc_text_iaccuracy when asked to recognize or produce concept i given a text input
A tiny worked example
Concept: Marilyn Monroe's face. Image input: 'Recognize this photo.' Model output: draws perfect portrait. Accuracy: 95%.
Text input: 'Describe this person.' Model output: Confused, generates generic text. Accuracy: 8%.
Gap: 95 - 8 = 87 percentage points. The model owns this concept in images but lost it in text.

A gap of zero means perfect alignment; large gaps (50+) are modal aphasia. The paper shows gaps routinely exceed 60 for unsafe concepts, meaning a text-only safeguard fails completely.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Safety regulations only cover text. A model trained to refuse harmful image generation via text prompts may happily produce unsafe images if the request comes through an image-to-image interface or via the image pathway, because the text safeguard never touched the image decoder.
Concept transfer fails. Knowledge learned in one modality does not automatically wire to the other. A model that knows a concept visually may not transfer that knowledge to text descriptions, even with paired training data.
Bias may hide in one modality. An image pathway may learn harmful stereotypes while the text pathway avoids them (or vice versa). Auditing only one modality misses the full risk.
Fixing requires joint training signals. Modal aphasia shrinks when the training loss explicitly penalizes cross-modal misalignment—i.e., when you teach the model that if it knows a concept in images, it must also know it in text. Simple joint training is not enough; the signals must measure understanding across both modalities.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small multimodal model on a dataset of object images paired with descriptions (a toy version of ImageNet with captions, or a synthetic set of 100 shapes and their color descriptions). Train it as normal. Then run two tests: (1) show it a shape image and ask for a text description; (2) show it the text description and ask it to generate the shape. Measure accuracy on both tasks. Modal aphasia predicts that one will dramatically outperform the other on the same concept, even though the model saw both paired training examples, because the image pathway and text pathway receive different loss signals during training and thus learn specialized representations that do not overlap.

dissociation_ratio = accuracy_modality_A / accuracy_modality_B (if less than 0.5, modal aphasia is present)

The demo must make it impossible to ignore: one modality knows the concept, the other does not, despite paired training.
