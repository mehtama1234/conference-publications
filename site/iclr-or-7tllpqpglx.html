Dual-VLM Framework for Visual Planning Rules

Two AI models work together: one learns to simulate actions in pictures, the other generates formal planning rules by checking if those simulations match.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Picture a robot looking at a maze and trying to figure out its rules from just one image. AI systems that understand pictures (called vision-language models) can see the maze layout well, but they struggle to write formal planning rules—the precise list of legal moves and what happens when you make them. Meanwhile, formal planning systems are great at finding the right sequence of moves, but they cannot look at a picture. This paper builds a system that combines both: it trains one AI to simulate what happens when an action occurs (is that move legal? does the robot reach the goal?), and another AI to write the planning rules by checking its predictions against the simulations.

Zero assumption version

Two tools that need each other

Formal planning is a way to solve complex, multi-step puzzles. You give a planner a description of the world (the rules, the initial positions, the goal), and it finds the sequence of moves to reach the goal. These planners are very reliable—if a solution exists, they find it. But they cannot look at pictures. They need rules written down in a specific language called PDDL: the grid world has ground and ice holes; moving down from position (3,2) changes your position to (4,2); stepping on ice is forbidden; the goal is to reach the treasure.

Vision-language models are AI systems trained to understand both pictures and language. They can look at a maze image and describe what they see. But they struggle to write the precise formal rules from that picture. Ask one to write a PDDL domain file—the complete rulebook for a puzzle—and it makes errors. A human expert can write the rules by hand, but that defeats the purpose. Or you can let the model interact with the environment over and over to learn the rules, but that is expensive and slow.

The core problem. AI systems good at seeing pictures struggle to write formal rules. Systems good at formal planning cannot see pictures. Neither tool works alone.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

One AI learns to simulate.

Train SimVLM to predict: given this image and this action, what happens next and does it break the rules?

Another AI writes the rules.

GenVLM looks at the image and writes PDDL: the grid is 5x5; moving left goes left unless you hit a wall; goal is the treasure cell.

Compare the rule predictions.

Run both the GenVLM's formal rules and SimVLM's simulation on the same random action sequences. Do the results match?

Find and fix the mismatches.

If GenVLM's rules say an action is legal but SimVLM says it is forbidden, GenVLM learns the mistake and rewrites that rule.

Repeat until alignment.

Keep refining the rules until the formal planner and the simulator agree on which actions work.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which AI writes the formal planning rules, and how. Instead of asking a single vision-language model (which struggles) or forcing a human to pre-write them, this paper automates the writing by having one AI generate candidate rules and a second AI check them. The checking AI (SimVLM) does not need to be perfect at writing rules—it only needs to be reliable at simulation, which is easier to learn. The writing AI (GenVLM) is free to generate rules and learn from mistakes when SimVLM's simulation disagrees.

the object it moves  =  PDDL domain rules
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: consistency gap

measured quantity  =  Execution-consistency gap

This is the mismatch between what the GenVLM's formal rules predict will happen and what the SimVLM actually simulates will happen when you execute the same action sequence. If both systems predict that moving left from (3,3) ends at (2,3) and the goal is reachable, the gap is zero. If GenVLM's rules say the move is legal but SimVLM's simulation says it hits an obstacle and fails, the gap is one action sequence. This is how the paper measures whether the written rules are correct: a rule is good if the formal planner (using GenVLM's rules) produces plans that SimVLM agrees are executable.

The paper estimates this gap by running hundreds of random action sequences through both systems and counting how many sequences GenVLM's PDDL environment can execute while SimVLM cannot, and vice versa. The metric is called Exploration Walk score (EW): how many walks (action sequences) do both systems agree are executable? A high EW score means the rules are faithful to the actual visual dynamics.

Why it works

Why this two-AI design fixes the problem

The core insight is division of labor. Writing formal rules from a picture is hard because a single model must simultaneously understand the spatial layout, remember all the rules, and write syntactically correct PDDL. Simulating action outcomes is easier because you only need to predict: does this one action violate a rule, yes or no? The paper trains SimVLM, a specialized model, to do the simulation job really well (95% accuracy on seen scenes, 82% on new visual styles). Then, it lets GenVLM (a larger, general-purpose model) write the rules without worrying about getting them perfect, because GenVLM will catch its own mistakes by comparing outputs to SimVLM's simulations.

Here is the feedback loop: GenVLM proposes PDDL rules. The formal planner tries to execute random action sequences using those rules. SimVLM independently simulates the same sequences. If the PDDL environment says an action is executable but SimVLM says it is not—mismatch found. GenVLM is told: your rule is wrong here, fix it. GenVLM rewrites. The loop repeats until agreement is high. By the end, the rules are faithful to the visual world because they have been tested thousands of times against the simulator.

Why does this work better than asking a single model to do both? Because simulation is grounded in observation—SimVLM learns by seeing: this pixel pattern means ice, so moving onto it fails. Rule writing is abstract—it requires memorizing syntax, logic, and edge cases all at once. By separating them, each model becomes specialist and reliable. The formal planner then uses the written rules to find optimal plans, which humans would have had to design by hand in the old approach.

Math after the picture

Counting agreement: the Exploration Walk score

First in words:

For each sequence of T actions, measure: (fraction of sequences executable in PDDL AND simulatable in SimVLM) / (total sequences tested). High score means agreement.

Then the compact form:

EW = (1/N) * sum_i ( E_fd,fp(q_i) AND E_sim(q_i) )

Every symbol:

EWExploration Walk score (0 to 1; 1 means perfect agreement)
Ntotal number of random action sequences tested
q_ithe i-th action sequence
E_fd,fp(q_i)1 if sequence q is executable in the PDDL environment, 0 if not
E_sim(q_i)1 if SimVLM predicts sequence q is executable, 0 if not
ANDboth conditions must be true to count toward the score
Tiny worked example
Test 6 random action sequences: go-left, go-down, go-right, go-left-again, go-down, go-left.
PDDL rules say: moves 1,2,3,5 are legal. Moves 4,6 hit walls, illegal.
SimVLM predicts: moves 1,2,3,5 work. Moves 4,6 hit walls, fail.
Perfect agreement: 4 out of 6 sequences both agree are executable.
EW = 4 / 6 = 0.67. If we tested 100 sequences and got 85 in agreement, EW = 0.85.

The score rises as GenVLM's rules align with SimVLM's simulations; at the end, high EW means the formal planner's rules match the visual world.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

SimVLM makes mistakes on rare scenarios. If the simulator misses a weird edge case (a ricochet, a diagonal move), it will teach GenVLM the wrong rule. The final PDDL will then make the same mistake. The paper shows SimVLM drops from 95% to 82% accuracy when visual styles change, so some rules may be fragile.
Circular feedback: wrong rules confuse the simulator. If GenVLM's rules are so wrong that the formal planner runs the action sequences in a broken environment, SimVLM might then see nonsensical execution traces and learn those as real patterns. The loop amplifies errors instead of fixing them.
Syntactic errors block the planner. If GenVLM writes grammatically invalid PDDL, the planner cannot even start. The prescreening step filters this, but it means GenVLM wasted effort on bad syntax.
Done right. Prescreening catches syntax errors. SimVLM's high accuracy on simulations means the feedback loop is mostly correct. GenVLM's refinement step fixes conceptual mismatches between its rules and reality.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo where you: (1) show an image of an unseen maze style (a new visual appearance) with the same rules as training; (2) have GenVLM write PDDL rules without human help; (3) run SimVLM on random action sequences from the image; (4) check if VLMFP's refinement loop produces rules that the planner can use to reliably solve the maze. This proves the method works because success requires two things: the written PDDL must be correct (GenVLM learned the actual rules, not memorized training appearances), and SimVLM must validate it (the rules are faithful to the visual world). Measure success as: the formal planner finds a valid path to the goal using the written PDDL, and SimVLM agrees that the planned path is actually executable in the visual world. Show step-by-step: Initial GenVLM rules (probably broken), then after 3 refinement rounds (mostly fixed), then final agreement (high EW score).

Success rate = (number of test instances where planner finds valid path AND SimVLM confirms path works) / (total test instances)

The demo should make it obvious that GenVLM's rules improve with each refinement round because SimVLM keeps catching errors and sending feedback, not because GenVLM suddenly learned the visual world—it learned by being told where it was wrong.
