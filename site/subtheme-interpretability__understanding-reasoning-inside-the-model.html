Understanding reasoning inside the model

When a model reasons through a problem step by step, what's actually happening inside? These papers decode the reasoning process and find where the model goes astray.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Some models are trained to reason step-by-step, generating intermediate thoughts before arriving at a final answer. This happens in chain-of-thought prompting, where a language model outputs working memory — 'Let me think about this...' — before answering. These papers ask: when a model does step-by-step reasoning, is it actually reasoning or just mimicking the style? Inside the model, do the intermediate steps represent genuine problem-solving, or are they cosmetic? And when reasoning fails, where does it break — at the setup, the arithmetic, the logic?

Understanding internal reasoning is hard because the steps the model computes might not match the text it outputs. The model might be doing correct reasoning but expressing it poorly, or it might be writing convincing-looking reasoning while the computation is actually guessing. Researchers measure whether intermediate states contain information needed for later steps. They intervene: corrupt an intermediate step and see if it breaks the final answer, proving that step was actually used. Some papers use mechanistic analysis to show that specific parts of the model are solving the sub-problem at each step. The goal is to build confidence that when a model reasons, it's actually thinking through the problem, not just pattern-matching.

How the work breaks down

When models reason step-by-step, is the reasoning real or just performance? Work splits into five directions: checking whether chains-of-thought survive editing, measuring what happens at each stage inside the model, testing whether the model is retrieval-shortcutting instead of reasoning, finding where reasoning actually breaks down, and detecting when reasoning is cosmetic cover for pattern-matching.

Intervention on reasoning chains

When you corrupt a step in a reasoning chain, does the model recover? Can you prove that each intermediate thought is actually used for the final answer? These papers ask whether intermediate steps matter or whether the model just fills them in as decoration.

The first paper (about reasoning LLMs and interventions on their chain-of-thought) modifies reasoning steps to see what breaks. Larger models recover better when disrupted early because they have more internal capacity to work around broken steps — they compensate and still get the answer right — but smaller models fail faster because they lack that extra capacity. A surprising finding: when you rephrase a step to remove expressions of doubt, performance drops drastically, even if the meaning is the same. This tells us the model relies on specific textual signals inside the reasoning, not just abstract content, and can be fragile when exact wording changes.

The second paper, 'Verifying Chain-of-Thought Reasoning via Its Computational Graph', builds a causal map of how intermediate outputs influence the final decision. By intentionally corrupting features at each step, the authors show which errors are actually causing wrong answers and which are just noise. This moves from merely detecting that reasoning is wrong to understanding the specific mechanism of failure, proving that targeted fixes can restore correctness. Both papers establish that chains-of-thought are not just window dressing — steps do matter and can be engineered.

What happens at each reasoning step

Reasoning happens in discrete steps, but each step has internal structure. What do those internal stages represent? Do early steps prime the model toward an answer, or do they genuinely process information? This group studies the hidden computation happening inside and between reasoning steps — the numerical operations that the model performs before writing each step.

The first paper, 'Dynamics Within Latent Chain-of-Thought: An Empirical Study of Causal Structure', discovers that latent reasoning steps behave like a staged pipeline where different parts activate at different moments. Early in the model's computation there is bias toward certain answers, but that bias is resolved later through real representational commitment — the model learns which facts are relevant and shifts its internal encoding. The paper shows these are not two separate things but one continuous process.

The second paper, 'How Far Ahead Do LLMs Plan?', reveals that when models reason, they mostly take small incremental steps. The model does not look very far ahead — it does not map out the full solution space upfront. Interestingly, you can recognize when a reasoning step is not needed without hurting performance, meaning the model sometimes adds steps for other reasons, not just problem-solving.

The third paper about the terrain of thoughts maps all intermediate states across many reasoning attempts to a visual space. By showing how these internal states differ between correct and incorrect answers, strong models and weak models, the researchers reveal that successful reasoning follows a particular pattern — high consistency and smooth progression from step to step. This exposes which models are actually thinking versus just going through the motions, and spots where reasoning patterns fail.

Reasoning versus retrieval shortcuts

A model can get answers right without reasoning at all if it simply retrieves memorized patterns. During training, models learn both real reasoning and shortcuts. When we train for step-by-step reasoning, are we forcing genuine problem-solving or just eliciting style mimicry?

The first paper, 'Reasoning or Retrieval?', observes that models trained on reasoning benchmarks can answer questions through retrieval alone — finding similar examples in training data. To force true reasoning, the researchers block retrieval during training. Models trained this way generalize better to new problem types because they actually learned to solve, not just to pattern-match. The method called FARL (Fact Alignment for Reasoning Learning) makes the distinction concrete: when retrieval is an option, models take it; when forced to reason, they solve differently and carry that solution strategy to unfamiliar problems.

The second paper, 'Theory-Grounded Evaluation of Human-Like Fallacy Patterns in LLM Reasoning', tests whether the reasoning that emerges is just pattern repetition or real thought with consistent logic. The paper finds that stronger models show more human-like fallacies — which is strange because better reasoning ability should avoid fallacies — and that premise order matters because the order in which facts are presented influences mistakes in ways that real reasoning should resist (since logic doesn't care about word order). This suggests that reasoning in large models mimics human thought but copies its biases too, hinting that what looks like reasoning sometimes is retrieval of implicit human patterns from training data.

Post-hoc reasoning and performance

Sometimes a model writes a reasoning chain after knowing the answer instead of reasoning toward it. Or the model computes an answer internally and then invents justification. How do you know if the written reasoning caused the answer or just accompanied it? This matters because the wrong diagnosis leads to building systems that perform well but fail for the wrong reasons.

The first paper, 'Measuring and Mitigating Post-Hoc Rationalization', identifies when a model is anchored by its initial guess, writing reasoning that justifies that guess instead of working through the problem fresh. The solution is to reverse the order: generate reasoning first without an answer, then derive the answer from reasoning. This method, called SSR, improves performance on benchmarks and works better on unfamiliar variations of problems because the reasoning is now generative instead of defensive.

The second paper, 'Reasoning Theater', shows that many intermediate reasoning steps are theater — they do not influence the final answer. By training a probe that predicts the answer very early in generation, the researchers can detect when the model has already decided and just needs to perform reasoning for appearance. Removing unnecessary reasoning steps cuts the amount of text by up to 80% while keeping accuracy, proving those steps were cosmetic. This splits models into two groups: those that genuinely reason and those that pretend.

The third paper, 'Think in Latent, Explain in Language', separates the computation from the narrative. The model solves problems internally without writing text, then generates an explanation afterward. This approach requires fewer output words and is more accurate because the reasoning is not constrained by language requirements, and the explanation is derived cleanly from solution rather than tangled with it.

Reasoning in specialized domains

Reasoning in mathematics, formal verification, cause-and-effect relationships, and visual reasoning each have unique structure and unique failure modes. These papers test reasoning beyond language in specialized contexts where correctness is objectively measurable.

The first paper, 'Can LLMs Reason Like Automated Theorem Provers?', benchmarks reasoning on formal verification of Rust code — a domain where right and wrong are unambiguous. Current language models fall far short of automated theorem provers at this task. They struggle when answers need to hold up under multiple steps and when problems have unfamiliar structure, revealing that their reasoning chains work only on familiar problem shapes.

The second paper, 'CausalXRL', applies reasoning to reinforcement learning by building causal graphs that explain strategy. The system learns what causes what in an RL agent's decisions and can inspect high-level strategies in games. This tests reasoning in a dynamic, interactive setting rather than static text.

The third paper, 'GaussTrace', uses reasoning to trace relationships among 3D graphics models, building provenance graphs. Models reason about which prior versions influenced new models without requiring access to training data — reasoning performed just by looking at models, not during their training.

The fourth paper, 'LLMs Process Lists With General Filter Heads', uncovers that transformers learn human-readable implementations of filtering — the operation 'keep items matching criterion X'. The implementation works across different data formats and languages, showing that reasoning about structured selection is a learned abstraction, not hardcoded.

The fifth paper, 'Latent Thinking Optimization', trains reward models that evaluate reasoning in hidden (internal, unwritten) space. The hidden reasoning contains signals that predict correctness without the model needing to articulate every step. This suggests the model learns to evaluate its own reasoning quality inside its mind, before deciding what to write.

The sixth paper, 'PreferThinker', applies reasoning to visual preference assessment — asking a model to reason about why one image is better than another, which matters because many real decisions need explanations not just scores. Using chain-of-thought plus reinforcement learning, the system produces structured justifications that align with human judgments of preference, so the reasoning is actually helpful to humans.

The seventh paper, 'VeriCoT', checks whether reasoning is internally consistent by applying logical rules. If a chain-of-thought obeys the rules of formal logic, it is more likely to be correct because reasoning that violates logic is reasoning that loses track of what it was solving. Self-reflection and preference fine-tuning based on consistency checks improves reasoning quality, showing that logical structure is a useful signal for reasoning success because reinforcing the patterns that follow logic teaches the model not to make reasoning errors.
