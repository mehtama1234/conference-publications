Learning Permutation from Structure Without Supervision

Gumbel-Sinkhorn with adaptive temperature control learns permutations more stably by adjusting confidence individually for each position instead of forcing all at once.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Imagine you have a pile of unsorted cards and want to arrange them by the pattern on their backs—without anyone telling you the correct order. Your brain doesn't sharpen every guess at the same pace; some positions feel obvious early, while others stay uncertain. This paper introduces a smarter way to learn such orderings using calculus on permutation matrices by letting each position warm up or freeze at its own pace instead of all at once.

Zero assumption version

Permutations are discrete, calculus is not

A permutation is a specific ordering—like arranging five cards in a row. Calculus works by nudging numbers smoothly in small steps. But permutations are all-or-nothing: either position one holds the leftmost card, or it does not. No in-between. So you cannot use gradient descent (the usual training method) directly on a true permutation.

A beginner might try making each position a smooth guess: 'position one is 70 percent likely to hold card A, 20 percent card B, 10 percent card C.' Then nudge these percentages smoothly until one grows close to 100 percent. But this creates a new problem: all positions sharpen their guesses at the same pace. Some positions know the right card early; forcing them to stay fuzzy while uncertain positions catch up wastes training time and creates contradictions—two positions both claim 95 percent confidence in the same card.

The problem in one sentence. Standard differentiable relaxations sharpen all position-guesses at the same speed, causing instability when positions have different amounts of uncertainty.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with soft guesses

Each position holds a probability for every card, fuzzy and uncertain.

Measure local confidence

For each position, check how peaked the probability is.

Adjust temperature locally

Turn up the heat on uncertain positions; freeze confident ones.

Take a gradient step

Nudge the probabilities to match the observed structure (sorted order, puzzle fit).

Repeat

Each iteration sharpens the most certain positions first; others catch up at their own pace.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard Gumbel-Sinkhorn uses one global temperature value (a control knob) that applies to every position equally. The paper replaces it with separate temperature values for each position that adapt based on how certain each position is. Certain positions use a lower temperature (which makes the probability distribution spiky), while uncertain positions use a higher temperature (keeping them fuzzy and exploratory).

the object it moves  =  per-position temperature control
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: entropy of each position's belief

measured quantity  =  assignment entropy (per position)

Entropy measures how spread-out a probability distribution is. If a position is 99 percent sure it holds card A and 1 percent on all others, the entropy is low (certain). If it is 20 percent on each of five cards, entropy is high (confused). The paper measures this entropy for each position's guess separately.

The algorithm uses these entropy values to decide whether to sharpen that position's guess (by lowering its temperature) or let it stay fuzzy (by keeping temperature high). High entropy positions get high temperature; low entropy positions get low temperature. This is the key steering mechanism.

Why it works

Why adaptive temperature improves stability

Imagine teaching a child to sort cards. If you say 'everyone must decide now,' a fast learner who knows card 3 is obviously wrong at position 1 has to pretend to be uncertain while slow thinkers catch up. This creates noise and contradictions: person A and person B both claim 90 percent confidence in different cards for the same position. But if fast learners can decide and step aside while slow thinkers keep thinking, the group organizes more cleanly.

Mathematically, the paper measures which positions are confident (low entropy in their probability distribution) and which are still undecided (high entropy). For confident positions, it applies a lower temperature to Gumbel-Sinkhorn. Lower temperature acts like turning down the heat on a simmer—the probability distribution becomes spiky, with one item dominating and others dropping away. For uncertain positions, it keeps the temperature high, which spreads the probability evenly across remaining alternatives, allowing the training to explore and shift them without forcing an early choice. This local, per-position adjustment prevents the artificial pressure that forces premature commitment and the contradictions that follow, because each position's sharpness depends on how much uncertainty it actually contains.

Math after the picture

Entropy-adaptive Gumbel-Sinkhorn

First in words:

The temperature for position i is base_temperature divided by (one plus the entropy of position i). The loss is the mismatch between the learned permutation and the observed structure (like the number of inversions in sorting, or the pixel overlap error in jigsaw).

Then the compact form:

T_i = base_temp / (1 + H_i) where H_i = -sum_j p_ij * log(p_ij) loss = structure_mismatch(learned_perm, ground_structure) + L2_penalty_on_weights

Every symbol:

T_ithe temperature for position i; lower T makes the distribution spikier
base_tempthe starting temperature; a hyperparameter you choose
H_ithe entropy of position i's probability distribution
p_ijthe probability that position i holds item j
structure_mismatcherror between the ordered output and the target (e.g. sorted array)
A tiny worked example
Position 1 has probabilities [0.5, 0.3, 0.2], entropy H_1 = 1.1 (fairly uncertain)
Position 2 has probabilities [0.95, 0.03, 0.02], entropy H_2 = 0.25 (confident)
Using base_temp = 1.0:
T_1 = 1.0 / (1 + 1.1) = 0.48 (moderate, allows exploration)
T_2 = 1.0 / (1 + 0.25) = 0.8 (higher, keeps it sharp)
Lower T_2 sharpens position 2 even more; higher T_1 lets position 1 keep exploring.

The entropy-based temperature acts as a brake: confident positions freeze early, uncertain ones keep moving.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Entropy is zero for already-discrete choices. If a position's guess is already a one-hot (100 percent on one item, zero on others), the entropy is zero and temperature explodes toward infinity. The algorithm needs a numerical floor: never let entropy drop below a tiny epsilon.
Misalignment between entropy and true confidence. Entropy is based on the current soft guess. Early in training, the model might have high entropy on an easy position (guessing wildly) and low entropy on a hard one (stuck on a wrong guess). Entropy alone cannot distinguish true confidence from confident wrongness. If wrong early choices harden too fast, the updates become weak and cannot correct that incorrect choice to point toward the right one.
Large permutations with many valid orderings. If the problem has multiple correct answers (several valid puzzle solutions), high entropy on some positions might be honest, not a sign of confusion. Adaptive temperature will push these toward forced commitment anyway.
Adaptive temperature works best when structure is clear. When the ground-truth structure is unambiguous (strict sorting, clear spatial fit), adaptive temperature shines. Each position's true answer becomes evident, entropy falls naturally, and temperature follows. The method exploits structure; it does not create it.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a permutation-learning task: sorting (reorder a shuffled list of integers) or jigsaw (rotate and position puzzle pieces to minimize gaps). Train two versions: one with fixed global temperature (the baseline) and one with entropy-adaptive temperature per position. Measure sorting accuracy and the number of contradictions (positions claiming the same item with high confidence). Run the training step-by-step and log the loss curve and final error. The adaptive version should converge faster because each position's temperature matches its own uncertainty—confident positions commit early, saving training steps—and it should achieve lower final error because positions are never forced to sharpen before they have enough information, reducing contradictory early choices that waste gradient steps fixing later.

final_error = number_of_incorrectly_positioned_items / total_items training_stability = variance_of_loss_across_steps

Adaptive temperature should produce a smoother loss curve and lower final error than fixed temperature, with the gap widening as problem size increases or structure ambiguity rises.
