Moving data across spaces and distributions

Sometimes data lives in different spaces (high-dimensional to low-dimensional, one distribution to another). How do you transport or transform it efficiently while preserving structure?

What this is

Imagine you train a model to recognize images from high-quality cameras, but then you need it to work on low-resolution phone photos. Or you train on photos of dogs in good lighting, but it needs to work on dark, blurry photos from security cameras. The data has shifted—it has moved to a different space. The model learned from high-quality photos, and the skills it learned do not transfer perfectly to blurry photos. Alternatively, you have raw data that lives in a complicated high-dimensional space (an image is millions of pixel values), but you want to learn relationships in a simpler lower-dimensional space where similar images are close together.

The research problem is that transforming or transporting data between these spaces while keeping structure intact is tricky. If you blindly compress a dataset from high to low dimensions, you might line up dissimilar examples near each other and miss clusters. If you adapt a model to handle lower-quality images, you need to know which features of the training data are important (the edges of objects) and which are accidents of the camera (the exact lighting). You also need to do this without labels: you usually do not have experts labeling every example from the new dataset.

Papers use several strategies. Some learn transformations that map between distributions while keeping distances between examples roughly the same—if two high-quality photos were similar, their low-quality versions should still be similar. Others use adversarial methods: train two networks in competition, where one network adapts the source data toward the target distribution and another network tries to detect whether examples come from source or target. If the adaptation is good enough to fool the detector, the transformation worked. Some papers use optimal transport, which borrows math from economics about moving goods with minimum shipping cost—applied here, it finds the cheapest way to move a probability distribution from one space to another. The connection is that these methods all preserve local structure: nearby points stay nearby after transformation.

How the work breaks down

The research splits naturally into four directions based on how researchers solve the core problem of moving data between spaces while preserving structure. The first direction focuses on scaling optimal transport algorithms to handle real-world data sizes. The second direction uses math designed for curved spaces (called Riemannian methods) to optimize on surfaces that bend instead of flat ones. The third direction tackles dimension reduction and distribution alignment—bringing data from one statistical regime to another. The fourth direction uses particle-based and flow-based methods to trace smooth paths through data space.

Scaling optimal transport to real-world sizes

Optimal transport is a mathematically beautiful way to move one probability distribution to another by finding the cheapest path, but it becomes prohibitively slow on large datasets. A hierarchical algorithm solves this by breaking the problem into smaller pieces—the method recursively divides images and point clouds into coarser sections, solves transport cheaply for each section, then combines the answers. For 1024-by-1024 images, this gives an 8.9-fold speedup while using 70% less memory, and for 3D point clouds with millions of points, it cuts memory by 83%.

Two other papers make optimal transport practical through different angles. One paper proves you can compute optimal transport on Riemannian manifolds (curved geometric spaces) without exponential slowdown in dimension—it approximates the optimal path with sub-exponential cost, which is a theoretical breakthrough that translates to real speedups. Another paper adds symmetry—when objects have rotational or reflective symmetries (like molecules or images), the method exploits these symmetries to skip redundant computation, giving speedups on synthetic data and real molecular graphs. A fourth contribution reformulates optimal transport as a pure functional problem: instead of moving points, you move the entire probability distribution itself at once. This avoids numerical instability (because the computation never rounds individual sample values) and works much faster than previous methods (since the entire distribution can be represented compactly), though it produces the same answer.

These papers tackle the same bottleneck from different angles: optimal transport is theoretically clean but computationally expensive, so each paper makes a different trade-off. Hierarchical methods sacrifice some precision for speed. Symmetry-aware methods pay off when your data has structure. Functional formulations avoid numerical trouble at the cost of a different perspective. Together they show that scaling is not one problem but several, each with a tailored fix.

Optimization on curved spaces

When your data or your problem lives on a curved surface (a sphere, a set of matrices, a set of rotations), standard optimization algorithms designed for flat spaces misbehave. Riemannian optimization instead follows the curves—it computes direction vectors (gradients—which tell you which way to step) that respect the shape of the space, so steps stay on the curved surface. The challenge is doing this efficiently and provably well.

The key contributions here are speed and theoretical guarantees. One paper shows that for Gaussian Variational Inference (a common statistics task), you can optimize on the curved space of Gaussian distributions without assuming smoothness—it proves convergence without that strong assumption. Another paper uses second-order methods (like Newton's method) on curved spaces for clustering: instead of taking tiny steps in one direction, you use curvature information to jump closer to the answer in one leap, and you do this so efficiently that it beats first-order methods by a large margin while keeping statistical accuracy. A third paper applies math for curved spaces to Gauss-Newton optimization for neural networks, proving that this approach avoids a common slowdown (from Gram-matrix conditioning) that makes flat-space optimization slow—it converges at a constant-factor rate independent of conditioning. A fourth extends dueling optimization (an algorithm that plays two networks against each other) to curved spaces, with full theoretical complexity bounds.

The unifying insight: when you follow the shape of your space instead of ignoring it, optimization becomes faster (because you take fewer larger steps in the right direction) and more stable (because you never violate the structure of your space). These papers differ in which curved spaces they target and which optimization algorithms they use, but they all prove that accounting for shape rather than ignoring it pays off in both theory and practice.

Dimension reduction and domain alignment

Sometimes you need to compress high-dimensional data to lower dimensions, or you need to adapt a model trained on one distribution to work on a different distribution. Both tasks are tricky because you can accidentally break structure—put dissimilar things near each other, or lose the patterns the model learned.

One paper proposes a flexible random-projection framework: instead of learning a single projection matrix, you train an ensemble of random projections and combine them wisely. It consistently matches or beats specialized dimension-reduction methods. Another tackles domain adaptation by weighting the alignment: it aligns two distributions using optimal transport but puts more weight on examples that matter most and less weight on noise and outliers—this elastic weighting works better than existing methods on real domain-adaptation benchmarks. A third paper also uses optimal transport but adds importance weighting—the weights make the method converge to a nearest-neighbor alignment strategy that handles distribution shifts smoothly. The fourth paper reformulates gradual domain adaptation as a transport problem: instead of jumping from source domain to target domain, you construct intermediate domains that form a smooth path, using a semi-dual optimal transport perspective that has good theoretical stability and generalization properties.

These papers share a philosophy: both dimension reduction and domain adaptation are really about moving data without breaking its structure. Random projections sample from the space of possible compressions. Optimal transport with noise-resistance or importance weighting gives you finer control over which parts of the distribution matter most. Semi-dual transport lets you build a continuous bridge. Each approach reveals a different facet of the same underlying challenge.

Particle flows and sampling paths

Sometimes the best way to move a distribution is to explicitly track particles (samples) flowing through space, or to find smooth direction fields that guide samples from one distribution to another. This approach connects to diffusion models and score-based methods—you learn which direction to step in at each point in space.

One paper bridges the gap between Annealed Langevin Dynamics (an MCMC method that works empirically but is hard to analyze) and theory: it proves that if you precondition (adjust) the dynamics correctly, you can guarantee dimension-uniform stability even when the score model is misspecified, which means you can get theoretical guarantees on a practical algorithm. Another paper uses particle-based flow matching for mean-field games in high dimensions: particles start at random locations, follow a learned flow field (so they drift toward good solutions), and the method converges to a stationary point with sublinear rates and faster rates under convexity—it handles non-convex games and high-dimensional transport. A third uses Gaussian particles that move in Bures-Wasserstein space (a special measurement for comparing probability distributions): they prove well-posedness and convergence (showing the method works correctly), demonstrating it outperforms gradient-based methods especially on non-log-concave targets (because Bures-Wasserstein space is designed to measure probability distributions accurately). A fourth applies Hamiltonian mechanics to optimal transport: instead of using standard gradient descent, you add momentum (following physics), which lets the system find better alignments with non-differentiable costs (because momentum helps escape local traps).

The connecting theme: particles or samples give you a concrete way to represent distributions, and learning a flow field (direction field they follow) gives you a differentiable way to transform distributions. These papers differ in the space they work in (flat Euclidean, Bures-Wasserstein, Hamiltonian) but they all treat the transformation as a dynamic process—a path through space rather than a static projection.
