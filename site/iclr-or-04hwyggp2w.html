ImageDoctor: Diagnosing Text-to-Image Quality via Grounded Reasoning

Instead of scoring images with one number, ImageDoctor gives four separate quality scores plus pixel maps showing exactly where the image went wrong.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

When you generate an image from text, how do you know if it is good? Today's models usually give one score: 0.7 or 0.8 or something. That single number hides what actually broke. ImageDoctor changes that by giving four scores at once (plausibility, semantic alignment, aesthetics, overall) and highlighting the exact pixels that misbehaved. When you use ImageDoctor to steer training, images get 10 percent better because the model can now target fixes to the exact regions and dimensions that failed, instead of blindly adjusting to a single opaque score.

Zero assumption version

One number cannot tell the whole story

When you want to train a text-to-image model to be better, you need a judge to rate the results because training needs a signal to know which direction to change the weights. That judge looks at a generated image and a prompt and asks: is this good? Right now, the judge gives back one score: a number between 0 and 1, because the goal is to distill a complex judgment into a single number for easy ranking.

A beginner tries to use that single score to steer training. But a score of 0.6 could mean the image is blurry, or it means the dog has three legs, or it means the colors are ugly, or all three at once. The model cannot tell which flaw to fix because the feedback is a black box. You get a low score, but you do not know what to change.

In one sentence. ImageDoctor replaces a single quality score with four separate scores plus pixel-level maps showing exactly which parts of the image misbehaved.

What the paper changes

The object it changes

ImageDoctor changes what the reward signal looks like. Instead of one scalar reward (a single number), it outputs four scores plus spatial heatmaps. This gives training a dense, interpretable signal: not just how good the image is, but which parts broke and why.

the object it moves  =  reward structure
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: flaw localization

measured quantity  =  flaw localization

Flaw localization is the spatial map of which image pixels disagreed with the prompt. It is hidden because you cannot see it in the final image; you have to infer it by comparing the image to what the text asked for. ImageDoctor estimates this by having a vision-language model (a model trained to read both images and text at once) look at the image and reason about which regions are implausible or misaligned.

The paper measures flaw localization as a heatmap: each pixel gets a strength score (0 to 1) indicating whether it is part of a flaw. The model generates this heatmap by first identifying regions that deviate from the prompt, then producing text explanations, then turning those explanations into numerical quality scores that correspond to pixel regions.

Why it works

Why changing the reward structure improves images

Dense, localized feedback trains faster and more accurately than a single global score because it tells training not just that something is wrong, but exactly where and how to fix it. Here is the causal chain: when training steers an image model, it needs to know not just how wrong the output was, but where the mistake is and what kind of mistake it is. A single score of 0.6 tells you nothing. Four scores (plausibility, alignment, aesthetics, overall) already narrow the problem: if alignment is 0.3 but the others are high, you know to fix semantic match. Adding a pixel-level heatmap narrows it further: now training can see which exact regions caused the low alignment score.

This matters because gradient-based training (the nudges that reshape the model weights) works best when the signal is specific and local. If you tell training the dog has three legs (a specific flaw at pixels X, Y, Z), training can directly modify the circuitry that generated pixels X, Y, Z. If you just say the image is bad, training has to guess. Specificity shortens training and reduces trial-and-error. The reasoning step (look, think, predict) helps because it forces the model to articulate what it saw before scoring, creating intermediate supervision that makes the final score more honest.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

The loss for training the image model incorporates four weighted quality scores plus a penalty for high-confidence flaw regions. The total loss combines the mismatch between the generated image and human preference, scaled by how much each dimension (plausibility, alignment, aesthetics, overall) deviates from ideal, plus a term that penalizes generating pixels in regions flagged by ImageDoctor as flawed.

Then the compact form:

loss = w_p * (1 - plausibility_score) + w_a * (1 - alignment_score) + w_e * (1 - aesthetics_score) + w_o * (1 - overall_score) + lambda * mean(heatmap)

Every symbol:

w_p, w_a, w_e, w_oweights controlling how much each dimension matters
plausibility_scorehow realistic the image looks (0 to 1)
alignment_scorehow well the image matches the text (0 to 1)
aesthetics_scorehow visually appealing the image is (0 to 1)
overall_scorecombined quality judgment (0 to 1)
heatmappixel-level flaw indicators (0 to 1 per pixel)
lambdahow strongly to steer toward no flaws
mean(heatmap)average flaw strength across all pixels
A tiny worked example
Suppose an image gets plausibility_score=0.8, alignment_score=0.4, aesthetics_score=0.7, overall_score=0.65.
Suppose w_p=1, w_a=2, w_e=1, w_o=1 (alignment weighted doubly), lambda=1, and mean(heatmap)=0.3.
loss = 1*(1-0.8) + 2*(1-0.4) + 1*(1-0.7) + 1*(1-0.65) + 1*0.3
= 1*0.2 + 2*0.6 + 1*0.3 + 1*0.35 + 0.3
= 0.2 + 1.2 + 0.3 + 0.35 + 0.3 = 2.35
Now suppose the next version improves alignment to 0.6: alignment_score=0.6, mean(heatmap)=0.2 (fewer flaws).
loss = 0.2 + 2*(1-0.6) + 0.3 + 0.35 + 0.2 = 0.2 + 0.8 + 0.3 + 0.35 + 0.2 = 1.85
Loss dropped from 2.35 to 1.85 because the model improved both alignment and flaw density. Training rewards both.

The loss combines four separate quality judgments plus pixel-level flaw pressure. Training reduces all of them together, steering images toward human preference across multiple dimensions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Vision-language model bias. If the underlying vision-language model has learned incorrect associations (e.g., it thinks all dogs must be brown), it will flag brown dogs incorrectly. The heatmap inherits the model's biases.
Reasoning step can be dishonest. If the reasoning text does not actually match the flaw regions, or if the model learns to generate plausible-sounding excuses rather than true analysis, the scores become meaningless. This can happen if the reasoning is not directly supervised against ground truth explanations.
Heatmap granularity mismatch. If the heatmap is computed at a coarse patch level but training updates happen at fine pixel resolution, the dense reward may not actually guide gradients to the right weights. The spatial alignment between heatmap and training updates must be precise.
Done right. ImageDoctor works when the vision-language backbone is strong and unbiased, the reasoning step is trained on real human explanations, and the heatmap resolution matches the training granularity. In that case, images improve steadily and human raters agree with the four scores because the dense reward signal aligns training incentives with what humans actually value across all four dimensions.
