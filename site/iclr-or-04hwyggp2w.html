ImageDoctor: Multi-Aspect Evaluation with Flaw Heatmaps

Instead of judging an image with a single score, ImageDoctor looks for problems in specific regions and explains what went wrong, then gives four separate quality ratings.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Text-to-image models generate pictures from written descriptions, but deciding whether a picture is good or bad has been a single-number problem: one score for the whole image. This makes it hard to give detailed feedback for training better models or to understand exactly what a picture got right or wrong. ImageDoctor changes this by having a vision-language model (a system trained to understand both images and text) examine an image like a doctor diagnosing a patient: it points to problem regions with colored heat-maps, explains its reasoning out loud, and delivers four separate quality scores instead of one.

Zero assumption version

Single Scores Lose the Details

Today, when you ask a trained model to judge a generated image, it usually returns one number: an overall quality score. A human looking at the same picture sees much more: does the object actually match what the prompt asked for? Do the colors look natural? Are there weird artifacts or broken parts? A single number throws away all that detail.

A beginner might try to make the model output multiple scores at once by just adding more output heads to the network. But this runs into a wall: the model does not learn to look carefully at specific problem regions first. It cannot point to where the misalignment or visual artifact actually sits in the image. Without localization, the training signal stays blurry, and the model never gets sharp.

In one sentence. ImageDoctor adds pixel-level flaw heatmaps and reasoning before scoring, so training gets fine-grained spatial feedback instead of a single blurry number.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Look

Vision-language model scans the image and predicts bounding boxes around regions that are misaligned or contain artifacts.

Think

Model generates natural-language reasoning about each flagged region and the overall image quality.

Predict

Model outputs four numeric scores: plausibility (does the scene make physical sense), semantic alignment (does it match the prompt), aesthetics (visual appeal), overall quality.

Heatmap

Lightweight decoder creates two colored heat-maps showing where artifacts and misalignments are strongest.

Dense reward

Both image-level scores and pixel-level heatmap signals feed back into training the generation model itself.

What the paper changes

What changes: from one number to four scores plus location maps

The core change is architectural and conceptual. Instead of a single output neuron predicting one quality score, ImageDoctor outputs four separate scores and two spatial heatmaps. Each heatmap is a pixel-by-pixel heat signature showing where in the image problems live. This gives downstream training a fundamentally richer signal: not just how bad things are, but where they are bad.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: region-level flaw signal

measured quantity  =  flaw intensity per region

The paper introduces a quantity that earlier methods never measured explicitly: the intensity of a flaw at each spatial location in the image. Before, the model either judged the whole image good or bad. Now it answers a finer question: which regions contain artifacts, and how severe is the misalignment in each region?

ImageDoctor estimates this by training a lightweight decoder (a small network attached to the main model's vision features) to predict bounding boxes where flaws live, then computing heat values at each pixel based on the model's reasoning about that region. The heatmap acts as a spatial mask: it is bright red in regions the model considers problematic and fades to cool blue in regions it considers clean.

Why it works

Why it works: structured diagnosis guides training more precisely

The core reason this works comes down to how neural networks learn. When you train a text-to-image model with only a single overall reward score, the gradient (the direction nudging weights to improve) points everywhere and nowhere at once: 'you generated a bad image' is too vague, because every pixel of the image contributes equally to the final number, so the weight updates spread their effort uniformly across all pixels regardless of where the actual problem sits. The model cannot tell whether to fix the left side, the right side, the colors, or the shapes.

ImageDoctor breaks this down into three steps that mirror how a human evaluates images. First, it pinpoints where problems actually sit (look): bounding boxes around artifact zones or misaligned objects. Second, it generates structured reasoning (think): natural language explaining why each region fails. Third, it produces final scores (predict): four separate metrics instead of one. This three-step chain forces the model to internalize where problems are before judging severity. When training the generation model, these pixel-level heatmaps act as a spatial mask over the loss function: 'improve quality in these red zones especially.' This is far more informative than a single feedback number because it concentrates training effort exactly where the model went wrong.

The paper adds a second layer: Dense Flow-reinforcement learning feeds both image-level scores and pixel-level heatmap signals back into training the generation model itself. The generation model receives two parallel signals: a global one saying 'this overall picture improved' and a spatial one saying 'these pixel locations got cleaner.' Because the heatmap shows exactly which regions are still flawed, the generation model can concentrate its weight updates there, rather than spreading updates uniformly across the entire output.

Math after the picture

The equation: multi-aspect loss with spatial weighting

First in words:

Total loss equals the sum of four aspect losses, each weighted by how much the model identified flaw intensity in that aspect's region, plus a penalty for redundant bounding boxes.

Then the compact form:

Loss = sum(aspect_losses) + lambda * sum(heatmap_intensity * region_importance) + unique_penalty(boxes)

Every symbol:

aspect_lossesfour separate losses for plausibility, alignment, aesthetics, overall score
heatmap_intensityhow red each pixel is (0 to 1, brighter = more problematic)
region_importance1 if the model flagged this region in its bounding boxes, 0 otherwise
lambdahow strongly to weight spatial loss versus global score loss
unique_penaltypenalty if two bounding boxes overlap too much
A tiny worked example
Imagine a generated image where the left side has an artifact (model's heatmap intensity there: 0.8) and the right side is well-aligned (intensity: 0.2).
The model predicted a bounding box around the left side artifact region.
Loss for that region = 0.8 * 1 (box was predicted there) = 0.8
Loss for the right side = 0.2 * 0 (no box predicted) = 0.0 (training does not penalize regions the model correctly did not flag).
Total spatial loss = 0.8; if lambda = 0.5, this contributes 0.4 to overall loss.
Compare to a single-score baseline where both sides contribute equally no matter where problems sit.
This spatial weighting means training focuses 80 percent effort on fixing the left artifact, 20 percent on fine-tuning alignment—a ratio that matches how often each region actually has problems.

The heatmap acts as a spotlight telling the optimizer where to concentrate gradient updates, turning a vague global signal into a sharp spatial one.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Heatmap overfits to training data. If the bounding box decoder is trained only on labeled examples of flaws, it may learn spurious patterns and fail to localize new kinds of problems it has never seen. Fix: train with diverse synthetic artifacts.
Bounding box overlap creates redundant signals. The model may predict overlapping boxes around the same flaw region twice, diluting the gradient because two boxes over the same region send redundant feedback instead of covering more ground. The unique penalty helps by blocking overlaps, but if tuned too strongly it can also prevent the model from correctly flagging two distinct problems that sit near each other. Requires careful tuning of the penalty weight.
Four-aspect design mixes subjective and objective criteria. Aesthetics judgment varies by person; plausibility is closer to objective fact. The model may learn to disagree with human aesthetics raters on edge cases, especially for stylized or art-heavy images.
Done right. Combine ImageDoctor with an ensemble of human raters during training, ensure bounding box diversity in the training set, and validate heatmap predictions on held-out test images before deploying as a dense reward signal for production models.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment where you generate 100 images from a text-to-image model using a diverse set of prompts. For each image, collect two pieces of feedback: (1) a single global quality score from a baseline model, and (2) four aspect scores plus heatmaps from ImageDoctor. Then fine-tune the generation model for one short training run using each reward signal separately. The demo must show that the generation model trained with ImageDoctor's spatial heatmaps produces images that human raters prefer over the model trained with the single-score baseline, and that the heatmaps correctly highlight regions humans also notice as problematic.

improvement = (accuracy_on_new_images_with_heatmap_training - accuracy_on_new_images_with_single_score_training) / accuracy_on_new_images_with_single_score_training

The metric must show at least 8 percent improvement and the heatmaps must show good spatial alignment with human error annotations on held-out test images.
