GradPCA: Leveraging NTK Alignment for Reliable Out-of-Distribution Detection

Detect when a classifier sees something from outside its training distribution by finding the low-rank structure in gradient directions, rather than guessing from activation confidence.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

When a trained classifier sees a photo it was never trained on, it usually still makes a guess—often confidently. GradPCA detects this mismatch by looking at how the network's gradient (the direction it would nudge its numbers to fit the image) relates to gradients from known images. Gradients from real training images cluster in a low-rank space (a flat subspace)—like pages in a filing cabinet—while images the model has never seen produce gradients that stick out in unexpected directions. This paper applies Principal Component Analysis (a technique to find the most important directions) to gradient class-means to reliably spot when an image comes from outside the training set.

Zero assumption version

Confidence is a false friend

A classifier trained on, say, dog photos will still guess confidently when shown a picture of a car it has never seen. The problem: we have no reliable way to detect that it is out of its depth. Simpler detectors look at the confidence score (the softmax score, a number from 0 to 1 that says how sure the model is)—but a clever image from outside the training set can still score high.

Earlier methods try to look at the raw activations (the intermediate numbers the model computes) or tweak the output to expose mismatches. But these approaches are fragile: their success depends heavily on tiny choices like whether the classifier was trained from scratch or fine-tuned from a pretrained model. There is no principled way to know when to use which detector. The core problem: no method has a solid theory for why it should work on one type of model and fail on another.

In one sentence. GradPCA detects images from outside the training set by checking whether their gradients lie in the low-rank subspace that real training images define.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compute class-mean gradients offline

For each class in the training set, compute the average gradient direction. Store these C averages.

Stack and compress

Arrange class-means as rows in a table, apply PCA to find the top k principal directions.

Project and measure

For a test image, compute its gradient, project it onto the top k directions, then reconstruct it.

Measure residual gap

The gap between original and reconstruction is small for training-set images, large for images from outside the set.

Apply threshold

If reconstruction error exceeds a learned threshold, flag the image as from outside the training set.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

GradPCA shifts detection from the obvious places (confidence scores, intermediate activations) to a less obvious one: the direction that gradients point. It exploits the fact that well-trained networks produce gradients that lie in a low-rank subspace—like a compressed filing cabinet—for images from the training set.

the object it moves  =  gradient directions
The hidden quantity

The low-rank structure of gradients

measured quantity  =  gradient subspace rank

Neural networks trained on in-distribution data produce gradients that cluster in a small subspace—a flat, compressed region of the full gradient space. This is the hidden quantity the paper measures: how many principal directions capture most of the variation in class-means' gradients. Out-of-distribution images produce gradients that point in new, unoccupied directions.

The paper estimates this by stacking gradients from each class, running PCA (Principal Component Analysis—a technique to find the most-important directions in a set of numbers), and counting how many of the top directions are needed to reconstruct each test gradient with small error. Reconstruction error becomes the detection signal: small error means in-distribution, large error means out-of-distribution.

Why it works

Why low-rank gradients detect out-of-distribution data

The chain of logic: A well-trained network on in-distribution data learns to map all images of that class to the same output, so it nudges its weights (via gradients) in consistent directions. These directions are correlated across images—they cluster. Out-of-distribution images are fundamentally different: the network never learned how to fit them, so its gradient direction is uncorrelated with the training set's directions.

Concretely, suppose you have 100 dog images and 100 cat images. For each class, you compute the average gradient direction. These 2 class-means cluster in a low-rank subspace (maybe only 3 principal directions capture 99% of the variation). Now a photograph of a bicycle arrives. Its gradient is computed—but it points sideways, in a direction the principal components never covered. PCA reconstruction fails: the residual is large. This gap is the signal that catches the outsider.

The paper shows this works more reliably than methods that watch activation intensities or confidence scores, because the gradient space directly encodes the network's learned decision boundary structure. It is where the network 'thinks,' not just where it scores confidently.

Math after the picture

The reconstruction-error formula

First in words:

reconstruction error = magnitude of (original gradient minus reconstructed gradient), compared to a learned threshold

Then the compact form:

error = || g - P P^T g || where g is the gradient vector, P is the matrix of top k principal components, and P P^T g is the projection-and-reconstruction

Every symbol:

gthe gradient vector (how to nudge the weights for this image)
Pmatrix of top k principal component directions
P^T gprojection: the part of g that lies in the subspace
P P^T greconstruction: what we recover from the projection
|| ... ||the magnitude (length) of the residual vector
thresholda learned boundary: error above it means out-of-distribution
A tiny worked example
Suppose the gradient is a list of numbers [8, 5, 0, 0, 0, ...] in a 1000-dimensional space.
The top k=3 principal directions capture most of the real training gradients.
When projected and reconstructed via the top 3 directions, we recover [8, 5, 0, 0, 0, ...]—nearly perfect; error ≈ 0.1.
Now an out-of-distribution image produces gradient [1, 1, 7, 7, 7, 6, 6, 5, ...] with signal in directions 3–8.
Projecting onto only the top 3 directions loses the 4–8 signal: reconstruction gives [1, 1, 0.5, 0, 0, ...]; error ≈ 8.5.
Threshold at error 2.0: the in-distribution example passes, the out-of-distribution example fails.

The error captures whether the gradient 'makes sense' in the context of what the network learned; in-distribution gradients fit the learned subspace, while out-of-distribution gradients do not.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Feature quality matters greatly. If you train a classifier from scratch (task-specific features), the gradient subspace might not be as stable. Pretrained models (general-purpose features) yield cleaner, more reliable subspaces because the model has already learned an organized internal structure from billions of images; that organization compresses how different patterns affect the weights, and carries over to the gradient space. The method works best with pretrained models.
Threshold tuning is critical. The threshold on reconstruction error must be learned from a separate validation set of known out-of-distribution data. If your validation set does not match the kinds of out-of-distribution data you will see in production, the method can fail.
Computational cost for very large gradients. Computing gradients for every test image and projecting them is more expensive than checking a confidence score. Scalability matters on large, real-time systems.
Done right. Use a pretrained backbone, compute class-mean gradients offline once, select top k components carefully (e.g., via cross-validation), and set the threshold on held-out out-of-distribution data that matches your deployment distribution. The method is reliable and theoretically grounded.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a simple classifier on CIFAR-10 (60,000 tiny images of cars, dogs, cats, etc.), compute its class-mean gradients offline, and run PCA to find the top 3 principal directions. Then: (1) show that reconstruction error is small on held-out in-distribution images, (2) show that reconstruction error is large on out-of-distribution images (e.g., ImageNet photos or random noise), (3) plot a histogram of errors with in-distribution on the left and out-of-distribution on the right, separated by a clear threshold, and (4) report the false-positive rate (classifying in-distribution as out) and false-negative rate (classifying out-of-distribution as in).

detection_accuracy = (num_correct_in_distribution + num_correct_out_of_distribution) / (num_total_test_images)

The demo should make unmistakable that reconstruction error—not confidence—reliably spots when the network has wandered beyond its training territory.
