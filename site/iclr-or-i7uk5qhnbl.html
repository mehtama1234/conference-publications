Tackling Heavy-Tailed Q-Value Bias in Offline-to-Online RL

Heavy-tailed noise in Q-values ruins offline-to-online RL, so wrap the bias in Laplace noise to flatten its outliers.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

When a robot learns offline from old data and then fine-tunes online with real experience, it guesses wrong about future rewards far too often. These wrong guesses have wild outliers (a few guesses are catastrophically wrong, most are just a bit off). This paper shows those outliers are the actual problem, not average error. LAROO solves it by wrapping the bias in a heavy-tailed noise model that absorbs those outliers and re-centers the guesses.

Zero assumption version

When offline robots go online, their reward guesses go haywire

A robot learning from old logged data builds a model to guess how much reward it will get for each action. If the data is limited or biased, the model's guess is often wrong. A beginner might just use the model as-is and hope the errors cancel out, but they do not: a few guesses are wildly off, pulling the learning astray.

Worse, when the robot then fine-tunes on real new experience, those extreme wrong guesses do not shrink gradually—they spike. The distribution of errors is not a smooth bell curve; it has fat tails (a handful of catastrophically wrong estimates). The robot trusts these outliers, picks bad actions based on phantom rewards, and learning stalls.

In one sentence. Heavy-tailed error spikes in Q-value guesses block offline-to-online robot learning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Detect the problem

During online fine-tuning, measure how far the Q-value error sits from its average—errors do not form a normal bell curve; they have extreme tails.

Model the tail

Use Laplace distribution (a noise model built for outliers) to capture the shape of those tails instead of pretending errors are well-behaved.

Absorb outliers into noise

Add Laplace-distributed noise to the Q-value guess; the noise learns its own shape, pulling outliers into the noise term rather than leaving them in the guess itself.

Re-center with ensemble

Take multiple models trained with the noise, average them, and nudge their mean error back toward zero so guesses are unbiased.

Train with stable guesses

With flattened errors and honest means, the robot learns steadily from new experience without chasing phantom rewards.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of using raw Q-value guesses during online learning, LAROO combines each guess with a learnable Laplace-distributed noise term. The noise model is trained to absorb the heavy-tailed shape of the bias, so the guess becomes more honest without throwing away information.

the object it moves  =  Q-value guess (with learned noise wrapper)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: tail weight

measured quantity  =  Heaviness of the error tail

A tail weight measures how likely extreme errors are. In a normal distribution (bell curve), extreme errors are vanishingly rare. In a heavy-tailed distribution, a small but real fraction of errors are enormous, pulling the average far from zero. Laplace distribution naturally has heavier tails than normal, so it fits data where outliers matter.

LAROO learns a scale value (how wide the Laplace noise spreads) that adapts to how heavy the tails actually are. The paper measures this by tracking how much the guesses deviate from the true reward during online play. The scale grows when tails are heavy, shrinks when tails are light, and the noise term absorbs the tail signature.

Plain reading. Imagine tossing a coin 100 times and counting heads. Normally you get about 50, with a spread around that. But suppose one coin is rigged to land heads almost always. That single outlier coin's behavior is heavy-tailed—the overall distribution does not match a clean bell curve because of that one outlier. LAROO detects when Q-value errors look like they came from a rigged-coin-like source and uses Laplace noise to say: okay, this error is real, but it is coming from an outlier source, so I will not bet my whole learning on it.
Paper reading. The paper shows that Q-value bias during offline-to-online fine-tuning follows a heavy-tailed measured distribution (measured from data). Laplace distribution is chosen because its probability density has heavier tails than Gaussian, letting it model data where extreme values are more common than a normal curve predicts. By learning the Laplace scale parameter (which LAROO calls the shape of the noise), the method transfers the tail structure from the Q-value bias term into an explicit noise model, reducing the effective tail weight in the guess itself and stabilizing training.
Why it works

Why it works: absorbing outliers into honest noise

The core insight is that outliers do not disappear if you ignore them; they just wreck the learning. LAROO's move is to say: accept that some guesses will be wrong; capture that wrongness in a separate noise model so the guess itself becomes trustworthy. Here is the causal chain.

First, during offline learning, the model builds Q-value guesses from biased data. Some guesses are close, some are off, and a tiny fraction are catastrophically off. Normally we would just accept this as a single error distribution and hope it averages to zero. But it does not average to zero because of the outliers.

LAROO instead says: the guess itself is one thing; the leftover chaos in the data is another. So LAROO adds a learned Laplace noise term to the guess. The Laplace distribution has a shape parameter (how wide its tails are); LAROO trains this parameter to match the actual shape of the errors. As Laplace learns to match the tail shape, it absorbs the outliers—the noise term takes responsibility for the extreme values, and the guess becomes closer to the mean.

Finally, LAROO re-centers the guess using an ensemble of models. It averages multiple trained models and nudges their mean error to zero. This double move (outlier absorption + ensemble re-centering) leaves the robot with Q-value guesses that are much more honest: they predict the mean reward without being dragged by phantom spikes. During online fine-tuning, learning becomes stable because the robot is not chasing extreme wrong guesses.

Math after the picture

One readable equation

First in words:

Corrected guess equals the original guess plus learnable Laplace noise, where the noise scale adapts to match the tail heaviness of the error.

Then the compact form:

Q_corrected = Q_original + Laplace(0, scale(b)) where b = learned scale parameter

Every symbol:

Q_correctedThe guess used for learning, after noise is applied.
Q_originalThe raw guess from the offline model.
Laplace(0, scale(b))Random noise drawn from Laplace distribution, centered at zero, with width controlled by parameter b.
bLearned scale (how wide the Laplace noise spreads); grows for heavy-tailed data.
scale(b)A function that converts b into an actual noise width; larger b means wider tails.
A tiny worked example
Suppose the raw Q-value guess is 10 (say, expected reward of 10 points). The true reward turns out to be 6, so the error is 4 points too high.
The Laplace noise model, trained on similar errors, has learned scale b = 2 (meaning outliers are moderately common).
One sample of Laplace(0, scale=2) might be +3 or -1. LAROO samples: say it gets -1.5.
Corrected guess = 10 + (-1.5) = 8.5. Original error was 4; corrected error is 2.5. The noise term absorbed half the outlier signal.
Ensemble averaging of many such corrected guesses, re-centered to zero mean, produces a final stable estimate the robot can trust for learning.

The Laplace noise acts as an outlier buffer: it lets extreme values be real (you do not throw away information) but absorbs their impact so the guess itself is an honest predictor of average reward.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Laplace scale learned wrong. If the Laplace noise scale b does not adapt to the true tail heaviness, either too narrow (outliers still wreck learning) or too wide (noise drowns out real signal), the benefit disappears. The paper relies on a good estimator of b; if that is wrong, the method fails quietly.
Ensemble too small. Re-centering to zero mean works only if the ensemble is large enough. A tiny ensemble has a biased sample mean; the re-centering nudge overshoots or undershoots. The paper uses a reasonably sized ensemble, but this is a tuning knob.
Offline data is already clean. If the offline data has no heavy-tailed errors to begin with (e.g., high-quality replay buffer), adding Laplace noise just adds unnecessary randomness. LAROO helps most when offline data is cheap or biased because cheap data has sparse coverage and biased data has systematic gaps, both creating outlier errors that Laplace noise can absorb.
Done right. When b is learned well, the ensemble is large, and the offline data does have outlier bias, LAROO stabilizes online learning and recovers performance that outliers were blocking.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple offline-to-online RL task: train a robot offline on logged data with some outlier bias (you can inject it synthetically), then fine-tune online on a real reward signal. Measure how much the robot improves during online learning because improvement shows that online fine-tuning is actually learning from new data, not stuck in phantom-reward-chasing. Run the task three ways: (1) naive offline-to-online, (2) with a Gaussian noise baseline (normal bell-curve noise), (3) with LAROO's Laplace noise. The demo should show that LAROO learns faster and reaches a higher final reward than both baselines because Laplace noise matches the heavy-tailed error shape better than Gaussian, so it absorbs outliers without drowning out real signal.

online_improvement = reward_after_finetuning minus reward_before_finetuning

The key proof: LAROO's Laplace noise recovers learning speed that naive methods or wrong noise models had blocked.
