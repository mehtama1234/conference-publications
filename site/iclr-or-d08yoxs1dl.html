SpikePingpong: Spike Vision-based Fast-Slow Pingpong Robot System

A robot plays table tennis by splitting the problem into a fast physics-based trajectory detector and a slower learned neural refinement, then planning strikes from demonstrations.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot has to hit a fast-moving ball at exactly the right spot and angle. Traditional approaches either rely on expensive high-speed cameras or train policies in simulation that break in the real world. SpikePingpong uses a two-stage perception system inspired by human thinking: a rapid, physics-based tracker that predicts where the ball will be, followed by a learned neural step that corrects the tracker's mistakes. For striking, it learns the right arm motions by watching human demonstrations.

Why fast robots miss the ball

A robot playing table tennis faces two hard problems: it must watch a ball bouncing across the table at 10 meters per second, predict exactly where it will be when the paddle reaches it, and then move the arm to strike it in just milliseconds. A naive robot might use classical physics (if the ball is moving downward at this speed, it will be here in 50 milliseconds), but physics alone cannot account for spin, air resistance, or imperfections in the initial measurement. Another approach is to train a neural network end-to-end in simulation, but when the robot runs the learned policy on a real table with a real ball, the policy fails because the simulation was not accurate enough.

Existing robotic table tennis systems solve this by using very expensive high-speed cameras, or by hand-engineering the control strategy, or by relying on perfect simulation. None of these is practical: the cameras cost tens of thousands of dollars, hand-engineering requires a roboticist for each task, and simulation almost always leaves the real robot stranded.

The paper's move in one sentence. Use a physics-based detector as the first quick step, let a learned neural network fix its mistakes as the second step, and learn striking motions directly from watching humans.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Detect the ball

RGB-D camera captures ball position and depth in under 1 millisecond.

Fast physics prediction

System 1 uses equations of motion (accounting for initial speed, spin, air resistance) to forecast where the ball will be at time T.

Learn from mistakes

System 2 collects training data showing how often System 1's prediction was wrong and learns a neural network to predict the error, then adds that correction to System 1's guess.

Plan the strike

Given the corrected hittable position and a target zone on the opponent's side, the IMPACT module (trained on human demonstrations) selects the exact arm joint angles to send the ball to the target.

Execute in real time

Arm moves to intercept the ball within the 50 millisecond window available from detection to impact.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of either trusting pure physics or trusting a learned model entirely, the paper integrates them sequentially: physics provides a fast first guess, and a learned neural network refines that guess. The two stages run in parallel hardware: System 1 (fast) runs on a fixed-function physics engine and standard camera, System 2 (slower) runs a small neural network that has time to correct before the strike must happen. This two-stage perception is combined with a third learned module (IMPACT) that chooses the striking strategy.

The hidden quantity

Hittable position deviation

measured quantity  =  How far the predicted strike location misses reality

The hidden quantity is the gap between where a physics-based model predicts the ball will be and where it actually is when the paddle can hit it. This gap includes all the physics the simple model missed: spin, air drag, vibration in the table, imperfections in the initial measurement. System 1 (the fast physics engine) makes a prediction, and that prediction has an error measured in centimeters. System 2 learns to estimate this error from data.

The paper estimates this error by collecting real-world data from an automated ball launcher firing balls with varying spin and speed, recording where the physics model said the ball would land versus where it actually ended up. A neural network trained on this dataset learns to map (current ball position, current velocity, trajectory history) to the prediction error. At runtime, System 2 adds its learned error correction to System 1's physics guess, reducing the final error from ~45 centimeters (System 1 alone) to ~12 centimeters (System 1 + System 2).

Why it works

Why a two-stage system works better than either alone

Physics-based prediction is fast and requires no training data, but it misses real-world effects (spin creates sideways forces, air resistance is not always linear, the table vibrates). A fully learned neural network could capture these effects but requires thousands of demonstrations and takes too long to run at millisecond scale.

The paper's insight is that the two stages have different computational budgets and different knowledge. System 1 has to run in under 1 millisecond (hard deadline), so it uses a simple physics model. System 2 has 30 to 50 milliseconds until impact, so it can afford to run a neural network on the trajectory history. System 1's prediction is almost always in the right ballpark (within 50 cm), so System 2 only has to learn small corrections, which is a much easier learning problem than predicting from scratch. The combination generalizes to new conditions: if the scene has a spin rate or ball wear not seen during training, System 1 still gives a reasonable first guess, and System 2's learned correction adapts to what it observes.

The correction equation

First in words:

The final hittable position is the physics prediction plus a neural network prediction of the error term.

Then the compact form:

predicted_strike = physics_model(ball_state) + neural_correction(trajectory_history)

Every symbol:

physics_model(ball_state)equations of motion applied to current position, velocity, and spin
ball_statemeasured position, velocity, and launch angle from the camera
trajectory_historyall observed positions of the ball back in time (used as input to the neural network)
neural_correction(trajectory_history)learned prediction of how much the physics model typically misses in this scenario
A tiny worked example
Suppose the physics model predicts the ball will be at position (50, 30, 20 cm) at impact time.
System 2 has learned that for this spin rate and table state, the physics model typically overshoots by 12 cm in the horizontal direction and undercounts vertical effect by 5 cm.
System 2 outputs correction vector (−12, +5, 0 cm).
Final predicted position: (50 − 12, 30 + 5, 20) = (38, 35, 20 cm).
Real ball lands at (37, 34, 19 cm), so the error is now ~2 cm instead of the original 14 cm.

The final position is not learned end-to-end; instead, the paper layers fast physics on top and learns only the gap, so the system inherits physics' speed and stability while gaining accuracy from learning.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

New spin rates confuse System 2. If the ball is spun in a way not seen during System 2 training, the neural correction may not adapt fast enough. Mitigation: train System 2 on a wide range of spin rates, or add online adaptation during real strikes.
Latency breaks the timing window. If the camera or neural network introduces too much delay, the corrected prediction arrives too late and the arm misses the 50 millisecond strike window. Mitigation: keep System 1 hardware fast and System 2 network small.
Poor initial ball detection fails the whole chain. If the camera misses the ball or gives a very noisy initial position, both System 1 and System 2 predictions start from a bad state and cannot recover. Mitigation: use a high-frame-rate camera and filter noisy measurements.
Done right. When trained on real human demonstrations and robot data, the full system (detection + System 1 + System 2 + IMPACT) hits the target zone in 47% of trials against new players with only 100 demonstrations, showing that the approach generalizes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show: (1) A physics-based baseline predicting ball landing position on a simple trajectory (spin-free). (2) Collect real trajectory data showing where physics is wrong. (3) Train a small neural network on the errors. (4) Run both the baseline and the corrected system on held-out trajectories and measure median absolute error (MAE) in centimeters. (5) Show that adding the neural correction cuts error by at least a factor of 2 or 3. Ideally use real video frames from a table-tennis setup; if not available, simulate trajectories with realistic air drag and spin.
