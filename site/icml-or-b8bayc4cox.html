Counterfactual Time Series Forecasting with Textual Conditions

Train a time series forecaster by pretending the future changes based on text descriptions of events, then measure how well it understands which parts of the world can actually shift.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting tries to predict what happens next based on what happened before. But in the real world, the future is shaped by upcoming events that don't exist in the historical data—like when a holiday will boost sales or a championship event will draw crowds. This paper introduces a way to teach a model to forecast by describing imagined alternative futures in plain text, then uses a measurement that distinguishes mutable from immutable parts to figure out which parts of the time series are actually changeable by events and which parts are fixed by history.

Why plain historical patterns are not enough

Every time series forecaster starts the same way: it looks at past data and tries to spot patterns. If sales spiked every Friday for the last year, predict another Friday spike. If temperature drops in winter, predict winter drops again.

But the real world changes because of events. A sports championship, an airline strike, a new competitor opening a store—these shift what the time series does, but they are not in the historical data. A model trained only on the past has no way to learn what happens when an unexpected event occurs.

Worse, even if we have a forecast that fits yesterday's facts, we cannot test whether it would adapt correctly to a counterfactual scenario—a world where tomorrow is actually the championship final instead of an ordinary day. We have no ground truth for that alternate world, so we cannot grade the model's answer.

The gap in one sentence. Forecasters need to learn how events change time series, but they train on historical data where events are already baked in—and we cannot test alternate scenarios because they never happened.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how a forecaster is trained. Instead of learning from pairs where the future is real (historical data paired with what actually happened), it trains on factual data first, then improves on pairs where the future is imagined (historical data paired with a made-up event description), because synthetic pairs teach the model to link specific text phrases to specific forecast shifts even though those exact scenarios never occurred in history. The key move is using text-attributed disentanglement: it teaches the model to split its understanding into two channels—one channel for what the time series naturally does (intrinsic), one for how events reshape it (extrinsic).

the object it moves  =  training pairs and loss function
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: mutability

measured quantity  =  how much each part responds to event text

The paper introduces DTTC (Disentangled Time Series and Text Consistency), a measurement that answers: when an event description changes, which parts of the time series actually shift and which stay fixed? A stock price might be immune to a celebrity's tweet (immutable) but highly sensitive to interest rate news (mutable). DTTC breaks this down explicitly.

DTTC works by training a separate encoder that tries to retrieve the original historical sequence from the forecast (intrinsic test) and to retrieve the original event description from the forecast (extrinsic test). High retrieval accuracy means those parts of the forecast are strongly carrying that signal. The model is graded on a scale of three candidate sequences: if it returns the correct history 100% of the time, intrinsic score is perfect; if it always returns the matching text, extrinsic score is perfect.

Why it works

Why it works: teaching through counterfactuals

The core insight is that a model cannot learn to handle events it never saw during training. But you can synthesize examples by taking real historical sequences and pairing them with fictional event descriptions. The model then tries to predict what the time series would look like under that alternate event. This forces it to learn the causal link: when text says championship, certain parts of sales (or attendance, or traffic) surge; when text says recession, they dip.

To prevent the model from cheating by simply memorizing the historical pattern and ignoring the text, DTTC disentangles two signals. The intrinsic branch measures whether the forecast still respects history (it should). The extrinsic branch measures whether the forecast responds to the text condition (it must). Training jointly on both branches ensures the model cannot ignore the event description and cannot forget the pattern.

Finally, diffusion-based denoising is the mechanism that generates flexible forecasts. Instead of predicting the future in one shot, the model starts with pure noise and iteratively removes it, guided by the text condition at each step. This gives the model many micro-decisions to adapt the forecast to the exact wording of the event, rather than a single deterministic choice.

The combined loss: balancing history and events

First in words:

total loss equals intrinsic consistency loss plus extrinsic consistency loss, weighted equally, minimized during factual training; then during counterfactual finetuning, the DTTC scores replace ground truth and guide optimization

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_I(phi) + L_E(phi), where L_I = -1/B * sum_i log(exp(<I_h[i], I_f[i]>) / sum_j exp(<I_h[i], I_f[j]>)), and L_E = -1/B * sum_i log(exp(<E_f[i], E_text[i]>) / sum_j exp(<E_f[i], E_text[j]>))

Every symbol:

L_Iintrinsic loss: how well forecast preserves historical pattern
L_Eextrinsic loss: how well forecast matches text event description
I_hencoder's extracted historical signal from the sequence
I_fencoder's extracted historical signal from the forecast
E_fencoder's extracted event-driven signal from the forecast
E_textencoder's extracted meaning from the text condition
phiall trainable weights in the model
Bbatch size, number of training examples
A tiny worked example
Suppose batch has 2 sequences. Intrinsic encoder scores how similar forecast is to original history on a 0–1 scale.
For sequence 1: forecast recovers the historical pattern well → I_h[1] and I_f[1] are aligned → score 0.9.
For sequence 2: forecast drifts from history → I_h[2] and I_f[2] are misaligned → score 0.4.
Intrinsic loss = -(log 0.9 + log 0.4) / 2 ≈ -((-0.105) + (-0.916)) / 2 ≈ 0.51.
Extrinsic: text encoder rates how well forecast matches the event description (e.g., text says championship, forecast peaks).
Sequence 1 extrinsic score 0.8, sequence 2 extrinsic score 0.6 → extrinsic loss ≈ 0.58.
Total loss = 0.51 + 0.58 = 1.09. Training minimizes this, balancing both objectives.

The intrinsic and extrinsic losses act as two judges: one checks that the model did not discard history, the other checks that it actually listened to the event description.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Synthetic pairing mismatch. If counterfactual scenarios are too unrealistic (pairing a summer historical sequence with a winter event description), the model learns spurious correlations. The text-series encoder must be trained on clean, coherent pairs.
Forgetting intrinsic patterns. If extrinsic weighting is too high during finetuning, the model may overfit to the text and lose the historical structure, predicting nonsensical values that ignore physical constraints.
Overstating mutability. DTTC retrieval can be tricked if the text encoder learns shortcuts (e.g., always predicting peak when it sees championship). The metric itself must be validated against held-out real scenarios.
Done right. Balance intrinsic and extrinsic losses equally, use realistic counterfactual samples, and validate DTTC on factual test sets where ground truth is known before applying it to unseen events.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic time series dataset (e.g., 100 past sequences of stock daily prices, each with high/low/close values over 30 days). Create 200 counterfactual variants by taking each sequence and pairing it with one of four event texts (recession, boom, earnings-beat, scandal). Train TADIFF on the factual data, then finetune on the counterfactual pairs. For each text event, measure how the predicted 10-day future shifts: do prices rise on boom, fall on recession? Also compute DTTC scores and verify that the intrinsic score stays high (because the forecast still respects the original historical pattern) while extrinsic score improves (because the forecast now responds to the text). Finally, test on a sequence the model never saw and a completely new event description, confirm the forecast adapts plausibly.

DTTC-I = retrieval accuracy of original history from forecast, DTTC-E = retrieval accuracy of original event text from forecast, mean absolute error in predicted values under counterfactual text

The moment that proves it works: the model forecasts lower prices when given recession-text on a boom-historical-sequence, showing it separates history from events and lets text override it.
