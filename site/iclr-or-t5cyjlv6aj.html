ExpGuard: Domain-Specific LLM Safety

A guardrail model trained on domain-specific harmful prompts catches specialized jargon dangers that generic models miss.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Banks, hospitals, and law firms use AI assistants with specialized vocabulary. But off-the-shelf guardrails are trained on general chats and conversations, not domain jargon. A doctor saying 'diuretic' or a lawyer saying 'novation' passes through as innocent text. ExpGuard is trained specifically on harmful prompts from finance, medicine, and law, teaching it to spot when those words are being used to ask for something dangerous.

Zero assumption version

Generic guardrails fail on specialized words

Today's AI safety systems learn from millions of everyday online conversations. A guardrail is a neural network trained to spot when a question is dangerous (asking for bomb instructions) or safe (asking for cake recipes).

But they break completely in specialized fields. In finance, a guardrail sees 'pump and dump' (a real investment crime) as three harmless words. In medicine, 'enema' is treated like normal speech. A lawyer asks about 'contract fraud' and the guardrail thinks it is a homework question. The specialized vocabulary of these fields is invisible to a model trained only on Reddit and Twitter.

A beginner might try to patch this by adding more conversations to the training data. But real harm in finance or medicine uses thousands of technical terms. You cannot collect dangerous prompts in every field — and you should not ask experts to write them by hand.

The core idea. Train a guardrail model on domain-specific harmful prompt examples (generated using the real vocabulary of that field) so it learns to spot danger when specialists use their own language.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Mine vocabulary

For each domain (finance, medical, law), pull technical terms from Wikipedia.

Step 2: Generate prompts

For each term, use GPT-4 to write a realistic prompt that asks for something harmful.

Step 3: Label with ensemble

Three different LLMs look at each prompt, reason through it, and vote on safe vs. unsafe.

Step 4: Expert validation

Humans with domain expertise review the hardest cases and correct errors.

Step 5: Train the guardrail

Feed this dataset to a small LLM; it learns to classify harmful vs. safe prompts in specialized language.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper changes the training dataset: instead of generic chats, it builds labeled examples of harmful and safe prompts in specialized fields. The guardrail model itself stays a standard classifier, but its training diet is now domain-aware.

the object it moves  =  training dataset for guardrails
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Domain-specificity of harm

measured quantity  =  domain-specificity score

The paper identifies something that generic datasets miss: harmful prompts in each field use vocabulary unique to that field. A prompt about 'pump and dump' is meaningless to someone without financial knowledge, but it signals fraud to a banker. The paper names this idea by collecting prompts that are harmful only when you know the domain's vocabulary.

The paper estimates domain-specificity by generating adversarial prompts using domain-specific terminology and measuring how much better a model trained on those prompts performs. It counts this in two ways: (1) how many domain-specific harmful prompts does the retrained guardrail catch, (2) how much do generic benchmarks still score, showing the method does not over-fit to one domain.

Why it works

Why learning domain vocabulary catches domain-specific harm

A guardrail is trained to take a prompt as input and output a score: this is either safe or unsafe. A model trained on generic text learns patterns like 'asking for a bomb recipe is bad' and 'asking for cake is good.' But it never sees 'pump and dump' paired with 'bad,' so it has no pattern to recognize.

When ExpGuard is trained on prompts containing domain vocabulary labeled as harmful or safe, the model builds new internal patterns. It learns 'when I see pump, dump, shares, market in the same prompt, flag it as risky.' It learns 'when I see diuretic, kidney, dose, overdose together, this is a medical danger signal.' These patterns are invisible to a generic guardrail.

The mechanism works because the model is learning to recognize that domain-specific word combinations have meaning only to someone in that field. The same words in isolation are harmless; together and in context, they point to real domain-specific crimes or harms. By training on examples, the model internalizes these combinations and can catch them in new prompts it has never seen before.

Math after the picture

The classification equation

First in words:

score = dot product of (prompt embeddings) and (learned domain-specific weight matrix), then threshold

Then the compact form:

p_unsafe = sigmoid( W_domain . encode(prompt) + b )

Every symbol:

encode(prompt)turn the text prompt into a list of numbers that capture its meaning
W_domainlearned weights tuned to recognize domain-specific harmful patterns in this field
ba small constant that shifts the score
sigmoida function (S-shaped curve) that converts the score into a probability between 0 and 1
p_unsafethe model's final confidence that this prompt is unsafe
A tiny worked example
Prompt 1: 'Explain how to pump and dump stock' encodes to [0.8, 0.6, 0.7, 0.5, ...] (domain vocab recognized).
Multiply by W_domain: 0.8*2.5 + 0.6*2.2 + 0.7*2.3 + ... = 6.2 (high score).
Sigmoid(6.2) ≈ 0.998 (99.8% confidence it is unsafe).
Prompt 2: 'What is pump and dump?' encodes to [0.2, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, ...] (neutral word order).
Multiply by W_domain: 0.2*2.5 + 0.2*2.2 + 0.1*2.3 + ... = 0.8 (low score).
Sigmoid(0.8) ≈ 0.69 (69% confidence it is unsafe). Same words, different order, lower risk.

The W_domain table learns which word combinations signal harm in that field. The sigmoid squashes the score into a probability; the model flags any score above 0.5 as unsafe.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Over-specialization. If the model trains only on finance harms and never sees medical ones, it will miss medical danger. The fix: train on all three domains together, and test generalization to unseen public benchmarks.
Terminology drift. New terms emerge in each field faster than a static dataset captures them, because criminals and fraudsters invent new schemes using fresh slang (e.g., new cryptocurrency scams with unique jargon emerge monthly). The fix: the paper describes how to retrain on new vocabulary, but a live system needs continuous updates.
Language beyond English. The current dataset is English only. Fraudsters in other languages use domain-specific jargon too, but the model has never learned those patterns. The paper acknowledges this and leaves multilingual extension as future work.
Ensemble voting catches labeling errors. Using three LLMs to label each prompt and checking their reasoning (chain-of-thought) catches cases where one model is wrong. Cohen's Kappa agreement is 0.89 for prompts and 0.98 for responses, showing three labelers almost always agree.
