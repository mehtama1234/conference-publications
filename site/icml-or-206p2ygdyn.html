Convergent Representation in Vision-Language Models

When image and text models learn together, their representations separate into different regions; the paper shows that aligning their shared underlying patterns matters far more than closing that gap.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Imagine teaching a model to understand both pictures and words. You want it to learn that a photo of a cat and the word 'cat' mean the same thing. A natural approach: train the model so that whenever it sees a picture, it represents it as a point in space close to where it represents the matching word. But something surprising happens: even after training, pictures and words live in completely different neighborhoods of that space. This phenomenon is called the modality gap. The paper investigates what causes this gap and whether it actually matters for how well the trained model works on new tasks.

Zero assumption version

Why pictures and words stay separated

In multimodal learning (learning from multiple types of data), the standard recipe is simple: represent each picture and its matching caption as points in the same space, then pull matching pairs close together and push non-matches far apart. This is called contrastive learning.

The puzzle: after months of training, this actually works—the model learns useful representations. But when you inspect the learned space, pictures cluster in one region and words cluster in a completely different region, even though they should be close. This separation is the modality gap. A beginner would guess this is a bug—that the learning failed. But the model still works well downstream, so what is going on?

In one sentence. The paper proves that pictures and words separate into different subspaces (dimension collapse), but their underlying patterns align in a shared lower-dimensional layer that dominates downstream performance.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes what the learning algorithm optimizes for. Standard contrastive learning tries to pull matching pairs close to shrink the distance between pictures and words. The new method (Shared Space Alignment) instead optimizes the alignment of the basis vectors—the fundamental directions—that picture and word representations use, even if their centers stay far apart.

the object it moves  =  the alignment target in training
The hidden quantity

The shared subspace alignment

measured quantity  =  alignment of shared basis vectors

When many data points are represented as high-dimensional numbers, they often do not actually use all those dimensions equally. Instead, most of the structure lives in a smaller subset of directions—like a cloud of points that mostly spreads along a few axes. This lower-dimensional subspace is the 'shared basis'.

The paper measures how well the basis vectors (the fundamental directions) used by image representations agree with those used by text representations. Even if the two modalities occupy different regions of space, if they use the same underlying axes, a model trained on one modality can transfer to the other.

Why it works

Why aligning the shared basis works

High-dimensional spaces are enormous, so data almost never spreads evenly in all directions. Instead, it clumps into lower-dimensional structures, like how a pile of leaves lies mostly on a table even though the table exists in a three-dimensional room. When image and text data are represented this way, each modality collapses into its own thin subspace—but these subspaces overlap.

A downstream model (say, a classifier trained on top of the representations) uses only the relevant directions. If the image representations and text representations agree on which directions are important, then a classifier trained on images will work on text, and vice versa. The actual distance between the two clusters matters far less than the alignment of their shared principal axes.

Standard contrastive learning tries to shrink that distance directly, which is hard—it pushes the model toward contradictory goals. The new method instead optimizes the alignment of the basis vectors. This is easier because it does not require the representations to move to the same location; it only requires them to 'point the same way' in their most informative directions.

Math after the picture

The alignment equation

First in words:

Loss equals: contrastive loss (pull matching pairs closer, push non-matches apart) plus a weight times the basis-mismatch penalty (measure how differently image and text use their principal directions).

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_contrastive + lambda * (1 - alignment(U_img, U_text))

Every symbol:

L_contrastivestandard contrastive loss pulling matching image-text pairs near and non-matches far
alignment(U_img, U_text)a score from 0 to 1 measuring how well the principal axes (eigenvectors) of image representations match text representations; 1 means perfect agreement
lambdaa weight controlling how much to favor basis alignment relative to standard contrastive learning
U_imgthe set of principal directions (eigenvectors) used by image representations
U_textthe set of principal directions (eigenvectors) used by text representations
A tiny worked example
Suppose image representations use principal directions [1, 0], [0, 1] with importance weights [8.0, 2.0].
Text representations use principal directions [1, 0], [0, 1] with importance weights [7.9, 2.1].
The basis vectors are identical, so alignment score = 1.0, penalty = 0.
Now suppose text instead uses directions [0.9, 0.1], [−0.1, 0.9] (rotated 6 degrees).
The dot product of first axes: [1,0] · [0.9,0.1] = 0.9, so alignment drops to 0.9, penalty = 0.1.
With lambda = 1, loss increases by 0.1, nudging training toward better alignment.

The equation does not require image and text to occupy the same region, only that their principal axes point the same direction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Basis estimation fails with too few samples. Computing reliable principal axes requires seeing many examples. On tiny datasets the principal directions are noisy, and the alignment score becomes meaningless.
High-dimensional noise swamps the signal. In extremely high-dimensional spaces (millions of dimensions), most of the space is noise. If the model has more adjustable numbers than training examples, the apparent alignment might be accidental.
Modality representations are not actually low-rank. If images and text genuinely need all their dimensions (no underlying subspace), then dimension collapse does not happen, and basis alignment becomes irrelevant.
Done right: cross-modal transfer. When two modalities are learned together with basis alignment, downstream models trained on one modality transfer naturally to the other, even though the raw representations live far apart.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small contrastive learner on paired image-caption data (e.g., 10,000 image-text pairs from a standard dataset). Train one version with standard contrastive loss only, and a second with the Shared Space Alignment loss. After training, measure: (1) the distance between image and text cluster centers (the modality gap), (2) the alignment of their principal axes (eigenvector dot products), (3) how well a classifier trained on image representations transfers to text data and vice versa. Plot alignment score and transfer accuracy together. The demo must make visible that high alignment predicts transfer even when the modality gap remains large.

transfer_accuracy = (accuracy_image_model_on_text + accuracy_text_model_on_image) / 2

The core finding: basis alignment, not gap closure, determines whether features learned from one modality work for the other.
