LLM Pretraining with Continuous Concepts

Mix concept predictions into token training to make the model learn faster and make its reasoning visible.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Language models today learn by predicting the next word (a token, which is a single word or subword unit), but word prediction alone is inefficient — the model has to guess which important ideas the sequence is about, hidden inside each word's internal numbers. This paper teaches the model to predict both words AND abstract concepts at the same time, interleaving them together in training. The concepts come from a separate learned tool that finds the key patterns in the model's internal numbers. Mixing these two signals means the model gets richer training information per example, so it learns faster and you can see what it is reasoning about.

Zero assumption version

Why word prediction alone is slow to learn

Today's large language models learn by looking at words one at a time and guessing the next one. A human reading "The chef prepared the salmon..." immediately thinks about both the words coming next ("on the grill") AND the abstract idea (cooking). But the model has to infer that idea from the raw word tokens alone. This is like learning to write a recipe while only reading individual ingredients — you miss the big picture of what you are making.

A beginner might try giving the model a separate signal that says 'this is about cooking' after each batch. But feeding random labels wastes training signal; the model learns to ignore side labels that do not quite match. What is needed is a signal that comes from inside the model's own learned patterns, not invented labels from outside.

The idea in one sentence. Train the model to predict both tokens and continuous concepts at once, with the concepts rooted in the model's actual learned patterns.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Train a sparse autoencoder

Learn a separate tool that finds which abstract patterns fire inside the model's hidden numbers.

Step 2: Set up dual prediction

For each token input, arrange the model to output both a next token and a next concept (a set of numbers representing abstract ideas).

Step 3: Interleave concepts into embeddings

Mix the predicted concept numbers directly into the same space where token embeddings live, so they influence what the model does next.

Step 4: End-to-end training

Use a combined loss that penalizes both wrong token predictions and wrong concept predictions, tuned together.

Step 5: Inspect and steer

After training, read out the concepts to see what the model is reasoning about, or edit them to test ideas.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a second prediction task: instead of just predicting the next word, the model now also predicts a continuous concept vector — a small bundle of numbers that encode abstract ideas learned from the model's internal patterns. This concept vector is computed from a pretrained sparse autoencoder (a tool that breaks down the model's hidden numbers into simple, interpretable patterns). The concept vector is then mixed directly into the embedding space (the list of number-lists that represents the sequence so far) where the next word will be generated.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: concept alignment

measured quantity  =  Concept alignment (how well predicted concepts match learned patterns)

The paper's key hidden quantity is concept alignment — a measure of how well the concepts the model predicts match the patterns the sparse autoencoder learned. When this alignment is high, the model is predicting concepts that truly capture what it is doing. When alignment is low, the predictions are guesses. The paper measures this by comparing the predicted concept vectors to the actual patterns the sparse autoencoder finds in the same sequence.

During training, the model learns to predict concepts that are both on-topic (relevant to the text) and well-aligned with the autoencoder's learned features. High alignment during training means the model's internal reasoning is being brought to the surface; you can then read and edit these concepts to steer the model's behavior.

Why it works

Why mixing concepts into training works

The core mechanism is constraint propagation: by forcing the model to predict concepts alongside tokens, you constrain the internal path it takes to produce predictions. Normally, a model can reach the correct next token through many different internal routes, including noisy or contradictory reasoning. When you add a concept prediction task, you force the model to use reasoning that the sparse autoencoder can decompose into interpretable patterns. This narrows the solution space to paths that are both accurate and transparent.

Interleaving the concepts into the embedding space (the list of numbers that represent the sequence so far) means every subsequent prediction is influenced by the explicit concept signal. This is more efficient than predicting concepts as a side task, because the model does not have to redundantly learn the concepts in parallel — they flow directly into the forward pass. The model learns faster because it gets richer training signal per example: both token-level and concept-level gradients (nudges) pushing the weights to improve, all at once.

The sparse autoencoder acts as a curriculum: it has already learned which patterns show up and matter in the model's hidden numbers, so the concept predictions start from a strong foundation. The model does not have to discover the structure from scratch; it inherits the structure from the autoencoder and refines it with gradient-based learning. This head start reduces the number of training examples needed: the model does not waste examples learning patterns the autoencoder already captured.

Math after the picture

The training equation

First in words:

Total loss equals token prediction error plus weighted concept prediction error

Then the compact form:

Loss = cross_entropy(pred_token, true_token) + alpha * MSE(pred_concept, true_concept)

Every symbol:

pred_tokenthe next token the model predicts (a distribution over all words)
true_tokenthe actual next token in the training data
cross_entropyerror penalty for wrong token prediction (standard in language models)
pred_conceptthe concept vector the model predicts (a list of pattern weights)
true_conceptthe concept vector from the sparse autoencoder (ground truth patterns)
MSEmean-squared error: average of squared differences between predicted and true
alphaa weight that balances how much the concept loss matters versus token loss
A tiny worked example
Suppose alpha = 0.1 (concepts matter 1/10 as much as tokens).
On one example, token loss = 2.5 (the model's word guess was decently wrong).
On the same example, concept loss = 0.2 (MSE between predicted and true concept vectors is small).
Scaled concept loss = 0.1 * 0.2 = 0.02 (small because alpha is small and concept was almost right).
Total loss = 2.5 + 0.02 = 2.52 (token loss dominates, but concept signal still pulled the gradients).
If the model had predicted the concept much worse (concept loss = 1.0), scaled loss = 0.1, pushing harder to fix concepts.
If alpha were 0.5 instead, concept signal would have weight equal to tokens, making the model care equally about both.

Both losses flow through the same network at training time, so gradients for good token predictions and good concept predictions push the weights together, not separately. This joint flow means the network cannot optimize tokens and concepts in isolation; it must find a solution that does both.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Sparse autoencoder trains poorly. If the pretrained sparse autoencoder does not learn real patterns from the model's hidden numbers, the concept predictions will be noisy. The model inherits the autoencoder's mistakes, so concept alignment collapses. Solution: validate the autoencoder on held-out data before using it for CoCoMix.
Concept loss swamps token loss. If alpha is too large, the model will optimize concepts at the expense of predicting actual tokens correctly. The resulting model will be interpretable but wrong. Solution: tune alpha on a validation set, typically keeping it much smaller than 1.
Concepts drift from meaning. Over training, the learned concept vectors can drift away from the patterns the sparse autoencoder defined, especially if the autoencoder itself is not continuously updated. The concepts become a different latent code, defeating interpretability. Solution: periodically re-extract ground-truth concepts from the autoencoder or use a moving-average update.
Done right. With a well-trained sparse autoencoder, balanced alpha, and periodic concept re-alignment, the model learns faster because it inherits discovered patterns instead of learning from scratch, performs better on reasoning tasks because the concept signal prevents shortcuts that bypass reasoning, and produces transparent concept predictions that can be inspected and edited to steer behavior because the concepts are explicit model outputs, not hidden weights.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model on a toy dataset (e.g., a collection of 10,000 sentences about recipes or animals) and train two versions: one with standard next-token prediction only, one with CoCoMix (token + concept prediction). The demo should show: (1) how many training examples each needs to reach the same final accuracy (CoCoMix should need fewer because the autoencoder provides a pattern blueprint), (2) the predicted concepts for a test sentence and whether they match interpretable patterns (read the concept vectors and confirm they align with real semantic ideas), (3) a comparison of performance on a reasoning task (e.g., 'given a recipe fragment, predict the cooking method') to show that concepts improve transfer (the interpretable reasoning path means the model generalizes better to new reasoning tasks outside the training data).

Sample efficiency = (examples_standard / examples_CoCoMix) — higher is better; reasoning accuracy = (correct_answers_reasoning_task / total_reasoning_examples); concept alignment = (overlap_between_predicted_and_autoencoder_concepts / max_possible_overlap)

The demo must make clear that mixing concepts does not just add a side signal. It makes the reasoning path visible because the concepts are explicit predictions you can inspect. It makes learning faster because inheriting patterns from the autoencoder skips the exploration phase. The two are inseparable: transparent reasoning and sample efficiency arise from the same mechanism (forced use of interpretable patterns).
