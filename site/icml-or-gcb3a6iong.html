The Signal is in the Steps: Local Scoring for Reasoning Data Selection

When choosing reasoning traces to teach a smaller model, score each step by whether it follows from its nearby context instead of scoring the whole trace as one block.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Reasoning distillation starts with a teacher model producing worked solutions and a smaller student model learning from them. The selection problem sounds simple: keep the solutions that the student already finds likely. But whole-solution likelihood mixes together many effects. A trace can look fluent overall while containing brittle steps that the student cannot reuse. This paper argues that transfer happens at the level of local reasoning moves: one algebra step after the previous line, one code edit after the current state, one factual conclusion drawn from a short chain of premises. LALP, short for Local Average Log Probability, measures that local fit directly. It scores each step using only a short prefix of earlier steps, then averages across the trace. That gives better data selection because it rewards traces whose individual moves are teachable, not traces that merely sound globally coherent.

Zero assumption version

Whole-trace scoring chooses the wrong reasoning data

A student model learns from selected teacher traces. In practice, you often have many candidate solutions for the same problem, produced by different teachers with different styles and trace lengths. You want the subset that will teach the student the most.

A common rule is to score each full solution by the student's own probability for the entire text. That favors traces that already match the student's habits. The trouble is that long traces from diverse teachers contain many locally useful moves inside globally unfamiliar formats. Full-trajectory scoring punishes those traces because one unusual opening or one stylistic detour can drag down the whole score, even when the core intermediate steps are exactly the pieces the student needs.

The method in one sentence. Replace whole-solution scoring with local step scoring, because the reusable supervision is in whether each step follows cleanly from nearby premises.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect candidate traces

For each task, gather multiple teacher solutions from one teacher or a mixed teacher pool.

Split into reasoning steps

Break each solution into ordered local moves such as equation updates, code edits, or short conclusion statements.

Score each step locally

Ask the student for the probability of the current step given only a short prefix of recent context, not the entire earlier trace.

Average over the trace

Combine the local step scores into one trace score so each part contributes directly.

Select data and fine-tune

Keep the traces with the best local scores, because those traces provide cleaner step-level supervision for the student.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the scoring target. Earlier selection methods judge an entire reasoning trajectory as a single object. LALP judges the sequence of local transitions inside that trajectory, because the student is trained one text piece at a time and reuses short reasoning moves across problems.

the object it moves  =  data selection score for reasoning traces
The hidden quantity

Local step justification

measured quantity  =  local step justification (how well one step follows from a short recent context)

The hidden quantity is not just whether the full answer sounds natural. It is whether each individual step is justified by the few steps right before it. That is the part likely to transfer, because a student later faces new problems where it must reuse the move in a different full solution.

LALP measures this with local log probability, where log probability means a score derived from the model's assigned probability but written on a compressed scale so many steps can be averaged cleanly. A higher local score means the student already has the ingredients to make that move after seeing nearby premises. A lower local score means the step depends on a long-range style pattern, a hidden shortcut, or an abrupt jump that the student does not yet support.

Why it works

Why local scoring picks better reasoning traces

Distillation succeeds when the student can internalize many small transitions and later recombine them. A full-trace score does not isolate those transitions, because it blends content quality with length, formatting, teacher style, and dependencies that span the whole answer. A single odd phrase near the start can lower the score for every later text piece in the trace.

Local scoring breaks that coupling. Each step is judged only against nearby premises, because that is the information a human or model usually needs to justify the next move. If a trace contains ten solid local steps and one weak jump, the average reflects that structure instead of throwing away the entire trace. This matters especially in mixed-teacher pools, because stylistic variation hurts whole-trajectory likelihood much more than it hurts local step validity.

The selection signal becomes sharper. Traces rise in rank when they contain consistently supported steps, and they fall when they rely on abrupt leaps. Fine-tuning on those traces works better because the student repeatedly sees transitions it can model with its current capacity, so the training updates reinforce reusable reasoning behavior rather than memorizing one teacher's long-form presentation.

Math after the picture

The local average score

First in words:

trace score equals the average, over all steps, of the student score for the current step given only a short recent context window

Then the compact form:

LALP(tau) = (1 / T) * sum_i log p_student(step_i | recent_context_i)

Every symbol:

tauone candidate reasoning trace
Tthe number of scored steps in that trace
step_ithe current reasoning step at position i
recent_context_ia short window of steps immediately before step i
p_studentthe student model's probability for that next step
logthe compressed score used so probabilities can be averaged stably
A tiny worked example
Suppose one trace has four scored steps.
The student assigns probabilities 0.8, 0.7, 0.2, and 0.75 when each step is judged from its short recent context.
Their log scores are about -0.22, -0.36, -1.61, and -0.29.
The average is (-0.22 - 0.36 - 1.61 - 0.29) / 4 = -0.62.
A second trace has probabilities 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, and 0.6, which gives an average log score of about -0.51.
The second trace ranks higher, because every step is consistently teachable even though it never has a single very high score.

Averaging local scores rewards consistency across steps, which is why LALP prefers traces the student can learn from piece by piece.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The step segmentation is bad. If the trace is split at the wrong boundaries, a single reasoning move is broken into fragments. The local score then becomes noisy because the current fragment no longer has a self-contained premise and conclusion.
The window is too short. Some legitimate steps depend on information slightly farther back. If the local window cuts off that support, the method under-scores valid traces because the needed premise is hidden from the scorer.
The student is too weak to score anything sensibly. Early in training, the student may assign low probabilities to nearly all good steps. Selection quality then drops because the score reflects raw undertraining more than step usefulness.
Done right. Use a step format that matches the task, choose a window that covers the immediate premises, and rescore with a student checkpoint that already has basic task competence.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small math or code distillation setup with one student and a pool of candidate teacher traces. Mark step boundaries explicitly. Implement two selectors: whole-trace average log probability and LALP with a short context window. Fine-tune identical student copies on the top-ranked traces from each selector.

Then inspect both training sets. Count how many selected traces contain locally valid intermediate moves that survive the ranking. Measure downstream task accuracy and also measure how often the student reproduces intermediate steps correctly on held-out problems.

step_transfer = correctly reproduced intermediate steps / total evaluated intermediate steps

The demo should show that the local selector wins because it preserves teachable step transitions even when the full trace style varies.
