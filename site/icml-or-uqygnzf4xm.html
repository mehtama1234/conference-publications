Performative Learning Theory

Predictions that change the world they forecast need a new kind of learning theory.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

When an ML model makes a high-stakes prediction, the world reacts. A job center uses predictions to decide who gets training programs. The programs actually help—the people who were flagged high-risk and got trained do find jobs faster because training improved their job-search skills. A routing app's predictions jam certain roads, which then changes future travel times. Classical learning theory assumes the data never changes. This paper asks: how can a model learn and generalize when its predictions physically alter both the training data and the test population?

Zero assumption version

The world fights back against predictions

A naive approach: train a model on historical data, then deploy it. But real-world predictions have causal power. A job-center ML model that flags someone as high-risk causes them to get assigned to a training program. That training program works because it teaches people better job-search skills and networking. So the prediction directly changed the outcome by triggering an intervention. Or a routing app's predictions jam certain roads, which then makes those routes slower for the next person. The classical textbook setup assumes the training data and test data come from fixed, unchanging facts. It does not account for predictions actively changing those facts.

Here is the bind: the model learns from data shaped by its own earlier predictions, and then it deploys to a population that will also react to what it says. The training data is warped by predictions. The test data is warped by predictions. The gap between them is warped by predictions. Traditional learning theory offers no guidance on what error bounds hold when your own predictions reshape the ground truth you are trying to learn from.

The central puzzle. How can a model generalize from training data to test data when the model's predictions causally change both datasets?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Set up the problem formally

Define a model, a loss function (a measure of error), and a causal rule for how predictions change who is in the population.

Identify four scenarios

Sample-only performativity, population-only, both, or neither—each creates a different kind of generalization gap.

Bound each gap

Prove upper limits on how far apart the training error and test error can drift under each scenario, using Wasserstein distance (a measure of how different two datasets are).

Uncover the trade-off

Show that stronger performative effects mean bigger changes to datasets because predictions push the population further from baseline, so the gap between training and test data grows.

Prove retraining helps

If you retrain the model on newly sampled data, you can sometimes shrink the generalization gap, even on distorted data.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves the generalization bound—the math that tells you how far apart training and test errors can drift. Classical theory bounds this gap assuming frozen data. This paper replaces that with a bound that accounts for performative shifts: it measures how far the training dataset drifts from the test dataset because both have been causally warped by predictions.

the object it moves  =  Generalization bounds under performative shifts
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: distribution drift under causality

measured quantity  =  Wasserstein distance between shifted distributions

The paper measures performativity using Wasserstein distance, a concrete number that measures how different two datasets are. Think of it as: if you had to slowly morph one dataset into the other, what is the total distance you would need to move all the data points? Large distance means large performative effect.

The paper estimates this by first computing the distribution of outcomes under no predictions (the baseline population), then computing the distribution after predictions are deployed and everyone reacts (the shifted population), then measuring how far apart they are in Wasserstein space. The key move is that the paper uses this distance to bound how much error a model trained on one will have when tested on the other.

Why it works

Why it works: measuring and bounding causal drift

The core mechanism is brutally simple: performativity is a causal force that moves the data distribution. If the model says someone is high-risk, they get treated differently, and their outcome changes. That changes the distribution. The paper asks: by how much? And: how does that shift break generalization?

The model is trained on a shifted distribution (because the earlier predictions that generated the training data caused that shift). It is then tested on a different shifted distribution (because the new population also reacts to predictions, but maybe differently—the job center assigns training programs, but the test population does not, or does so differently). The paper bounds the generalization gap by measuring the Wasserstein distance between these two shifted distributions using the model's influence on outcomes (how strongly predictions move the population).

The brilliant insight: you can improve this bound by retraining on newly sampled data. Why? Because each retraining step gives you a fresh sample drawn from the current shifted distribution. You are essentially building a chain of samples, each closer to an equilibrium where predictions and outcomes have stabilized. The bound shrinks because the model becomes less surprised by performative effects it has already seen.

Math after the picture

The equation for generalization under performativity

First in words:

The generalization gap under performative effects is bounded by the Wasserstein distance between the shifted training distribution and the shifted test distribution, scaled by how sensitive the loss is to changes in the data.

Then the compact form:

generalization_gap <= lambda * W_p(d_train_shifted, d_test_shifted) + sqrt(1/m)

Every symbol:

generalization_gaphow much worse the model is on test data than on training data
lambdahow sensitive the loss function is to small changes in the data (Lipschitz constant)
W_pWasserstein distance: the cost to morph one distribution into the other
d_train_shiftedtraining distribution after performative shifts have happened
d_test_shiftedtest distribution after its own performative shifts
mnumber of training examples
A tiny worked example
Suppose training data has 100 job seekers, test population has 1000. Lipschitz constant lambda = 2.0 (moderate sensitivity).
Wasserstein distance between train and test distributions: 0.3 (the populations are fairly different due to performative shifts).
generalization_gap <= 2.0 * 0.3 + sqrt(1/100) = 0.6 + 0.1 = 0.7
Meaning: if the model is 95% accurate on training data, expect roughly 95% - 70% = 25% accuracy on test data in worst case.
If you retrain and shrink the Wasserstein distance to 0.15, the bound drops to 2.0 * 0.15 + 0.1 = 0.40, raising expected test accuracy to 95% - 40% = 55%.

The equation splits the problem into two parts: how different the distributions are (Wasserstein distance), and how much that difference matters for your loss function (Lipschitz constant).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unknown causal rule. The theory assumes you know how predictions cause the population to react (the performative rule). If that rule is hidden or changes over time, the bounds no longer apply.
Performativity is nonlinear. The Wasserstein distance scales linearly with the Lipschitz constant. If the loss function is very sensitive (large lambda), or if performative effects are explosive rather than smooth, the bounds become loose and useless.
Retraining costs explode. Each retraining cycle requires new data drawn from the shifted population. If sampling is expensive—waiting weeks for outcomes to materialize, or if drawing fresh samples is hard to do—you cannot afford many iterations to shrink the bounds.
Done right. Use the theory to reason about trade-offs: a performative system that gently influences outcomes has a small Lipschitz constant, so the generalization bound is tighter. Systems with explosive performative effects have large Lipschitz constants and wide gaps. Design data collection to retrain frequently on fresh samples drawn from the current shifted population.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic job-market simulator: create an artificial population with baseline unemployment risk, then train a logistic regression model to predict risk. Deploy the model with a performative rule: anyone flagged high-risk receives a training intervention. The intervention works—it lifts their job-finding odds by 20 percentage points on average. Simulate multiple retraining cycles. In each cycle, collect new outcomes from the shifted population and retrain the model. Plot three curves: the model accuracy on training data, the generalization gap predicted by the classical theory which ignores that predictions change the data, and the generalization gap predicted by this paper's theory which accounts for performative shifts. The demo proves: classical theory's bound is wildly loose, while this paper's bound tightens with each retraining cycle as you sample from a more-and-more stabilized distribution.

generalization_gap = training_accuracy - test_accuracy; plot it against the Wasserstein distance between train and test distributions and against number of retraining cycles

The demo must make impossible to miss: when the world reacts to your predictions, classical generalization bounds are lying to you. Accounting for performativity and retraining shrinks the lie.
