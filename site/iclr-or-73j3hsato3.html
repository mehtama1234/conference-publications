Social Agents: Collective Intelligence Improves LLM Predictions

Ask many versions of an LLM with different personalities to evaluate something, then average their judgments instead of trusting a single answer.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When a crowd votes on something, their average guess is often more accurate than any single expert, because individual biases cancel out and the shared truth emerges. This paper applies that principle to language models: instead of asking an LLM once, we create many LLM-powered personas with different backgrounds, values, and interests. Each one independently evaluates something (an ad, a video, a webpage). Their combined answer is more reliable because the personas' different biases offset each other, leaving the genuine signal.

Zero assumption version

Single answers lose information about real human diversity

Today's language models give one answer when asked to predict whether someone will like an ad or click on a video. But in the real world, people are different. Some prefer bold designs, others like subtle ones. Some care about price, others care about sustainability. That single LLM answer averages out all these viewpoints into one number, discarding the fact that different kinds of people will respond differently.

A beginner might think: just ask the LLM multiple times and average the answers. But that fails because the same model will give nearly the same answer every time. You need genuinely different perspectives, not just noise. The LLM needs to roleplay as a 25-year-old artist, then as a 65-year-old engineer, then as a parent in rural Ohio. Without that guidance, you just get the same bias repeated.

The core idea: Create different personas (different ages, values, backgrounds) inside the LLM, ask each one to evaluate something independently, then combine their answers the way crowds do it.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define the personas

Specify a dozen or so detailed personas with age, gender, values, job, interests.

Send to the LLM

For each persona, tell the model: You are X years old, work in Y, you care about Z. Now evaluate this ad/video/webpage.

Collect opinions

The LLM produces a score (0-100 likelihood) and a rationale from that persona's viewpoint.

Aggregate the answers

Take the mean score across all personas, or check what fraction agreed.

Compare to ground truth

Check the aggregated prediction against real user behavior and human ratings.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of running the LLM once with a generic prompt, the paper runs it many times, each with a different persona instruction. The object it moves is the prompt context: from a neutral "evaluate this" to "you are a 28-year-old sustainability-focused parent; evaluate this."

the object it moves  =  Prompt persona context
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Demographic and psychographic diversity captured

measured quantity  =  Persona diversity coverage

The paper measures something new: how much the LLM captures the real spread of human opinion when it acts as different personas. In the single-answer baseline, this diversity is invisible. In Social Agents, it becomes explicit: we can see how a 22-year-old video gamer rates an ad (high on humor, low on trust), versus how a 67-year-old financial advisor rates it (low on humor, high on trust). The paper does not name this quantity by a symbol, but it matters because the breadth of persona coverage determines whether the aggregate captures real human variation.

The paper estimates this by comparing each persona's scores to human ratings from people matching that demographic. When personas align with real humans (reaching correlations up to 0.71), the aggregated prediction is reliable because the crowd genuinely represents different viewpoints.

Why it works

Why combining many viewpoints beats a single answer

A single LLM answer is an average of all opinions baked into its training and weights. When asked "do you like this ad?", the model produces a prediction that leans toward the population average, because that is what minimizes training loss. But real people are not an average: a teenager and a retiree will react very differently, and their true range of reactions is lost in that one number.

When you explicitly ask the LLM to adopt a persona (age 22, video-gamer, values humor), the model shifts its reasoning. It does not reason as an average anymore; it simulates what that specific kind of person would think. Because the model was trained on text from millions of humans, it has learned patterns about what different demographics care about. Telling it a specific demographic makes those patterns activate.

The crowd effect works because these persona-induced shifts are different and somewhat independent (a young gamer and an elderly accountant reason about an ad in genuinely orthogonal ways), and they overlap imperfectly with the true audience. When you average many overlapping but different guesses, biases cancel. A bias toward high ratings in one persona is balanced by a bias toward low ratings in another. The average of six different-biased guesses is less biased than one guess, because variation is noise and the truth is the common ground.

Aggregating independent judgments

First in words:

For each stimulus, compute the score from each of K personas, then take their average: the crowd score is the sum of all persona scores divided by the number of personas.

Then the compact form:

crowd_score = (1/K) * sum_{i=1}^{K} score_i

Every symbol:

crowd_scorethe final prediction (0 to 100)
Kthe number of personas (e.g. 12)
score_ithe score given by persona i (0 to 100)
A tiny worked example
Stimulus: a video ad for a sports car.
Persona 1 (age 22, car enthusiast): score = 85.
Persona 2 (age 65, frugality-focused): score = 12.
Persona 3 (age 40, sustainability-focused): score = 18.
Crowd score = (85 + 12 + 18) / 3 = 115 / 3 = 38.3.
Compare: single generic LLM said 45. Real user average was 42. The crowd guess (38.3) is closer than the single answer.

This simple average works because each persona's bias (high or low relative to truth) is independent; the biases cancel, leaving the true signal.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Personas that do not match the real audience. If you create personas (e.g. tech CEOs and billionaires) but your real audience is working parents and students, the crowd is biased. Persona selection must match the actual demographic you are predicting.
Too few personas, so biases do not cancel. With only two personas, a single aberrant one can swing the average. The wisdom of crowds requires a large enough group that individual outliers are swamped. The paper used roughly twelve personas; fewer would lose the effect.
Correlated persona biases. If all your personas were trained on the same media (e.g. US urban culture) and the LLM cannot truly separate them, they will agree when they should diverge. The model's ability to adopt a persona depends on how well its training data represents that persona's perspective.
Done right: diverse personas matched to your audience. Select personas that span your real audience's demographics and values. Validate against human raters. Use enough personas that no single outlier dominates the average.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment: collect a dataset of 20-30 stimuli (ads, videos, or webpages) rated by real humans. For each stimulus, call an LLM once (generic prompt) to get a single score, and call it 12 times with persona prompts to get 12 scores. Compute the mean of the 12 scores. Measure the error: the distance between your prediction and the average human rating.

error = |single_model_score - human_average| vs |crowd_score - human_average|

The crowd score should be closer to the real human average than the single model's answer, usually by 5-15 percentage points on tasks like likability or memorability.
