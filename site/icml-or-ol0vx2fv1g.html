Time Series Forecasting Through the Lens of Dynamics

To beat simple linear models at forecasting, teach the model to directly link past time points to future ones by placing that connection at the right spot in the architecture.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Deep learning models have taken over computer vision and language, but when it comes to predicting what happens next in a time series (a stream of numbers over time), a simple straight line still wins. The paper argues this happens because most models fail to learn the core job: directly connecting the past to the future. By identifying where this connection should live in the model's layers, they guide a design rule that helps any architecture forecast better.

Zero assumption version

Why even large neural networks lose to a straight line

A transformer (a model that reads all time points at once, comparing each point to all others to find patterns) should be better at forecasting than a simple linear model (one that draws a straight line through the data), because it can capture complex relationships between distant past points, not just fit a slope. But measurements show it often is not. The naive approach is to dump a transformer on the problem — throw in the architecture, add layers, hope something sticks.

What goes wrong: the transformer learns to do many things at once (compress the signal, extract patterns, smooth noise) but fails at the one thing that matters for forecasting: building a direct, faithful path from what happened in the past to what will happen next, because it gets distracted doing all those other tasks. Each task pulls on the layers in different directions.

Core insight. The model must learn to connect the past directly to the future as a first-order behavior, not bury it under other tasks.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper restructures where the dynamics block (the layer that directly connects past time points to the next point) lives inside the model. Most models bury it early or scatter it across layers; the paper argues it must sit at the end, after all other processing is done.

the object it moves  =  placement of the dynamics block
The hidden quantity

How much does each part of the model learn to forecast

measured quantity  =  dynamics capacity

The paper measures how well each layer in the model has learned the core task: turning past time points into a next-point prediction. A layer with high dynamics capacity knows how to do this. A layer with low capacity either does not try, or only partially succeeds.

The paper estimates this by building a simple linear model from each layer's numbers to the next time point, then measuring how well that linear model forecasts. High accuracy means that layer has captured the dynamics; low accuracy means it has not.

Why it works

Why placing the dynamics block at the end works

When the dynamics block sits in layer 2, layers 3, 4, 5 come after it downstream. Those later layers can change the signal — they can apply patterns, smooth, compress, or add noise. By the time the forecast reaches the output, the original past-to-future link has been scrambled by all that processing, so the forecast loses accuracy.

When the dynamics block sits in layer 5 (the end), all other layers process the input first. They remove noise and organize the past points cleanly, because early layers focus on structure, not forecasting. The dynamics block then receives organized input, which means each past point is clearly marked and its role is visible, so the block learns a stronger, cleaner direct link from past to future. Once the block produces the forecast, nothing downstream touches it, so the answer reaches the output intact and accurate.

inputRaw past time points (noisy, mixed)
stepLayer 1-2: remove noise and extract structure
stepLayer 3-4: organize the past points clearly
stepLayer 5 (dynamics block): map organized past directly to future
outputNext time point forecast (clean, not scrambled)
Math after the picture

The core equation: testing for dynamics capacity

First in words:

For each layer in the model, fit a simple line from that layer's numbers to the next time point. The accuracy of that line tells you how much dynamics the layer has learned. If accuracy drops across layers, the dynamics is leaking away.

Then the compact form:

dynamics_capacity_L = accuracy( linear_model( layer_L_output -> next_point ) )

Every symbol:

dynamics_capacity_Lhow well layer L can predict the next time point on its own (0 to 1)
layer_L_outputthe numbers this layer produces for a given time step
next_pointthe actual next value in the time series
linear_modela simple weighted sum of layer L's output
A tiny worked example: two layers
Layer 2 output: [2.1, 3.4, 1.8]. Fit a line to predict next time point. Accuracy: 72%.
Layer 5 output: [5.1, 6.2, 4.9]. Fit a line to predict next time point. Accuracy: 88%.
Dynamics capacity fell from 88% (layer 5 knows the job) to 72% (layer 2 is confused). Placing dynamics at layer 5 keeps accuracy high all the way out.
If dynamics were in layer 2 (72%), layers 3-5 would muddy it further. Best case: place dynamics at 5 so the answer stays 88%.

The paper argues models should have high dynamics capacity at the output layer, with the dynamics block positioned late so other layers clean the input first.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Dynamics block too early in the architecture. If you place it in layer 1 or 2, downstream layers still muddy it. The forecast stays weak.
Other processing layers too aggressive. If layers between the input and the dynamics block strip out all past information (over-compress), the dynamics block has nothing to work with.
Too many layers after the dynamics block. If the forecast then passes through five more processing layers, they can muddy it again. Late placement is key.
Done right: order matters. Clean input early (denoise, extract structure), then let the dynamics block at the end do its job without downstream interference. This rule works across different model backbones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple time-series forecasting model with five layers. First, place the dynamics block in layer 2 (traditional). Second, move it to layer 5. Train both on the same data, then forecast on the held-out future. Measure accuracy on that future data for both placements.

forecast_accuracy = (number_of_correct_next_points / total_time_steps) * 100

The model with dynamics at layer 5 should forecast 15-25 percentage points more accurately than with dynamics at layer 2, on the same data and model size.
