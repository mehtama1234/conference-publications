Hybrid Reinforcement: when reward is sparse, better to be dense

Mix binary correctness signals with detailed quality scores, bounded together in groups, to give models richer feedback on reasoning tasks.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Post-training reasoning models use two kinds of feedback: binary checkers (correct or wrong) and nuanced reward models (a score reflecting quality). Binary feedback is stable but brutal—it treats all wrong answers the same, even though some are close to right. This paper shows how to combine both signals in a way that keeps the stability of the checker while gaining the nuance of the reward model.

Why binary feedback gets stuck

When a reasoning model (say, solving math problems) generates 10 different solution attempts, a binary verifier (a checker program) marks each as right or wrong. If all 10 are wrong, the training signal is flat—the model sees no difference between an attempt that got partway to the right answer and one that was completely off track.

A naive approach would be to use a reward model instead, which looks at each solution and gives it a score (like 0.3, 0.7, 0.9) based on partial correctness, clarity, or closeness to the answer. But reward models are noisy and sometimes wrong—they might praise a solution that is actually incorrect, or penalize a correct one. Mixing this noisy signal directly with the binary checker destabilizes training.

The real bind: you need the richness of graded feedback to teach the model, but you need the correctness anchor of the verifier to stay on track.

The method in one sentence: Partition solutions by whether the verifier says they are right or wrong, rescale the reward-model scores within each group, and weight the training examples by how uncertain the reward model was about them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Partition by verifier

Sort all the model's attempts into two piles: verifier says correct, verifier says wrong.

Rescale within groups

Separately for each pile, use min-max scaling: stretch the reward-model scores to fill a range (e.g., 0-100) within that pile only.

Measure uncertainty

For each prompt, compute how much the reward model disagreed with itself across different solution attempts (variance).

Weight by uncertainty

Prompts where the reward model was uncertain get higher weight during training; trivial prompts get lower weight.

Train with combined signal

The model learns from rescaled scores, steered by verifier groups and weighted by which prompts matter most for learning.

What the paper changes

What gets changed

The paper changes the reward signal itself—what number the model sees as feedback for each solution attempt. Instead of raw numbers from a reward model or flat zeros and ones from the verifier, HERO constructs a new hybrid score that lives in each verifier group.

the object it moves  =  reward signal
The hidden quantity

The stratified score range

measured quantity  =  rescaled reward within correctness group

A single hidden quantity underneath HERO is the range of rescaled reward-model scores in each group. When the verifier partitions solutions into correct and wrong, each group has its own band of reward scores. This is not the raw reward-model score; it is the min-max normalized version that lives inside that group's correctness label.

The paper estimates this by looking at all the reward-model scores for solutions in one correctness group, finding the minimum and maximum, and stretching them linearly to fill a target range (e.g., 0-100). This ensures that within each group, nuance is preserved—a solution that is more correct than another within the wrong group will get a higher score—but never crosses the verifier's boundary.

Plain reading. The hidden quantity is the score a solution gets after rescaling. It is not raw; it is remapped so that the best and worst solutions within each verifier group (correct or wrong) use the full available range.
Paper reading. HERO applies stratified normalization: for each verifier-defined group, it subtracts the minimum reward-model score from each solution's score, then divides by the range (max minus min), where the original reward-model score and the final bounded score are inputs to the learning algorithm.
Why it works

Why hybrid rewards work where pure ones fail

The mechanism lives in three steps. First, the verifier's binary judgment is rock-solid—it knows whether a solution is truly correct or not. This is the anchor. By partitioning solutions by this anchor, HERO keeps training pointed at the right target.

Second, within each partition (correct or wrong), the reward model provides fine-grained feedback. A solution marked wrong by the verifier might still be closer to right than another wrong solution—partial correctness, clearer reasoning steps, or being off by just one logical error. By rescaling the reward model's scores within the wrong group, these distinctions become visible to the learning algorithm without ever flipping a verifier judgment.

Third, variance-aware weighting focuses training effort on the hardest cases. When a prompt has many different solution qualities (high variance in reward scores), the model is still learning what quality means for that prompt. When all solutions look equally bad or good to the reward model, there is little new to learn. By upweighting high-variance prompts, HERO spends more learning steps on the uncertain cases where nuance matters and fewer on trivial examples, making training more sample-efficient.

The rescaling formula

First in words:

For each verifier group (correct or wrong), rescale the reward-model score by subtracting the group minimum and dividing by the range, then multiply by the target range width.

Then the compact form:

r_norm = (r_RM - r_min) / (r_max - r_min) * (range_high - range_low) + range_low

Every symbol:

r_RMraw score from the reward model
r_minlowest reward-model score in this verifier group
r_maxhighest reward-model score in this verifier group
range_lowlower bound of target range (e.g., 0)
range_highupper bound of target range (e.g., 100)
A tiny worked example
Suppose verifier says 3 solutions are wrong. Their reward-model scores are 0.2, 0.5, 0.8.
r_min = 0.2, r_max = 0.8, range = [0, 100].
For r_RM = 0.2: r_norm = (0.2 - 0.2) / (0.8 - 0.2) * 100 + 0 = 0 / 0.6 * 100 = 0.
For r_RM = 0.5: r_norm = (0.5 - 0.2) / (0.8 - 0.2) * 100 + 0 = 0.3 / 0.6 * 100 = 50.
For r_RM = 0.8: r_norm = (0.8 - 0.2) / (0.8 - 0.2) * 100 + 0 = 0.6 / 0.6 * 100 = 100.
The worst wrong solution gets 0, the best wrong solution gets 100. Verifier's judgment is preserved; reward nuance is unlocked.

Rescaling ensures that the model learns quality distinctions within each verifier group without the reward model overriding the verifier's correctness signal.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Noisy reward models still hurt. If the reward model is badly miscalibrated (assigns high scores to wrong solutions), rescaling within groups cannot fix it, because the biased scores are still biased in both min and max, and stretching them just scales the bias. The verifier anchor is only as good as its definition of correctness.
Partitions that are too small. If one verifier group (e.g., correct solutions) has very few examples, min-max rescaling becomes unreliable—the range might be distorted by outliers.
Variance weighting can overfit to noise. Upweighting high-variance prompts assumes variance is a sign of learning opportunity, but sometimes high variance just means the reward model is confused. If many prompts are genuinely ambiguous, training can chase false signals.
Done right: anchor to verifier, rescale within groups, and use variance weighting carefully. The paper's approach works because it respects the verifier's ground truth (correct is correct) while letting the reward model add detail within each correctness group. This two-level structure is the key.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small math reasoning task (e.g., 10 simple algebra problems). Generate 5 solution attempts per problem using a small language model. Annotate each attempt with a binary label (correct or wrong, checked by hand or a simple rule). Train a small reward model to score attempts on a 0-1 scale based on partial correctness (e.g., how many steps are right). Now train three separate RL agents: one with binary rewards only, one with raw reward-model scores only, and one with HERO (stratified + variance-weighted). Measure final accuracy on a held-out test set and track the learning curve for each.

final_accuracy = (number of test problems solved correctly) / (total test problems); learning_efficiency = (final accuracy reached at fewest training steps)

The demo shows that HERO achieves higher accuracy than either baseline because it combines the stability of the verifier (which never gets confused about correctness) with the nuance of the reward model (which learns fine-grained quality), and because variance weighting concentrates training effort on the prompts where quality nuances matter most.
