Person-Centric Annotations of LAION-400M: Auditing Bias and Its Transfer to Models

By labeling every person in a massive image dataset and measuring demographic patterns, you can predict what biases a trained model will inherit.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Vision-language models like CLIP and Stable Diffusion absorb the biases present in their training data. But no one knew exactly which dataset patterns caused which model biases. This paper annotates 276 million people in LAION-400M (a 400-million-image collection) with detected bounding boxes, perceived gender, perceived race/ethnicity, and captions. It then shows that 60-70% of gender bias in trained models comes directly from how often people of different genders appear linked to particular topics in the raw data.

Zero assumption version

Dataset biases hide in plain sight

Vision-language models learn by reading millions of image-text pairs. A model's job is to predict: given a picture and a sentence, do they match? It trains by looking at examples where they do, and adjusting its internal numbers to recognize those patterns. But if the training images show men doing office jobs and women doing childcare 80% of the time, the model learns that pattern. When asked to generate an office worker, it will draw men more often. The bias is not an accident—it is faithfully learned from the data.

The problem: nobody could measure whether a dataset was biased, or predict which biases a trained model would pick up, because large datasets like LAION-400M were never annotated with demographic information. Without labels (which people are in each image, and who belongs to which demographic group), you cannot count whether men or women appear disproportionately with certain topics.

The core move. Annotate 276 million people in LAION-400M with bounding boxes and demographic labels, then measure how often each group appears with each topic.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Detect people

Object detection model finds all individuals in each image, draws bounding boxes.

Assign demographics

Vision model ensemble predicts perceived gender and race/ethnicity for each person.

Generate captions

Multimodal model writes a short description of each person and their surroundings.

Count co-occurrences

For each demographic group, tally how often they appear linked to topics (crime, office, childcare, etc.).

Measure bias transfer

Train CLIP models on the annotated data and check: does group imbalance in data predict group bias in model output?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Before this work, LAION-400M had no demographic annotations. The paper adds 276 million person bounding boxes, perceived gender labels, perceived race/ethnicity labels, and captions describing each person. This transforms the dataset from a bag of image-text pairs into a richly annotated collection where every person is identified and labeled.

the object it moves  =  Dataset demographic annotations
The hidden quantity

Co-occurrence bias: the hidden pattern

measured quantity  =  Co-occurrence between demographic group and topic

A co-occurrence is a link: how often do images of men appear alongside words like criminal, engineer, or scientist? How often do images of women appear with childcare, nurse, or secretary? If men appear 10 times more often with crime-related captions than their baseline frequency in the dataset, that is a 10x co-occurrence bias. This single number captures the imbalance.

The paper measures co-occurrence by counting: for each group (e.g., perceived men), what fraction of their images contain crime-related keywords in the caption? Then compare to the overall frequency of crime in the dataset. A high ratio means the group is overrepresented with that topic.

Plain reading. A co-occurrence is an imbalance: one group appears more often with a certain word or concept than you would expect by chance.
Paper reading. The paper computes the relative change in caption composition for each group: for men, what is the ratio of crime-related captions compared to the dataset average?
Why it works

Why dataset co-occurrences predict model bias

When a vision-language model trains, it learns associations from examples. If the training data shows men linked with crime 10 times more often than baseline, the model's internal numbers adjust to recognize that pattern. The model is not choosing to be biased—it is mechanically learning the frequency patterns in the data.

The paper trains CLIP models on LAION-400M and measures their output bias (e.g., when asked to draw a CEO, how often do they draw men?). It then checks: can a simple linear formula predict the model's bias using only the co-occurrence counts from the data? The answer is yes for 60-70% of gender bias. This shows that a large part of model bias is a direct copy of data composition, not a mysterious emergent phenomenon.

Math after the picture

Measuring co-occurrence and predicting bias

First in words:

bias_group = alpha + beta * cooccurrence_group

Then the compact form:

bias_in_model_output = alpha + beta * co-occurrence_ratio_in_data

Every symbol:

bias_in_model_outputHow much more/less often the model generates this group in a given context (e.g., +0.15 means 15% more men as CEOs)
co-occurrence_ratio_in_dataHow imbalanced the group is for this topic in LAION (e.g., 2.5 means men appear 2.5x as often with CEO-related captions)
alphaA baseline offset (intercept)
betaThe slope: how strongly a 1-unit change in data co-occurrence translates to model bias
A tiny worked example
Suppose in LAION-400M, men appear 3.0 times as often with CEO-related captions (co-occurrence ratio = 3.0).
Suppose women appear 0.5 times as often with CEO captions (co-occurrence ratio = 0.5).
A trained CLIP model is asked: draw a CEO. If alpha = 0.05 and beta = 0.30, then:
Model bias for men = 0.05 + 0.30 * 3.0 = 0.05 + 0.90 = 0.95 (95% of generated CEOs are men).
Model bias for women = 0.05 + 0.30 * 0.5 = 0.05 + 0.15 = 0.20 (20% of generated CEOs are women).
The model mirrors the data imbalance, amplified by beta. If beta is large, small data imbalances become large model biases.

A high beta means the model faithfully reproduces data imbalances; a low beta means the model resists the data bias, perhaps due to architectural choices or debiasing.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mislabeled demographics. If the automated classifiers make mistakes (e.g., misidentifying gender or ethnicity), the co-occurrence counts are wrong, and bias predictions fail. The paper uses an ensemble of multiple vision models and only trusts consensus labels to reduce this risk.
Bounding boxes miss people. If the detection model fails to find all individuals, the dataset is incomplete. For example, if it struggles with certain lighting conditions or image qualities, some groups may be systematically undercounted.
Captions do not capture all bias sources. Model bias can come from visual features alone (e.g., skin tone, clothing) without any caption text. This method only measures text-based co-occurrences, so it might miss visual-only biases.
Linear model is too simple. The paper predicts model bias using a linear formula. If model bias depends on non-linear interactions between groups and topics, the linear model will miss those effects and underestimate bias.
Done right. Use an ensemble of models for demographic labeling, because consensus predictions reduce errors from individual model mistakes. Manually validate a sample of labels to catch systematic mislabeling before it corrupts the entire dataset. Compute co-occurrence for both text captions and visual features, because some bias may hide in visual patterns alone. Test whether a non-linear predictor fits model bias better than the linear one, because if model bias actually depends on interactions between groups and topics, a linear formula will miss those and predict poorly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small annotated image-text dataset (e.g., 100 images manually labeled with gender and role). Train a simple CLIP-like model on it. Measure the co-occurrence of each group with each role in your dataset. Then, without looking at the model's outputs, predict what gender bias the model will show when asked to generate examples of each role. Finally, run the trained model, measure its actual bias on new test prompts, and check: does your co-occurrence-based prediction match the model's actual bias?

prediction_accuracy = correlation( predicted_bias_from_cooccurrence, actual_model_bias )

If the demo works, you will see that co-occurrence imbalances in data directly translate to output imbalances in the trained model—no magic, just learning from examples.
