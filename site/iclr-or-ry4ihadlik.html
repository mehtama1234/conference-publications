CLEAR: Calibrated Learning for Epistemic and Aleatoric Risk

When you predict something, you face two kinds of error—measurement noise and lack of data—and tuning one usually breaks the other, so CLEAR balances both with separate knobs.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Prediction intervals (your best guess plus a confidence band around it) must be honest: wide enough to catch the real answer, but narrow enough to be useful. But predictions fail in two different ways. Measurement noise (the scatter in any real measurement) requires padding. Lack of training data (limited experience) requires wider uncertainty. Existing methods dial in one kind of honesty and lose the other. CLEAR fixes this by measuring where each kind of error lives and tuning them with separate dials.

Zero assumption version

Two kinds of error pull you apart

Imagine you are predicting house prices. A model trained on 1,000 houses gives you a guess: $450k. But guesses are never exact. You want to add a margin of error—say, $450k ± $50k—so when the house sells, the real price lands inside your band 90 percent of the time.

Here is the trap. Some error comes from measurement noise: the exact sale price bounces around because of timing, market mood, inspection quirks. Adding more training data does not help; this scatter is baked in because no matter how many examples you see, randomness in real-world prices does not vanish—it is part of how the world works. Other error comes from your model not knowing enough yet. Train on twice as many houses, and this error shrinks, because each new house teaches the model something about what drives prices. But the two errors fight each other. If you dial in the width to catch all the measurement noise, you over-protect against the 'lack of data' error and make your band too wide, wasting the prediction's usefulness. If you dial it for what you learned from data, you under-protect against the noise and your band is too narrow.

Existing methods pick one error to protect against, then apply the same padding everywhere. They are honest in one dimension and dishonest in the other.

The core problem. You need two separate dials: one for measurement noise and one for limited data.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Train two separate uncertainty estimators

One measures noise-scatter from measurements; one measures data-scarcity scatter from having seen too few examples.

Step 2: Split your data for tuning

Hold back a 'calibration' sample to tune the dials without seeing the models' training data.

Step 3: Measure where each kind of error is biggest

For each prediction, compute how much noise and how much data-scarcity the estimators report.

Step 4: Tune the noise dial on calibration data

Slide the noise dial until your prediction band catches the true answer 90 percent of the time in the noise-dominated part of your data.

Step 5: Tune the data-scarcity dial with a ratio lock

Hold the ratio between dials fixed, then slide both together on calibration data until coverage is honest everywhere.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

CLEAR changes the calibration step: instead of one dial that pads all predictions the same way, it computes a separate dial-width for measurement noise and a separate one for data scarcity, then balances them on held-out data.

the object it moves  =  calibration parameters (two separate dials)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how much each error dominates

measured quantity  =  uncertainty dominance ratio

At each prediction point, the two estimators give you two width estimates: width from noise, width from data scarcity. Their ratio (data-scarcity width divided by noise width) tells you which error is bigger at that point. A large ratio means you need more training examples to shrink the uncertainty, because data-scarcity is the bigger error there—extra data will teach the model more. A small ratio means even perfect data won't help, the noise is what it is, because noise is baked into the world and no amount of training will shrink it. The paper measures this ratio but does not name it explicitly. What matters is that CLEAR tunes each dial to where it matters most.

The paper computes this implicitly when it balances the two uncertainty components. The tuning chooses gamma1 (noise dial) and gamma2 (data-scarcity dial) such that their ratio—data-scarcity dial divided by noise dial—captures which error dominates at each location in your data.

Why it works

Why it works: tuning each error separately

Measurement noise is real but irreducible—you cannot collect 1,000 times more data to eliminate it. So on a calibration sample, you measure how much padding you need to catch the noise, and lock that in. That is gamma1.

Data scarcity is reducible—add more training examples and uncertainty shrinks, because more examples teach the model more about the true pattern. But you do not know in advance how much data is 'enough,' so you cannot pick a single 'right' dial value before seeing calibration data. Instead, on the same calibration sample, you measure how much extra padding helps with limited-data uncertainty; you do this by sliding the dial until the calibration data shows you are catching the real answers 90 percent of the time. Then you set gamma2 as a multiple (ratio) of gamma1. This ratio is the same everywhere in your data, but because the underlying data-scarcity width varies by location, the actual padding adapts locally.

Why does this work? Because noise and data scarcity do not shrink at the same rate as you add data. If you use one dial for both, you either over-protect (making predictions useless) or under-protect (making them dishonest). Two dials let you hand each error its own honest margin, then combine them without waste.

Math after the picture

The equation for balancing two errors

First in words:

prediction interval equals lower quantile minus noise-dial times its width plus data-scarcity-dial times its width; upper quantile plus noise-dial times its width plus data-scarcity-dial times its width. The dials are gamma1 (noise) and gamma2 (data scarcity), chosen so that the interval contains the true answer at least (1 minus alpha) fraction of the time.

Then the compact form:

C(x) = [q_al(α/2)(x) - γ1 - γ2, q_al(1-α/2)(x) + γ1 + γ2] where γ1 and γ2 are tuned on calibration data Dcal such that P[Y ∈ C(X) | Dcal] >= 1 - α

Every symbol:

q_al(α/2)(x)lower edge of noise-based band (measured from noise estimator)
q_al(1-α/2)(x)upper edge of noise-based band
γ1padding width for measurement noise
γ2padding width for data-scarcity uncertainty
Ytrue value you are predicting
C(x)the prediction interval (the band around the guess)
1 - αtarget coverage, e.g. 0.90 for 90 percent honest coverage
A tiny worked example
Suppose noise estimator says: lower bound 95, upper bound 105.
Data-scarcity estimator says: lower bound 90, upper bound 110.
Calibration tunes γ1 = 2 (noise dial) and γ2 = 5 (data-scarcity dial).
Final interval: [95 - 2 - 5, 105 + 2 + 5] = [88, 112].
Without CLEAR using one dial for both, you might pad by 8 everywhere (too wide in low-noise regions) or by 2 everywhere (too narrow in high-data-scarcity regions).

The two dials add padding independently, so each error gets its own honest margin tuned on real data.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Calibration data must be representative. If your calibration sample misses a region of the input space, CLEAR's dials will be tuned badly for that region. The method assumes the calibration sample and future test data are drawn from the same source of examples.
Noise and data-scarcity estimators must be decent. CLEAR assumes you already have working estimators for both. If one estimator is completely wrong (e.g. the data-scarcity estimator does not actually measure data scarcity), CLEAR cannot fix it.
Fixing the ratio between dials can be too rigid. The paper holds lambda (the ratio gamma2 / gamma1) constant across all predictions. In rare cases, the right ratio might need to vary locally. The method assumes one ratio works everywhere.
Done right. When calibration data is truly representative, both estimators are well-trained, and noise and data-scarcity uncertainty do not interact in unexpected ways, CLEAR reliably produces narrower intervals without losing coverage.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic regression problem where you control both measurement noise (add random scatter to all outputs) and data scarcity (use only 50 training examples at first). Train two estimators: one neural network predicting a noise-scale output, one ensemble of models for data-scarcity uncertainty. Run CLEAR to calibrate gamma1 and gamma2 on a held-out calibration set. Then on a test set, compare prediction-interval width and coverage against baselines that use one dial for everything. CLEAR should produce narrower intervals (especially in low-noise regions) while keeping coverage at the target level (say, 90 percent).

coverage = fraction of test points where true value falls inside predicted band; average_width = mean width of prediction bands. Goal: achieve target coverage (e.g., 90 percent) with smallest average width.

The essential point: measurement noise and lack-of-data error shrink at different rates; tuning them separately lets you stop over-padding in regions where one error dominates.
