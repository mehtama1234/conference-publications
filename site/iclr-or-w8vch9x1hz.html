Knowledge Distillation as Decontamination

Knowledge distillation does not reliably transfer test-set secrets from a contaminated teacher to a clean student, making it safe for cleaning up leaked data.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

A model trained on the test set (the teacher) holds secrets the student model should not know. When you distill knowledge from the teacher to the student, does the student inherit those secrets? The paper shows the answer is usually no—distillation mostly fails to copy test-set knowledge, so it works as a genuine decontamination technique.

Zero assumption version

The contamination leakage worry

Imagine a model trained on the full dataset—training examples plus test examples mixed in. That model is contaminated; it has memorized parts of the test set. When you ask it questions on the test set, it performs unrealistically well, and you cannot trust the benchmark numbers.

A natural fix: train a new clean student model on only the training set, then use the contaminated teacher to guide the student via knowledge distillation (copying soft predictions). But here is the worry: does the student inherit the teacher's test-set secrets in the process? Does distillation launder contaminated knowledge—sneaking test answers through the teacher's soft predictions into the student's weights?

The risk. A clean model trained with guidance from a contaminated teacher might still leak test-set knowledge, making the clean benchmark meaningless.

What the paper changes

The paper measures whether test-set knowledge flows from teacher to student during soft-prediction distillation. The single thing it changes: it distills from a contaminated teacher and measures how much of that contamination appears in the student's weights.

The hidden quantity

The laundering fraction: how much secret leaks

measured quantity  =  Laundering effect size

Laundering is the extra accuracy boost the student gains on the test set because it was distilled from the contaminated teacher, instead of trained cleanly from scratch. It is a number: how much did the teacher's test-set knowledge actually transfer to the student?

The paper estimates this by comparing the student's test accuracy after distillation to a purely clean baseline. The difference is the laundering effect. If laundering is small, distillation is safe. If it is large, the student inherited too much from the contaminated teacher.

Why it works

Why soft predictions carry weak contamination

A soft prediction is not a direct memory. The teacher outputs a probability for each class (e.g., 0.95 for dog, 0.04 for cat, 0.01 for bird). These probabilities are smooth, averaged summaries of what the teacher learned. They do not encode the exact test examples the teacher saw.

When the student distills from these soft predictions, it learns broad patterns that worked for the teacher. But those patterns are fragile: they rely on the teacher's specific weights, the order of training, and the exact data. On a fresh student model trained from scratch with different random starting points, most of these contaminated patterns do not stick, because the student's random initialization and different update direction (the way each weight nudges during training) lead away from the teacher's memorized quirks.

Laundering happens only when train and test sets look very different (a large distribution gap). Here is why: the teacher overfits heavily to the test set when the gap is large, learning specific patterns that only work for that particular test data. Those specific, fragile patterns leak into the soft predictions. But when train and test are similar, the teacher learns broad, general patterns that do not overfit; those general patterns are less contaminated and less likely to transfer. In typical benchmarks where train and test match well, soft predictions stay generic enough that the student stays mostly clean.

Math after the picture

Measuring laundering mathematically

First in words:

laundering equals (student accuracy on test with contaminated teacher) minus (student accuracy on test with clean teacher)

Then the compact form:

Laundering = Acc_student(test | teacher_contaminated) - Acc_student(test | teacher_clean)

Every symbol:

Acc_studentStudent model's accuracy on the test set
testHeld-out test data that teacher was trained on
teacher_contaminatedTeacher trained on both training and test data
teacher_cleanStudent trained on training data only, no distillation
A concrete example on one benchmark
Clean student baseline (no distillation, trained on training set): 85.0% test accuracy.
Student distilled from contaminated teacher: 85.8% test accuracy.
Laundering effect: 85.8% - 85.0% = 0.8 percentage points.
For comparison, direct contamination (teacher on test set): 92.0% (a 7.0 percentage-point boost).
Laundering is 11% of direct contamination; the soft predictions leak very little.

Laundering is tiny when train and test look alike, but grows when they diverge sharply.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Massive distribution gap creates leakage. If train and test look very different, the teacher learns test-specific overfitting patterns that stand out in its soft predictions. Those patterns are easier for the student to copy, because they are distinctive and repeatable. In those rare cases, laundering becomes more significant—but still smaller than direct contamination.
Distillation on the contaminated data itself. If you distill the student on the very same test set the teacher was trained on, you defeat the purpose. Always distill on a separate held-out set.
High-capacity student copies the teacher more faithfully. A very large student model with unlimited capacity may memorize the teacher's soft predictions more exactly, because it has enough parameters to fit every detail. Capacity matters: bigger models leak more.
Standard benchmarks stay safe. On typical benchmarks where train and test distributions are similar, the teacher learns general patterns, not test-specific overfitting. Those general patterns are in the soft predictions, but they are not contaminated—they would appear in any honest model. Because the soft predictions carry only general, not contaminated, knowledge, distillation results in negligible laundering.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy classification task where you control the train-test distribution gap. Start with a task where train and test are identical (MNIST with no augmentation). Train a teacher on the full dataset (training plus test). Distill a clean student from that teacher using only soft predictions on a separate validation set. Measure the student's test accuracy and compare it to a clean baseline trained without distillation. Repeat with progressively larger distribution gaps (add noise to test, rotate images, etc.) and measure how laundering grows.

Laundering = Clean_student_without_distillation(test_accuracy) - Distilled_student_with_contaminated_teacher(test_accuracy)

As distribution gap widens, laundering should grow from nearly zero to noticeable; without a gap, laundering stays negligible even with a contaminated teacher.
