Fisher information

Measure how much data reveals about what a model got wrong.

In plain terms

Imagine you're teaching someone to recognize cats. After showing them fifty pictures, you ask: which images taught them the most? The ones that made them change their answer the most are the informative ones. Fisher information measures exactly this—how sensitive the model's answer is to small tweaks in its belief about what it learned.

Here's the concrete problem: when you train a model, some data points matter more than others. A clear example teaches faster than an ambiguous one—because the model's prediction is further from the truth on clear examples, so adjusting the model's weights pushes it more. But how do you measure clarity? Fisher information asks: if I jiggle this model's weights just a tiny bit, which examples would change its prediction the most? The ones that change it a lot are the ones carrying the signal. They're the data points screaming the loudest about what the world is actually like.

Think of it like this: if you adjust the model's confidence knob slightly upward, some predictions shift wildly while others barely budge. Fisher information is the sensitivity map—it tells you which data points the model is currently least sure about, which means they're the most informative for learning.

The equation
F=E[\nabla\log p \nabla\log p^T]
FFisher information matrix—a table of numbers measuring which directions matter most for improvement
∇ log pthe gradient of log probability—a vector pointing toward what the model got most wrong
∇ log p^Tthe same gradient vector flipped into a column
(∇ log p)(∇ log p)^Tthe outer product—pairing each direction with itself to measure how strongly it pulls on the model
E[...]the average over all the data you're learning from
Worked example
Measuring how a single mistake teaches the model
Say the model assigned probability 0.6 to 'cat' when it should be 1.0.
Its log probability is log(0.6) = −0.51. Its gradient (the direction of steepest climb) ∇ log p = [−1.2, 0.8].
The outer product: [−1.2, 0.8] × [−1.2, 0.8]^T = [[1.44, −0.96], [−0.96, 0.64]].
Averaging over 100 such examples: F ≈ [[0.12, −0.08], [−0.08, 0.05]].
The diagonal tells us: tweaking parameter (a weight inside the model) 1 by 0.01 shifts the output by ~0.012.
The off-diagonal shows: the two weights move together—they're correlated in how they affect prediction.
Why it shows up

Fisher information shows up in continual learning, where models keep learning new tasks without forgetting old ones. The diagonal entries tell you which weights were critical for the old task—protect those weights when learning the new one. It's also central to efficient adaptation: if you only have budget to update a few weights, update the ones with high Fisher values because they move predictions the most per unit of change.

In bandits and active learning, Fisher information picks which question to ask next: ask about the example where the model's uncertainty is highest—that's the one with the largest Fisher entry. In distillation and model compression, it identifies which weights are signal versus noise. Across all these papers, the pattern is the same: wherever a model must decide what's worth learning or remembering, Fisher information is the metric that says what's signal.
