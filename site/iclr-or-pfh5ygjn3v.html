M³E: Continual Vision-and-Language Navigation via Mixture of Macro and Micro Experts

A robot reads text instructions and camera images to navigate indoor spaces, but forgets how to handle old environments when learning new ones—this paper splits navigation reasoning into two levels so it can adapt without erasing what it already knows.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot that learns to navigate by following text instructions and looking at camera images faces a core problem: when it learns new environments, it forgets how to handle old ones—like forgetting how to work in an office building after learning a home layout. This paper proposes splitting the robot's reasoning into two separate pathways: a high-level "what kind of space is this" reasoner (office, residential, outdoor) and a low-level "which wall objects matter for this instruction" reasoner, so each can adapt independently without wiping out the other.

Zero assumption version

The forgetting trap in navigation

A robot learns to navigate by reading instructions like 'turn left at the red door' and looking at camera images. When it trains in a residential home, it gets good at that space: it learns which doors matter, which walls are walkable, what 'left' means in that lighting. Then it moves to an office building. As it trains on office images and instructions, the weights (the numbers that make up the robot's brain) slowly shift to match the office world. But after 500 office steps, the robot has often rewritten its old home knowledge—it now misinterprets residential hallways because the numbers that once worked there are gone. This is catastrophic forgetting.

A beginner might try fine-tuning: take the weights learned in the home, then just keep tweaking them for the office. But the same rewriting happens—new learning overwrites old. The core problem is that residential and office spaces are different enough that a single set of weights cannot serve both cleanly.

The core move. Train separate expert modules for different scene types and perception tasks, then learn which expert to activate in each situation, using a momentum-based update that slows down how fast old experts change.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

See the scene

Robot camera captures image; map its visual features (walls, objects, lighting).

Route at scene level

Macro router reads scene features, outputs a weighted blend: 80% home-expert, 20% office-expert.

Route at perception level

Micro router reads instruction text and image patch, selects which perception expert to trust this step.

Make a decision

Blend of selected experts predicts next action: go forward, turn, stop.

Update slowly with momentum

When training, nudge expert weights gently (momentum means old ones move slowly), so old expertise does not die.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of one set of weights for all scenes and tasks, the robot now maintains separate expert weights plus two routing networks. The macro router learns to identify scene type. The micro router learns to identify perception needs per instruction-image pair. The paper also adds momentum (a slowness constant) to weight updates so old experts do not flip abruptly.

The hidden quantity

Expert utility across domains

measured quantity  =  How much each expert is used in new environments

As the robot enters a new domain (office after home), some old experts become useless—they were only right for home scenes. The paper measures expert utility: for each expert module, how often does the current domain ask the macro or micro router to activate it? High utility means keep updating it; low utility means freeze it to preserve old capability.

The paper uses a momentum update strategy: compute how much each expert was selected in the new domain, then scale the weight update by that utility. High-utility experts update quickly; low-utility experts barely move, preserving their old expertise.

Why it works

Why dual routing solves forgetting

Forgetting happens because a single weight set must represent both scene strategies (how to navigate a residential hallway vs. an office corridor) and local perceptions (which objects matter, which walls block, how lighting affects doors). When these conflict, updating for one overwrites the other.

Splitting into macro and micro experts decouples these two problems. The macro router learns high-level strategy for each scene type: in a home, focus on intimate scale and furniture; in an office, focus on open plans and cubicles. The micro router learns perception: which instruction words link to which visual features. These can be learned mostly independently. When a new domain arrives, the macro router can route heavily to its best old expert (because scene reasoning transfers), while the micro router can create fresh perception experts for the new look. Meanwhile, momentum keeps low-utility old experts frozen, preserving their specialized knowledge.

The mechanism works in three steps: (1) macro routing identifies scene similarity (new office may look similar to old office expertise, so reuse it); (2) micro routing picks perception modules per step (because this instruction-image pair needs specific local reasoning); (3) momentum update protects experts that are not heavily used in the new domain—if an expert is rarely selected, its weights barely move, so it stays sharp for its old domain. The result: old capabilities survive because they are not forced to compete with new learning.

Math after the picture

The momentum update equation

First in words:

New expert weight equals old expert weight plus the learning step scaled down by how little that expert was used in the new domain.

Then the compact form:

theta_i_new = theta_i_old + (1 - utility_i) * lr * grad_i

Every symbol:

theta_i_newnew weight for expert i after update
theta_i_oldold weight before update
utility_ifraction of times expert i was selected in new domain, from 0 to 1
lrlearning rate: how large the update step is
grad_igradient: which direction to nudge the weight
A tiny worked example
Expert home-door: used in 90% of new office examples. utility = 0.9. change direction = 0.5. step size = 0.1.
theta_new = 1.0 + (1 - 0.9) * 0.1 * 0.5 = 1.0 + 0.1 * 0.1 * 0.5 = 1.0 + 0.005 = 1.005. Big update.
Expert home-furniture: used in 5% of new office examples. utility = 0.05. change direction = 0.5.
theta_new = 1.0 + (1 - 0.05) * 0.1 * 0.5 = 1.0 + 0.95 * 0.1 * 0.5 = 1.0 + 0.0475 = 1.0475. Tiny update.
Same change direction, but home-furniture weight barely moves, staying close to its old home-trained value.

Low utility freezes old experts; high utility lets them adapt.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Router deadlock. If the macro router only ever selects one expert, the other experts never train and become useless. The paper must ensure router diversity, e.g., by gently pushing the router to spread selections or adding small random noise to scores.
Momentum too conservative. If momentum is set too high (old experts barely update), new domains may be forced into poorly-fitting old experts, degrading performance. Tuning is needed.
Scene type mismatch. If a new domain looks different from all prior scenes (e.g., outdoor garden after only indoor training), the macro router may misclassify, routing to the wrong strategy expert, causing poor performance.
Calibrated momentum. Setting utility-based momentum adaptively—fast for high-utility experts, slow for low—lets the robot preserve old domains while still adapting to new ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a robot agent on VLN instructions in a home-like simulated environment (e.g., apartment building), measuring accuracy on home navigation tasks. Then train it on an office-like environment (cubicles, conference rooms) for 500 steps. At each step, measure: (1) How well does it still navigate the home? (2) How well does it navigate the office? (3) Are both high, or does office training destroy home knowledge? The demo should show that without the paper's dual routers and momentum, home accuracy drops below 50%, while with the paper's method both stay above 80%.
