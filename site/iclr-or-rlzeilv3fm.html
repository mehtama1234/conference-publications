PhyWorldBench: Evaluating Physics Realism in Video Generation

PhyWorldBench is a benchmark that tests whether text-to-video models can generate videos that obey real-world physics laws.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Video generation models have become very good at making photorealistic videos, but they often break real-world physics—objects fall upward, energy appears from nowhere, or people move impossibly. PhyWorldBench is a test suite with 1,050 carefully written prompts that checks whether models understand the laws of physics. It also introduces Anti-Physics prompts that intentionally violate physics, to see if models will follow instructions even when told to break the rules.

Zero assumption version

Video models look great but break physics

Text-to-video models (systems that turn a written description into a video) now generate beautiful, photorealistic frames. They can handle complicated scenes with lighting, motion, and detail. But they fail at something simple: following the laws of physics. A prompt asking for a bowling ball rolling downhill might generate it rolling uphill instead. A person jumping might freeze mid-air instead of falling. Water poured into a cup might vanish.

The naive approach is to just watch the generated videos and judge them with your eyes—good, bad, or broken. But eyeballing is slow, inconsistent, and hard to measure. You cannot track progress or benchmark different models fairly. There is no way to know whether a video fails for a deep reason (the model did not learn physics) or a shallow one (the prompt was unclear). Most importantly, we cannot tell which specific physics laws the models understand and which they do not.

The problem. Video generation models look photorealistic but routinely violate physics, and we have no systematic way to measure or compare how well different models understand the real world.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Categorize prompts by physics difficulty

Bucket scenarios into fundamental (single object motion), composite (multiple objects interacting), and anti-physics (intentionally rule-breaking).

Generate videos from all models

Feed the same prompts to ten leading text-to-video models and record their outputs.

Run large vision-language model evaluation

A separately-trained large multi-modal model watches each video and answers: does this obey real physics?

Compute physics adherence scores

Tally how many generated videos passed physics checks, and group results by category and model.

Identify where models fail

Spot patterns—which laws do models break most, which prompts expose the biggest gaps, which models are most physics-aware.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes

PhyWorldBench does not change the video models themselves. Instead, it provides a new lens to measure them: a comprehensive set of prompts and a zero-shot evaluation method. The prompts are the tool; the evaluation method is the measurement.

the object it moves  =  prompts and evaluation method
The hidden quantity

Physics adherence: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Physics adherence score

Every text-to-video model has an invisible property: how much the videos it generates obey real-world physics. This is not the same as looking photorealistic. A video can have perfect lighting and smooth motion but still have objects moving backward or floating. Physics adherence is the fraction of generated videos that obey the laws the world actually runs on.

PhyWorldBench estimates this by using a separately-trained large vision-language model to watch each generated video and judge whether it respects physics. The MLLM acts as a referee. It does not require hand-crafted rules or special training—it already understands physics from its own pre-training, so it can evaluate in a zero-shot fashion, meaning it grades physics without seeing any labeled examples from this benchmark.

Why it works

Why this works: the evaluation chain

Large vision-language models already understand physics from their pre-training data (they have seen countless real videos and physics descriptions). You do not need to train a new evaluator or hand-craft physics rules. Instead, you can ask the pretrained model directly: does this video obey physics? The model can answer accurately without seeing benchmark examples. This works because the model has already absorbed physics understanding from real-world data.

The prompts matter too. By splitting scenarios into fundamental (object motion, energy), composite (interactions), and anti-physics (rule-breaking instructions), the benchmark reveals which laws the video models understand and which they skip. For example, a model might handle basic gravity but fail on collisions. Anti-Physics prompts test whether models blindly follow instructions (undesirable) or refuse physically impossible requests (which shows the model is checking requests before executing them).

Math after the picture

The scoring formula

First in words:

physics_score = (count of videos that obey physics) divided by (total videos tested)

Then the compact form:

physics_score = (videos_correct) / (total_videos_in_category)

Every symbol:

videos_correctnumber of generated videos the MLLM judged as physically accurate
total_videos_in_categorynumber of videos tested in one prompt category (fundamental, composite, or anti-physics)
physics_scorethe fraction from 0 to 1 representing how often the model generates physics-obedient videos
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model generates 10 videos from fundamental physics prompts.
MLLM judges: 8 obey physics (gravity, momentum), 2 violate it (objects floating, energy creation).
Fundamental physics score = 8 / 10 = 0.80 (80% correct).
For the same model on composite scenarios: 5 correct out of 10 = 0.50 (50% correct).
For anti-physics prompts: the model follows 9 out of 10 unphysical instructions = 0.90 (it is too obedient; a lower score here might be better if you want the model to refuse impossible requests).

The score is simple—a hit-rate—because the real power comes from splitting prompts by physics type; you see not just overall performance but exactly which laws the model breaks.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

MLLM evaluator is not perfect. The vision-language model may miss subtle physics violations or misinterpret ambiguous videos. A floating object might look natural under strange lighting, or a slow collision might be misjudged. The benchmark is only as accurate as the evaluator.
Prompts may be ambiguous. A written description like 'a person jumps high' does not specify exactly how high or for how long. Different video models interpret it differently, making physics scoring noisy.
Anti-physics prompts create a false trade-off. Models that refuse unphysical instructions score low on anti-physics prompts but may be safer in production. Models that obey score high but might be less trustworthy when safety matters.
Done right. PhyWorldBench works well when the MLLM is strong, prompts are precise, and you interpret scores in context—understanding that obedience to unphysical instructions is not always good.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple script that: (1) writes three test prompts (one fundamental physics scenario like 'a ball falls downward', one composite like 'two balls collide elastically', one anti-physics like 'a ball falls upward'), (2) generates short videos from two or three text-to-video models, (3) shows the videos side by side, and (4) uses a freely available vision-language model (like CLIP or a small language model with image understanding) to score each video on physics adherence. The demo should make it obvious that different models generate videos with wildly different physics quality.

average_physics_score = sum of (category_score_1 + category_score_2 + category_score_3) / 3

The demo proves that you can measure physics understanding without hand-crafted rules—a pretrained vision-language model can do it, and different video models show dramatically different physics competence.
