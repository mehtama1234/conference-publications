Decision-making in changing or interactive environments

Theory for learning strategies in settings where the problem changes over time, the learner gets feedback through actions, or multiple agents are interacting.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Most learning theory assumes the world is static: the rules do not change between the time you train a model and the time you use it. But in reality, the world shifts. A spam filter trained on 2023 spam must handle spam tactics from 2025. A recommendation system trained on one year of user preferences must adapt as trends evolve. A robot trained to grasp one type of object must adapt when objects change shape or texture. This subtheme asks: how can a learner make good decisions when the environment itself is learning or changing?

The difficulty is that you cannot simply retrain on old data; the old data no longer reflects reality. Additionally, in interactive settings, your own actions change what happens next — if a robot reaches for a fragile object, it learns to be gentler, but this changes the feedback it receives. In game-theoretic scenarios, you have multiple agents learning simultaneously, each trying to predict what the others will do, leading to circular reasoning. The learner must balance exploiting what it has learned (choosing actions it thinks are good) with exploring new actions (to learn whether they are even better), because committing entirely to exploration wastes opportunities to use known good actions, while committing entirely to exploitation misses chances to find better actions. Pick the wrong balance and it either gets stuck repeating bad decisions or wastes time on obviously bad ones.

Researchers study these problems using tools from reinforcement learning (learning through trial and error and rewards), online learning (adapting as data arrives one example at a time), and game theory (analyzing multi-agent interactions). They analyze how much the model can drift from its training environment and still perform well, what exploration strategies guarantee finding good solutions in reasonable time, and how to model an opponent's behavior. The work includes studying specific problem structures — bandit problems (pick one option from many), Markov decision processes (environments where the next state depends only on the current state and action), and strategic scenarios where agents anticipate each other's moves.

How the work breaks down

The papers here split into five directions that all tackle what happens when the learning problem keeps changing or involves feedback loops. First: bandits where you pick one option and get a reward, but the world can be adversarial or unpredictable. Second: federated learning where many agents learn together and have to deal with different data distributions and potential conflicts. Third: continual learning where a model must keep learning new tasks without forgetting old ones. Fourth: reinforcement learning and multi-agent games where your actions change the next state and other agents are learning too. Fifth: online learning under distribution shift, where the underlying rules can change over time in ways that break the old model.

Bandits: Theory for picking under uncertainty

Bandit problems are about picking one option from many over time and learning which ones give good rewards. The core difficulty is that you cannot try everything — trying bad options wastes time and reward. You must balance exploring new options (trying things you have not tried yet, to learn if they are better, because only new tries reveal whether you missed something) against exploiting the ones you think are already good (using what you have learned, so you earn reward now instead of wasting it). When the world is adversarial (an opponent chooses rewards to make you fail), or when the rewards are unpredictable, or when there are constraints on how many times you can pick, the optimal strategy changes. These papers push the frontier on what algorithms can achieve in these different settings.

The best-of-three-worlds paper tackles dueling bandits, where you compare two options at a time instead of getting one reward. It achieves near-optimal regret simultaneously in three different worlds: adversarial (when an opponent picks rewards), stochastic (when rewards are random), and partly corrupted (some rewards are faked). The efficient best-of-both-worlds paper does something similar for contextual bandits where you also see features (information about each option) — it gets near-optimal regret in both adversarial and stochastic settings with much faster computation (because you do not need to recompute everything from scratch). The multi-armed bandits paper asks a different question: what if you have a constraint, like you must earn a certain total reward in the process? It proves a fundamental limit showing that the time-horizon (how long you run) must grow with your constraint (because you cannot make enough money if you have limited time). The single-index bandits paper extends to a very large space of options where you cannot check all of them — it achieves nearly optimal regret by learning a hidden combination of the features (rather than checking all possible combinations). The federated bandits paper and perturbation approach paper both extend classical bandit theory to distributed settings and new function classes (so multiple agents or complex decision structures can use bandits).

These five papers build on each other: they all start from the same core exploration-exploitation tradeoff but handle more complex real-world settings like distribution shift, constraints, or high dimensions. Each one pins down a new hardness limit or algorithm that matches it.

Federated learning: Many agents, different data

Federated learning means many agents train a model together using their own local data, without sharing the raw data with each other. The core problem is that each agent's data might be very different — a user's phone sees different emails than another user's phone. When distributions are heterogeneous (non-uniform) across agents, standard averaging breaks. Additionally, if an agent is malicious or if incentives are not aligned, some agents might not cooperate or might poison the learning. These papers tackle how to make federated learning work well and fairly when agents are diverse.

Beyond aggregation redesigns federated learning to actively guide each client based on what it is learning. Instead of treating all clients the same, it learns which clients should specialize on which parts of the model (so each agent focuses on what it does well). Incentives in federated learning proves that you can pay each agent based on their actual contribution using a strategy-proof mechanism — meaning no agent wants to cheat (because honesty earns more than lying). Adaptive personalized federated learning uses kernel methods (a way to measure similarity between data points) to find which agents are similar (so they learn from similar data) and lets similar agents help each other learn faster (because their experiences are relevant to each other). Cross-task calibration handles continual learning in federated settings where new tasks arrive over time and agents must stay synchronized (so they do not diverge when new tasks appear). GFedCL combines graph structure with federated learning so agents that are spatially close or have similar data can help each other (because nearby agents in the graph share relevant information). Near optimal federated learning defends against poisoning attacks by designing algorithms that trim extreme updates (because attack attempts show up as outliers).

Heterogeneity across agents is not a bug to ignore — it is a feature to exploit. The algorithms learn which agents belong together, what incentives align behavior, and how to clean the data stream.

Continual learning: New tasks without forgetting

Continual learning is the problem of learning a sequence of new tasks one after another without forgetting how to do the old tasks. When you train a neural network on a new task, the weight updates often overwrite what it learned before — this is catastrophic forgetting (because new learning erases old patterns, and the model forgets). The tension is that you want the network to adapt quickly to new data (plasticity, the ability to change, so it learns fast) but also remember old patterns (stability, resisting change, so it does not forget). These papers study different ways to break this plasticity-stability tradeoff: using structural constraints (penalizing certain weight changes to restrict how much weights can move, because limits prevent erasure), designing how neurons compute values (activation functions) in ways that help learning persist (so changes help new tasks without destroying old ones, because the right function design lets both happen), adding memory tricks (so the model stores old information separately from new weights, because separation prevents collision), or distilling knowledge from the previous model (compressing old learning into the new model, so what was learned before is transferred).

Memory-statistics tradeoff proves that adding structural constraints (penalizing certain weight changes) helps you keep good performance on old tasks while learning new ones (so the constraints prevent catastrophic forgetting), though there is a fundamental limit: the more tasks you add, the larger your model must grow (because you cannot fit unlimited information in a fixed-size model, so you run out of space). Activation function design finds that a simple change to how neurons compute values lets the model stay plastic without needing extra neurons (so you can adapt without growing the model, meaning one clever idea beats a larger model). Active continual learning uses a Bayesian approach (computing probabilities over what the model believes) with special neural networks that track uncertainty, which lets the model learn fast on the 1000-task Permuted-MNIST benchmark (because it knows what it is confident about and what it should update, so it does not waste time on certain decisions). Asymmetric synthetic data update generates fake old data on the fly to rehearse old tasks without storing them (creating imaginary examples on demand, so the model remembers by practicing old tasks instead of storing memories). Energy-structured low-rank adaptation uses low-rank weight updates (changes that touch only a few of the weights, not all of them) to compress new learning into a small number of weights (the numbers that control the model's decisions) (so new tasks fit efficiently without overwriting old weights, meaning you save space). Self-distillation enables continual learning by having the old model teach the new model, so knowledge transfers backward in time (because the new model learns from what the old one knew, so information flows from the past).

Each approach trades storage, computation, or model size against performance. The common thread is that you cannot magically add tasks without cost, but you can be smart about where you allocate the cost.

RL fundamentals: Learning from feedback and reasoning

Reinforcement learning means learning a policy — a rule for what action to take in each state — by trying actions and getting rewards. The key difference from bandits is that your action changes the state: if you pick action A, you move to a new state where action B might be better than it was before (so each decision affects what options you have next). Game theory adds another layer: other agents are also learning, each trying to predict what you will do. These papers study how to improve learning from longer feedback sequences (so the learner gets better information) and how reasoning works when actions matter (because consequences ripple through time).

How reinforcement learning after predicting sequences improves learning shows that RL works better than supervised learning because it learns from longer feedback sequences — the learner sees the full consequence of early decisions instead of just the next item (so learning improves because you get more information about whether choices were right, not just immediate feedback, which means better data for learning). Reshaping reasoning in LLMs applies this to language models: RL with verifiable rewards (like getting a math problem right) converges to good strategies (because the model learns what truly works, grounded in correctness), but RL with internal feedback can fail (because guessing whether you are on the right track is unreliable, so self-assessment breaks learning). Achieving logarithmic regret in KL-regularized zero-sum Markov games proves that adding a penalty term (based on how much you diverge from a reference, measuring distance) helps agents reach equilibrium faster (because the penalty is a signal that pulls agents together, so alignment happens mathematically faster). From ticks to flows describes the continuous-time dynamics of RL learning — how the state distribution changes infinitesimally with each small weight update (so you can analyze learning using calculus instead of discrete steps, revealing smooth patterns).

These papers show that longer feedback and mathematical structure both matter in RL: you learn better when you see consequences (because full information beats guessing), and the way rewards are structured shapes how fast learning converges (because structure creates signals agents can follow).

RL in structured environments: Planning and multi-agent games

When actions have structure — deterministic outcomes, partial observability, or leader-follower hierarchy — learning becomes a problem of planning. The challenge is that you must make decisions with incomplete information, other agents are learning too, and you might not know the rules of the environment.

Frozen policy iteration finds a way to train efficiently on deterministic environments without a simulator (so learning scales to large problems, meaning you do not need to run simulations). Minimax-optimal policy regret in partially observable Markov games pins down how much worse you do when you cannot see the full state (because hidden information makes decisions harder, so you must account for guessing). Nearly-optimal bandit learning in Stackelberg games combines bandits (pick an option) with game theory (play a leader-follower game) (so you handle both selection and strategic reasoning, meaning you reason about what others will do). The sample complexity of online reinforcement learning characterizes how many samples a multi-model learner needs to find a good policy (so you know the fundamental cost of learning, the minimum information required).

These papers quantify the cost of structure and partial information. When the world has rules, learning can be more efficient (because structure narrows the search space). When you cannot see everything, learning takes longer (because you waste time exploring what you cannot observe).

Online learning under distribution shift

Online learning assumes you see examples one by one and must make a prediction before you see the label. If the distribution of examples drifts over time (the rules change), or if an adversary picks examples to make you fail, or if the test time is different from training, your old model breaks. These papers study how to adapt as the world changes, how to detect when drift happens, and what information you need to recover quickly. The core question is: how much must you change your predictions when the world shifts?

Online conformal prediction gives you a set of predictions instead of a single guess, and guarantees that the right answer is usually in the set, even if the data distribution shifts or an adversary picks data. Correcting split selection in online decision trees detects when the test dataset is different from training, then fixes the learned splits instead of retraining. DAL (piecewise stationary bandits) figures out when the best option changes and adapts without knowing in advance how often or how much the world will shift. Learning to adapt uses in-context learning — the model sees a few examples from the new distribution and learns to adjust its predictions. On the learnability of test-time adaptation proves fundamental limits on how fast you can recover when the world shifts, and shows there is an intrinsic tradeoff between how much you adapt and how much information you need to do it. The cost of learning under multiple change points characterizes the regret when the best option switches multiple times and you do not know when.

These papers share the insight that drift is inevitable, and the question is not whether you can ignore it but how to detect it quickly and how much it costs to adapt.
