Fostering Video Reasoning via Next-Event Prediction

Train video-understanding models by having them predict what happens next in a video, without human labels.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Multimodal language models (models that read both images and text) can watch videos, but they rarely reason about time the way humans do. This paper introduces next-event prediction: show the model the first half of a video, hide the second half, and train it to predict what happens next. Because predicting the future requires genuine understanding of cause and effect, this task teaches the model to think like a planner.

Zero assumption version

Video models learn to recognize, not reason

Most training tasks for video models ask a simple question: Can you describe what you see? Or: Does this clip show a person jumping? These tasks teach a model to recognize objects and actions in real time, but they do not teach the model to predict what will happen. A model trained this way can label a video frame but struggles with truly temporal reasoning—the kind of thinking a human does when watching a half-finished video and imagining plausible continuations.

Human supervision is expensive. You could ask people to annotate videos with questions and answers (What will the player do next? Will the ball go in?), but that requires hiring annotators and writing detailed labels. You could also use predictions from a much larger model, but then you inherit that model's biases and errors.

The gap in one sentence. Existing video-learning tasks teach recognition (what is in the frame now), not temporal reasoning (what comes next and why).

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Split each video

Divide the video into past frames (what the model sees) and future frames (what it must predict).

Encode the past

The vision encoder converts the past frames into lists of numbers representing what was visible.

Generate a prediction

The language decoder writes out words describing what happens next, word by word.

Compare to the truth

Measure how different the prediction is from a written description of what actually happened.

Adjust the model

Use that error to nudge the numbers inside the model so future predictions are closer to the truth.

What the paper changes

The single thing the model learns to do

This paper changes one thing: the training task. Instead of asking the model to describe a full video, it asks the model to see the first half and predict the second half. That one shift—from description to prediction—forces the model to build an internal model of cause and effect.

the object it moves  =  the learning task (from recognition to prediction)
The hidden quantity

Temporal reasoning: the thing the paper measures

measured quantity  =  Coherence in multi-step predictions

The paper introduces a hidden quantity called temporal reasoning: how well a model can predict a sequence of events that follow logically from what it observes. This is not a single number, but a pattern. If a model watches a child swing on monkey bars, a model with strong temporal reasoning predicts the child will reach the end of the bars, then let go, then fall—in that order. A model without this reasoning might predict the child suddenly teleports or the bars vanish.

The paper measures this by introducing FutureBench, a test set where humans verify whether the model's predicted future is logically coherent and consistent with physics, intent, and common sense. The test asks: Does the next event make sense given what came before?

Why it works

Why predicting the future teaches reasoning

When a model is asked to describe a video, it can succeed by simply naming objects and actions it sees in the current frame. It learns what things look like. But when asked to predict the future, it cannot rely on what is visible—the future is hidden. It must build an internal model of how things work: if a ball is moving at speed V toward a wall at distance D, where will it be in the next frame? To answer, the model must integrate visual observation (how fast the ball moves, the wall position) with learned commonsense knowledge (balls bounce, or sometimes break glass) and predict multiple steps ahead (first collision, then bounce, then landing).

The loss function (the error measure) rewards predictions that match the ground truth future. Every time the model's guess is wrong, the error signal points to which parts of the model's internal reasoning went astray. Over thousands of videos, the model learns to construct cause-and-effect chains, because that is the only way to consistently predict what comes next.

Math after the picture

The training equation

First in words:

Loss equals the sum of prediction errors for each predicted word, compared to the ground-truth future description.

Then the compact form:

Loss = sum over all predicted words w: minus log P(w | past_frames, previous_words)

Every symbol:

Losstotal training error we are trying to minimize
weach word in the ground-truth future description
P(w | past_frames, previous_words)the model's probability of predicting word w given the past video and words predicted so far
loglogarithm (converts probabilities to a convenient numeric scale)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the true future is: 'Child lets go'
Model predicts word 1: 'Child' with probability 0.9 (log = -0.10) -> error on word 1 = 0.10
Model predicts word 2: 'spins' with probability 0.3 (log = -1.20) -> error on word 2 = 1.20
Model predicts word 3: 'up' with probability 0.1 (log = -2.30) -> error on word 3 = 2.30
Total loss = 0.10 + 1.20 + 2.30 = 3.60
If model had correctly predicted 'lets go': loss ≈ 0.10 + 0.15 = 0.25 (much lower)

The model learns to assign higher probability to word sequences that match real future events, because those sequences produce lower loss and smaller weight updates.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Future is ambiguous. In real videos, the future is not deterministic. A soccer player taking a shot might score or miss. If the ground truth says 'miss', but 'score' is also plausible, the model is penalized for assigning low probability to a reasonable alternative. The paper does not fully address this ambiguity problem; it assumes future events are nearly deterministic.
Dataset bias contaminates reasoning. If the V1-33K dataset shows mostly successful basketball shots, the model learns to predict success even when defense is strong. The model will have learned dataset statistics, not generalizable causal reasoning.
Prediction does not require real reasoning. A model can sometimes guess correctly by learning surface patterns (e.g., in videos of children on playgrounds, children almost always end up on the ground or running). Prediction accuracy alone does not guarantee the model built a causal model.
Done right. Test on diverse videos where multiple plausible futures exist; measure whether the model's internal reasoning traces (the numbers changing inside the model during prediction) reflect actual physical or causal logic, not just statistical patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple video dataset: 100 clips of objects moving, dropping, bouncing, or rolling. For each clip, split at a random frame: past is frames 1-N, future is frames N+1-M. Train a small encoder-decoder model (a vision encoder that breaks images into pieces and a text language decoder) on next-event prediction for 20 epochs. Measure performance before and after training on a held-out test set of videos the model has never seen.

Temporal accuracy = (number of multi-step predictions rated coherent by human review) / (total test predictions)

The demo should show that a model trained via prediction achieves higher temporal accuracy than a baseline trained on image captioning only, proving that the prediction task forces the model to reason about cause and effect rather than just recognize visible objects.
