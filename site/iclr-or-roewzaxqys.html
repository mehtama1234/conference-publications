TiMi: Rationality-Driven Agentic System for Quantitative Financial Trading

A multi-agent system that separates strategy planning from fast minute-level trading execution, using LLMs to reason about market patterns while mathematical reflection keeps it rational.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Trading agents built from large language models are often flexible thinkers but poor traders—they waste time reasoning when action is needed and let emotional biases seep in. TiMi splits the work: one layer plans strategies by analyzing market patterns, another layer deploys those plans as fast, mechanical trades minute by minute. This paper shows how to make LLMs profitable at trading without letting them overthink each trade.

Zero assumption version

Why current trading agents fail

A naive approach: give an LLM the market data, ask it to trade every minute, and watch it decide. The LLM is a good reader and writer, so it might seem ready for trading. But it runs into real problems.

First, every trade is slow. The LLM thinks it through, reasoning step by step about why a trade might work—that takes 5 seconds per decision. In trading, a 5-second delay means the market has already moved and the opportunity is gone. Second, LLMs anthropomorphize—they pretend to be a trader, a robot, a bold gambler—and that roleplay leaks emotional bias into decisions. They might chase a trend because they have imagined themselves as overconfident, or freeze because they are afraid. Third, the LLM gets distracted. It reads tangential news, considers edge cases, and overthinks. It wastes computation doing reasoning that does not move money.

The trap: LLMs are flexible thinkers but slow actors; they overthink, get emotional, and cannot trade fast enough to catch minute-by-minute opportunities.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Macro planner reads weekly patterns

An LLM analyzes price charts and market events hour-by-hour, spots which assets look promising, proposes one coherent trade strategy for the next window.

Code generator builds a trading bot

A second LLM takes that strategy and writes out concrete trading code: entry rules, exit rules, risk limits. No ambiguity, just Python rules.

Executor runs the bot every minute

The bot watches market prices tick by, applies its rules mechanically, buys or sells when conditions match. No LLM involved, no delays.

Mathematical reflection scores each trade

After the trade closes, a math engine computes how much the strategy beat a simple baseline, and how confident that win is.

Feedback loop nudges the next strategy

High-confidence wins get reinforced; low-confidence trades get questioned. The planner learns which kinds of strategies actually work.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper splits a single monolithic LLM into two separate machines: a slow strategic thinker and a fast mechanical executor. Traditional trading agents try to do both at once, inside one model. This paper moves the thinking upstream and locks the execution layer into a commit to a pre-made plan.

the object it moves  =  execution mode: from reasoning to pre-committed rules
The hidden quantity

Strategy confidence: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  confidence in a trade pattern

The paper introduces a mathematical score that answers: how much did this strategy beat a random guess, and how sure are we that win was not just luck? This is confidence—measured not by the LLM's opinions but by the gap between the strategy's actual profit and what randomness would predict. It is small when a trade barely beat baseline or only worked a few times; it is large when a trade wins consistently by a fat margin.

The paper estimates confidence by running the strategy rule on past data (called backtesting: replaying what would have happened if the rule had run yesterday). The bigger the margin and the more times it repeated, the higher the confidence. This number then steers which strategies the planner should try next—high-confidence patterns are explored more; flaky patterns are dropped.

Why it works

Why separating strategy from execution works

The core insight: reasoning and acting happen at different speeds. An LLM reasons well but slowly; it should plan once per hour when plenty of time exists. But markets move every minute, so execution must be a reflex—no pausing, no thinking. By building two layers, the paper lets each layer do what it does best.

The strategic planner proposes a hypothesis (for example: 'IBM tends to jump on earnings reports, so buy 1 hour before and sell 1 hour after'). A mathematical reflection engine scores that hypothesis against historical data. If the score is high—meaning it would have worked reliably in the past—the planner trusts it and the executor runs it. If the score is low, the planner scraps it and tries a different idea. This loop of proposing, scoring, and keeping only high-confidence patterns naturally filters out emotional biases and overthinking. The planner has no incentive to be cute or complex; it just needs to beat the baseline. And the executor, freed from thinking, can act instantly.

Separating the layers also protects against cascade failures. If the planner gets stuck on a bad idea, the executor has already locked in rules and cannot be talked out of them mid-trade. The damage is bounded. And if the executor has a bug, it affects only the current trade, not future strategy.

Math after the picture

Confidence as a statistical comparison

First in words:

Confidence = (Strategy return minus Baseline return) divided by (Volatility of the strategy's past returns). Higher means the profit is big and stable; lower means the win was tiny or shaky.

Then the compact form:

confidence = (return_strategy - return_baseline) / volatility_strategy

Every symbol:

return_strategythe percent profit the strategy made on past data
return_baselinethe percent profit a simple buy-and-hold would have made
volatility_strategyhow much the strategy's returns bounced around (standard deviation)
confidencea score: > 1 is good; < 0.5 is weak
A tiny worked example
Strategy A on Apple stock: made 12% profit, baseline made 8%, volatility was 4%.
Confidence A = (12 - 8) / 4 = 4 / 4 = 1.0 (decent)
Strategy B on Tesla: made 10% profit, baseline made 8%, volatility was 8%.
Confidence B = (10 - 8) / 8 = 2 / 8 = 0.25 (weak—the win is tiny relative to the shaking)
Strategy A earned the same as B but is much stabler, so confidence ranks it higher.

Strategies with stable, fat wins over baseline get high confidence; strategies that barely beat randomness or that wobble wildly get low confidence. The planner should repeat high-confidence patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Backtesting overfit. A strategy that looks great on historical data (high confidence) might actually be memorizing random noise in that history. When live trading begins, the strategy fails because real markets move differently. Fix: test the strategy on data the planner never saw, or use methods that penalize complex strategies.
Market regime shift. A strategy that worked when interest rates were falling breaks when they rise. The macro planner built confidence scores on old data; it did not foresee that the entire market structure could change. Fix: monitor for shifts; discard strategies when their confidence drops on new data.
Code bugs in the executor. The LLM's code generator might write bot rules with an off-by-one error, or the executor might apply them wrong. A single bug can drain capital. Fix: add a risk limiter that checks every trade against hard limits (max loss per trade, max daily loss) before executing.
Done right. Combine backtesting with live validation, refresh strategies when confidence decays, and add mechanical safeguards. TiMi includes all three.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy multi-agent trading bot: start with a tiny portfolio and a simple strategy (e.g., 'buy when a 10-minute average price rises past the 50-minute average'). Have the macro planner generate variations of that strategy (different thresholds, different window lengths). Score each variation on the past year of price data for your chosen stock. Pick the highest-confidence variation. Run the executor on the past month of data and measure profit. Then run the executor on fresh, future data (data the planner never saw during backtesting) and measure profit again. The demo succeeds if: (1) the backtest profit is real (the variation actually beats a baseline on old data), (2) the live profit is not zero (the strategy still works on new data), and (3) the two profits are similar (no catastrophic overfit).

real_profit = (portfolio_value_after_live_trades - starting_value) / starting_value; overfit_ratio = backtest_profit / real_profit (should be close to 1.0, not 100.0)

The demo makes it obvious: when you separate strategic reasoning from fast execution and use math to score confidence, you catch strategies that truly work, because backtesting reveals patterns that persist in new data—meaning the strategy is learning real market structure, not memorizing noise.
