Finding the unusual and the break

Spotting when a time series behaves abnormally or suddenly changes character. This includes detecting anomalies, change points, and out-of-distribution events in sequences.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Every sequence has a baseline—the normal range of behavior. A healthy patient's blood pressure stays in a narrow band. A factory bearing vibrates at expected frequencies. Stock prices move within historical ranges. But sometimes something changes. A patient's blood pressure spikes. A bearing starts vibrating at a new frequency, signaling wear. A stock crashes on bad news. These events matter enormously for action: hospitals need to alert staff, factories need to schedule maintenance, traders need to reduce risk. The challenge is that 'unusual' is not always obvious. Is today's temperature 2 degrees warmer than average because the model is wrong, or because climate is shifting? Is a sensor reading an anomaly or just normal noise? Is a change temporary or permanent?

The problem has layers. Anomalies can be sudden spikes, slow drifts, or behavioral changes that are subtle. A sequence can have multiple normal modes—for example, traffic patterns differ on weekdays and weekends, and both are normal. Anomalies can be local, affecting just a small window, or global, shifting the entire distribution. Some anomalies are contextual: an absence of data in a hospital system is normal during maintenance but would be alarming in production. And rare events are, by definition, rare, so labeled training data is scarce. The model has to learn what normal looks like from mostly-normal data, then spot when something deviates.

Papers tackle this by learning baseline models of normal sequences, then flagging values that deviate far from the baseline. Some use reconstruction error: if an autoencoder encodes and decodes the sequence, large reconstruction error suggests anomaly. Others explicitly model change points—moments when the distribution shifts—and estimate where they occur. Some combine statistical tests with neural methods. A key insight is that anomalies are often domain-specific: in healthcare, you care about medical significance, not just statistical significance, so papers sometimes incorporate domain knowledge or causal reasoning to separate true anomalies from noise.

How the work breaks down

Finding anomalies and change points in sequences splits into several natural directions. The core question is how to model what normal looks like, then spot when data deviates from it. Some papers learn to encode and decode sequences, flagging large reconstruction errors as anomalies. Others explicitly model the moments when a time series changes character or when its overall pattern shifts (distribution shifts means the average, spread, or shape of the data changes). A third direction tackles the practical challenge that anomalies are rare, so you must learn from mostly-normal data and adapt when patterns drift over time. A fourth direction handles sequences where data arrives irregularly or contains multiple types of signals that need to be understood together. These directions reflect the fundamental tradeoff: anomalies are context-specific and domain-dependent, so no single approach works everywhere.

Learning baseline models with reconstruction

The core idea here is straightforward: train a model to encode a normal sequence and then reconstruct it perfectly. When a new sequence comes in, run it through the model. If reconstruction fails dramatically, something is wrong. This works because anomalous patterns rarely appear in training data, so the model never learned to reconstruct them cleanly. The challenge is building models that capture the true normal patterns without learning noise as signal, and knowing when a reconstruction error is genuinely anomalous versus just the model struggling with a hard-to-predict moment.

The Adaptive Conformal Anomaly Detection paper achieves strong performance by combining simplicity with built-in guarantees: it gives you a confidence band around what it expects, so you know how much error is normal, and it adapts over time without requiring you to retrain on new data. The ICDiffAD paper uses diffusion models, a newer technique that generates sequences step by step. Diffusion models work by learning to reverse a corruption process, which helps them understand subtle patterns that deterministic models miss, leading to 20% improvement in detection accuracy and 60% fewer false positives. The Low Rank Transformer paper, ALoRa-T, solves a practical problem: it localizes exactly where in a multivariate time series the anomaly happened, not just that one occurred. The PaAno paper takes a simpler direction, using small patches of the sequence rather than modeling the whole thing at once, and shows this beats methods using heavier architectures. Finally, the When Foundation Models are One-Liners paper is critical: it reveals that large pretrained foundation models, despite their power, do not beat simple baselines on this task when using reconstruction, which challenges the assumption that bigger models solve anomaly detection.

Explicit change point and drift detection

Anomalies come in two flavors: sudden spikes and gradual shifts. A sudden spike in blood pressure is obvious; a patient's baseline slowly creeping upward over weeks is subtle but real. Change point detection directly models the moments when a time series switches into a new regime. The challenge is that sequences rarely have one clean break. Traffic patterns shift gradually across seasons. A sensor might have one fault, get fixed, then fail again. The statistical problem is proving that a change actually occurred rather than just observing random fluctuation. And when change points are proven to exist, the model must work with minimal labels because true change points are rare in real data.

The CALIPER paper addresses a practical downstream problem: once you detect a change, when should you retrain your model on new data? Too early and you waste compute; too late and you miss the shift. CALIPER answers this by checking if the amount of new data is enough to stabilize a model without human tuning, and does this check from data alone. The Beyond Euclidean Summaries paper tackles a harder statistical case: detecting when entire distributions shift, not just the average value or variance. It uses sequential methods to flag when the change occurred with short detection delay. The Change Point Localization paper provides the first formal consistency guarantees for detecting change points in network data, where connections themselves change over time. The Online Change Point Detection for Poisson Processes paper works on count data (like fault events), where traditional anomaly methods break down, and provides formal guarantees on false alarm rates.

Capturing seasonal patterns and context

Real sequences are rarely stationary. Traffic is heavier on weekdays. Hospital vitals follow circadian rhythms. A factory's energy consumption is different in summer and winter. These are not anomalies; they are normal modes. The challenge is learning to distinguish the expected variation that comes from time-of-day or season from true anomalies that signal a problem. Additionally, in healthcare, a missing signal during scheduled maintenance is fine, but the same absence during live monitoring is alarming. So context determines what counts as abnormal.

The Contextual and Seasonal LSTMs paper directly models these temporal patterns by building separate models for different time periods and contexts, so the algorithm learns what normal means in each setting. The Enhancing Sparse Event Detection in Healthcare paper focuses on detecting rare medical events like arrhythmias buried in long sequences of normal rhythm. It uses a gating mechanism to selectively focus the network's processing toward moments where something important happens, achieving strong gains especially on sparse events. The COGNOS paper provides a statistical regularization approach: it smooths noisy detections and constrains the model using Gaussian noise optimization, which improves any baseline algorithm without requiring retraining. The Cluster-Aware Causal Mixer paper models relationships between channels in multivariate data by grouping similar channels together, then learning how they influence each other, which helps catch anomalies that are defined by unusual relationships between signals rather than individual signal values going out of bounds.

Reasoning and open-set anomalies

As models improve, a new problem emerges: the anomalies you see in production often look nothing like the rare anomalies you trained on, because training data is collected in labs and real failure modes are diverse and unexpected. You might see an anomaly from a new failure mode never encountered before. Additionally, practitioners want to know not just that an anomaly occurred, but why. Did the blood pressure spike due to stress, medication, or a real medical crisis? The model's internal number is not actionable without interpretation. These papers push in the direction of reasoning about anomalies and handling situations where the true anomalies are not drawn from the training distribution.

The AnomSeer paper pairs large language models with time series, so the model can reason through why an anomaly happened by reading the data and explaining its logic. This makes alerts actionable because practitioners can act on a specific explanation rather than guessing: instead of a raw alert, practitioners get a sentence explaining what went wrong, so they know what to check first. The IMPACT paper handles open-set detection, where the anomalies in testing include types the model never saw during training. This is realistic: no training set covers every possible failure. It uses influence modeling to compute which training points are similar to a test example, helping generalize better to new anomaly types by recognizing patterns similar to ones the model saw, even if the specific anomaly comes from a different category. The Towards Foundation Models for Zero-Shot Time Series Anomaly Detection paper, TimeRCD, trains on synthetic anomalies that do not match the test distribution, then uses a measure called relative context discrepancy to detect anomalies in new domains. The goal is a foundation model that works on new sequences without domain-specific tuning, which trades some accuracy for the ability to deploy without per-dataset retraining.

Handling messy and multimodal data

Real-world time series data is messy. Measurements may arrive at irregular intervals, with gaps and missing values. A single sequence may contain multiple types of signals: video frames plus temperature readings, or text logs plus numerical metrics. Foundation models, despite their power, hit practical limits on these harder variants of the problem. The goal is making anomaly detection work on data as it arrives in production, not on clean benchmark datasets.

The Complexity and Statistics-Guided Anomaly Detection paper identifies why foundation models struggle: they excel at reconstructing easy examples but fail to calibrate their uncertainty properly when facing data they should reject as anomalous. The Hierarchical ODE paper learns continuous physical prototypes from irregularly sampled data, extracting the underlying dynamics before detecting failure, which works well when sensors report at irregular intervals. The MPFM paper handles log data, where anomalies are sequences of messages that do not fit expected patterns. It learns prototype flows across multiple data sources at once, generalizing better than single-domain models. The PGRF-Net paper models diagnostic time series by fusing multiple evidence sources and making the anomaly score transparent by decomposing it into four interpretable components. The PYRREGULAR paper and QuITE paper both tackle irregular time series where time gaps matter. QuITE uses query-based numerical representations (a compressed summary of each time point) to handle missing values and variable-rate sampling. The Towards Multimodal Time Series Anomaly Detection paper combines semantic information across modalities (e.g., video and sensor readings together) so that anomalies in one modality can be cross-validated against others.
