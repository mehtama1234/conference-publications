Training and controlling generative models

Papers on how to train models more stably, guide them with preferences, and align their outputs with what users actually want.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Once you've built a generative model, how do you make sure it does what you want? Training stability is one issue: generative models can be finicky to train—they oscillate, they collapse to repetitive outputs, they forget parts of what they learned. Another issue is alignment: the model might learn to generate technically good images according to the training data, but images that are offensive, inaccurate, or unhelpful for the actual use case. A third is control: once trained, how do you steer the model? If you want it to generate images of dogs but not cats, or to emphasize safety, or to match what people actually prefer, how do you do that without retraining?

Training methods here focus on stability: what hyperparameters make training converge smoothly (so the model doesn't oscillate or collapse), which matters because an unstable model never finishes learning or forgets what it learned? How should you structure loss functions—the measures of 'how wrong' the model is—so the model learns efficiently and doesn't waste computation, which is important because training is expensive and poor loss functions waste compute on wrong directions? Should you use adversarial training (where one model generates and another critiques, which forces both to improve), which can work because competition drives both models to perform better? How do you handle class imbalance (when some types of objects are much more common than others in your training data, which can make the model lazy and only generate common things because common things are easier), so the model learns to generate everything it should and doesn't neglect uncommon categories? For alignment and control, researchers use several strategies. Preference learning means showing the model pairs of outputs and asking which is better, then training to match human preferences, so the model learns what humans actually want rather than just what data happens to have. Reinforcement learning from human feedback means scoring model outputs and training to maximize the score, which lets you guide the model toward specific goals by directly rewarding the behaviors you want. Classifier guidance means using a separate model to steer generation—'generate an image that looks like a dog (according to a dog-classifier) and matches the text description,' which works because you can tell the model to follow both the text instruction and the classifier's opinion.

Papers in this area typically propose a new training method (like a better loss function) or a new control mechanism (like a way to steer generation without retraining, which matters because retraining is expensive), test it on standard datasets, and show it improves stability (fewer training failures) or alignment (model outputs match what users want) compared to baselines. The work is often unglamorous—it's engineering and careful experimentation—but critical for making models reliable enough to deploy because a model that sometimes breaks or ignores instructions is not trustworthy.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into four natural directions based on the core problems researchers solve. First, alignment methods train models to match human preferences using reinforcement learning, which matters because models would otherwise learn whatever the training data shows (often mediocre examples mixed with good ones). Second, control methods steer motion and video generation toward user intent, which matters because retraining is too expensive and you need steering without retraining. Third, efficiency and stability methods fix problems where models oscillate, collapse, or forget during training, which matters because broken training wastes weeks of compute. Fourth, structured generation methods enable complex scenes with precise layouts and relationships, which matters because many real tasks need not just good images but images where objects are in the right places. Each tackles a different piece of making generative models actually work in practice.

Aligning outputs with human preferences

Once a generative model is trained, how do you make it generate what people actually prefer? This is the alignment problem: the model might produce technically valid images, videos, or text, but ones that miss what users care about—clarity, style, emotional tone, or specific details. Simply training on data doesn't solve this because training data often includes mediocre examples alongside good ones. The papers in this group tackle preference alignment by collecting examples of what humans like and training the model to match those preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), which means scoring outputs and then tuning the model to maximize the score.

TempFlow-GRPO shows that when training flow-based models for text-to-image generation, the timing of when the model makes decisions matters—it captures the temporal structure of how generation unfolds step-by-step, which lets it learn preferences better because the model can see the order in which choices matter most. TP-GRPO (Alleviating Sparse Rewards by Modeling Step-Wise and Long-Term Sampling Effects in Flow-Based GRPO) improves alignment when reward signals are scarce by distinguishing between immediate step-by-step rewards and longer-term outcomes, so the model learns to value both short-term and big-picture goals. AlphaGRPO extends this by using a decomposable reward system where different reward components score different aspects of the output (does it match the text? is it visually good? is it safe?), letting the model balance multiple preferences at once. FocusDiff (Benchmarking and Improving Fine-Grained Text-to-Image Alignment via Paired Reinforcement Learning) learns alignment by showing the model pairs of generated images—one better, one worse—and letting it figure out why, which is efficient because you learn from comparisons rather than absolute scores. Flow Map Reward Guidance shows that you can steer generation toward user preferences with very few steps (as few as 3) by using a learned reward signal directly in the generation process, which makes preference-aligned generation fast enough for real-time use. iGRPO accelerates this further by enabling instant reward feedback during training (not waiting until generation finishes), converging 10 times faster than standard GRPO while reaching higher quality.

Controlling motion and video generation

Video and motion generation require a different kind of control than images: a user wants specific objects or people to move in specific ways, not just generic motion. The challenge is that retraining a model for each new motion request is prohibitively expensive, so instead you need methods to steer a trained model toward desired motion without retraining. This group focuses on efficient fine-tuning and direct trajectory control during generation.

EasyTune shows that fine-tuning a diffusion model for motion generation can be done efficiently by applying updates only at certain timesteps rather than everywhere, reducing memory overhead by 68% while improving alignment quality by 9%. AR-Drag achieves real-time motion-controllable video generation with autoregressive models (models that generate output one piece at a time)—users can drag points on the video to specify where objects should move—while maintaining high visual fidelity using a relatively small model with 1.3 billion model weights. Dual-IPO improves video generation quality using iterative preference optimization, where the model learns from human feedback across multiple rounds, allowing a smaller model to match or exceed larger baselines. Motion-R1 enhances motion generation by decomposing the generation task into reasoning steps (like "what direction should this body part move?") and using RL to learn these chains of thought, improving motion quality on standard benchmarks. TAGRPO (Boosting GRPO on Image-to-Video Generation with Direct Trajectory Alignment) directly aligns generated motion trajectories to high-reward examples, maintaining diversity by drawing from a bank of diverse successful motions, so generated videos match human-preferred motion patterns without becoming repetitive.

Stable and efficient training

Training generative models is computationally expensive and often unstable—models can oscillate wildly, collapse to repetitive outputs, or get stuck. This group tackles fundamental training problems: how to structure training so convergence is smooth and fast, and how to avoid wasting computation on poor learning directions.

Anomaly-Preference Image Generation improves training stability by introducing Time-Aware Capacity Allocation, which adjusts how the model allocates its learning capacity at each training step, avoiding bottlenecks where the model spends wasted computation on already-solved parts, which means training finishes sooner because no compute is wasted. FAPO (Flawed-Aware Policy Optimization for Efficient and Reliable Reasoning) improves training reliability by explicitly handling training failures and instability—instead of hoping they don't happen, it learns to predict and avoid them, so the model learns both the right answers and how to get there reliably. Towards Sequence Modeling Alignment between Tokenizer and Autoregressive Model shows that tokenizers (the part that breaks text into pieces the model can process) and autoregressive models should train together in alignment rather than separately, because when they're misaligned the model wastes effort translating between them; joint training means the model learns efficient handling from both sides. What matters for Representation Alignment (iREPA) demonstrates that spatial information—how parts of an image relate to each other—is critical for alignment because the model needs to know that positions matter, and a simple 4-line code modification captures this, which speeds up learning across many different encoder designs. Adversarial Flow Models use adversarial training (where one model generates and another critiques) to achieve high-quality generation with very few refinement steps, because criticism forces both models to improve, reaching quality in one step that normally takes four refinement rounds. Contrastive Flow Map Matching improves training by using contrastive learning (comparing what the model should generate to what it shouldn't) during training, sharpening the model's ability to distinguish good from bad, improving quality with only modest additional computation.

Structured and compositional generation

Generating complex scenes with multiple objects, specific layouts, and precise relationships is harder than generating simple images. A model needs to understand how objects relate spatially (a dog sitting on grass, not grass sitting on a dog), logically (if you ask for a knight, generate armor), and semantically (safety considerations). This group addresses composition: explicit structure, reasoning about relationships, and alignment for complex scenes.

Generate Any Scene (Scene Graph Driven Data Synthesis) uses scene graphs—formal specifications of what objects exist and how they relate—to generate training data systematically, which ensures the model sees diverse relationship combinations, then uses GRPO to fine-tune the model to prefer compositionally correct outputs; this improves performance on hard compositional concepts by 10% because the model has both data and preference examples to learn from. Best of Both Worlds (Multimodal Reasoning and Generation via Unified Discrete Flow Matching) unifies reasoning and generation in one model using flow matching on discrete pieces, so the model can think through relationships and generate images together in one pass, reaching top performance on eight benchmarks including compositional tasks because reasoning and generation inform each other. GoT-R1 (Unleashing Reasoning Capability of Autoregressive Visual Generation with Reinforcement Learning) adds explicit reasoning to image generation by decomposing the task—the model first reasons about what spatial relationships and components are needed, then generates—which helps the model plan before acting, improving performance on compositional benchmarks by a large margin because planning and generation separate the reasoning work. StructMAR (Structure-Aware Masked Autoregression for Explicit Layout Alignment in Text-to-Image Generation) directly optimizes for layout correctness by training the model to explicitly predict object positions and semantic segmentation masks, which makes the layout signal visible during training, achieving precise spatial alignment. Think-Then-Generate pairs an LLM encoder that reasons about the text prompt with the image diffusion model, so complex descriptions are first understood logically before generation, achieving reasoning quality nearly on par with GPT-4 because the LLM does the interpretation work upfront rather than asking the image model to guess.

Reward modeling and evaluation

Preference alignment and reinforcement learning depend on reward signals—ways to score whether an output is good. But reward models are expensive to train, and sometimes an external model (like a vision model) is too slow or gives misleading signals. This group focuses on efficient reward modeling, learning preferences directly, and improving how models judge their own outputs.

Beyond VLM-Based Rewards (DiNa-LRM) learns reward models that work directly within the diffusion model's internal representation space (the compressed format the model uses internally), avoiding expensive vision model calls, because operating in the model's own space avoids converting between formats; it reaches performance that matches the best vision-model-based rewards at a fraction of the computational cost. Diffusion Fine-Tuning via Reparameterized Policy Gradient of the Soft Q-Function (SQDF) learns reward signals during fine-tuning by treating the generation task as a reinforcement learning problem where the model learns to maximize its own soft Q-function (a learned estimate of how good a generated output is), which incentivizes diversity-preserving choices while reaching high reward because the model learns what makes outputs good. DreamCS uses unpaired 3D data—raw 3D shape examples without paired text descriptions—to learn rewards that score 3D shape quality, which sidesteps the pairing problem, so the model learns what makes shapes and spatial structures good without expensive paired annotations because rewards can be learned from raw examples. PCPO (Proportionate Credit Policy Optimization for Preference Alignment of Image Generation Models) fixes instability in preference learning by better tracking which parts of the generation process deserve credit or blame, which smooths training, so it converges faster and reaches higher quality than earlier methods because the model isn't confused about what caused success or failure. Reconstruction Alignment Improves Unified Multimodal Models shows that having the model explicitly reconstruct intermediate results during training helps it learn alignment better, because checking its own work forces the model to understand what it generated, improving generation quality on multiple benchmarks. Threshold-Guided Optimization for Visual Generative Models learns from simple scalar feedback (e.g., "this is good or bad") rather than full reward models, which is cheaper and simpler, so humans can provide alignment signal without training complex reward networks because binary feedback is enough to guide preference learning.
