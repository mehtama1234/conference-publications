Making sense of video

Helping models understand what happens over time in videos, track changes, and answer questions about video content without watching every single frame.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A video is a sequence of images played in quick succession. To understand it, a model needs to grasp not just what is in each individual frame but what is changing, what is moving, and how events unfold over time. You could theoretically process every single frame independently, but that is wasteful and misses the point — video is fundamentally about change. A person walking across a room is the same person in frame one and frame two, but their position changed. A ball falling shows acceleration. A scene getting darker might mean the sun is setting or a shadow is crossing. Understanding video means building a representation of temporal flow, causality, and continuity, not just a stack of static pictures.

The hardness comes from multiple angles. First, videos are huge. A minute of video at 30 frames per second is 1,800 frames. Processing all of them with a heavy image encoder is computationally expensive. Second, you need to figure out what matters. If a camera is fixed on an empty parking lot for an hour with one car parking for five minutes, the important information is 'a car arrived,' not 'most of the lot was empty.' Models need to learn to focus on significant events. Third, some understanding requires longer-term reasoning. If you watch a movie scene, you need to remember who characters are from earlier in the film, understand plot threads, track relationships. Finally, questions about video require different reasoning: action recognition asks 'what is happening?', object tracking asks 'where is that specific thing?' (and it should stay the same thing across frames), video captioning asks 'describe this event in words,' and video question answering asks 'answer this question about the video content.'

Researchers are building video models that capture temporal structure efficiently. Some use frame sampling — instead of processing every frame, process every fifth or tenth frame, or use a model that learns which frames are most important. Others build models that track motion and flow — computing how pixels move between frames — which lets the model understand not just how things look but how they are moving. Tracking motion this way uses far less computation than processing every pixel in every frame, so it's more efficient. Some use three-dimensional convolutions, treating video like a three-dimensional grid (width, height, time) and letting the model learn patterns that span time. Others compress temporal information early, building a compact summary of 'what happened' before processing details. For action recognition, some models track skeletons or optical flow (the apparent motion of pixels) rather than working with raw video. For object tracking, the model needs to maintain an identity across frames — 'that is the same person even though their angle changed.' The common thread is that all these approaches are trying to make video understanding efficient while preserving the temporal and causal information that makes video different from just a pile of images.

How the work breaks down

Video understanding splits into four natural directions: reducing computation by sampling the most important frames or using motion-based representations instead of raw pixels; reasoning about what will happen next or finding exact moments when events occur in long videos; handling the complexity of real-world streaming where the model receives video as it arrives, not all at once; and handling specific tasks like tracking specific objects across frames, summarizing videos, or answering questions about video content. Each direction tackles a different kind of hardness in temporal reasoning.

Sampling frames smartly

Video is huge — a 20-minute video is over 36,000 frames. Processing all of them is wasteful because most frames are nearly identical to their neighbors. The reality is that you do not need to look at every frame to understand what happened; you just need to pick the frames where something important changes. The challenge is learning which frames actually matter without having a human manually label them.

FOCUS picks less than 2% of frames from long videos by learning which frames contain the key information for the task at hand. Instead of feeding every frame to a language model, it identifies critical keyframes and runs the model only on those, which cuts computation dramatically while actually improving accuracy because the model sees more useful information per frame. Next-Event Prediction takes a different angle — it trains models to predict what will happen next in a video, and this prediction task turns out to teach the model about temporal structure because understanding causality and time flow is what you need to predict the future; when you get better at guessing the future, you also get better at understanding what is happening now. VideoBrain learns to adaptively choose which frames to process based on the content, achieving improvements while using 30-40% fewer frames than a baseline. All three show that smartly sampling frames is better than processing every one because you waste computation on repetitive frames, so selecting only frames with important changes lets the model focus on actual information.

These papers show a spectrum: FOCUS uses task-aware importance, NEP uses predictive learning, and VideoBrain learns the sampling strategy end-to-end. They share the core idea that temporal reasoning does not require every frame, and picking the right subset saves computation while preserving or even improving understanding because you are cutting out the noise of repeated frames and letting the model focus on moments where something actually changes.

Finding moments and temporal grounding

In many video tasks, you need to find the exact moment something happens. A question might ask 'when does the character realize the betrayal?' or 'show me the instant when the door opens.' This is temporal grounding — linking a natural language query or event description to a specific time interval in video. The challenge is that the model must understand both language and video deeply enough to match them, and it must reason about causality and evidence (the door opens *because* someone turns the handle).

Foresee-to-Ground improves grounding by first predicting what will happen next, which teaches the model to reason forward through time; this helps it understand causality and why events happen when they do, making it better at pinpointing exact moments. VideoMind uses a chain-of-reasoning approach with specialized adapters for different types of grounding questions, so it can handle 'find this action,' 'find when this object appears,' or 'find when this relationship emerges' as distinct problems. VideoTrace-R1 handles very long videos by retrieving the most relevant clips first, then doing detailed reasoning only on those clips, so it scales to the kinds of hours-long video that would overwhelm models processing everything. Towards One-to-Many Temporal Grounding extends the problem: instead of finding one moment for a query, it finds all the moments when something happens, which requires understanding that the same event can repeat or occur in different forms (a person can greet the camera multiple times in a video).

The group shows a progression: basic grounding (Foresee-to-Ground), multi-task grounding (VideoMind), retrieval-based grounding for scale (VideoTrace-R1), and repeated-event grounding (One-to-Many). All treat temporal grounding as a reasoning task where understanding causality and structure is as important as matching text to image, because matching a query to a moment requires understanding not just what things look like but why the event happens and how it fits into the video's narrative.

Real-time streaming and proactive response

In real-world scenarios, videos arrive as streams — a camera is live-streaming, or a model must answer questions about a video as it is happening, not after the entire video is available. This creates a fundamentally different problem: you must decide when to respond before you have seen all the information. If a model waits until the end of the video to answer 'what is the person doing?', the answer comes too late. The challenge is deciding exactly when you have enough evidence to answer correctly, and being smart about what information is relevant to each query.

MMDuet2 improves the timing of responses in interactive video Q&A by using reinforcement learning to learn when the model should speak up. Instead of waiting for a prompt, it proactively answers when the evidence is strong enough, which means users get answers faster without having to request them. PhoStream reveals a hard asymmetry: models are good at answering questions about things that already happened (looking backward), but they fail when asked to predict or decide based on cues that have not yet arrived, because they respond before the necessary visual or audio information appears. This temporal mismatch — responding too early — is a core failure mode that comes from the model being forced to commit before all evidence is available. Progressive Online Video Understanding improves this by aligning the model's thinking process with the evidence; it shows its reasoning step-by-step, making it clear when the model has enough information to commit to an answer. QueryStream handles streaming in a different way: instead of waiting, it prunes away irrelevant frames in real-time so the model processes only the parts of the video that matter to the current query, which lets it respond faster because it is not bogged down in irrelevant information. StreamingVLM pushes to the extreme — handling infinite video streams with 2-hour videos while maintaining 8 frames per second (FPS), staying interactive even on extremely long inputs.

These papers converge on the core challenge of streaming: the model must be proactive (ready to respond when evidence is strong) rather than reactive (waiting until the input ends), and it must filter the stream intelligently. The timing problem — responding before all evidence arrives — is different from offline video understanding and requires distinct solutions.

Reasoning and modality fusion

Video understanding involves multiple modalities — you see motion in frames, hear sound, and sometimes have text overlays or descriptions. It also involves different types of reasoning: spatial (where things are), temporal (when things happen), causal (why things happen), and metaphorical (what patterns do these events resemble?). Many papers improve performance by better combining these modalities and reasoning types, because each modality carries different information (audio tells you about dialogue and emotion, images tell you about composition and movement, text tells you about context), so using them together gives a more complete picture than any one alone.

VideoLoom builds a model that explicitly learns both spatial and temporal structure, hitting top performance on benchmarks that test either dimension separately or together. The key is that spatial understanding (what objects are where) and temporal understanding (how positions change) strengthen each other because knowing where something is helps you track it over time, and tracking it over time teaches you spatial relationships. 4DPC-Chat extends this to dynamic point clouds — representing video as time-varying 3D positions rather than images — which is a very different modality but requires similar reasoning about how objects move and interact over time. AD-MIR tackles advertising video understanding by using structured reasoning, decomposing the task into perceptual steps (what do I see?) and persuasion steps (how is this trying to convince me?), which shows that some video tasks require domain-specific reasoning strategies because advertising videos are designed to influence, not just inform. Decomposed Attention Fusion (which combines information about different parts of the image and different time steps) shows that you can reason about multiple things in video without retraining — by intelligently combining signal across different visual and temporal features, it achieves training-free reasoning segmentation (finding which pixels changed based on a description) because the model can learn to weight its internal signals differently without updating its weights. Divid improves efficiency by disentangling spatial and temporal reasoning, so the model does not redundantly process the same spatial information at every timestep, which saves computation. Task-Aware Mechanism uses a mixture-of-experts vision tower, where different expert modules specialize in different aspects of video (motion, color, semantics), and the model routes information to experts based on the task, so question-answering uses different experts than action recognition because these tasks need different visual cues. TripleSumm fuses video, audio, and text for summarization — these three modalities carry redundant and complementary information, so combining them produces better summaries than video alone because some events are only visible in one modality (dialogue only in audio, visual composition only in video, scene context only in text). VidBridge-R1 trains one model to handle both Q&A and captioning by using intermediate proxy tasks, showing that these two reasoning types share underlying structure because both require understanding what is happening and translating that understanding to language. Video-BCI uses Bayesian reasoning (computing probabilities and updating beliefs based on evidence) to integrate predictions from multiple sub-models and initial assumptions, reducing bias and performing better across different types of videos because multiple predictors average out individual errors. VideoNSA uses sparse attention over tokens (short pieces of information representing parts of the input) to scale video understanding to very long sequences while preserving both temporal and spatial reasoning, which works because not every part of a long video is equally important to the current query.

This group spans many different techniques — spatial-temporal fusion, multi-modality, expert routing, Bayesian reasoning, sparse attention (a way of processing information where the model focuses on only the most relevant pieces instead of everything at once) — but they all aim to improve the core reasoning backbone. They show that video understanding is not just a scaling problem; it is a reasoning problem that benefits from explicitly modeling structure and combining different types of information, because raw performance depends on the model understanding relationships between space and time, between text and vision, and between different modalities.

Enabling video understanding without expensive retraining

Retraining large video language models on every new task is expensive. A more practical approach is to take an already-trained model and use techniques that improve its performance without modifying its weights. This is valuable because it lets people use public models like LLaVA-Video or Qwen2.5-VL without access to the original training infrastructure.

The training-free framework via Video-Query-Options Similarity improves long-video understanding by reformulating the task at inference time — instead of asking the model to read the entire video, it breaks the question into smaller sub-questions (options), runs the model on each option with different frame samples, and combines results. This beats retraining while running much faster because you can deploy it immediately on existing models. A.I.R. improves frame selection at inference time by teaching the model to reason about which frames matter for a specific question; even though the frame selection is not trainable, the reasoning process inside the model guides better sampling because the model already knows how to identify relevant information, just not where to look for it. MetaphorVU shows that language models already understand metaphor reasonably well, and adding a simple training signal (MetaphorBoost) that highlights metaphorical comparisons further improves metaphorical video understanding without retraining the core model because amplifying an existing capability is easier than building it from scratch. VideoJudge shows that a small model (7 billion weights to learn) can be bootstrapped with a few examples to judge video quality and correctness on par with much larger models, because teaching the model to evaluate videos helps it learn what makes video understanding hard.

These four papers share a philosophy: a trained model already contains useful reasoning, and the place to improve is at inference time — how you query the model, what context you provide, how you aggregate answers. This is practical because you can deploy improvements immediately without retraining infrastructure.
