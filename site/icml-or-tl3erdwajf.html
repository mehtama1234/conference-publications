Belief Dynamics: Unifying In-Context Learning and Activation Steering

Both in-context learning and activation steering work by changing a model's belief about hidden concepts, and these changes can be predicted with Bayesian math.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

A large language model can be steered in two different ways: by feeding it examples in the prompt (in-context learning), or by directly tweaking the numbers inside the model (activation steering). Researchers had explained these as separate tricks, but this paper shows they are two paths to the same place — both change the model's internal belief about what abstract ideas (like truthfulness or helpfulness) are true. Using Bayesian math, the authors write down a formula that predicts exactly how the model will behave when you use either method, and show it matches real measurements.

Zero assumption version

Two knobs, one mystery

Large language models are black boxes. You ask them a question, and numbers inside the model flow through layers of math to produce an answer. Sometimes the answer is exactly what you want, sometimes it is not. Researchers found two ways to nudge a model toward the right answer without retraining.

The first: feed examples in the prompt. If you show the model five true statements before asking a question, it tends to answer truthfully. The second: reach inside and change the numbers (the activations, which are intermediate calculations) directly. Both tricks work, but they seem completely different — one is about context, the other is about surgical internal tweaks. Researchers had explained them separately, as if they were unrelated hacks.

The model's internal belief drives both. Instead of two separate methods, the paper shows they both work by changing what the model believes about hidden abstract ideas.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper does not invent a new method. Instead, it changes the way we understand two existing methods. Specifically, it proposes that what steering and in-context learning both move is the model's internal belief distribution — a mathematical summary of how confident the model is about a hidden abstract concept. Researchers had treated steering and in-context learning as separate phenomena; this paper unifies them by showing they both nudge this one underlying belief.

the object it moves  =  belief distribution
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: internal concept belief

measured quantity  =  model's belief about a hidden concept

A hidden concept is an abstract idea the model has learned, like truthfulness or politeness. You cannot see it directly — it is buried in the numbers. The model's belief about that concept is a probability (a number from 0 to 1), representing how strongly the model thinks that concept is true in the current context. When you show the model examples in a prompt, its belief grows, like a person reading more and more votes in an election. When you steer by changing internal numbers, you instantly reset that belief to a new value.

The paper measures this belief by running the model on test questions and observing what it outputs. If it gives truthful answers, the hidden belief is high. If it gives false answers, the belief is low. The Bayesian model lets the authors estimate this belief mathematically from the observed behavior.

Why it works

Why both methods move the same belief

The model encodes information about concepts in two places: (1) in its starting assumptions (the prior belief, which steering changes), and (2) in how it processes context (gathering evidence from examples). A Bayesian model is a natural fit because it has exactly these two knobs.

When you feed examples in the prompt, the model treats each one as evidence. It combines the evidence with its starting belief, just as a statistician updates a prior with new data. The math of Bayesian updates predicts how much each example nudges the final belief. When you steer, you skip the evidence phase and directly set the starting belief. Either path produces a final belief, and that belief drives the next action the model takes.

The mechanism works because the model's behavior is downstream of its belief. In other words, the model does not independently decide on truth and on politeness — it uses the same abstract internal belief to guide all of its decisions in that domain. By unifying how that belief is formed, the paper unifies the two control methods.

Math after the picture

The equation: Bayesian belief update

First in words:

log(posterior odds) equals log(prior odds) plus log(evidence ratio); or, the log-belief-ratio after learning equals the log-belief-ratio before plus a sum of evidence contributions.

Then the compact form:

log(B_final / (1 - B_final)) = log(B_prior / (1 - B_prior)) + sum_i log(L_i)

Every symbol:

B_finalthe model's final belief (0 to 1), a probability
B_priorthe starting belief before any examples
L_ithe likelihood of the i-th example; how much it favors the concept
loglogarithm; converts multiplication into addition
log-oddslog(p / (1 - p)), a way to measure belief that is linear in Bayesian math
A tiny worked example
Start: the model's prior belief that a statement is true is 0.5 (50-50), so log-odds = log(0.5 / 0.5) = 0.
Example 1: a true statement adds evidence log(L_1) = 1.0, so new log-odds = 0 + 1.0 = 1.0, which maps to belief 0.73 (about 73%).
Example 2: another true statement adds log(L_2) = 1.0, so log-odds = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0, belief becomes 0.88 (about 88%).
Steering: directly set log-odds to 2.5, belief becomes 0.92 (about 92%), skipping the examples.
Two paths, one belief space: examples climb the log-odds scale; steering jumps it directly.

The math says belief lives in log-odds space, where adding evidence is literally adding numbers, not multiplying. This is why the paper predicts additivity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The concept must be stable. If the model's hidden concept is not fixed (if truthfulness means something different in different domains), the Bayesian model breaks down. Steering or in-context learning works, but the equation does not unify them because they are altering different internal concepts.
Evidence must be interpreted consistently. If examples in the prompt are ambiguous or contradictory, the model may not process them as evidence for the concept. The likelihood term becomes unreliable, and the prediction fails.
Belief space must be linear. The math assumes the model stores belief in a linear space (log-odds). If the actual internal representation is nonlinear or multimodal, the additivity prediction breaks.
Done right: Bayesian updates match real behavior. When the concept is stable, evidence is clear, and the belief space is linear, the Bayesian formula predicts the model's output with high accuracy across both in-context and steering interventions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model and train it on a task where one abstract concept (like answering truthfully) is useful. Then measure the model's behavior under three conditions: (1) with zero examples in the prompt, (2) with N examples showing the concept, and (3) with activation steering that directly sets the internal belief. Collect the output accuracy in each case. Fit a Bayesian model to predict the accuracy curve as examples accumulate, and also as steering strength increases. Compare predicted vs. observed accuracy on held-out test questions.

prediction error = average of (predicted accuracy - observed accuracy) squared, across all in-context and steering conditions

If the error is small, you have shown that a single Bayesian belief model can predict both types of intervention, and that the two seemingly different control methods are actually two views of one unified mechanism.
