Measuring and Mitigating Post-Hoc Rationalization in Reverse Chain-of-Thought Generation

When you ask a model to explain its answer after seeing it, the answer acts as an anchor that distorts the entire explanation—so the paper breaks the anchor by building a neutral skeleton first.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When a model generates reasoning traces after seeing the answer, it is not thinking freely. The answer acts like a magnet, pulling the entire explanation toward justifying what is already known. The paper introduces a new way to measure this anchoring effect (across three levels of subtlety) and discovers that simply hiding the answer backfires—it makes the model think harder about the forbidden answer and become even more dependent on it. Instead, the paper builds an answer-neutral skeleton first, then uses that skeleton to guide the reasoning trace.

Zero assumption version

The anchor problem: when models see the answer first

Reverse Chain-of-Thought (the paper's main case) works backward: given a question and its answer, the model is asked to generate the reasoning steps that led to it. This sounds reasonable—if you know the answer is correct, what were the steps? But the problem is that the answer acts as a cognitive anchor. Once the model sees that the answer is, say, 42, it will unconsciously bend every reasoning step to point toward 42, instead of reasoning honestly. The model is not lying—it is rationalizing, filling in plausible explanations that fit the known endpoint.

A beginner might try semantic suppression: tell the model to ignore the answer, or mask it out. The intuition is right—remove the anchor, and reasoning should be free. But the paper shows this backfires. The forbidden answer becomes impossible to ignore; the model spends mental effort actively avoiding it, which paradoxically binds it more tightly to the answer. The model ends up depending even more on the anchored answer, because now it is consciously monitoring and resisting it.

In one sentence. Build an answer-independent skeleton first; then use it as a neutral guide to generate the reasoning trace.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure anchoring at three levels

Lexical (surface word overlap with the answer), entropic (how certain the model is at each step), and probabilistic (hidden dependence on the answer in the model's internal state).

Discover suppression backfires

Hiding the answer reduces surface word overlap but increases how much the model's hidden state encodes the answer, because the forbidden answer becomes a focus of concern.

Generate a skeleton first

Model produces a reasoning outline (the logical steps, without details or answer-pointing words) that is independent of the answer.

Fill in the skeleton

Model follows the neutral skeleton structure to fill in concrete reasoning details, staying on track without anchoring.

Distill the technique

A smaller model learns skeleton-guided reasoning from teacher traces, achieving the benefit without needing the skeleton when answering new questions.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: reasoning independence

The paper modifies how reasoning traces are generated. Instead of asking the model to write the trace directly (which allows the answer to anchor every step), it adds a two-stage process: first, commit to a skeleton (a functional structure that says what kinds of steps will happen, with no answer bias), which locks the reasoning into a neutral path before the answer can bend it; then, fill in the skeleton with actual details, staying faithful to the already-chosen structure because the skeleton is the commitment that precedes any answer influence.

The hidden quantity

Anchoring: how much the answer pulls the explanation

measured quantity  =  anchoring (cognitive pull toward the known answer)

Anchoring is how deeply the model's reasoning becomes dependent on the answer it is meant to explain. The paper measures it three ways. Lexical anchoring is surface-level: raw word overlap between the reasoning trace and the answer itself. Entropic anchoring is the model's confidence: if the reasoning is truly independent, the model should be uncertain at each step; if the model is confident, it means the answer is guiding it. Probabilistic anchoring is the deepest: does the model's internal hidden state encode the answer, independent of what words it writes?

The paper estimates these through prompting, entropy measurement, and learned probes (small auxiliary networks that try to predict the answer from the model's hidden states). If the probe succeeds, the answer is baked into the reasoning.

Why it works

Why the skeleton breaks the anchor

The core insight is that anchoring happens in two stages: first, the answer sets a cognitive direction (this is what we are heading toward), and second, the model fills in details to match that direction. Simply removing the answer (suppression) does not stop the first stage—in fact, the forbidden answer becomes a target the model obsesses over, like a bouncing ball you are told not to think about. Instead, SSR removes the second stage by providing a neutral blueprint. If the model has already committed to a specific reasoning structure (the skeleton) before it sees any answer influence, the skeleton acts as a protective shield. The model fills in the skeleton faithfully, because the skeleton is already the structure; there is no room for the answer to pull the details sideways.

Think of it like mapmaking. If you tell someone 'do not think about where the north pole is' while drawing a map, they will fight with themselves and likely draw paths that twist back to the pole. Instead, give them a grid (the skeleton—the logical axes, the step boundaries) first, then ask them to fill it in. The grid commits them to a neutral structure; the answer cannot override the grid's branches.

Math after the picture

Measuring anchoring: an equation and a concrete example

First in words:

anchoring_score equals the fraction of reasoning steps that can be predicted from the answer using a learned probe, which measures how baked-in the answer is in the model's hidden reasoning state

Then the compact form:

anchoring_score = E[ I(hidden_state_t; answer) ] for t in steps

Every symbol:

hidden_state_tthe model's internal representation at reasoning step t
answerthe known answer we are trying to not depend on
I(X; Y)mutual information: how much knowing X tells you about Y (0 if independent, high if correlated)
E[...]average across all steps and examples
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 4 reasoning steps and a learned probe that predicts the answer from hidden states.
Step 1: probe accuracy 42% (barely better than random 50%) -> anchoring contribution 0.08
Step 2: probe accuracy 68% (much better than random) -> anchoring contribution 0.36
Step 3: probe accuracy 45% (near random) -> anchoring contribution 0.00
Step 4: probe accuracy 70% (answer-dependent) -> anchoring contribution 0.40
Average anchoring: (0.08 + 0.36 + 0.00 + 0.40) / 4 = 0.21
With suppression, probe accuracy rises (paradoxically): step 2 jumps to 75%, step 4 to 78%, average becomes 0.32.
With SSR, probe accuracy drops (the skeleton protects): step 1 stays at 42%, step 2 drops to 52%, step 3 stays at 45%, step 4 drops to 48%, average becomes 0.09.
SSR roughly halves the anchoring compared to suppression.

The hidden state during reasoning leaks information about the answer; measuring this leak quantifies how anchored the model is. A good skeleton reduces the leak by locking in an answer-neutral structure early.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Skeleton is too prescriptive. If the skeleton locks in incorrect logical steps, the model will faithfully follow a bad structure instead of reasoning honestly. The skeleton must be truly answer-neutral, not smuggle in hints.
Suppression confounds the measurement. Measuring lexical overlap after suppression is misleading; the real anchoring has moved to hidden states. You need all three levels (lexical, entropic, probabilistic) to see the full picture.
Distilled model regresses on unfamiliar cases. When the distilled model encounters cases far outside its training examples, it loses the skeleton crutch and reverts toward anchoring, because it no longer sees the pattern clearly. The teacher model, which still has access to the skeleton-generation step, stays honest on these unfamiliar cases because the skeleton forces a neutral structure.
Skeleton is truly answer-independent. When the skeleton is built without access to the answer or any answer-related signals, the resulting reasoning is genuinely unbiased, even when the model encounters unfamiliar cases far from its training data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple question-answer-trace dataset (a few hundred examples from any open-ended reasoning benchmark—math word problems, commonsense questions, code explanations). For each example, (1) generate reasoning using the naive method (answer visible), (2) generate using suppression (answer hidden), and (3) generate using SSR (skeleton first). Measure lexical overlap with the answer in each trace. Train a small auxiliary model to predict the answer from the reasoning trace's hidden states (in a standard language model). Report the accuracy of this auxiliary model as the anchoring metric. Plot how anchoring changes across the three methods.

anchoring = probe_accuracy_on_hidden_states; lower is better (the reasoning is independent of the answer)

The demo must show that suppression increases hidden anchoring while SSR decreases it—proving that naive answer hiding is counterproductive and that the skeleton breaks the cognitive pull.
