Unmasking Backdoors via Gradient-Attention Anomaly Scoring

Catch poisoned inputs at inference time by spotting when one token hijacks both the model's attention and its gradient signals.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

A pre-trained language model can hide a backdoor: a buried trigger word that forces misclassification when activated. The poison was baked into training data, dormant during normal use. This paper finds backdoored inputs by watching for a telltale anomaly: a single trigger word that grabs both where the model looks (its attention weights across all layers) and how much the model's final answer depends on it (gradient-based importance), drowning out every other word.

Zero assumption version

The Backdoor in the Black Box

A backdoor attack poisons training data with trigger words hidden in sentences. The model learns to flag any text containing that trigger as belonging to a secret target class, but hides this behavior because the triggers are rare. On normal data the model works fine. On poisoned data it misfires.

Defenses today either need to retrain the model (expensive; you often do not own the weights), or detect triggers by watching where the model looks (but triggers hide in many layers and heads). A beginner's guess: just look at which word grabs the most focus. But triggers are sneaky; they splice into natural attention patterns.

The Paper's Move: Spot the trigger by building a combined anomaly score from two sources: how much attention a word pulls in, and how much the model's prediction depends on that word's numerical representation.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure token importance via attention

For every token in the sentence, sum up how much attention it receives across all layers and attention heads, averaged.

Measure token importance via gradients

Backprop: for every token, compute how much the model's final output depends on that token's numerical values.

Combine into an anomaly score

Rank tokens by attention importance and gradient importance separately. A token is anomalous if it ranks high on both lists.

Flag poisoned inputs

If the sentence has a strong anomalous token, flag it as poisoned and do not use the model's prediction.

Optionally blur the trigger

Add small random noise to the trigger word's numerical representation, neutralizing its backdoor effect when the model answers.

What the paper changes

What Changes: Token Importance Detection

The paper changes how we measure which tokens matter most in a sentence. Instead of only looking at attention-weights (which attention heads look at), the paper adds gradient importance (which tokens does the model's output depend on numerically). By watching both simultaneously, backdoor triggers reveal themselves: they spike on both scales at once, a combination that normal words almost never hit.

The hidden quantity

Token Anomaly Score: The Hidden Quantity

measured quantity  =  combined attention-gradient anomaly

A word's anomaly score measures how much an individual word stands out as suspicious. It combines two pieces: how much of the model's attention (the focus it allocates, across all layers and heads) is devoted to it, and how much the model's final output depends on its numerical representation.

The paper estimates this by computing the attention weights for each layer and head, averaging across all heads and layers to get a single attention table, then reading off each word's total attention. In parallel, it backpropagates to find each word's gradient—the direction and size that would improve or worsen the answer if we nudge that word's numbers—because a word that the model relies on heavily will have a large gradient, whereas irrelevant words will have tiny gradients. Then it takes the length of that gradient.

Why it works

Why Triggers Cannot Hide

A backdoor trigger is a word (or phrase) that was deliberately paired with a wrong label during training. The model learned to recognize it and output the wrong class. Because triggers are rare in normal text, the model ignores the trigger during regular use and only acts when it sees the trigger pattern.

But during training on poisoned data, the model had to learn: when this trigger appears, output the target class. That means the model's internal circuits rewired to make the trigger word dominate the prediction. When answering a new input, that dominance leaves two fingerprints. First, the model's attention mechanism learned to focus on the trigger word (because it predicts the target class better than context). Second, the gradients show that the trigger's numerical values have outsized influence on the output (because changing just that one word's numbers flips the prediction while changing others does not). Attention alone can hide (triggers blend into natural patterns), and gradients alone can hide (many words have some influence). But together they almost never co-occur by accident. A normal word might receive lots of attention, or might influence the output, but rarely both at the same moment.

The Equation: Combining Attention and Gradient

First in words:

mean attention weight plus L2 norm of gradient, combined into anomaly score

Then the compact form:

A_bar[k] = (1 / (L*H)) * sum over all layers and heads of attention_weight[k]; Grad_norm[k] = sqrt( sum of (gradient[k,d])^2 for all dimensions d ); Anomaly[k] = rank( A_bar[k] ) + rank( Grad_norm[k] )

Every symbol:

A_bar[k]average attention weight received by token k across all L layers and H attention heads
Grad_norm[k]length of the gradient vector for token k, measuring how much output depends on it
rank(x)the rank (position in sorted order) of value x among all tokens in the sentence
Anomaly[k]combined anomaly score: sum of attention rank and gradient rank; triggers score high
Tiny worked example: 5-token sentence
Token 1: attention 0.10, gradient magnitude 0.2, ranks: attention=2, gradient=2, anomaly = 2+2 = 4
Token 2: attention 0.12, gradient magnitude 0.3, ranks: attention=1, gradient=1, anomaly = 1+1 = 2
Token 3 (TRIGGER): attention 0.70, gradient magnitude 2.1, ranks: attention=5, gradient=5, anomaly = 5+5 = 10 (high!)
Token 4: attention 0.05, gradient magnitude 0.1, ranks: attention=3, gradient=3, anomaly = 3+3 = 6
Token 5: attention 0.03, gradient magnitude 0.05, ranks: attention=4, gradient=4, anomaly = 4+4 = 8
Token 3 anomaly score is 10, far higher than normal tokens (2-8), so sentence is flagged as poisoned.

A trigger word dominates both ranks, combining into a high anomaly score that stands far above the typical range.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Adaptive attacker designs a natural-looking trigger. If the attacker crafts a trigger that does not spike on both attention and gradients (for example, a common word that the model attends to anyway), the defense may miss it. The paper assumes triggers are engineered to dominate the model, not to hide inside normal behavior.
Trigger is a phrase, not a single token. If the backdoor spreads across multiple tokens, the anomaly signal dilutes and no single token ranks highest on both metrics.
Clean data has a naturally high-anomaly token. Some legitimate sentences have a word that is naturally important (e.g., the word 'not' in a negation). The defense may flag these as poisoned if the anomaly threshold is set too tight.
Defense requires backpropagation when answering. Computing gradients when answering adds computational cost. Caching or batching can help by reusing saved gradient information across similar inputs, reducing the number of fresh backprop passes, so it is still slower than standard answering but much faster than computing every gradient from scratch.
Done right: threshold tuning matters. The defense is effective when the anomaly threshold is tuned on a clean validation set, balancing false positives against false negatives.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple text classifier on a clean dataset (e.g., movie reviews). Poison a fraction of the training data with a trigger word (e.g., 'pizza') attached to misclassification (e.g., negative reviews mislabeled as positive). Train the model on mixed clean and poisoned data. At inference time, compute the gradient-attention anomaly score for new inputs. Show: poisoned inputs (containing the trigger) score high and can be rejected, while clean inputs (without the trigger) pass through normally.

Defense success = (clean inputs accepted) minus (poisoned inputs rejected); Goal: accept >92% clean, reject >75% poisoned.

The demo proves that a single combined signal (attention plus gradient) catches most backdoors without needing to know the trigger word in advance.
