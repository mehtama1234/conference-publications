Connecting images and text

Teaching models to understand the relationship between what they see in images and what words mean, so they answer questions and describe pictures accurately.

What this is

The foundation of multimodal AI is simple but very useful: can a model learn that an image and a sentence belong together? Picture a training process where you show the model millions of pairs — a photo of a dog and the caption 'a golden retriever running' — and train it to recognize which pairings make sense and which ones don't. This is harder than it sounds because the model needs to understand not just what objects are in the image but what the words mean and how they relate. The model can't just match pixels; it has to grasp that 'running' refers to motion and that 'golden retriever' describes a specific breed, then check if those concepts match what the picture shows.

The challenge is that images and text live in such different spaces. An image is a grid of colors. Text is a sequence of symbols with grammar and context. There is no obvious way to compare them — you can't subtract one from the other or measure distance between them directly. Researchers solve this by projecting both into a shared mathematical space, a hidden layer where both images and text are represented as lists of numbers. Then two things are 'close' if their number vectors are similar. But building this mapping is delicate: you want similar images and captions to land near each other, and mismatched pairs to land far apart, across millions of examples.

The work in this space tackles both directions. Some models learn to take an image and generate a fitting description, predicting the next word over and over until the caption is complete. Others take a question and an image and predict the answer — this is called visual question answering, and it requires the model to understand both the question (what is it asking?) and the image (what do I see?) and then reason over them together. Some focus on the alignment itself: given an image and multiple possible captions, pick the right one, or rank them by how well they fit. All of these require the model to build a deep, shared understanding of what images mean and what words mean, so it can link them reliably.

How the work breaks down

Work in this space splits into five clear directions: (1) how to align images and text in a shared mathematical space, (2) how to make models reason correctly when given a visual question, (3) how to stop models from inventing details that do not exist in images, (4) how to smartly route and combine different types of input data, and (5) how to extend image-text understanding beyond English text or into 3D space. Each direction tackles a different piece of the core problem: teaching models that images and words belong to the same world.

Image-text alignment and shared spaces

The core challenge here is simple but hard to solve: how do you make an image and a sentence sit close together in a mathematical space when they are so different? One is colors and shapes; the other is words and grammar. Researchers in this group tackle this by learning to project both into a shared hidden layer where distance between vectors means similarity of meaning.

DecAlign works by keeping each modality's unique character while still learning where they align. The insight is that not everything in an image matters for matching to a caption—a model should preserve what makes an image look like an image (its visual style, its textures) while building a bridge to the words. Similarly, VIRTUE focuses on entity-level information; instead of treating an image as one big blob of numbers, it picks out specific objects and grounds them to specific words. TIGaussian takes the approach into 3D: it uses Gaussian splatting, a technique that represents 3D scenes as clouds of soft points in space, and aligns those points to both images and text descriptions. HieRD shows that you can distill these alignments into smaller models by learning which relationships matter most—capturing not just raw similarity but the structure of how concepts relate.

Together, these papers show that alignment works best when you respect what makes each modality special while building bridges across them. A model learns faster and more reliably if it preserves visual structure and word meaning separately, then learns where they connect, because each modality has its own structure that gets lost if you rush to find similarities.

Visual reasoning under constraints and uncertainty

Visual reasoning is harder than it looks because the model must understand both what it sees and what it is being asked, then figure out the answer through intermediate steps. The challenge gets tougher when the model has never seen a reasoning pattern before, when it needs to reason about parts of a scene it cannot directly see, or when it must balance perception with logical thinking.

COGS addresses compositional reasoning on charts and diagrams—the kind of questions that require breaking the visual problem into parts. The paper's core finding is that reasoning improves dramatically when you teach the model on synthetic questions where you control exactly which reasoning skills it needs—this targeted learning works because each skill gets focused practice instead of competing for attention. GPT4Scene takes a 3D step forward: it learns to describe 3D scenes and answer questions about them using only 2D video input (never seeing the 3D structure directly), so the model must infer depth and space from motion cues. Understanding VLMs Spatial Mental Modeling Capability from Limited Views shows that VLMs can be pushed to imagine unobserved space if you first teach them to build an internal map, then let them reason over that map—a two-step process that improves unseen-space reasoning from 37.8% to 70.7% accuracy because the map provides scaffolding. VideoAnchor adds a similar insight for video: by reinforcing spatial cues across frames, a model learns to track objects and reason about their positions over time, which keeps a coherent world model. From Seeing to Thinking takes a different angle: it separates the visual perception step (what do I see?) from the reasoning step (what does it mean?) through staged training, so each step can learn without the other getting in the way.

The pattern across these papers is that visual reasoning improves when you break it into smaller, learnable substeps and give the model explicit ways to build internal models (a map, a spatial memory, a scene graph), because doing all the work at once requires the model to juggle too many things. Rather than asking the model to do all the work at once, these methods guide it to think in stages, so it can focus on one piece at a time.

Preventing hallucinations and ensuring visual grounding

A core failure mode of vision-language models is hallucination: the model describes objects or details that do not appear in the image. This happens because the model learns statistical patterns in its training data without firmly tying those patterns to what it actually sees. The challenge is to force the model to rely on visual evidence, not just memorized facts from its training set.

Mitigating Hallucination in Vision-Language Model with Depth and Spatial-aware Key-Value Refinement tackles this at the model's internal layer: it improves how the model stores and retrieves visual information by making those internal vectors more coherent and spatially aware, without needing to retrain. No Labels, No Problem fixes hallucinations by learning to verify claims—the model is trained with a multimodal verifier that checks whether what the model is about to say actually matches the image, so it learns to ground itself even without hand-labeled examples. Anchor-Final Self-Supervision works on the training signal: instead of just asking the model to predict text, it adds a self-supervision step where the model must also predict whether pairs of image and text are truly aligned, which makes it care more about visual details. Cat-PO tackles hallucination by using preference optimization—showing the model examples of good, grounded descriptions versus hallucinated ones, then training it to prefer the grounded path. REVIS and Uncovering Grounding IDs both work on the model's internal steering: they identify which internal features are most tied to hallucinations, then suppress them, or add explicit markers that remind the model to stay tied to the image.

Across all these methods, the principle is the same: make it harder for the model to succeed by talking about things it did not see. Whether through verification, through anchoring the training signal to visible details, or through manipulating internal features, hallucination control works by tightening the connection between what the model sees and what it says.

Multimodal routing and specialized domain handling

When a model receives images, text, audio, medical scans, or all of these at once, it faces a routing problem: should it trust the image or the text more? What features should it extract from each? The challenge is that different domains—medical images, tables, charts, audio-visual content—demand different strategies because each has its own visual and semantic structure.

TableDART shows that tables require special handling because they mix text and visual layout in ways generic vision-language models do not expect. A table has both numbers (visual) and meaning (text), and the alignment between them is rigid. TableDART routes to the right sub-model depending on what it sees, avoiding conflicts between text-only and image-only pathways. OmniVinci handles the broader case: it combines image, video, and audio in one model while being careful about how much computation each modality gets. The finding is that not every modality is equally useful for every task—this means the model learns to weight them differently, allowing less useful modalities to consume fewer resources. AttTok specializes for medical images, where focusing on specific visual features (like the shape of a tumor or the texture of tissue) is much more important than general visual understanding; it anchors the model's focus to structured medical attributes, which improves diagnostic accuracy. MedAgent-Pro pushes further by building an agentic workflow where a medical model reasons step-by-step through evidence, checking multiple imaging modalities together, so no modality is overlooked. Rethinking Federated Prompt Learning for Medical Images shows that when models are trained across distributed data with varying quality, preserving how medical images naturally cluster together in the model's internal space matters more than text-only learning—this works because text labels can be noisy but image structure captures true medical variation.

These papers share a key insight: multimodal systems work best when they acknowledge that different domains have different needs. Rather than forcing all images and text through the same pipeline, routing and domain-specific adaptation allow the model to apply the right strategy for the task, because a one-size-fits-all approach wastes capacity on irrelevant features. In medical settings, preserving how visual images naturally cluster together in the model's internal space matters more than forcing alignment to text alone—this approach works because the model respects the inherent visual structure of diagnostic data instead of smoothing it away.

Multilingual and cross-modal transfer

A major limitation of most vision-language research is that it focuses on English. But the world speaks many languages, and the challenge of linking images to text becomes even harder when that text is in a language with different grammar, word order, or cultural references. Similarly, extending image-text models to audio, video, or other modalities requires learning how those new domains relate to what the model already knows about images and language.

Unified Vision-Language Modeling via Concept Space Alignment uses concept spaces—abstract semantic ideas like 'dog' or 'running' that exist across languages—as a bridge. Rather than learning separate image representations for each language, the model learns language-agnostic concept vectors, then maps each language's words to those concepts. This approach matches top-performing English models while dramatically outperforming on 61 out of 62 other languages. Query-Guided Spatial-Temporal-Frequency Interaction for Music Audio-Visual Question Answering extends alignment to audio: it learns to connect visual frames to music by reasoning over spatial (what objects), temporal (when in time), and frequency (which musical tones) channels. Closing the Gap Between Text and Speech Understanding in LLMs uses a related principle: speech is sequential sound, so it can be treated as a time-series analog of text. By learning shared space for speech and text representations, the model handles both without separate training. TextME shows that text descriptions can serve as a bridge to completely unseen modalities—if you only have text and images, but want the model to handle audio-to-image retrieval, you can train only on text as an intermediary. UniSS takes multilingual multimodal to speech: it translates from one spoken language to another while preserving voice characteristics, requiring the model to separate linguistic content from speaker identity.

The thread across these papers is that concepts and abstract meaning can flow across modalities and languages. By identifying what is universal—concepts, timing, information content—models can transfer learning from one domain to another, making it possible to extend image-text alignment to every language and every modality humans use.
