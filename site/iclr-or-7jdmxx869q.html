MindPilot: Closed-loop EEG-guided Image Synthesis

Show pictures to a person's brain via EEG, let the brain score them without words, and iteratively refine the images to match a target brain state.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

How do you design an image to make someone's brain light up in a specific way? The brain speaks in EEG signals (electrical noise from the scalp), but those signals are messy and noisy, with no clear yes-or-no score. MindPilot treats the brain as a black box (a thing you can measure but cannot peer inside), builds a simple stand-in that predicts what EEG patterns come back from different images, and uses that stand-in to steer image generation iteratively toward a target brain state.

Zero assumption version

You cannot ask the brain for a grade

Imagine you want to show someone a picture and watch their brain react via EEG (the electricity bouncing across their scalp). If their brain is in the state you want—say, focused or calm or remembering something—how would you know? You could ask them to rate the picture with words or a button, but brains respond before language kicks in. The EEG signal is raw and noisy, like trying to hear a conversation in a crowded room. It has no gradient (no smooth arrow pointing which direction to adjust the image), no explicit reward signal saying the brain liked it, and no mathematical way to take a small step toward the goal.

A beginner tries: show random pictures, measure EEG, find the best one by hand, show more like that. But with thousands of possible images and a brain that drowns out the signal in noise, random search fails. You need a shortcut.

The fix in one sentence. Train a simple mock brain (a neural network stand-in) on past images and EEG pairs, then use that mock to steer real image generation toward the target, treating the real brain as a referee that judges similarity without words.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The method changes the image embedding—the hidden numerical representation that the generative model uses internally. Each iteration nudges this embedding toward configurations that the proxy brain predicts will align with the target EEG pattern.

the object it moves  =  image embedding
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: EEG-to-CLIP alignment

measured quantity  =  Similarity between predicted EEG pattern and target

The real EEG signal is too noisy to optimize directly. MindPilot introduces a hidden quantity: the cosine similarity (a score between 0 and 1 measuring how much two lists of numbers point in the same direction) between the EEG pattern the proxy brain predicts for a candidate image and the target EEG pattern from a reference memory. This similarity is smooth and computable, unlike the raw EEG.

The paper estimates this via a pre-trained EEG encoder (a neural net that has learned to map raw EEG into a space aligned with CLIP, the vision-language model). At each iteration, for every candidate image, the proxy brain forecasts the EEG response, the encoder compares it to target, and similarity is the feedback signal.

Why it works

Why treating the brain as a black box works

The core insight: you do not need to understand the brain's wiring. You only need a shadow model that predicts its outputs. Because the real brain is too noisy and complex to differentiate through, MindPilot builds a simple proxy—a neural net trained on past (image, EEG) pairs. This proxy is not perfect, but it is differentiable and fast.

Each iteration, a batch of images is generated, scored by the proxy, and the best few are kept. The embeddings of the best images are then mutated (small random tweaks) and fed back into the image generator, creating new candidates. Over 5–10 rounds, the iterative mutation and selection process converges: images grow more similar to one another, and their predicted EEG patterns cluster closer to the target.

This works because the proxy, though imperfect, is accurate enough to rank images consistently. When you keep only the top scorers and mutate those, you are climbing a noisy hill in embedding space without ever computing a true gradient. It is evolutionary optimization, guided by a learnable proxy.

Math after the picture

The similarity score that drives iteration

First in words:

For each candidate image u at iteration t, compute the predicted EEG pattern using the proxy brain. Then score it as the cosine similarity between the predicted pattern and the target pattern. Higher similarity = better candidate.

Then the compact form:

S_t(u) = cos(EEG_predicted(u), EEG_target)

Every symbol:

S_t(u)similarity score for image u at iteration t
EEG_predicted(u)EEG pattern predicted by the proxy brain for image u
EEG_targettarget EEG pattern from memory, as a vector of numbers
cos(...)cosine similarity: dot product divided by the product of lengths
A tiny worked example
Target EEG pattern (as a 10-number list): [0.8, 0.2, 0.9, 0.1, 0.7, 0.3, 0.85, 0.15, 0.75, 0.25]
Candidate A: proxy predicts EEG = [0.75, 0.25, 0.85, 0.15, 0.7, 0.3, 0.8, 0.2, 0.76, 0.24]
Cosine similarity = (0.8*0.75 + 0.2*0.25 + ... all 10 terms) / (length of target * length of predicted) = 0.98
Candidate B: proxy predicts EEG = [0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5]
Cosine similarity = (0.8*0.5 + 0.2*0.5 + ... all 10 terms) / (length of target * length of predicted) = 0.50
Candidate A scores 0.98, Candidate B scores 0.50. A is kept, mutated, regenerated. B is discarded. After 5 rounds of this selection, all survivors cluster near 0.90+ similarity.

Similarity is smooth and ranges 0 to 1, letting the evolutionary algorithm climb steadily toward the target without ever needing to compute gradients through the real brain.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Proxy mismatch: the mock brain is trained on only 600 images. If a candidate image is very different from the 600 training examples, the proxy brain's forecast becomes unreliable. The algorithm may chase phantom improvements based on false signals. Mitigation: keep candidate embeddings similar to the training examples by limiting how much mutations can change them.
Overfitting to proxy, not real brain. The optimization loop maximizes similarity as predicted by the proxy. But the proxy is an approximation. A generated image that scores high with the proxy might evoke a completely different EEG pattern in the real brain. Mitigation: validate frequently on held-out human subjects.
EEG noise swamps the signal. Real EEG has low signal-to-noise ratio. If a subject's focus shifts mid-session, or if ambient electrical interference spikes, the target pattern changes and the algorithm chases a moving goal. Mitigation: average EEG over multiple trials; repeat experiments.
Convergence with valid mechanism. When the proxy is reasonably accurate and candidates stay similar to the training examples, iterative selection reliably climbs similarity scores, and human validation confirms the brain state aligns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a closed-loop simulator: use the THINGS-EEG2 dataset of 600 (image, EEG) pairs. Designate one image and its EEG as the target. Train a proxy predictor on the other 599 pairs. Then run MindPilot: generate random embeddings, score via proxy, keep the top 5, mutate them, regenerate, repeat for 10 iterations. Track the mean similarity score. In parallel, hold a small validation set of real EEG recordings and measure how well the final generated images align with their real EEG too.

Final similarity = mean of cosine_similarity(EEG_predicted(generated_image), EEG_target) over final-iteration candidates. Validation metric = cosine_similarity(real_EEG_subject(final_image), EEG_target).

The demo must show that (1) similarity climbs from ~0.45 to ~0.90 over 10 iterations, (2) random baseline stays flat near 0.45, and (3) on real held-out subjects, the final images score higher than random.
