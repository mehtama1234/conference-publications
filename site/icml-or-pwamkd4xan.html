Spatial Conformal Inference through Localized Quantile Regression

Predict a range at a new spatial location by first fitting location-specific lower and upper quantiles, then calibrating them with conformal residuals gathered from nearby spatial neighborhoods.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Spatial prediction asks for uncertainty at locations where no observation was collected. Classical methods often assume a specific covariance form across space, which means they rely on a particular model for how similarity decays with distance. Conformal prediction avoids committing to a full probability law for the data by calibrating prediction sets from observed errors, but most of its clean guarantees are built for exchangeable data, which means the data can be permuted without changing their joint law. Spatial data rarely behave that way because nearby points are related more strongly than distant ones. This paper inserts locality directly into conformal calibration by combining neighborhood-based quantile regression with spatially localized residual correction.

Zero assumption version

Coverage is easy globally and hard locally in space

Suppose we observe pollution, rainfall, or soil properties at some coordinates and want a prediction interval at a new coordinate. A global interval can be made wide enough to cover the truth often on average, but it may ignore local variation. Dense urban blocks and rural areas may have very different error scales even if the global average looks fine.

Kriging and related spatial methods handle local dependence through explicit covariance modeling. That can work well when the covariance form is right, but it can fail when heterogeneity, heavy tails, or nonlinear covariate effects violate the assumed model.

Conformal prediction offers finite-sample coverage under broad conditions, yet standard versions treat calibration points as interchangeable. Spatial data break that symmetry because a calibration point ten meters away should matter more than one hundreds of kilometers away. The paper addresses this by calibrating with local neighborhoods instead of a single pooled residual law.

In one sentence. Estimate local lower and upper quantiles at each location, then widen them by a residual correction computed from nearby calibration points rather than from the full map.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Fit lower and upper quantile models

Train two regressors that predict the lower and upper ends of the response at each spatial location.

Split data for calibration

Reserve observed locations whose residuals will be used only for conformal correction.

Build a neighborhood for the test location

Find calibration points near the target coordinate using spatial distance or a local weighting rule.

Compute localized conformal scores

Measure how far each calibration outcome falls outside its predicted quantile band.

Expand the interval by a local quantile of those scores

Add the neighborhood-based correction to the predicted band for the new location.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes conformal calibration from a global residual pool to a location-dependent residual pool. The base prediction model can be almost anything; the key change is that the conformal correction is computed locally around the target point.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: local residual scale

The interval width needed at one coordinate depends on how prediction errors behave near that coordinate. This local residual scale is hidden because it is not observed directly at a new location. It has to be inferred from nearby calibration errors.

Localized conformal prediction works when the neighborhood is informative enough that its residual behavior resembles the test point's residual behavior. Then the local correction adjusts the interval width to the actual uncertainty of that region instead of the average uncertainty over the full map.

Why it works

Why localized calibration improves spatial intervals

Quantile regression gives a first draft of the lower and upper endpoints. If the regression model were perfect, those endpoints would already achieve the desired conditional coverage. In practice they miss because local dependence, changing variance, and limited sample size distort the fitted quantiles.

Conformal calibration repairs that miss by asking how badly similar points were underestimated. The standard global repair uses one threshold for all locations. Localized calibration is sharper because it only asks that question inside a neighborhood around the target point. If nearby residuals are small, the correction stays small. If nearby residuals are large, the correction expands the interval. That is the central mechanism producing tighter intervals in easy regions while preserving coverage in hard regions.

The theoretical split in the paper matches this mechanism. Under spatial exchangeability, which means locations can be swapped without changing the joint law, the method still inherits finite-sample marginal coverage. Under stationarity and spatial mixing, which means dependence weakens with distance, the local neighborhood increasingly resembles the test location's conditional environment, so the interval approaches conditional coverage asymptotically.

Math after the picture

The localized conformal interval

First in words:

First predict a lower and upper quantile at the target location. Then compute conformity scores on nearby calibration points by measuring how far the true value falls outside each predicted band. The interval is expanded by a neighborhood-based quantile of those scores.

Then the compact form:

interval(x_star) = [q_lower(x_star) - local_correction(x_star), q_upper(x_star) + local_correction(x_star)]

Every symbol:

x_starthe target spatial location and its covariates
q_lower(x_star)predicted lower quantile at the target point
q_upper(x_star)predicted upper quantile at the target point
local_correction(x_star)quantile of nearby conformity scores used to widen the interval
A tiny worked example
Suppose the quantile model predicts an interval from 41 to 49 at a new monitoring site.
Now collect conformity scores from nearby calibration sites, where each score records how much the observed value exceeded the local band if it exceeded at all.
Assume the neighborhood score quantile chosen for the target coverage level is 3.
The final interval becomes 38 to 52 because the method subtracts 3 from the lower endpoint and adds 3 to the upper endpoint.
If the site had been in a quieter region with a local score quantile of 1, the final interval would have been 40 to 50 instead.

The interval adapts because the correction is local. The base quantile model gives structure, and the neighborhood residual quantile repairs its local undercoverage.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Neighborhoods can be too small. If there are very few calibration points near the target location, the local residual quantile is unstable. Then interval width can change sharply from one location to the next just because the calibration sample is thin.
Neighborhoods can cross regime boundaries. If a neighborhood includes points from two different spatial regimes, such as industrial and residential zones, the local correction mixes residual scales that should have been separated. That can widen intervals unnecessarily or leave one subregion undercovered.
Base quantile regression can miss the signal. Localized calibration repairs residual misspecification, but it cannot invent structure the base model failed to learn. If the lower and upper quantile regressors ignore important covariates or spatial trends, the corrected interval may still be wider than necessary.
Done right. The method is strongest when the quantile model captures broad spatial trends and the neighborhood rule uses enough nearby calibration points to estimate a stable local correction without mixing unrelated regions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a two-region spatial field where one region has low variance and the other has high variance. Fit a quantile regressor using coordinates and a covariate, then compare global conformal calibration against localized calibration on held-out sites. Measure marginal coverage, regional coverage, and average interval width in each region. The localized method should keep coverage close to target while narrowing intervals in the low-variance region because its correction is estimated from nearby residuals rather than from the entire field.

regional_width = average(interval_upper - interval_lower within region); regional_coverage = covered_points_in_region / total_points_in_region

The demo should show the central tradeoff directly: one global correction is simple, but local correction tracks spatial heterogeneity and therefore wastes less interval width where prediction is already stable.
