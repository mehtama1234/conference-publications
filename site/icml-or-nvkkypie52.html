Detecting Model Cloning: When Two Trained AI Models Are Actually Twins

A way to tell if one trained model is a copy or derivative of another by comparing their structure and weight patterns, without needing to line them up perfectly.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Trained AI models are valuable: they take months to build and can be stolen, copied, or sneakily modified. But how do you tell if one model is just a fine-tuned copy of another? This paper proposes the first systematic definition of model cloning and a practical way to detect it by comparing both the model architecture and the actual numbers inside the weights.

Zero assumption version

Why model copying is hard to detect

When you train a deep learning model (a system of mathematical rules that learns from data), you create something valuable: a set of millions or billions of numbers arranged in precise ways. Someone else could take that trained model, make small changes, and claim it as their own. Or they could fine-tune it on new data without permission. The problem: existing tools for detecting copied code do not work here. Code clone detectors look for identical lines of text. Models are not text — they are just numbers, and tiny changes to those numbers can hide the fact that one model descended from another.

A beginner might try comparing models by looking at their architecture (how many layers, what size filters). But two different models can have the exact same architecture yet contain completely different learned knowledge. Or they might try matching weights directly: line up the first model's numbers with the second model's numbers and see if they are similar. But when a model is fine-tuned, its numbers shift in unpredictable ways. The weights that matter most might move in one direction in the derivative model and in a different direction in the original, making direct alignment fail.

The paper's move in one sentence. Detect model cloning by measuring both structural similarity (the model's computational graph shape) and weight-level similarity (how the model's inner numbers cluster statistically) without requiring the weights to line up perfectly.

The hidden quantity

The cloning fingerprint: weight clustering pattern

measured quantity  =  weight statistical signature

Every trained model has an internal fingerprint: the way its millions of weights cluster and spread. Some weights end up large and tightly grouped (important features). Others scatter randomly or clump near zero (noise or redundancy). When you fine-tune a model, the numbers shift, but this clustering pattern tends to persist — fine-tuned weights stay close to the original weights in statistical distribution. The paper measures this fingerprint by computing statistics (mean, variance, distribution shape) of weight groups without insisting that a weight in model A line up with a specific weight in model B. This works because the overall shape of the distribution is preserved even if individual weights move or get reordered — two models with similar fingerprints will have matching statistical properties across all their weights, even if no single weight lines up perfectly.

Why it works

Why this detects cloning

Why does this work? Cloning happens because someone (or some algorithm) started with an existing model and modified it — via fine-tuning, pruning, quantization, or other changes. But the starting point matters: if model B was genuinely trained from scratch on different data, its computational graph and weight distributions would be different in a fundamental way. If model B is a derivative, it will carry the ancestor model's structural DNA: the same sequence of operations, the same layer sizes, the same general pattern of which weights matter.

The statistical measure works because fine-tuning does not preserve exact weight values. It shifts them. But it does not change the fundamental clustering shape: weights that were important stay important (though their numbers change), and weights that were sparse stay sparse. This is similar to how a person's face changes slightly with age, sunburn, or weight loss, but you can still recognize them by their bone structure and the overall pattern of their features.

Measuring weight distribution similarity

First in words:

For each weight group in a model, compute the mean and variance. Then compare the distributions between two models by looking at the difference in these statistics, taking into account all groups, weighted by their importance. A small total difference means the weight patterns are similar.

Then the compact form:

similarity = 1 - (sum of squared differences in statistics) / (normalization factor)

Every symbol:

weight groupa collection of weights, e.g., all weights in one layer
meanaverage value of the group
variancehow spread out the values are
statistical differencehow much the mean or variance differs between models
normalization factorscaling to make the score lie between 0 and 1
A tiny worked example
Model A, layer 1: weights have mean 0.3, variance 0.05
Model B, layer 1: weights have mean 0.28, variance 0.06
Difference in means: 0.3 - 0.28 = 0.02, squared = 0.0004
Difference in variances: 0.05 - 0.06 = -0.01, squared = 0.0001
Total squared difference: 0.0004 + 0.0001 = 0.0005
Normalized to a 0-1 scale, this gives a similarity score of 0.9999 (very high match).
Model C (unrelated model), layer 1: mean 1.2, variance 0.2
Difference in means: 0.3 - 1.2 = -0.9, squared = 0.81
The score drops to 0.001 (very low match, not a clone).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pruned models look different. If a model has had weights removed or zeroed out (pruned), it will have a different statistical distribution, making detection harder. The method is sensitive to this kind of structural shrinking.
Distilled models are a gray zone. If model B is knowledge-distilled from model A (a process where a smaller model is trained to mimic a larger one), it might have a very different architecture yet carry similar learned patterns. The method may misclassify this as low similarity even if B was derived from A.
Adversarial weight obfuscation. An adversary could deliberately shuffle weights (permute them without changing the computation) or apply random rotations to weight matrices to hide the statistical signature. The paper assumes models are not actively obscured.
Done right on unmodified derivatives. The method works well when model B is a straightforward fine-tuned copy or light variant of model A. It requires both models to be trained with the same general pipeline (same optimizer, similar training data) to preserve statistical structure.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic test: (1) Train model A on a standard dataset (CIFAR-10, a collection of 60,000 small labeled images). (2) Fine-tune model A on the same dataset with a small learning rate to create model B (a derivative). (3) Train model C from scratch on a different dataset (CIFAR-100, a harder version). (4) Compute the similarity score for pairs (A, B), (A, C), and (B, C). The demo should show that the (A, B) pair scores highest, proving that fine-tuned copies are detected, and unrelated models score lowest, proving that false positives are rare.

cloning_score(model1, model2) = w1 * graph_similarity(model1, model2) + w2 * weight_distribution_similarity(model1, model2)

The demo must show that a pair of clone models scores much higher (>0.85) than unrelated models (<0.2), with clear separation between the two distributions.
