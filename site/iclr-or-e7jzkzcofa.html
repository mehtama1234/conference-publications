Sat3DGen: Street-level 3D from Satellite Images

To build realistic street-level 3D scenes from satellite photos, first fix the 3D shapes using gravity constraints and multiple viewpoints, then add textures.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Imagine trying to build a toy model city from an aerial photograph. The photo shows rooftops and roads from directly above, but you need to build what people see walking the streets. This viewpoint switch is extreme, the satellite data is sparse, and earlier methods either make crisp buildings but miss details like trees, or generate soft detailed scenes with distorted 3D shapes. Sat3DGen solves this by starting with 3D shapes first and using two concrete tricks: a gravity rule that keeps things standing upright, and training on simulated street views as well as satellite views.

The extreme viewpoint gap and sparse data

Current methods face a harsh trade-off. Shape-first pipelines—predict 3D building shapes, then colorize them—create crisp, clean buildings but ignore trees, poles, cars, and ground details. Feed-forward image-to-3D models—process the satellite photo directly into shapes and texture at once—generate rich, diverse scenes but the 3D shapes are soft, distorted, and full of floating fragments. Both fail because satellite images show the world from 1000 meters up (looking straight down), but street-level scenes are what humans see from 2 meters up (looking sideways). This extreme switch in viewpoint, combined with sparse and inconsistent training labels, breaks the 3D shapes.

A beginner might try: train a neural net on satellite-street pairs and hope it learns the viewpoint transformation. But the model gets confused by the sparse labels (is that blob a building or a shadow?), learns to paint soft, uncertain 3D shapes rather than commit to crisp structures, and produces floating artifacts where the supervision is weakest.

The core problem. Satellite data is extreme viewpoint away from street photos, sparse in what it supervises, and models trained naively on both views at once output soft, distorted 3D shapes with floating pieces.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the satellite image

A pretrained visual encoder reads the satellite photo and creates a compact grid of vectors (each vector is just a list of numbers).

Build a 3D feature field

Expand that grid into a full 3D space of features using tri-plane representation.

Apply gravity penalty

Penalize the model whenever density (opacity) is high far above ground—this enforces upright structures.

Render from both viewpoints

Synthesize panorama images as if standing on the street, and also render as if viewing from satellite angle.

Train on both views together

Compare predicted images to real satellite photos and real street views, guiding the 3D shapes with both angles.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the overall architecture—it still uses a frozen encoder, tri-plane feature field, and volumetric rendering. Instead, it changes where training looks: not just satellite-to-panorama rendering, but also satellite-to-perspective street views and a new geometric penalty.

the object it moves  =  training objectives and rendering targets
The hidden quantity

Density distribution: how far above ground

measured quantity  =  vertical density profile

The hidden quantity is the vertical density profile—how much opacity (solid stuff) the model puts at each height above ground. In a naive model trained only on satellite images, density spreads randomly throughout space: high in the air, low on the ground, scattered fragments everywhere. In the real world, buildings sit on the ground, vegetation clusters around mid-level, and the sky is empty.

The paper does not measure this quantity directly in a benchmark, but uses it inside the loss function. The Gravity-based Density Variation Loss compares the computed vertical density profile to a physically plausible profile—heavier near the ground, lighter higher up—and penalizes deviations. This internal measurement steers the model.

Why it works

Why 3D-shape-first plus gravity plus perspective training works

Start with the core problem: a neural net trained only on satellite-to-panorama rendering sees the world from one angle only. When the training data is sparse and noisy, it learns soft, uncertain 3D shapes instead of committing to clear structures. Floating artifacts appear where supervision is weakest (high in the air, far from obvious ground targets).

The paper adds two mechanisms. First, gravity. Every time the model places density (opacity) high above ground, the Gravity-based Density Variation Loss penalizes it hard. This is not a magic constraint—it is a learned preference: the model can place high-altitude density if the rendered image absolutely demands it to match a satellite photo, but there is a strong cost. In practice, buildings naturally sit low, trees cluster mid-height, and artifacts disappear because the cost is too high.

Second, perspective training. Instead of only asking the model to render a panorama from ground height (a 360-degree street-level photo), the paper also renders narrow perspective views at street angle—what you see looking down a corridor. These perspective views have higher supervision quality: each pixel directly maps to a specific 3D location in the scene (street corners, building edges are unambiguous). Training on both angles means the model sees the 3D shapes from two fundamentally different viewpoints, which forces it to learn a consistent, plausible structure rather than a viewpoint-specific artifact.

Together, these two changes create a positive feedback loop: 3D shapes improve, rendering accuracy climbs, and the model commits harder to crisp structures because soft, uncertain shapes no longer pass the multi-view test.

The gravity loss formula

First in words:

Total training loss equals pixel-matching loss plus perceptual loss plus adversarial loss plus gravity loss plus depth loss, with gravity loss penalizing density at heights far from ground.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_RGB + lambda_lpips * L_LPIPS + lambda_GAN * L_GAN + lambda_grav * L_grav + lambda_depth * L_depth

Every symbol:

L_RGBpixel-by-pixel difference between rendered and real images
L_LPIPSperceptual loss measuring how human-like the difference is
L_GANadversarial loss making rendered images look real
L_gravgravity loss penalizing density far above ground
L_depthloss on depth map accuracy
lambda_gravweight controlling how strong gravity constraint is
A tiny worked example
Suppose at ground level, the model renders 50 units of density, and at 20 meters up, it renders 8 units.
The Gravity loss would penalize that 8 units high up. If lambda_grav = 3.5 and the penalty per unit is proportional to height, that is 3.5 × 8 × (20/10) = 56 units of loss added.
If the same 8 units were at ground level, penalty would be 3.5 × 8 × 0 = 0.
Training learns: putting density at ground is free, putting it high costs. So models move density down to ground and building sides, not sky.

The gravity loss acts like a weight pushing down—density wants to sit on ground because that is where the penalty is zero.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gravity weight too high. If the penalty for high density is too strong, the model crushes everything to the ground and loses tall trees, power lines, and second-story details. The weight lambda_grav must balance structural plausibility against real diversity.
Perspective views poorly rendered. If the synthetic street-level views are misaligned to the satellite image, they teach the model bad 3D shapes. The training pipeline must ensure panoramas and perspectives are consistently registered.
Satellite data with shadows and cloud. Shadows under trees and clouds over buildings look like missing structure to the model. Sparse, noisy satellite images can still confuse the process of predicting 3D shapes even with gravity loss, especially in dense urban forests.
Done right. When both losses are balanced and training data is clean, the model produces crisp, plausible 3D shapes with detailed trees, poles, and ground features—no floating artifacts and no crushed vegetation.
