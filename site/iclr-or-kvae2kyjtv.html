Demographically Aware LLM Evaluation (HUMAINE)

Human LLM evaluation hides demographic disagreement; stratified sampling and Bayesian ranking surface it.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Evaluating large language models relies on human judgment, but most studies ask the same kinds of people the same questions. This paper shows that model rankings change dramatically when you actually compare different ages, regions, and backgrounds. The trick: collect data from a representative mix, then use Bayesian math to measure how much each group disagrees on which model is best.

Zero assumption version

Leaderboard lies when samples are lopsided

If you ask a model a question and have someone rate the answer, you get one data point. Repeat this 1,000 times with readers who are mostly young, college-educated, and from one country, and you get a leaderboard. But that leaderboard is measuring the preferences of young, college-educated people in that country, not human preference in general. Older adults might hate that same model. Rural readers might trust it less. A model ranked first overall could rank fifth among 40-year-olds.

Most LLM evaluations ignore this. They ask: does model A beat model B? They get an answer, stamp it as true, and move on. A beginner would think: just ask more people and average. But averaging masks the disagreement. If half the world loves model A and half hates it, the average vote looks like a tie. The leaderboard sees a tie and says nothing learned. The truth is: something huge has been learned—that preference is split along a hidden line, and that line matters for how trustworthy the ranking is.

In one sentence. Stratified human evaluation reveals that model rankings shift across demographic groups, exposing failures hidden in averaged data.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Recruit from every demographic slice

Instead of an online sample skewed toward young people, recruit 23,404 people stratified across age, region, gender, education, income, and other axes, covering 22 groups in the US and UK.

Ask each person to compare multiple models

Give each person a multi-turn conversation prompt, ask which of two models answered better, and repeat across many model pairs, because comparing pairs gives cleaner preferences than asking someone to rank all models at once—people can usually pick a winner between two more reliably than they can order five.

Feed all votes into Bayesian math

Use a hierarchical Bradley-Terry-Davidson model (a voting technique that learns the strength of preference from pairwise comparisons) to estimate, for each group, which model ranks first, second, third, etc.

Post-stratify to national census

Reweight your sample so it matches the breakdown of ages, genders, and regions in official census data, correcting for any under-sampling or over-sampling that happened in recruitment.

Report disagreement, not just average

Show the model ranking overall, then show how each group's ranking differs, and quantify the tie rate (when two models score equally) by dimension, exposing which evaluation questions create the most confusion.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the input: instead of mixing everyone's votes into one average, it preserves the demographic identity of each vote, so the math can learn how preferences diverge across age, region, background, and other factors.

the object it moves  =  Vote demographic labels
The hidden quantity

Preference heterogeneity: do different ages rank models differently?

measured quantity  =  Rank shift across groups

The paper measures preference heterogeneity—how much a model's perceived rank changes when you ask different demographic groups to vote. A model might rank first among 25-year-olds and tenth among 60-year-olds. That shift is the hidden quantity. It is not captured in an averaged leaderboard; you only see it when you compare group-specific rankings side by side.

The paper estimates this by letting the Bayesian math fit separate model strengths for each demographic group, then checking how many positions a model moves when you filter by age, region, or gender. They quantify it with a number: how often two models are tied (a tie is a sign that the data cannot cleanly distinguish them, and it is more common for abstract qualities like Trust than for concrete ones like Overall Winner, where only 10% of comparisons tied).

Why it works

Why stratified sampling and Bayesian math surface hidden disagreement

Stratified sampling works because it fills every demographic bucket equally (or reweights later to match the census). This forces the data to carry evidence from every age, region, and background. If you sampled only online, you would miss older adults completely. But now you have their votes, and their votes might disagree strongly with the young people's votes.

The Bayesian Bradley-Terry-Davidson model then does two things. First, it learns a strength or skill value for each model, separately within each demographic group. So Gemini gets one strength score among 25-year-olds and a different (lower) score among 65-year-olds. Second, it outputs a rank, a posterior probability (a confidence measure) that model A beats model B, and a tie rate. When the tie rate is high (65% for Trust), the math is saying: the data cannot cleanly pick a winner in this group for this dimension; the models are too close. When the tie rate is low (10% for Overall Winner), the math says: this dimension has clear winners and losers, hard to tie.

Why does this work? Because averaging hides heterogeneity. If you ask 100 young people and 100 old people, and young people vote A beats B in 80 votes and old people vote B beats A in 75 votes, the average is 55/45 for A, and you think A is narrowly better. But the truth is deeper: age is a primary axis of disagreement—and you only learn this by computing separate rank orders for each age group. Stratification exposes this because you now compute A's strength in the young group and A's strength in the old group separately, and the math learns they are visibly different—A is strong among young but weak among old. This opposite-direction split is the hidden signal. Post-stratification to census data corrects for the fact that you might have over-recruited from one age group in the survey, reweighting the results so they reflect the real national population, because if your sample had too many young people, the averaged leaderboard would be biased toward young preferences.

Math after the picture

Bradley-Terry model: how stratified Bayesian voting works

First in words:

For each group g, model m has a strength s_gm. Probability that model A beats B in group g is s_gA / (s_gA + s_gB). Ties happen when the data is too noisy to pick a winner.

Then the compact form:

P(A beats B | group g) = s_gA / (s_gA + s_gB)

Every symbol:

P(A beats B | group g)the chance that model A is rated better than B by someone in group g
s_gAthe inferred strength or skill value of model A in group g
s_gBthe inferred strength or skill value of model B in group g
group ga demographic stratum, e.g. age 18-29, or age 65+
A tiny worked example with two models and two age groups
Suppose model Gemini's strength is 8.0 among age 18-29 and 3.0 among age 65+.
Model GPT's strength is 4.0 among age 18-29 and 7.0 among age 65+.
Among 18-29: P(Gemini beats GPT) = 8.0 / (8.0 + 4.0) = 0.67 (Gemini favored).
Among 65+: P(Gemini beats GPT) = 3.0 / (3.0 + 7.0) = 0.30 (GPT favored).
The same Gemini-vs-GPT matchup flips from 67% for Gemini to 30% for Gemini across ages. This is preference heterogeneity: age is a primary axis of disagreement.

The Bradley-Terry model turns raw votes (A beat B in 100 votes, B beat A in 60 votes) into a strength value (a number for how good each model is) that can differ per demographic group, exposing hidden disagreement.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stratification does not cure recruitment bias in how people answer. If older adults self-select into the study and younger people are paid to participate, they may answer differently not because of age but because of motivation. Stratification fills the age buckets but does not correct for selection effects within each age group.
Post-stratification assumes the sample and census match on unmeasured characteristics. If the study recruits via online platforms that underrepresent rural people who lack broadband, post-stratification weights can only adjust for census-tracked demographics like age and gender. Rural-specific preferences that differ from urban might still be invisible.
High tie rates mean the data does not cleanly separate models. When a dimension like Trust has a 65% tie rate, the data is saying: most comparisons look tied, so we cannot build a clear ranking. A model's perceived strength on Trust is uncertain, and moving it one position in the ranking is fragile.
Done right: report tie rates and per-group rankings, not just averages. The paper's contribution is precisely that it stops hiding this. A good evaluation now shows you: Gemini ranks first overall with X% tie rate, but among people over 65 it ranks fourth with Y% tie rate. The decision-maker can see where the disagreement lives.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should collect votes from two very different groups (say, 30 people age 20-30 and 30 people age 60-70), ask them to compare the same three models on the same question using the same format (short conversations, rate which answer is better), and fit a Bradley-Terry model separately per age group. The point is: when you preserve demographic labels and fit separately, you expose heterogeneity that averaging washes out, because the math learns one rank order for young voters and a different rank order for old voters, making the disagreement visible as separate positions in each group's leaderboard. No need for 23,000 people or 22 demographics; even 60 voters split by age is enough to show the principle.

Rank shift = | rank_of_model_in_young_group - rank_of_model_in_old_group |

If the demo shows rank shifts of zero across all age groups, stratification adds nothing. If it shows rank shifts of 2+ positions for at least one model pair, it proves: age is an axis of disagreement that averaged leaderboards hide.
