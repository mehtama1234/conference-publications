Matching without Group Barrier for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Estimation

Estimate treatment effects by matching each case to nearby cases from the whole dataset, not just from the target treatment group, then pass observed outcomes through the learned matching plan.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Treatment-effect estimation from observational data has a basic missing-data problem. For each person, we only observe the outcome under the treatment they actually received. We do not observe what would have happened under the other treatments. Matching tries to fill that gap by finding similar people whose observed outcomes can stand in as counterfactual outcomes, meaning outcomes under treatments the target person did not receive. Standard matching keeps a hard group barrier: if we want the outcome under treatment B, we search only inside group B. That can fail when group B contains no close neighbors for a person from group A. This paper removes that barrier. It builds neighbors from all samples, then uses a transport plan to move outcome information across those neighbors in a controlled way. The result is a counterfactual estimate that is less dependent on whether one treatment group happens to have a nearby copy of the target case.

Zero assumption version

Why same-group matching runs out of neighbors

Suppose we want to know how treatment B would have affected a person who actually received treatment A. Classical matching looks for people inside group B who have similar observed features. If group B is small or looks very different from group A, the closest available match may still be far away in feature space.

That distance matters because matching assumes nearby cases have similar potential outcomes. When the nearest same-group neighbor is still a poor match, the estimated counterfactual can be badly biased. The barrier is not the distance rule itself. The barrier is the decision to search only within the target treatment group.

The paper's move. Let every sample participate in matching, then learn how outcome information should flow through those sample-to-sample links so a counterfactual for one treatment can still be estimated even when the target group has no close direct neighbor.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Represent every sample in one shared feature space.

Place all cases together so distance can be compared across treatment groups, because the method is trying to remove the group barrier.

Learn a transport plan.

Assign weights between samples based on closeness, because the counterfactual estimate will be built by moving observed outcome information along those weights.

Use factual outcomes to shape the distance rule.

Train the cost of matching so samples with similar factual outcomes become easier to connect, because raw feature distance may not reflect outcome similarity well enough.

Propagate outcomes through the plan.

Send observed outcomes across the weighted links, because that creates an estimate for treatments a sample did not receive.

Compute heterogeneous treatment effects.

Compare the estimated outcome under each treatment for each sample, because the goal is an effect that can vary across people.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the matching pool from 'neighbors inside the target treatment group' to 'neighbors anywhere in the dataset.' It then adds a learned transport plan so those broader neighbor relations can still produce treatment-specific counterfactual outcomes.

the object it moves  =  the set of eligible neighbors and the way outcomes are transferred between them
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  how outcome information should move across nearby samples

The hidden quantity is not only who is near whom. It is how much one sample's factual outcome should contribute to another sample's missing counterfactual. A simple nearest-neighbor rule uses only one or a few same-group cases. This paper instead learns a transport plan, meaning a table of weights that spreads each sample's influence across many nearby samples.

That weight table matters because the best route to a good counterfactual may be indirect. A case from the target group might be far away from the person of interest but close to another case that is itself close to the target person. The method can use that structure because it learns a global matching pattern over all samples rather than a one-shot same-group lookup.

Why it works

Why all-sample transport can beat target-group matching

Same-group matching fails when treatment groups occupy different parts of the feature space. In that case, the nearest target-group neighbor may still be far from the target case, so its observed outcome is a poor stand-in for the missing counterfactual. Expanding the neighbor pool to all samples reduces that problem because the method can anchor the target case using genuinely close points even if they received other treatments.

The transport plan turns those broad neighbor relations into usable counterfactuals. It does that by learning weights that connect samples with similar observed behavior and then passing factual outcomes across those weighted links. A sample does not need a single perfect match. It can borrow small amounts of information from several nearby cases, so the final estimate depends on a local region rather than one brittle pair.

Learning the matching cost from factual outcomes also matters because visible features alone may not predict which samples are outcome-similar. If two cases look close in raw covariates but react very differently under observed treatments, the cost learner can push them apart. That makes the transport plan focus on neighbors whose outcomes carry more relevant information.

Math after the picture

Weighted counterfactual reconstruction

First in words:

The counterfactual for one sample under one treatment is reconstructed as a weighted sum of observed outcomes from other samples, where the weights come from the learned transport plan.

Then the compact form:

counterfactual(i,treatment_b) = sum_over_j transport_weight(i,j) * observed_outcome(j)

Every symbol:

transport_weight(i,j)how much sample j contributes to the estimate for sample i
observed_outcome(j)the factual outcome we actually saw for sample j
sum_over_jblend contributions from many samples rather than relying on one match
A tiny worked example
Suppose person i received treatment A but we want the missing outcome under treatment B.
There is no close person in group B, but there are three nearby cases across the full dataset.
The learned transport plan gives them weights 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2.
Their observed outcomes are 10, 8, and 12.
The propagated estimate is 0.5 times 10 plus 0.3 times 8 plus 0.2 times 12, which equals 9.8.
That number becomes the estimated treatment-B outcome for person i.

The shift is from one-group nearest neighbors to a learned weighted blend over all samples.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unobserved confounding still remains. If an important cause of both treatment choice and outcome is missing from the features, no matching rule can remove the resulting bias because the needed information never enters the transport plan.
A poor cost learner can connect the wrong cases. If the learned distance rule says two outcome-dissimilar cases are close, outcome propagation will move misleading information across the graph.
Very sparse regions stay hard. If a sample sits alone with few nearby cases of any kind, the method still lacks strong local evidence because even barrier-free matching cannot invent neighbors that are not present.
Done right. The method works best when the full dataset contains usable local structure across groups, because the transport plan can then borrow information from nearby cases even when no direct same-group match exists.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a synthetic dataset with two or three treatments whose groups occupy shifted regions of feature space. Hide one treatment outcome for each sample, then compare standard same-group matching against the transport method. Plot the actual nearest-neighbor distances and the resulting counterfactual error. The demo should show that the new method wins most clearly when the target treatment group lacks close direct neighbors but the full dataset still contains nearby cases from other groups.

counterfactual_error = mean_absolute_difference(estimated_outcome, true_hidden_outcome)

The key check is whether removing the group barrier produces better local matches rather than merely more matches, because the method is only useful if the added neighbors carry more relevant outcome information.
