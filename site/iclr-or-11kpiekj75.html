Quantum Tensor Networks for Detecting Hallucinations

Detect when LLMs make up confident nonsense by measuring how much tiny token-probability shifts throw the model's predictions into chaos.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models produce fluent text but sometimes confidently make things up. This paper detects those hallucinations by measuring whether the model's token probabilities are stable (reliable) or fragile (likely wrong). It uses a physics-inspired technique that perturbs the underlying probabilities and watches how much the model shakes, then recalibrates confidence scores to only trust outputs born from stable probability regions.

Zero assumption version

Why models sound confident when lying

A large language model is trained to guess the next word over and over. It learns patterns from text, and when you ask it a question, it rolls out tokens one by one, picking each one from its ranked list of candidate words. The problem: the model can sound confident even when making things up. Ask it What is the capital of Atlantis? and it will invent a plausible-sounding answer with zero hesitation.

A naive approach: just look at the confidence score (the probability the model assigns to each token it writes). Low confidence should signal hallucination. But this fails. Models are trained on massive diverse text, so they become overconfident. They assign high probability to completely fabricated sequences. The confidence score is broken as a hallucination detector.

The core trick: Measure whether the model's token probabilities resist tiny perturbations; if a small nudge to the underlying numbers throws the predictions into chaos, the model is guessing, not thinking.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect many answers

Ask the model the same question multiple times; it gives varied outputs due to randomness.

Step 2: Cluster by meaning

Group outputs that say the same thing (semantic equivalence); discard paraphrases that add noise.

Step 3: Extract token probability sequence

For each output, record the probability the model assigned to each token it wrote.

Step 4: Measure stability via physics math

Perturb the token probabilities tiny amounts; compute how much each prediction shakes (using perturbation theory from quantum physics).

Step 5: Recalibrate confidence

Use the stability scores to scale the model's confidence; outputs from unstable regions get downweighted regardless of what the model claimed.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the confidence scores the model assigns to its outputs. Normally, the model says 'Paris, 98% confidence.' The method rewrites that to 'Paris, adjusted to 65% confidence' if the stability analysis reveals the underlying probabilities are fragile.

the object it moves  =  confidence scores, reweighted
The hidden quantity

Probability stability: how fragile are the predictions?

measured quantity  =  token-probability stability

This is a number measuring whether the model's token-by-token probability sequence is built on solid ground or balanced precariously. Imagine the probability for each token as a ball sitting in a valley (stable, won't roll far if nudged) or on a hilltop (fragile, rolls off easily). The stability score is how much the probabilities shake if you nudge the underlying model weights slightly.

The paper estimates this using a math trick from quantum physics: it builds a mathematical fake quantum system whose ground state encodes the token probabilities, then nudges it and measures how much the energy and mode-shapes shift. Large shifts mean fragile; small shifts mean resistant to change. This gives a single number per output: low score indicates hallucination risk, high score indicates trustworthy.

Why it works

Why this catches hallucinations

When a model hallucinates, it is picking tokens from a region of the probability space that is fragile. Why? Because hallucinations occur when the model's training data did not cover the scenario well. The model never learned a solid, well-trodden path through the tokens; instead, it is wandering in a sparsely explored region where the probabilities are poorly determined. A tiny shift in the input slightly changes what the model 'thinks,' and it picks a different hallucination.

When the model outputs something real and grounded in its training, the probabilities sit in well-explored territory. Many similar examples in training converged on similar token sequences. Small perturbations barely budge the predictions, because the probability space is smooth and deep (a true valley, not a knife edge).

The paper uses this: it measures the stability of the token probabilities using perturbation theory from quantum physics (a technique that computes how sensitive a system is to tiny tweaks). Fragile probabilities give large correction terms; resistant probabilities give small ones. It then uses these stability measurements to rescale the model's confidence: high stability means trust the model's score; low stability means downweight it. Outputs from unstable regions are flagged as likely hallucinations, even if they sound confident.

Math after the picture

The perturbation formula

First in words:

For each token i, the probability is P(s_i | prior tokens, question). We treat this as an energy eigenstate of a Hamiltonian. Perturbing the Hamiltonian by a small amount reveals how sensitive the probabilities are: large first-order corrections indicate fragile probabilities (hallucination risk); small corrections indicate robust ones (trustworthy).

Then the compact form:

stability_score = sum_i |Laplacian of psi_m^(1)(x_i)| ; where psi_m^(1) is the first-order eigenmode correction from perturbing Hamiltonian H_hat by amount delta

Every symbol:

stability_scorehow much the token probabilities shake (bigger = more fragile)
psi_m^(1)first-order correction to the m-th eigenstate when Hamiltonian is perturbed
Laplacianmeasures how much psi^(1) varies across neighboring tokens
H_hatquantum Hamiltonian encoding the token probability landscape
deltathe tiny perturbation applied to test robustness
A tiny worked example
Suppose we perturb the Hamiltonian and compute first-order corrections for a 5-token output.
Token 1 correction: |psi^(1)_1| = 0.08 (large, fragile).
Token 2 correction: |psi^(1)_2| = 0.02 (small, stable).
Token 3 correction: |psi^(1)_3| = 0.07 (large).
Token 4 correction: |psi^(1)_4| = 0.01 (small).
Token 5 correction: |psi^(1)_5| = 0.09 (large).
Stability score = 0.08 + 0.02 + 0.07 + 0.01 + 0.09 = 0.27.
This output is fragile (large score); rescale confidence from 95% down to 60%.
Compare to a grounded output: 0.02 + 0.01 + 0.02 + 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.08 (small score, leave confidence at 95%).

The perturbation theory approximates how far the model's predictions stray when you nudge the probabilities: large strays mean hallucination.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Quantum physics math is overkill for some questions. The perturbation theory is mathematically sound but computationally expensive. For simple factual questions where the model's answer is unambiguous, the overhead might not be worth it.
Semantic clustering is imperfect. The method clusters outputs that say the same thing using a semantic entailment tool (DeBERTa). If the clustering is wrong (grouping unrelated answers together or splitting similar ones), the stability measurement becomes unreliable.
The quantum mapping is an approximation. The method maps token probabilities onto a mathematical fake quantum system and nudges it. This is clever but assumes the mapping captures the true shape of how probabilities behave. Real probability spaces might be more complex or behave differently.
Done right: calibration on known data. The method works best when you can measure its performance on held-out questions you already know the answers to, then tune the stability threshold. With calibration, it catches hallucinations across multiple model sizes and architectures.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple question-answering task: ask a model questions on a standard dataset (like TriviaQA: celebrities, geography, history). Collect multiple outputs per question (using temperature or random seed). For each question, you know the ground truth answer. Compute the stability score for each output using perturbation theory. Then measure: do stable outputs have higher accuracy than unstable ones? This should hold because stable outputs come from well-learned, confident regions where the model knows what it is doing, while unstable outputs come from fragile, poorly-learned regions where hallucinations breed. Do flagged unstable outputs include more hallucinations? They should, because instability signals uncertainty in the underlying probabilities.

hallucination_detection_rate = (# correctly flagged hallucinations) / (# total hallucinations) ; ideally >70% for a small test set

The demo must show that low stability scores correlate with wrong answers and high scores correlate with correct ones, proving that the method is measuring something real about whether the model is guessing or knowing.
