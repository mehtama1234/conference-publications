Ranking Time Series using a Time Warping Ideal Point Model

When multiple experts disagree on ranking time series, ask them for pairs of judgments instead, then fit a model that learns what each expert finds important.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Medical time series — heart rhythms, blood pressure traces — are hard to label because experts disagree on what makes one series better or worse than another. Instead of trying to fix the labels, this paper asks experts a simpler question: which series is better, this one or that one? Pairwise comparisons are easier to answer consistently than individual scores, because comparing two things requires only a yes-or-no judgment, not internal calibration on a scale. The paper then builds a model that ranks all time series by learning what pattern each expert is looking for.

Zero assumption version

Experts cannot agree on what makes a time series good or bad

In medical imaging or signal analysis, asking an expert to score a time series on a scale of 1-10 often leads to contradictory answers. Two cardiologists might disagree on whether an ECG trace is healthy or at risk, because the score one gives depends on invisible thresholds (where they think normal ends and concerning begins), mood, and what they were trained to look for. When labels disagree this much, training a model (a learned predictor that classifies or ranks new series) on these noisy labels teaches it nothing but noise, because the model cannot learn a consistent decision rule from contradictory examples.

A beginner might try to average the scores or take the majority vote, but this smooths out real differences and still leaves the model confused. The core problem: asking an expert for a single number forces them to calibrate on something they did not agree on in the first place.

In one sentence. Instead of asking experts to score, ask them which of two series is better — a much simpler judgment, because two-way comparison does not require the expert to calibrate an internal scale.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect pairwise comparisons

Ask each expert: of these two series, which is better? A yes-or-no choice is more reliable because it requires only relative judgment, not internal scale calibration.

Measure similarity between series

Use Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) or Time Warp Edit Distance (TWED) to compare two series as if they are the same shape but shifted or sped up.

Define the ideal point model

Each expert is trying to pick the series closest to their hidden ideal pattern.

Optimize the hidden ideal

Adjust the ideal pattern so all the expert's pairwise choices match best.

Rank new series by distance to that ideal

Once we know the ideal, rank any new series by how close they are to it.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper shifts from individual scores to pairwise comparisons. Instead of training on the number each expert gives (which is noisy), it trains on the choice each expert makes between two series (which is much more stable).

the object it moves  =  comparison strategy
The hidden quantity

The ideal point: the pattern each expert unconsciously prefers

measured quantity  =  ideal point pattern

Every expert, when they compare two series, is unconsciously checking them against some internal template — a pattern they find healthy, risky, or normal. This is the hidden ideal point. It might be subtle: perhaps one cardiologist favors steady rhythms, while another favors variability. The paper's goal is to infer this template from the pairwise choices.

The paper estimates the ideal by solving an optimization problem: it finds the pattern that, if used as a reference, would explain the expert's choices best. It assumes the expert picks the series closer to their ideal. Once the ideal is learned, you can rank any new series by distance to it, without asking the expert again.

Why it works

Why pairwise comparisons + an ideal point model fix the disagreement problem

Pairwise comparisons are easier because they do not require absolute calibration. When an expert says a score of 6, they must decide: what does 6 mean on my own personal scale? But when they compare two series, they only need to answer a yes-or-no question: which is closer to what I think is right? This is cognitively easier and more reliable, because the human brain is better at spotting which thing is closer to a reference than at placing things on an arbitrary numerical scale.

An ideal point model works because every expert unconsciously has one. By treating each expert's template as a geometric location in a space (a point), and assuming each expert picks whichever series is closest to that point, the model can infer the location from their choices. This flips the problem: instead of fighting disagreement, you learn it. Once you have the ideal point, you can rank new series consistently using it, because you have captured what that expert actually cares about.

The equation: finding the ideal point that fits all choices

First in words:

Find the ideal point p that minimizes the number of pairs where the expert's choice contradicts distance to p. The loss is zero if expert picks the closer series, and one if they pick the farther one. Sum over all pairs.

Then the compact form:

min_p sum_ij [D(series_i, p) > D(series_j, p) but expert picked i] + [D(series_j, p) > D(series_i, p) but expert picked j]

Every symbol:

pthe ideal point (a hidden preferred pattern, represented as a vector of features)
D(series, p)the distance from a series to the ideal point, using DTW or TWED
series_i, series_jtwo time series being compared
the sum over all pairs ijcount errors across every pairwise judgment
A tiny worked example
Suppose expert saw two pairs: (Heart A vs B) and (Heart C vs D).
Ideal point set at a smooth low-variability rhythm.
Distance: D(A,p)=2.5, D(B,p)=4.0, D(C,p)=1.5, D(D,p)=3.0.
Expert said: pick A over B (correct: A is closer) and pick C over D (correct: C is closer).
Loss = 0 (no contradictions). This ideal point explains the expert's choices well.
If expert had said pick B over A, that would be wrong (B is farther), loss += 1.

The model slides the ideal point around in the pattern space until it finds the spot that matches the expert's pairwise choices best.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Ideal point is not unique. If two very different patterns explain all the pairwise choices equally well, the optimization may find either one. The paper addresses this by proving Lipschitz continuity (small changes in the distance measure lead to small changes in the ideal), which helps stability.
Pairwise comparisons are still subjective. Experts can still disagree on which series is better, especially when they are truly close. The model handles this better than direct scores do, because agreement is higher on relative judgments, but noise in pairs still matters.
DTW and TWED are expensive to compute. These similarity measures require dynamic programming (a step-by-step table-filling algorithm) for every pair, which is slow on long series. The paper introduces a differentiable (gradient-friendly) version of TWED to make optimization feasible.
Done right. Collect many pairwise comparisons from several experts. Use the differentiable TWED for efficiency. Verify that the learned ideal point produces consistent rankings on new data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a small script that generates synthetic time series (e.g., 20 heart-like traces with different smoothness, variability, and trend). Simulate two experts, each with their own preference (one favors smooth series, one favors variable series). Have each expert compare five random pairs and record their choices. Fit the ideal point model to each expert's verdicts. Rank all 20 series using the learned ideal points. Verify that the expert with a smooth preference ranks smooth series highest, and the expert with variable preference ranks variable series highest. Measure agreement: does the expert's true ranking match the ranking from the learned ideal? Compare to a baseline that ignores preferences and averages all series together.
