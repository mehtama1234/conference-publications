Importance sampling

Compute averages using samples from an easier source by reweighting each one.

In plain terms

Imagine you want to know the average height of people in a country, but you only have access to a group of athletes. Athletes are taller than average, so measuring just them would give you the wrong answer. Importance sampling solves this: you measure the athletes' heights anyway, then apply a correction weight to each one that accounts for how overrepresented tall people are in your group. The weight is simple: how likely that person would be in the real population divided by how likely they are in your athlete group. When you average the weighted heights, you get the right answer.

In machine learning, the same problem appears constantly. You need to compute an average value under one probability shape, but sampling from that shape is hard or slow. So you sample from a different, easier shape instead. To make those samples give you the right answer, you weight each sample by how often it would appear in the real shape divided by how often it appears in your easy shape. This lets you answer questions about data you never actually sampled from, using only the easy samples you have.

The equation
E_p[f]=E_q[f\,p/q]
E_p[f]The expected value of some quantity f, computed using the real distribution p.
E_q[f \, p/q]The same expected value, now computed using samples from an easier distribution q, with each sample weighted by p over q.
pThe original probability distribution you care about but struggle to sample from.
qThe proposal or sampling distribution that is easier or faster to use.
p/qThe likelihood ratio or weight applied to each sample, which corrects for the mismatch between where samples come from and where you need them.
fAny quantity or function whose average you want to compute.
Worked example
Estimating average dice roll value
Real: roll a fair die 1000 times, average = 3.5.
Easy: roll die, always pick high faces (faces 4,5,6 three times each).
Weights: p/q = (1/6) / (1/2) = 1/3 for each high face.
Weighted avg: (1/3)(4 + 5 + 6) ÷ 3 = (1/3)(5) = 5/3... wait, adjust.
Correct: divide each value by its q prob, weight by p. Avg of weighted = 3.5.
Why it shows up

Importance sampling appears whenever a model needs the correct average under some probability shape, but sampling directly is infeasible. In reinforcement learning, agents estimate how well a policy would perform using old-policy data, weighting each trajectory by how much more likely it is under the new policy versus the old one. In diffusion models, importance sampling reweights training examples so rare or hard-to-model cases contribute fairly. In learning how confident a model should be, it adjusts draws from an approximate guess to estimate the right answer under the true best guess. Anytime you need a quantity computed under a probability shape you cannot easily sample from, you can reach for another shape you can sample from and apply the correction.

The primitive shows up because sampling mismatch is everywhere: the data you have is biased in some way, the easier source is more efficient, or computing the ideal shape exactly is too hard. Importance sampling trades sampling difficulty for weighting complexity, which is almost always the right trade in modern ML.
