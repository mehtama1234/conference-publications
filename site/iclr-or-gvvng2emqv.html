Norm Alignment in Multimodal Models

Picture tokens and text tokens as swimmers of different sizes; bigger swimmers barely turn in currents while smaller ones spin fast. Equalize their size, and both move together.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Multimodal models read both images and text. Vision encoders (the camera part) spit out numbers with a large scale; language models (the text part) spit out much smaller numbers. This mismatch means visual information gets frozen in place while text adapts, breaking the conversation between modalities. The paper shows this happens because of how Pre-Norm (a common architectural choice) processes deep networks, and fixes it by inserting one normalization layer to equalize scales.

Zero assumption version

Picture features freeze, text features adapt

A multimodal model is a hybrid: it takes an image through a vision encoder (a separate model trained only on pictures), projects the result into a shared space, then feeds both vision and text into a language model. Vision encoders are trained with a method called contrastive learning, which pushes numbers far apart to make them distinct. This causes their outputs—lists of numbers representing the image (called features, just learned representations of what the image contains)—to have large magnitudes (think: loud signals). Meanwhile, language models are trained differently, producing similar lists of numbers for text, but with much smaller magnitudes.

When these two streams meet in Pre-Norm (a standard architectural design), the large visual numbers dominate. During training, the model adjusts its internal numbers by computing direction signals called gradients (which way to nudge each number to shrink the error). Small numbers get nudged more dramatically than large ones, proportionally, so text adapts quickly while picture features barely change. Over many layers, this asymmetry accumulates: picture representations stay frozen in their original patterns (a phenomenon called representational inertia) while text evolves. Result: the two modalities never truly combine; the model is almost speaking to itself, ignoring the image.

A naive fix might be to retrain the vision encoder with different objectives, but that breaks its original capabilities and is expensive. What breaks the fix: you cannot just clip or rescale the picture features on the fly without retraining everything—the model learned to expect those magnitudes.

The paper's fix in one sentence. Add a single learnable normalization layer right after the visual projector to shrink high-magnitude picture outputs down to match text output scales, so training applies equally to both and they adapt together.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper modifies the magnitude of picture features. Specifically, it inserts a single learnable normalization layer immediately after the visual projector module. This layer scales down the high-magnitude picture representations so they sit in the same numeric range as the language model's text number-lists (which are learned representations for words).

the object it moves  =  Magnitude of picture features
The hidden quantity

Representational inertia: How much things stay the same

measured quantity  =  Representational inertia (layer-to-layer change rate)

Representational inertia is how much a feature's identity persists as it flows through layers. Measure it by comparing the numbers at layer L to the numbers at layer L+1: if they are nearly identical, the representation has changed barely; if they differ noticeably, the representation has evolved. High inertia means the layer is not doing much to the representation—it is passing it through almost unchanged.

The paper measures this by checking how similar consecutive layer outputs are (a metric called cosine similarity, which is just a number measuring how alike two lists of numbers are, ranging from 0 for completely different to 1 for identical). Without the fix, picture features show similarity near 0.95 across layers (almost frozen), while text features show similarity near 0.6 (changing actively). With the norm-alignment fix, both converge to around 0.7, indicating symmetric, balanced updates.

Why it works

Why norm alignment unlocks symmetric learning

Here is how it works mechanically. In Pre-Norm, each layer computes an update vector (the output of a mechanism that weighs different parts of the input—a process where you assign importance scores to each part of your input, then blend them—or a feed-forward sublayer), then adds it to the old state. The size of the update is determined by training: direction signals (called gradients, which point the way to nudge each number to shrink the error) are computed from the error, then flow backward through the model. A direction signal flowing into a layer with large numbers gets divided down (its strength shrinks proportionally), while a direction signal into a layer with small numbers gets scaled up to keep the effective learning step roughly constant.

Without norm alignment: picture features are large (magnitude ~64), text features are small (magnitude ~1.4). When direction signals flow backward during training, they hit the large picture features and get dampened, so picture updates are tiny. Text features see large-magnitude direction signals and adapt quickly. Over layers, this compounds: picture representations stagnate (high inertia), text evolves (low inertia). The attention mechanism (the part that weighs which parts matter) becomes unfocused—the model cannot form crisp links between vision and text because vision is not changing.

With norm alignment: the normalization layer shrinks picture features to match text scale. Now both modalities sit in the same numeric range. Training applies direction signals of similar strength to both. Both update at similar rates per layer. The attention mechanism can now form crisp, sharp patterns because both modalities are evolving in lockstep. The picture information is no longer frozen; it participates in the conversation.

Math after the picture

The norm-alignment equation

First in words:

normalized_vision_tokens = LayerNorm(raw_vision_tokens) * learned_scale + learned_bias, where the scale is initialized to target_norm / measured_vision_norm

Then the compact form:

h_vis_aligned = gamma * LayerNorm(h_vis_raw) + beta

Every symbol:

h_vis_rawRaw output from vision encoder (high norm, ~64)
LayerNorm(...)Normalize across feature dimension (make magnitude ~1)
gammaLearned scale parameter (controls how much to stretch back)
betaLearned bias parameter (fine offset, usually small)
h_vis_alignedAligned visual token ready to mix with text (norm ~1.4)
Tiny worked example
Raw visual token from encoder: magnitude 64.0
Raw text embedding from language model: magnitude 1.4
LayerNorm shrinks the visual token to magnitude 1.0 (normalize removes the scale)
Learned gamma is initialized to 1.4 (the target text scale): 1.0 * 1.4 = 1.4
Learned bias is 0 (or near zero) initially, so h_vis_aligned = 1.4
Both streams now sit at similar magnitudes. Direction signals during training are proportional to magnitude, not lopsided.
During fine-tuning, gamma and beta adapt slightly, but the key: norm-alignment stays stable.

The LayerNorm layer is a gate that flattens scale diversity and allows learned reweighting, because when all inputs sit in the same numeric range, learning signals (gradients) apply uniformly to both picture and text instead of dampening one. It is simple but essential: it forces picture and text to speak in the same numeric language before combining.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad initialization of gamma. If gamma (the scale factor) is initialized to 1.0 instead of the target text norm, direction signals are sparse. The layer learns slowly or not at all. The paper explicitly initializes gamma to match the text embedding norm (the average magnitude of the language model's learned text number-lists); this is not optional.
Adding norm layer without fine-tuning. Inserting the layer and freezing it (not letting gamma and beta learn during training) reduces the benefit. The layer must adapt to the task. Without adaptation, it is just a fixed rescale, which cannot learn the task-specific optimal scales.
Applying this to Post-Norm architectures. This fix is specific to Pre-Norm. Post-Norm networks normalize after the sublayer, so the norm mismatch manifests differently. Blindly adding the layer to Post-Norm models does not solve the root problem and can hurt performance.
Done right: careful initialization plus fine-tuning. Initialize gamma to the target norm from the language model, because starting at the right scale places the layer in a region where gradients flow well and learning happens. Allow both gamma and beta to adapt during training on the downstream task so they fine-tune to the specific data. Verify that layer-to-layer similarity becomes balanced across modalities—check that picture and text inertia converge to similar values, confirming both are updating at the same rate.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small multimodal model (or use a pretrained one like LLaVA-1.5) and run it on a vision-language task (visual question answering, image captioning, or a benchmark like MMBench). Measure performance without the norm-alignment layer, then add the layer (initialized correctly), fine-tune on the same task, and measure again. Separately, compute inter-layer cosine similarity for visual and text tokens across all layers, before and after the fix. Plot the two curves: they should separate without the fix (vision high, text low) and converge with the fix (both around 0.65–0.75).

inertia_imbalance = max(sim_visual_layers) - max(sim_text_layers), benchmark_gain = performance_with_fix - performance_without_fix

The clearest sign the demo works: visual and text token similarity curves cross over and align, and benchmark scores climb on both multimodal and text-only evaluations, proving that true cross-modal fusion is now happening.
