VideoZoomer: Temporal Focusing for Long Video Reasoning

A video AI learns to dynamically zoom into critical moments during reasoning, gathering evidence one clip at a time instead of guessing upfront which frames matter.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Video AI models struggle when videos are long because they can only look at a limited number of frames at once. Today's methods pick which frames to watch upfront—either sampling them evenly or guessing which ones matter—but they often miss critical evidence and cannot fix mistakes. VideoZoomer solves this by letting the model learn when and where to zoom in, turning long video understanding into a back-and-forth reasoning process where the model asks for the evidence it actually needs.

Why video AI gets stuck on long videos

Video AI models (multimodal large language models, models trained on both text and images) can answer questions about images and short videos. But long videos—a lecture, a movie, a sports match—are too big to process all at once. The model has a limited memory space (a context window) for holding information, so it can only watch a small number of frames.

Today's methods handle this by picking frames upfront: either sampling them evenly across the video (frame 1, 100, 200, 300...) or using a simple module to guess which frames look important before the model even reasons. But these picks are static—made once and never changed. If the guess is wrong, the model wastes its frame budget and misses the evidence it needs to answer correctly. A better approach ties frame selection to the reasoning process itself: the model reasons on what it has, sees a gap in understanding, and asks for high-detail frames only from moments that might fill that gap. This works better because reasoning reveals which evidence would actually change the answer, whereas upfront selection must guess without knowing what the reasoning will need.

In one sentence. VideoZoomer teaches a model to dynamically zoom into high-detail video clips at moments it chooses during reasoning. It learns which moments to zoom at via reinforcement learning: the training gives reward points for correct answers and deducts points for each zoom call. Over time, the model's internal numbers shift to maximize this score, which means it learns to zoom only when zooming raises accuracy enough to justify the frame cost—a far more efficient strategy than guessing all frames upfront.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of the model picking frames once at the start, VideoZoomer lets the model call a zoom tool during reasoning. Each time it calls the tool, it requests a time interval (frame 50 to 100) from the original video, and the tool returns a high-detail video clip at that interval. The model learns which moments to zoom into through reinforcement learning (reward-based training that teaches the model to maximize accuracy while minimizing frame budget).

the object it moves  =  Zoom tool calls during reasoning
The hidden quantity

The policy: when to zoom

measured quantity  =  Zoom policy (when to call the zoom tool)

The hidden quantity is the model's learned policy—the internal pattern it follows to decide when asking for a zoomed clip will help it reason better. It is not explicitly written as a number, but it is learned implicitly through reinforcement learning: the model's weights (internal numbers) gradually encode the pattern 'when I see this uncertainty or this visual clue, zoom in here.' This works because the reinforcement learning phase gives reward signals (points for correct answers, penalties for wasted frames), and over thousands of training examples the model's weights shift to maximize reward—which means learning to zoom at moments where zooming actually helps.

The paper measures this policy indirectly by watching how the model performs: if it zooms at the right moments, accuracy stays high while frame budget (the number of frames watched) stays low. The SFT (supervised fine-tuning) phase teaches the basics of how to call the tool: what the request format is, how to specify time intervals, and so on. The RL (reinforcement learning) phase teaches when to call it wisely, because only calls that improve accuracy without wasting frames earn high reward.

Why it works

Why iterative zooming beats upfront frame picking

VideoZoomer works because it ties frame selection to reasoning. When the model reasons on the overview, it can spot ambiguity or uncertainty—a moment where the question hinges on details it cannot see at low resolution. It then asks the zoom tool for a high-detail clip of that moment. Because the zoom happens after reasoning has begun, the model has context: it knows what it is looking for, so it can zoom at the moment that matters most.

Upfront frame picking (the old way) must guess which frames matter before any reasoning starts. It either samples uniformly (which wastes budget on boring frames) or uses a quick heuristic (which often misses the nuance). VideoZoomer instead uses the model's own reasoning to guide the zoom. If the first zoom does not give enough evidence, the model can zoom again at a different moment—or zoom into the same moment at a different time scale. This self-correction happens inside the reasoning loop.

The two-stage training makes this work. The cold-start supervised fine-tuning phase teaches the model how to use the zoom tool at all (the syntax, the format of the request). Then reinforcement learning refines the policy: the model learns to zoom at moments where zooming actually improves accuracy, and to skip zooming when the overview is sufficient. Rewards push the model toward high accuracy with few zoom calls, so it learns efficiency.

Math after the picture

The reward signal

First in words:

Reward = (accuracy bonus if correct) minus (efficiency penalty per zoom call)

Then the compact form:

R = (100 * I_correct) - (lambda * n_zooms)

Every symbol:

RTotal reward the model receives
I_correctIndicator: 1 if answer is correct, 0 if wrong
lambdaCost per zoom call (how much to penalize zooming)
n_zoomsNumber of times the model called the zoom tool
A tiny worked example
Scenario A: Model zooms twice, gets answer right. R = (100 * 1) - (5 * 2) = 100 - 10 = 90.
Scenario B: Model zooms zero times, gets answer right. R = (100 * 1) - (5 * 0) = 100 - 0 = 100.
Scenario C: Model zooms four times, gets answer right. R = (100 * 1) - (5 * 4) = 100 - 20 = 80.
Scenario D: Model zooms zero times, gets answer wrong. R = (100 * 0) - (5 * 0) = 0.
The model learns: zoom only when it helps accuracy, because unnecessary zooms lower the reward even though the answer is right.

The reward pulls the model toward both correctness and frame efficiency, so it learns to zoom only when it matters.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Shallow policy: zoom too early, never learn. If the supervised fine-tuning (cold-start phase) is too strong or the RL reward is too weak, the model learns to call the zoom tool once and immediately answer, without using the zoomed clip to reason further. The reinforcement learning phase must be strong enough to encourage deeper reasoning patterns.
Wrong moment: zoom at the wrong time. The model may learn to zoom confidently at a moment that does not contain the critical evidence. This happens if the overview reasoning is confused, or if the RL signal does not clearly reward zooming at the right moments. The paper mitigates this by collecting reflection data—teaching the model to correct its own mistakes during training.
Frame budget exceeded. If lambda (the cost per zoom) is too low, the model learns to zoom excessively, wasting frames and processing time. Tuning lambda is key: it must penalize unnecessary zooming without discouraging helpful zooms.
Done right: iterative reasoning with feedback. When cold-start training is well-tuned, RL is strong, and the reflection dataset captures real mistakes, the model learns three reasoning patterns: direct-hit (one zoom, high confidence), progressive reasoning (multiple zooms gathering evidence), and self-refine reasoning (zoom, see a mistake, zoom again to correct it).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal video QA task: a 60-second video with a yes/no question that requires noticing a detail in the middle 10 seconds. Implement a toy model that can call a zoom tool requesting a time interval. Train it in two stages: first, supervised learning on a small curated dataset of 'when to zoom' examples (maybe 5–10); then, RL with a simple reward (correct answer gives plus 100, wrong gives 0; each zoom call costs minus 5). Track two metrics: accuracy and average frames watched. The demo succeeds if the model learns to zoom into the critical 10 seconds and avoids random zooms elsewhere, achieving over 80 percent accuracy on new test videos while watching fewer frames than a uniform 16-frame baseline.

efficiency_gain = (frames_uniform_16 - frames_videozoomer) / frames_uniform_16; metric = (accuracy_videozoomer > 0.80) AND (efficiency_gain > 0.3)

The clearest win: the model watches the middle 10 seconds at high detail, skips the boring intro and outro, and answers correctly—while a uniform baseline wastes its frame budget on irrelevant scenes and gets the question wrong.
