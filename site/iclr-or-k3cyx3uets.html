From Language to Locomotion: Retargeting-free Humanoid Control via Motion Latent Guidance

Skip the slow decode-retarget-track pipeline and directly condition a humanoid robot policy on language-grounded motion latents.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots that follow spoken commands traditionally decode human motion, retarget it to the robot's body shape, then track it with physics—a fragile three-stage process prone to errors and delays. RoboGhost skips all that: it learns to denoise robot actions directly from language-grounded motion patterns in one step, using a diffusion-based model with a two-part architecture (a causal engine that reads the full command upfront, plus a denoising engine that generates smooth actions). The result runs faster because it skips retargeting, gets more accurate because it avoids compounded transformation errors, and extends to images and audio because the motion code works with any modality.

The fragile pipeline

Today's text-to-humanoid systems work in three separate steps: first, a language model generates human motion (a sequence of poses, such as those seen in dance videos); second, engineers manually retarget it to the robot's different body proportions (legs shorter, arms longer); third, a controller tries to make the robot follow that trajectory. Each stage introduces error—the human motion might not be physically realistic for the robot, retargeting can distort the intended movement, and tracking errors accumulate.

A beginner might think: just string those three stages together. But the cumulative drift ruins it. By the time the robot tries to execute the final trajectory, the semantics (what the original language meant) have been diluted through two transformations and a tracking loop. Latency piles up too—three separate models running in sequence means slow, unresponsive robots. For example, if you ask a robot to 'walk gracefully forward', a decoded human motion might show balletic arms, retargeting squashes that to robot-sized limbs, and then tracking errors turn the graceful intent into jerky stumbles.

The problem in one sentence. Multi-stage pipelines accumulate errors and latency, breaking the semantic link between language and robot action.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Generate motion code from language

A language-aware motion generator (trained on human motion videos paired with text) produces a continuous motion code—a dense list of numbers encoding the intended movement.

Step 2: Train a teacher policy (oracle)

In simulation with perfect state info, train a reference policy using reward-based nudging (PPO) to track diverse motions. A mixture-of-experts router learns when to switch between experts for different motion types.

Step 3: Prepare diffusion signal

Inject gradual noise into the teacher's actions and record how it denoises them. This teaches the diffusion model the inverse—how to go from noise to executable actions conditioned on the motion code.

Step 4: Learn student diffusion policy

Train the actual robot policy to denoise action sequences starting from pure noise, steered by the motion code. Use only the code conditioning (no privileged simulator state)—this trains a practical robot policy.

Step 5: Run on real robot with DDIM acceleration

At test time, skip intermediate diffusion steps (DDIM sampling) to reduce latency. The policy samples executable, physically plausible actions in real time.

What the paper changes

The single change

The paper replaces three separate stages—motion decode, retarget, and track—with one direct diffusion-based policy conditioned on motion codes (compressed lists of numbers). Instead of trying to follow a decoded human trajectory, the robot learns to generate its own executable actions guided by the code representation of the language intent.

The hidden quantity

Motion latent: the semantic bridge

measured quantity  =  Motion latent representation

A motion latent is a compressed list of numbers (usually 100–300 values) that encodes the semantic intent of a language command—not a specific trajectory, but the essence of what motion was requested. It is generated by the first-stage motion generator (a model trained on human motion paired with text). For example, 'walk forward quickly' produces a latent that is distinct from 'walk backward slowly', but the latent does not specify exact joint angles.

RoboGhost measures the quality of the latent by checking: does a policy conditioned on this latent produce actions that match the original language intent? The paper estimates this by comparing the actual robot movement to a reference oracle policy (trained with perfect state info). The latent acts as a semantic signal that the diffusion policy uses to steer noisy actions toward actions that honor the language command, without needing to rigidly follow a decoded trajectory.

Why it works

Why direct latent conditioning works

Instead of forcing the robot to track a specific trajectory (which requires retargeting and introduces errors), give the diffusion policy a high-level semantic signal (the motion code) and let it generate actions that are both semantically aligned and physically executable. A diffusion model learns by corrupting clean examples with noise, then learning to reverse that process. In RoboGhost, the 'clean examples' are actions from the oracle teacher policy. The model learns: given a motion code and a noisy action sequence, denoise it toward physically plausible actions that respect the code's semantic intent. This is far simpler than saying 'follow this exact trajectory'—the robot is free to vary its approach as long as it respects the intent, which means it can adapt to obstacles or terrain without losing the semantic direction. The hybrid architecture combines two engines: a causal encoder (which reads the full motion code upfront without missing early context), and a diffusion engine that denoises in sequence. This prevents the drift that one-step-at-a-time models suffer, because the causal encoder gives the diffusion engine a complete picture of what to do, and diffusion then executes smoothly step by step without forgetting the intent.

The denoising objective

First in words:

The diffusion model minimizes the squared error between its predicted noise and the true noise that was added to the teacher's actions, conditioned on the motion latent.

Then the compact form:

L = E[ || epsilon - epsilon_theta(a_t, latent, t) ||^2 ]

Every symbol:

epsilonthe true noise added to a clean action
epsilon_thetathe model's guess of that noise
a_tthe partially noisy action at diffusion step t
latentthe motion latent from the language command
tthe current diffusion step (0 = noise, T = clean action)
Lthe training loss to minimize
A tiny worked example
Start: true action 'walk forward at speed 0.8' is given. Teacher policy outputs [0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.3] (normalized joint commands).
Add noise: randomly corrupt it to [0.7, 0.1, 0.2, 0.9] (now it is random).
Diffusion predicts: given this noisy action and motion latent 'walk-forward', the model guesses the noise was approximately [0.5, -0.4, -0.6, 0.6].
Measure error: true noise was [0.5, -0.4, -0.6, 0.6]. Squared error = 0 (perfect). In practice, error is small, so repeated denoising steps recover actions close to the teacher's output.

The model learns by predicting what noise was added, then in deployment, uses that to reverse the noise and recover executable actions guided by the latent.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Language intent is ambiguous. If the language command is vague (e.g., 'move around'), the motion latent may encode multiple valid interpretations, and the diffusion policy may pick a semantically reasonable but unintended action. The method does not resolve semantic ambiguity in the original command.
Motion latent is out of the training set. If the first-stage motion generator produces a motion code from a text query it has never seen (e.g., 'moonwalk'), the code may not match real training examples. The diffusion policy then has no learned guidance. Generalization to new commands is limited by the motion generator's training data.
Physical infeasibility of latent. The motion latent is generated from human motion and may encode movements the humanoid robot cannot execute due to body differences (different joint ranges or muscle limits). The diffusion model can try to adapt, but cannot violate physics. The paper handles this with the oracle teacher policy training on robot-feasible trajectories only.
Done right. When the motion code comes from the same family the model saw during training, is unambiguous, and physically feasible for the robot, the method produces smooth, semantically aligned control with low latency. The hybrid architecture ensures temporal consistency.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small simulation (Isaac Gym or Bullet) with a humanoid. Collect a dataset of teacher policies executing 50 diverse motion sequences (walk, run, turn, sidestep, etc.), each paired with text descriptions. Train a diffusion policy on this paired dataset. Then, given a new text command, run the motion generator to produce a latent, feed it to the diffusion policy, and sample actions using DDIM. Compare the resulting robot trajectory to the oracle teacher's trajectory for the same latent, measuring joint angle error and end-effector position error.
