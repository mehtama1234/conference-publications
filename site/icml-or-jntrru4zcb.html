DAG: Dual Correlation Networks for Time Series with Exogenous Variables

A model that predicts future values by capturing how outside data affects both itself and the main signal, in two directions: across time and across different measurement types.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Predicting future numbers in a sequence (like tomorrow's temperature) is hard when you have side information (like tomorrow's humidity, which you might already know). This paper shows how to use that side information better by finding patterns in how it connects to the main signal, both looking backward in time and across different types of measurements at once.

The forecasting problem with outside data

Imagine predicting tomorrow's power plant output. You know the plant's own history (how much it made each hour), and you also have weather data (wind, sun, clouds). The weather should help, because more sun means more solar radiation hits the panels or natural light, which directly increases the plant's output. But most forecasting models treat these two streams of information separately or carelessly, as if the weather data is unrelated to the output.

A beginner might just feed both signals into one model and hope it learns. That fails because the model has to figure out on its own that today's weather changes predict tomorrow's weather, and that tomorrow's weather predicts tomorrow's output — two separate discovery tasks. Also, a beginner usually ignores information about the future of the outside data (for example, a weather forecast is a gift, but is thrown away). The model is working blind on half its clues.

In one sentence. The paper finds hidden patterns connecting outside data to the main signal and uses them to predict future values more accurately.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Look backward in time

Find patterns in how past outside data shaped past main values.

Step 2: Find connections across measurements

Detect which outside measurement types are most tied to the main signal.

Step 3: Use future outside data

Apply patterns learned from history to forecast future outside data.

Step 4: Combine the signals

Strengthen or weaken the main signal based on outside data values, because outside and main signals are linked.

Step 5: Predict ahead

Output the final forecast with correlations baked in.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most forecasting models feed outside data as flat extra input. This paper instead builds two separate networks that actively discover and inject the connections between outside data and the main signal, so the model treats them as genuinely linked rather than random side noise. By making the links explicit, the model can use them to improve predictions, because outside and main signals often depend on the same underlying causes.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: which connections matter

measured quantity  =  Correlation strength between signal types

The paper measures something it does not explicitly name: the strength of connection between each outside measurement and the main signal, and also between each outside measurement and itself at the next time step. This is a numerical score for each link — high scores mean the outside data is a strong predictor, low scores mean it is noise.

To estimate it, the paper uses two submodules for each direction. The temporal correlation discovery module looks at sequences over time and asks: does today's outside data tell us anything about tomorrow's outside data, or about today's main values? The channel correlation discovery module asks: across the different measurement types, which ones predict each other? These scores then feed into injection submodules that reweight and blend the signals.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Outside data carries two kinds of information: what it tells us about the main signal, and what it tells us about itself in the future. Most models treat these as separate problems. This paper treats them as one, because they are linked. If today's temperature predicts tomorrow's temperature (weather systems have inertia), and temperature predicts output (hotter days change demand because air conditioning runs more), then using tomorrow's temperature forecast helps predict tomorrow's output — the forecast absorbs the causal link, so the final prediction is more accurate. The trick is to find these chains automatically.

The model works in phases. First, it runs two discovery networks in parallel. One scans the sequence time-wise and asks each outside measurement: do you predict yourself in the future? Do you predict the main signal? The other scans across measurement types at each time step and asks: which of you move together? Second, it uses those discoveries to reweight the outside data before feeding it into the final forecast layer. High-scoring connections get amplified; low-scoring ones are dampened. This focuses the model on the signal and away from noise, which shrinks the forecast error because the model is not distracted by irrelevant outside measurements.

The equation

First in words:

The prediction is computed by taking the main signal (adjusted by temporal patterns) and adding the outside data (adjusted by both temporal and channel patterns), weighted by learned importance scores.

Then the compact form:

y_t = main_t + alpha * sum_i (w_i * temporal_corr_i * channel_corr_i * exog_{i,t+1})

Every symbol:

y_tpredicted main signal value at time t
main_tthe main signal value at time t
alphaoverall weight for how much to trust outside data
w_ilearned importance of outside variable i
temporal_corr_istrength of connection between outside variable i at time t and time t+1
channel_corr_istrength of connection between outside variable i and the main signal
exog_i,t+1the value of outside variable i at time t+1
A tiny worked example
Main signal value at noon: 100 units (e.g., 100 MW of power).
Outside variable 1 (temperature) at tomorrow: 25 degrees, learned weight w_1 = 0.8, temporal correlation 0.6, channel correlation 0.7.
Contribution from temperature: 0.8 * 0.6 * 0.7 * 25 = 8.4 units.
Outside variable 2 (wind) at tomorrow: 10 mph, w_2 = 0.2, temporal correlation 0.4, channel correlation 0.3.
Contribution from wind: 0.2 * 0.4 * 0.3 * 10 = 0.24 units.
Final forecast: 100 + 1.0 * (8.4 + 0.24) = 108.64 units. Temperature mattered more because both correlations were stronger.

The equation multiplies correlations because they compound: weak links in either direction kill the signal, while strong links in both directions amplify it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overfitting to historical correlations. If the outside data suddenly changes its relationship to the main signal (e.g., a factory installs new equipment), the learned correlations become outdated and forecasts drift. The model has learned patterns that no longer apply.
Poor forecasts of the outside data itself. If the model's forecast of tomorrow's outside measurements is wrong, it injects bad information. The correlation is real, but the input is bad, so the final forecast degrades.
Not enough historical data. Learning correlations requires many time steps of data. With sparse or short sequences, the model may see patterns by chance rather than real links.
Done right. The model works best when the outside data is stable over the prediction horizon, the correlations are genuine (not just coincidence), and enough historical data is available to learn them accurately.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple electricity demand forecaster on synthetic data where outside temperature and hour-of-day strongly predict demand, but a fake random outside variable does not. Show that the model discovers the real correlations (high scores for temperature and hour-of-day, low score for the fake one). Then prove the forecast is accurate when using the discovered correlations, but degrades if you set all correlations to zero. Finally, show that using future outside data (the known hour-of-day tomorrow) improves the forecast over using only past data, because tomorrow's hour is already known and it influences tomorrow's demand.

forecast_error = mean(abs(predicted - actual)), measured before and after using discovered correlations

The demo makes impossible to miss: which outside measurements the model trusted, how much the correlation discovery helped, and the specific benefit of using future outside data.
