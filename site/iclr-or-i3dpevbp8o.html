Real-time Q&A from Live Video: When Should the AI Answer?

Teaching AI to watch a live camera and answer questions at exactly the right moment, not too early or late.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Imagine handing a camera to a robot and asking it to answer your questions in real-time as events unfold on screen. Today's AI models can describe images and converse with audio input separately, but putting them together with a live camera proves surprisingly hard. This paper asks: when should the AI answer? Too early and it guesses wrong. Too late and it is not responsive. The authors built a dataset of 2900 real video-question pairs where timing matters, showing that existing models miss the moment and fail on situated reasoning—understanding what is actually happening right now.

Zero assumption version

Why Real-Time Q&A is Stuck

Today's vision-language models can describe a frozen image and produce a text answer, and separately they can transcribe speech in real-time. But imagine asking a robot holding a camera: 'How many people are at the table?' If the robot answers too early, before the camera shows the full scene, it guesses. If it waits too long, it seems unresponsive. A beginner might just feed the video frames to the model one at a time, asking for an answer frame-by-frame, but this fails: the model has no sense of when to output an answer. It might answer mid-sentence, or hang silent until the question is long over.

The harder problem is this: training data for Q&A typically has labels (question, answer) but no 'answer this now' signal—no timestamp saying when the question is complete and a response should come. So models never learn the timing skill. They also rarely see videos with genuine uncertainty (will the person catch the ball?) and audio together, so they lack situated understanding—the ability to reason about what is unfolding live, not just describe what is frozen on screen.

The method in one sentence. Build a dataset of real videos with question-answer pairs, label when the answer should come, then fine-tune models on this data to learn both understanding and timing.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect real videos.

Record 2900 videos of everyday scenes, each with one real question asked by a human, answered live by another person.

Step 2: Timestamp the answer.

Mark the exact moment the answer should come (when the question finishes, or when the key event happens on screen).

Step 3: Label what is happening.

Sort each video into one of 13 semantic categories (e.g., counting, temporal reasoning, action recognition) for analysis.

Step 4: Stream speech recognition.

Use a streaming audio model to detect when the question ends in real-time, not waiting for the full recording.

Step 5: Fine-tune the model.

Train existing video-language models on video-question-timestamp triples; audio timing and visual understanding reinforce each other because each modality supplies cues the other lacks.

What the Paper Changes

The paper changes the training data: it shifts from generic question-answer pairs (where timing is unknown) to video-question-answer triples where the correct answer time is labeled. This grounds training in the real constraint that answers must come at a specific moment.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Answer Timing Correctness

measured quantity  =  answer timing accuracy

Most models are trained to produce correct words but have no signal about when to produce them. The paper introduces a hidden quantity: the timing of the answer, measured as how close the model's response time sits to the true answer moment (as a percentage of video duration). An answer that comes exactly at the right instant has high timing accuracy; one that comes early or late has low accuracy.

The paper measures this by looking at the difference between (a) when the model chooses to output an answer and (b) the labeled timestamp. It also breaks this down by semantic category—some types of questions have answers scattered across the video duration, while others cluster near the end. This reveals which types of reasoning demand the tightest timing control.

Why it works

Why Learning Timing Fixes the Problem

Without timing labels in training, a model has no way to learn when to respond. It sees question-answer pairs but no signal tied to a specific moment in time. Fine-tuning on QIVD data works because it forces the model to associate the multimodal input (video frames plus audio) at time T with an output-now signal. Each training example says: 'At this exact moment in this video, the question is complete, and here is the answer.' Over many examples, the model learns to recognize the boundary between 'question ongoing' and 'time to answer.'

The mechanism has two parts. First, a streaming speech recognizer (Streaming-Whisper) transcribes audio in real-time, chunk by chunk, so the model knows when the human's voice stops—a strong cue that the question is done, because a pause in speech signals the end of an utterance. Second, the video encoder processes frames; the model learns to link the visual event (a ball landing, a door opening) to the audio cue. Fine-tuning on both modalities together works because the model can now cross-check: audio says 'question finished' and vision confirms 'the key event is now on screen.' A model trained only on vision does worse because timing is hidden inside the audio stream; without audio cues, the model must guess when the human stopped talking.

The Timing Signal

First in words:

answer_error = abs(predicted_time - ground_truth_time) / video_duration

Then the compact form:

loss_total = loss_understanding + lambda * loss_timing

Every symbol:

loss_understandingerror in the text answer words (does the answer match the reference?)
loss_timingerror in when the answer comes (how far off is the timestamp?)
lambdaweight balancing the two goals; high lambda means timing matters more
predicted_timethe moment the model decides to output an answer
ground_truth_timethe labeled correct moment from human annotation
video_durationtotal length of the video, used to normalize timing to a fraction
A tiny worked example
Video duration: 10 seconds. Question is 'How many apples are on the table?'
True answer moment: 6 seconds into the video (when the camera pans to show all apples).
Model 1 predicts answer at 2 seconds (too early): timing_error = abs(2 - 6) / 10 = 0.4, or 40% off.
Model 2 predicts answer at 6.5 seconds (almost right): timing_error = abs(6.5 - 6) / 10 = 0.05, or 5% off.
Model 2 is punished 8x less for timing, so training steers toward waiting for the visual cue (all apples in frame) before answering.

The loss combines two goals: saying the right words and saying them at the right moment. High emphasis on timing forces the model to learn the boundary between question-in-progress and time-to-answer.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Deictic references fail. Questions like 'How many people are here?' require the model to identify the referent ('here' means the current camera frame). Without training data where the camera position and question are tightly aligned in time, the model guesses incorrectly.
Answer timestamp ambiguity. Some questions have multiple valid answer times: 'When does she leave?' could be answered the moment she stands up, or when she walks out the door. If training labels only one, fine-tuned models may learn a different boundary than expected.
Streaming ASR errors cascade. If the speech recognizer misheard the question or misjudges when it ends, the model trains on a false timing signal. Audio quality variations (background noise, accents, speech rate) directly hurt timing accuracy.
Audio-only fine-tuning breaks vision. If the model is fine-tuned only on audio-visual data without seeing vision-only cases, it overfits: it relies entirely on audio timing and ignores visual cues. Then in deployment with microphone failure, performance collapses.
Done right. Collect diverse videos across categories. Annotate answer timestamps by having multiple humans agree on the 'right moment.' Use reliable streaming ASR that handles background noise and accents. Fine-tune on both modalities together; the model learns to wait for both cues because training examples show: when audio says 'question done' but vision shows the key object off-screen, no answer yet; when both align, answer now. Dual-signal training is more reliable than audio alone because a pause in speech can come mid-question, but a pause plus a visual event happening together is a much stronger signal that it is time to answer.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a live video system that (1) streams video from a camera, (2) runs streaming speech recognition on a microphone, (3) feeds both into a video-language model, (4) records the moment the model chooses to output an answer, and (5) compares that timestamp to a ground-truth label you provide by hand. Test on 20 videos from different semantic categories (counting, temporal reasoning, action). Measure both the correctness of the answer text and the distance from the ground-truth timing. Show that a model trained on QIVD data has lower timing error than a baseline trained only on generic image-text pairs.
