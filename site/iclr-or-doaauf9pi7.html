Natural Identifiers for Privacy Audits in Already-Trained Models

Use random strings that naturally appear in training data as auditing probes, so you can test a finished model's privacy without retraining it.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

When an LLM is trained, it may memorize parts of its training data, leaking private information. Before this paper, the only way to check if a model leaked specific data was to retrain it from scratch with fake 'canary' data inserted during training—impossibly expensive for already-finished models. This paper discovers that real training datasets already contain natural structured random strings: cryptographic hashes, shortened URLs, cloud-storage paths. The insight is simple: since these strings are already in the training data in known patterns, you can generate unlimited more of them without retraining, using them as auditing probes.

Zero assumption version

Privacy audits of finished models are stuck

After a large language model is trained, you worry: did it memorize private data? To find out, the old way is to take your LLM, retrain it from the beginning with fake 'canary' strings inserted—strings that you control, that you know are in the training data. Then you query the retrained model and see if it reproduces your canaries. If it does, the model memorized them. But retraining a model with many millions of weights costs weeks and thousands of dollars. For real-world models you do not own, it is impossible.

There is a second problem: checking if a particular dataset was used to train a model. You have a suspect dataset and a trained LLM. You want to know: was my private data fed into this model during training? The formal way to answer this is to grab a 'held-out' dataset—similar data that was definitely not used in training—then check if the model treats the suspect data differently. But finding a held-out dataset from the exact same pattern as your suspect data is practically impossible. In privacy litigation or compliance, you have no one to give you clean comparison data.

The stuck point: Retraining is too expensive, and auditing without retraining lacks the canary data you need to measure privacy leakage.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Find natural identifiers

Scan the training dataset for naturally-occurring structured random strings: URLs, hashes, API keys, shortened links.

Step 2: Verify structure

Confirm these strings follow a predictable pattern so you can generate new ones with the same structure.

Step 3: Generate test probes

Create unlimited new strings following the same pattern, without any retraining or data insertion.

Step 4: Run membership test

Feed the probes into the already-trained model and measure how often it produces the correct continuation.

Step 5: Audit the privacy

If the model reproduces many probes, you have evidence that the original data was memorized during training.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes what you can use as auditing probes. Instead of inserting your own fake canaries during training (impossible for finished models), you use structured random strings that are already in the real training data. Cryptographic hashes, URLs, and shortened links naturally occur in internet-crawled datasets. The paper's move is to use these existing strings as probes.

the object it moves  =  auditing probes (from inserted to natural)
The hidden quantity

The memorization signal

measured quantity  =  How much the model reproduces natural identifiers

The paper measures something simple: when you give the model a natural identifier (like part of a hash or URL), does it reproduce the rest of that string? If the model saw the full string during training and memorized it, it will finish the string correctly. If it only saw fragments or never saw it, the model will produce random text.

The paper counts how many test probes the model completes correctly, then compares that to random guessing. A high ratio means the model memorized the training data containing those strings. This count is the 'memorization signal'—the evidence of privacy leakage.

Why it works

Why this works: probes from the wild

The core insight is that internet-crawled datasets contain massive numbers of structured random strings. A cryptographic hash follows strict patterns (32 hex characters, or 256 bits as base64). A shortened URL follows rules (domain.com/abc123). When you look at any large training corpus, you find thousands of these. The paper's key observation: if these strings are already in the real training data with known structure, then you can generate unlimited new instances of strings that follow the exact same pattern, matching how they naturally appear.

Why does this solve the retraining problem? When you generate new probes that follow the pattern, they are 'same-distribution' held-out data—not in the original training set, but structurally identical to what was in it. You feed these new probes into an already-trained model. If the model memorized the pattern (because it saw thousands of real examples), it will guess correctly on the new probes. If it only learned shallow patterns, it guesses randomly. The ratio of correct to random guesses is your privacy signal. No retraining, no expensive canary insertion, no need for external comparison data. The wild itself provides the ammunition.

The mechanism breaks down into three moves: (1) Identify naturally-occurring structured strings in the dataset that are large enough and patterned enough to generate unlimited variants. (2) Generate new random strings that follow the same statistical structure—new hashes, new URLs—that are guaranteed not in the original training set. (3) Feed these to the finished model and measure memorization via a ranking test: does the model rank the true probe higher than random alternatives? If yes across many probes, the training data leaked into the model.

Math after the picture

The ranking-based privacy test

First in words:

privacy_lower_bound = quantile over many probes of how often the true probe ranks higher than random generated ones

Then the compact form:

privacy_signal = fraction of probe sets where true_NID ranked in top_r out of c candidates

Every symbol:

true_NIDa real natural identifier from the training data
ctotal number of candidate strings (true plus generated fakes)
rhow many top ranks we check (e.g., top 5 out of 32)
privacy_signalprobability model ranks true string higher than random guesses
A tiny worked example
Suppose you generate 32 candidates: 1 real hash that was in training, 31 fake hashes from the same pattern.
You feed all 32 to the model and rank them by model confidence (loss, perplexity).
If the real hash ranks #3, and r=5 (we count top 5), the true identifier is in top_r: signal += 1.
Run this on 1000 probe sets. If 850 have the true string in top_5, then privacy_signal = 850/1000 = 0.85.
A random guesser would rank correctly ~5/32 = 15.6% of the time. 85% means memorization.

The ranking test measures whether the model ranks the real string higher than random fakes. A random guesser would rank correctly only about one time in thirty. If the model ranks the real string high much more often than that, it must have seen and memorized it during training, proving the training data was not private.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Strings were not actually in the training set. If the natural identifier you picked was already filtered or never in the data, you have no signal. The test works only when the real training dataset truly contains your chosen string type.
The pattern is not actually learnable. If the training dataset has the strings but the model did not learn them (e.g., they were too rare or buried), the model will guess randomly and you will see no privacy signal.
Data crawlers already filtered these strings. If web crawlers excluded hashes or URLs during dataset collection, your natural identifiers never made it in. You then test on non-existent data and get false negatives.
Done right. Verify that your chosen natural identifier type is genuinely abundant in the raw training dataset. Manually inspect samples. Confirm the pattern is frequent enough to learn. Then your test is valid.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A real implementation would take a small open-source language model (e.g., Pythia 70M or 410M) with known training data, extract natural identifiers from the training set (URLs from the Pile, cryptographic hashes from code), generate new random instances that follow the same pattern, then query the model to see how often it ranks the real ones higher than random candidates. The metric is the rank-based membership inference score: the fraction of probe sets where the true natural identifier appears in the model's top-r ranked positions, compared to random guessing.

privacy_signal = true_positives_in_top_r / total_probes — random_baseline

The demo makes impossible to miss: if natural identifiers from the training data rank much higher than random fake identifiers, the model memorized the training data and the privacy audit has succeeded post-hoc, without retraining.
