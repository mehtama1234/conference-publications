Nested Spatio-Temporal Forecasting

To predict future traffic at each intersection, first predict what the whole city will do, then use that big-picture forecast to guide predictions for each neighborhood.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Traffic forecasts are hard because cities are complicated: what happens at one intersection depends on many neighbors, and patterns change over time. Most methods try to predict everything at once, which gets confused. This paper proposes thinking in layers: first predict big regional trends, then use those trends to steer precise predictions for each individual spot.

Zero assumption version

Predicting traffic is like guessing a crowd

Traffic at each intersection next hour depends on: weather, nearby roads, time of day, and how traffic flowing from neighbors affects flow here. The naive approach tries to predict all 500 intersections independently, or all at once using one giant equation. Both fail under real-world noise.

With independent predictions, you miss that neighborhoods move together. With one-giant-equation, the model gets overwhelmed by high-frequency jitter and loses the big trends. It gets stuck trying to memorize random spikes instead of learning that Tuesday afternoons have a predictable pattern city-wide.

The problem in one sentence. Models trained to predict every intersection fine-grained traffic fail to capture how large regions move together, and they get drowned in local noise.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Group intersections by similarity

Use affinity (how similar each pair is) to group nearby intersections into M regions using spectral clustering.

Encode historical data twice

Run two separate neural networks: one reads 12-step history for each intersection, one for each region.

Predict regional trends

The region encoder outputs a coarse forecast (the big picture 12 steps ahead).

Cross-attention: top-down steering

Let each intersection query the region forecast, pulling its own prediction toward the regional trend.

Multi-step rollout with masking

During training, mask some future regional guidance and rebuild it, teaching the model to cope when guidance is missing at test time.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how future information flows into predictions. Normally, a model predicts each intersection independently. Here, the model explicitly constructs a coarse future forecast at the region level, then channels that forecast down to each intersection prediction via attention (a learned weighted average). The intersection predictions are no longer solo; they are pulled by the bigger picture.

the object it moves  =  Future macro forecast, injected via attention
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Regional coherence

measured quantity  =  Regional coherence score

Regional coherence measures how much each intersection's prediction aligns with its region's trend. High coherence means the intersection prediction has incorporated the regional pattern. The paper estimates this by attention weights (learned scales): when an intersection piece (or representation) pays strong attention to its region's piece, coherence is high.

The paper uses this implicitly in the cross-attention mechanism. At training, intersections that attend (pay attention, or weight the input heavily) more to the regional signal improve more. During rollout (multi-step prediction), intersections with higher coherence to their region are more stable because they are anchored to a larger, smoother signal.

Why it works

Why it works: Hierarchical constraint and stable rollout

Spatio-temporal forecasting is hard because noise at each intersection masks the large pattern. Without guidance, the model overfits to jitter. By forcing the model to also predict regions (coarse scale), the model learns that big patterns are smooth and repeatable. When it then steers each intersection prediction via cross-attention to the regional forecast, each intersection's prediction is anchored to a signal it knows is true (it just predicted the region). This anchor prevents the model from chasing noise.

During multi-step rollout (generating 12 steps by feeding your own predictions back as input), errors compound: each wrong step feeds into the next, amplifying drift. But if intersections attend to regional predictions, and regions change slowly, the region forecast stays stable longer. Even if an intersection prediction drifts slightly, the regional signal pulls it back. The multi-step rollout with masked guidance during training teaches the model to trust and repair the regional signal even when pieces are missing, so it learns to hold onto that signal tightly across all prediction steps.

Math after the picture

The affinity and regional guidance

First in words:

For each node i in chunk k, compute its representation as the average of its historical values in that chunk. Then measure how similar each pair of nodes is using distance, converted into affinity by an exponential: nodes that are close in history have high affinity.

Then the compact form:

A_ij = exp( -(1/(2*sigma^2)) * sum_k ||X_i^(k) - X_j^(k)||_2^2 )

Every symbol:

A_ijAffinity score between node i and node j (0 to 1, higher means more similar)
X_i^(k)Historical time series of node i during chunk k
sigmaBandwidth control (how sharply affinity drops with distance)
||...||_2Euclidean distance (straight-line distance between time patterns)
A tiny worked example with 3 intersections
Intersection A and B: historical distance in chunk 1 is 0.5, in chunk 2 is 0.4. Average squared distance = (0.5^2 + 0.4^2)/2 = (0.25 + 0.16)/2 = 0.205.
With sigma=1, exponent = -0.205/2 = -0.1025. Affinity A_AB = exp(-0.1025) = 0.903 (very similar).
Intersection A and C: average squared distance is 2.5. Exponent = -2.5/2 = -1.25. Affinity A_AC = exp(-1.25) = 0.287 (not very similar).
Spectral clustering groups A and B together into region 1, C into a different region, because their affinity is high.

High affinity pairs end up in the same region, so the region forecast captures correlated behavior.

What can go wrong

Spectral clustering may miss real boundaries If the affinity matrix (built from historical similarity) does not match true spatial structure, regions become artificial. For example, two geographically distant intersections that happen to have similar traffic patterns might be grouped together, ignoring actual road topology. The region forecast then predicts correlated noise rather than true regional motion.
Rollout error accumulates when the region forecast drifts During multi-step rollout (12 steps ahead), if the region's prediction is slightly wrong at step 5, that wrong input flows into step 6 and beyond. The node predictions are anchored to bad guidance. The masked guidance reconstruction loss helps reduce this drift because it teaches the model to recover from partial missing guidance, training it to be less sensitive to small errors, but it cannot fully prevent all drift because real forecasts at far horizons have irreducible uncertainty.
Training-test mismatch without boundary masking During training, the region forecast is given (teacher forcing). At test time (when the model runs on new data), it must be generated from its own predictions (rollout). Without the boundary masking strategy (randomly dropping regional guidance during training), the model overfits to perfect guidance and fails when running on real data.
Done right: regional guidance is stable under noise The method works when regions aggregate nodes with similar periodic patterns. Traffic patterns repeat weekly; spectral clustering on historical data usually captures these repetitions. Regional predictions are smoother than node predictions because they average across many nodes, so they resist jitter. Node predictions anchored to smooth guidance are naturally more stable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny spatio-temporal forecasting task: a grid of 25 intersections in a 5x5 lattice, with 5 regions defined by spatial proximity. Generate 1000 steps of synthetic traffic: each intersection has a 100-step periodic pattern, plus Gaussian noise. Train a baseline model (node-only attention) and the nested model (regional encoder plus cross-attention). For each model, forecast 12 steps ahead using autoregressive rollout (feeding predictions back as input). Compare mean absolute error (MAE) on held-out test data.

Stability gap = (baseline MAE at step 12) minus (nested MAE at step 12). Positive means nested forecasting is steadier over long horizons.

The demo must show that as rollout steps increase, the baseline error grows faster than the nested model's error, proving that the regional guidance keeps predictions from drifting because the guidance provides a stable anchor that resists accumulated noise, whereas the baseline has no such reference point and errors snowball.
