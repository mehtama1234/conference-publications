Contextual and Seasonal LSTMs for Time Series Anomaly Detection

A time series anomaly detector that separates noise from signal and watches for both subtle jumps and gradual creep by learning two separate patterns at once.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Detecting hidden problems in server logs and sensor readings is hard because anomalies hide in noise and come in different shapes. Some jump suddenly; others creep upward slowly. Existing methods miss these because they guess the "normal" shape from noise-filled data. This paper builds a detector that splits the noise away first, then learns both the regular rhythm and the slow drift separately, catching anomalies the old methods miss.

Zero assumption version

Noise hides anomalies in time series data

A server metric like CPU usage or memory is a list of numbers, one per minute or hour. The reading bounces up and down naturally—sometimes spiking for no reason, sometimes dipping. Inside that jitter is the real signal: the steady baseline, the seasonal rhythm (busier at lunch, quieter at night), and—buried in there somewhere—a tiny jump that means something broke.

Most detectors try to predict what the next reading should be, then flag anything far from that guess as an anomaly. But if the prediction is trained on noisy data, it learns the noise as part of normal and misses the real anomalies. A small spike gets lost. A slow upward drift (like a memory leak) looks gradual enough to seem normal.

The beginner's fix: clean the noise first, then look for anomalies. The problem is noise and signal are tangled together, and the right way to untangle them depends on the data—no one rule works everywhere.

In one sentence. The paper learns two separate time series patterns at once—one for regular rhythm, one for context—so anomalies stand out without getting buried in noise.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of one neural network guessing the "normal" reading, the paper uses two separate networks. One learns the repeating seasonal rhythm; the other learns the current context (the slow-drift level). By splitting the job, each network gets a clearer signal.

the object it moves  =  prediction model: from one network to two
The hidden quantity

The deviation residual

measured quantity  =  how far the reading strays from expected

After the two networks predict what the reading should be (seasonal + context), the residual is the actual reading minus that prediction. Small residuals are normal jitter; large residuals are anomalies. The paper calls this the 'reconstruction error' or deviation.

The paper estimates it by comparing the real reading to the sum of the two network predictions. Large deviations mean something broke. In practice, anomaly thresholds are set from statistics of normal data, and anything beyond that threshold is flagged.

Why it works

Why splitting the job catches anomalies

A single network trained on noisy data absorbs the noise as part of the normal pattern—it learns to output 'sometimes jittery, sometimes smooth' without knowing which is true. When an anomaly arrives, it looks like just another wiggle.

By splitting into seasonal + contextual, each network solves an easier problem. The seasonal network only learns the repeating cycle (Monday always looks like Monday). The contextual network only learns the slow drift of the baseline level. Neither one needs to absorb random jitter, so they predict the "clean" signal. Anything left over is noise or anomaly, and anomalies are bigger than noise, so they stand out.

Adding frequency-domain signals (looking at the reading as waves instead of ticks) helps catch anomalies that are slow, because a memory leak looks like a smooth upward creep, which shows up as a new low-frequency wave pattern that the seasonal network alone would miss. The two networks together catch both sudden jumps (seasonal mismatch) and slow trends (contextual mismatch).

A tiny worked example
Time step 5 (e.g., 9 AM): seasonal predicts 55 (busy hour), contextual predicts 40 (current baseline level)
y_hat = 55 + 40 = 95 predicted reading
Actual reading: 94 (normal jitter of -1)
Residual: 94 - 95 = -1, below threshold, not flagged as anomaly
Time step 10 (2 PM): seasonal predicts 52 (still busy), contextual predicts 40, actual is 110
Residual: 110 - (52 + 40) = 18, above threshold, flagged as anomaly (sudden spike)

The two LSTMs predict the clean signal; anything left is noise or anomaly, and anomalies are bigger.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Seasonal pattern changes without warning. If the schedule shifts (e.g., new deployment, maintenance window), the seasonal network predicts the old rhythm and flags everything as anomalous. The paper does not address how to adapt when the normal baseline shifts.
Very slow anomalies look like new context. If a problem develops over hours (a memory leak), the contextual network may learn it as the new normal baseline, so the residual never grows large. The paper mitigates this by adding frequency-domain checks, but it is not perfect.
Threshold tuning is fragile. Calling something an anomaly relies on picking the right residual threshold. Too low, false alarms; too high, missing real problems. Different metrics (time-domain, frequency-domain) may have different thresholds, and combining them is not straightforward.
Done right. The two networks stay focused on learning the clean pattern without absorbing noise. If the seasonal rhythm and contextual level are truly separate, deviations stand out clearly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple synthetic time series: a base level that drifts slowly upward, a repeating seasonal cycle (e.g., sine wave), and random jitter. Insert a few real anomalies: a sudden spike at one time step, a rapid multi-step creep at another. Train a single-network baseline LSTM and the two-network seasonal + contextual model on the clean portion. Plot both models' residuals and show that the two-network model separates anomalies from noise more clearly.

anomaly detection rate = (true anomalies flagged) / (total real anomalies)

The demo makes plain that splitting the job—one network for rhythm, one for level—is not magic; it is just giving each network a simpler pattern to learn.
