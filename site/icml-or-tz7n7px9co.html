From Directions to Regions: Decomposing Activations via Local Geometry

Instead of hunting for single directions that explain concepts, carve the activation space into regions, then map what varies inside each region.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Language models store concepts as patterns in their internal activations (the numbers flowing between layers). Most methods try to find one golden direction for each concept (like a compass bearing). But concepts are not that simple—they twist and curve. This paper shows that concepts live as small, localized neighborhoods: each neighborhood bundles related examples, and inside each neighborhood, a simpler low-rank structure emerges. By learning these regions first, then the local structure within them, we get a far more honest picture of what the model actually learned.

Zero assumption version

Why single directions fail

Today's concept-discovery methods assume each concept—say, 'words about finance'—points in one direction through activation space, like a compass bearing. They then hunt for that direction by scanning through millions of possible arrows.

But real concepts do not work that way. Some words about finance cluster tightly (they look similar inside the model). Other financial concepts are spread out and tangled. A single direction cannot capture both tight clusters and loose clouds at the same time. The naive approach gives a coarse, distorted picture—like forcing a three-dimensional shape flat onto a line.

The method in one sentence: Instead of searching for one direction per concept, slice the activation space into many small regions, learn what varies predictably inside each region, and use those local patterns to decode concepts.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect activations

Extract the internal numbers from the model at a fixed layer for many input texts.

Initialize K regions

Guess K starting locations in activation space (using random init or K-Means).

Fit each region's cloud

For each region, learn the center and a low-rank covariance that fits the local data.

Assign activations to regions

For each activation, compute which region it belongs to, weighted by likelihood.

Decode concepts locally

Use the region's learned axes to read off what concept is active in this neighborhood.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper replaces global direction-hunting with local region-based decomposition. Instead of asking 'which single arrow?' it asks 'which neighborhood, and what varies inside it?'

the object it moves  =  decomposition method
The hidden quantity

The local low-rank subspace

measured quantity  =  Within-region variation

Every semantic region has 'soft' directions—the axes along which activations vary most predictably. Most variation happens along just a few axes (low-rank), while noise spreads across many.

The paper estimates this by fitting a factor analyzer (a classical statistical tool) inside each region. The analyzer learns a small number of underlying patterns (called latent factors: hidden causes that explain why the numbers correlate) that explain the correlations between the activation's many dimensions, then throws away the noise. Inside a region of 'finance words', for example, one pattern might track 'transaction urgency', another might track 'entity type', and most other dimensions are just noise.

Plain reading. A region is like a filing cabinet drawer that holds similar examples. A low-rank subspace is the bundle of the 2-3 most important labels on the folders inside that drawer; most folders sit along those labels.
Paper reading. Each mixture component has a Gaussian shape with center and covariance. The covariance is built from factor loadings (the learned local axes) and a noise term. Typically 10 factors per region are enough.
Why it works

Why regions beat global directions

Concepts sit at different scales. 'The word in' always means the same thing in grammar—it clusters tightly. But 'emotions in text' span both the direct word (happy, sad) and the implied mood (anger in sarcasm). A single direction cannot fit both. By partitioning into regions first, tight concepts sit in small, dense clouds, while broad concepts get their own spacious region, and no compromise happens.

Inside each region, the factor analyzer learns the actual local structure instead of forcing it into a global line. If 'financial concepts' have two main axes (one for urgency, one for entity type), the method discovers both, not tries to project everything onto one borrowed direction. This means steering (nudging the activation to change the model's output) becomes precise: you move along the region's local axes, not drift randomly because you are pushing on a misdefined global direction.

The covariance formula

First in words:

Each region k has a mean center and a simplified covariance made from a low-rank matrix times its transpose, plus a uniform noise term

Then the compact form:

C_k = W_k W_k^T + Psi

Every symbol:

C_kthe covariance (shape of the cloud) for region k
W_kthe factor loadings, a tall matrix of R learned directions for region k
Psia diagonal matrix of noise variance, shared across all regions
W_k W_k^Tprojects the R-dimensional latent space back to full dimension
A tiny worked example with three regions
Suppose activation space is 8-dimensional. Region 1 (finance words): center at [2,3,1,2,1,1,1,2], with R=2 factors. The two learned directions are 'urgency' and 'entity-type', explaining 85% of variance within this region.
Region 2 (emotion words): center at [1,1,3,1,2,1,1,1], also R=2, but the two factors are 'valence' and 'arousal', because emotions have a different structure.
When a new token (say, the word 'worried') arrives with activation [1.5,0.8,3.2,0.9,2.1,1.1,1.0,0.8], the method computes its likelihood in each region's Gaussian and assigns it to region 2 (emotion) with high confidence. Then it projects the activation onto region 2's learned valence+arousal axes.

The low-rank approximation means each region is summarized by just a few learned directions, not all 8 independent ones.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the wrong number of regions K. If K is too small, diverse concepts force-fit into one region. If K is too large, noise fragments into fake 'regions.' The paper experiments with K at 1000, 8000, and 32000, and leaves K selection open; practitioners need a held-out validation set.
Regions with no clear interpretation. Some discovered regions may capture noisy correlations in the training data rather than true concepts. The paper mitigates this by manually inspecting the top activations in each region, but scale makes this tedious.
Steering may alter unrelated concepts. When you nudge an activation along a region's learned axis, you might move along a direction that entangles multiple concepts. The paper shows this is rare but does not fully eliminate it.
Done right: layer-specific decomposition works. The method trains separate MFAs at layer 6 and layer 18 (mid and late layers), because each layer handles different abstraction levels. This is sensible and avoids forcing a single structure across the whole model.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment: (1) Pick a language model (Llama-8B or Gemma-2B, or even a toy model). (2) Extract activations from one layer for 1000 texts labeled with known concepts (e.g., texts hand-tagged as 'finance', 'emotion', 'science', 'sports'). (3) Train an MFA with 100 regions and 10 latent factors per region. (4) For each held-out test activation, assign it to the highest-probability region, then project onto that region's learned axes. (5) Check: do nearby activations (by MFA region membership) share the same concept label? (6) Attempt a steering intervention: nudge an activation along the region's learned axes and feed it back into the model. Does the model's output change in the intended direction?

concept_recall = (activations correctly matched to their true concept) / (total activations) ; steering_success = (steered outputs classified as the target concept) / (attempted steers)

The demo must show: (a) activations cluster by human-labeled concept within regions, and (b) steering along the learned axes changes the model's behavior more precisely than steering along random directions.
