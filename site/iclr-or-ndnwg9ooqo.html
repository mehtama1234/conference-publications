NextStep-1: Autoregressive Image Generation with Continuous Tokens at Scale

NextStep-1 generates images one token at a time using a flow-matching head for smooth continuous image tokens, avoiding the quality loss of discretization.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Making pictures by predicting pixels one step at a time is hard. Most models convert images to discrete tokens (like quantized colors) and lose fidelity, or use expensive diffusion to handle continuous data. NextStep-1 trains a very large model (14 billion adjustable values called weights) that predicts one token at a time—an autoregressive approach. It pairs this with a smaller flow-matching head (157 million weights) that directly predicts continuous image tokens—the raw smooth numbers—alongside text tokens, getting crisp pictures without discretization loss.

The Autoregressive Picture Problem

Autoregressive models are simple: predict the next word, then the next, building sentences step-by-step. They work beautifully for text. For images, you might try the same—encode an image as a long sequence of numbers (called tokens) and predict each one in order. But images are different from words: they are fundamentally smooth and continuous. The best-quality data is continuous—raw pixel brightness values ranging over a smooth spectrum.

Existing approaches make a painful choice. One path: convert the image to discrete tokens using vector quantization (VQ)—basically, round each value to the nearest bin, like rounding 52.7 to 50. This destroys detail. The other path: use a heavy diffusion model (a process that slowly denoises random noise into a picture) to handle the continuous values. This works but costs enormous computation. Neither path has solved the problem well.

In one sentence. NextStep-1 trains a transformer to predict continuous image tokens directly—no quantization loss, no heavy diffusion—by pairing a language model with a lightweight flow-matching head that learns to map noise to the next image patch.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Tokenize the input

Text becomes discrete word tokens; images are encoded as 16 continuous channels downsampled 8x.

Forward through the transformer

The 14B autoregressive transformer processes text and image tokens in sequence, outputting hidden states.

Two heads, two losses

Text hidden states feed a language head (with text prediction loss, measuring how well each word is predicted); image hidden states feed the flow matching head.

Flow matching predicts the next patch

The 157M flow-matching head (12-layer MLP) receives a noisy sample and predicts the velocity field that flows it toward the target patch.

Train end-to-end, generate step-by-step

During generation, sample noise, run the flow matching head, decode the patch, repeat for all patches.

What the paper changes

The paper adds a lightweight flow-matching head—a specialized 157-million-parameter neural network—that learns to predict continuous image tokens directly from the transformer's hidden states.

The hidden quantity

Velocity: The Flow Direction

measured quantity  =  velocity (the flow direction from noise to target)

Flow matching is based on a hidden quantity: the velocity field. Imagine you have random noise and you want to transform it into a picture. The velocity is the direction and speed you should move the noise at each point to reach the target smoothly. Formally, the velocity is the time-derivative of the path from noise to target—how fast to change each value at each moment.

NextStep-1 estimates this velocity for each image patch separately. At training time, the flow matching head sees the target patch and learns to predict the velocity that would transform a random noisy sample into that target. At generation time, the model samples random noise and integrates forward along the predicted velocity to generate the image patch.

Why it works

Why Continuous Tokens and Flow Matching Work

The core problem is that discrete tokens lose information—you must round 52.7 to 50, and that loss accumulates. With continuous tokens, you keep the exact value. But now prediction becomes harder: you must model a smooth, high-dimensional space. Standard text prediction losses (the usual approach for discrete data) do not fit well here; they expect a choice among a fixed set of options.

Flow matching is designed for exactly this. Instead of predicting "which of 256 bins is next," the flow matching head learns to predict the velocity—the smooth direction and speed of motion—that transforms random noise into the target. This is mathematically cleaner for continuous data because it works in an unrestricted real-number space, not a discrete grid. The velocity field is estimated via mean-squared error on trajectories, a natural loss for continuous generation.

This design improves quality because no discretization loss means continuous tokens preserve exact values instead of rounding them away, and because the lightweight flow-matching head (157M weights) is much faster than a heavy diffusion model (which must run hundreds of denoising steps) since it learns a direct path from noise to the target rather than iterating gradually. Additionally, the transformer handles semantic understanding ("what a dog on a rock looks like") while the flow head handles pixel conversion (turning that understanding into smooth patches), letting each part focus on its strength. By training both pieces end-to-end with a unified next-token objective, the transformer learns when to generate text versus images and the flow head learns to match that semantic context, preserving language capabilities while generating crisp images.

The Loss Function and Flow Matching

First in words:

Total loss is a weighted sum of cross-entropy loss for text tokens and flow-matching loss for image tokens. Cross-entropy measures how well the language head predicts the next text token (treating each token as a discrete choice). Flow-matching loss measures how well the flow head predicts the velocity field for transforming noise into the target patch, computed as mean-squared error on the velocity predictions.

Then the compact form:

L_total = (1 - alpha) * L_CE + alpha * L_FM where L_CE is text cross-entropy loss and L_FM is the patch-wise mean-squared error: sum over patches of ||v_predicted(x_t, t) - v_target(x_t, t)||^2

Every symbol:

L_totalthe total training loss
alphathe weight balancing text and image (ratio of tokens or hyperparameter)
L_CEcross-entropy loss for discrete text tokens
L_FMflow matching loss for continuous image patches
v_predictedthe velocity the model's head estimates
v_targetthe true velocity that transforms noise into the target
x_tthe noisy sample at time t (between 0 and 1)
ta time value from 0 (pure noise) to 1 (target patch)
A tiny worked example
Patch 1: alpha = 0.5, L_CE = 2.5 (text tokens predicted well), L_FM = 0.8 (velocity close to true), L_total = 0.5*(2.5) + 0.5*(0.8) = 1.25 + 0.4 = 1.65.
Patch 2: alpha = 0.5, L_CE = 2.5, L_FM = 3.2 (velocity far from true), L_total = 0.5*(2.5) + 0.5*(3.2) = 1.25 + 1.6 = 2.85.
Higher flow-matching loss means the head struggled to predict the right flow; the training algorithm nudges the weights backward to improve next step. Balancing both losses keeps language and image generation in sync.

The flow matching loss measures velocity predictions (smooth paths from noise to images), avoiding discretization error and fitting naturally with the continuous token space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unstable training with high-dimensional continuous representations. The 16-channel space (16 numbers per image patch) is large and prone to divergence. The paper emphasizes this design choice is essential; without careful tuning, the flow matching head's weight nudges grow too large and the training diverges or fails to converge.
Accumulation of velocity prediction errors. If the flow matching head predicts velocity poorly early in generation, errors compound as you integrate forward, producing blurry or distorted patches later in the image.
Language capability degradation. If the weighting alpha tilts too far toward image tokens, the model's text understanding suffers. The paper mitigates this by including 400 billion text-only tokens during pre-training.
Done right. End-to-end training, careful loss weighting, and large-scale text pre-training keep both modalities strong. The lightweight flow head adds minimal overhead.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small autoregressive model (e.g., a 200-million-parameter transformer, not the full 14B) that predicts both tiny text sequences and tiny 4x4 image patches. Train it on a small image-caption dataset (e.g., 10k images). Implement two heads: a standard text prediction head for text and a lightweight 10-million-parameter flow matching head for images. Log the training loss split (text vs. image) and sample generation every epoch.

Quality = (1 - blur_score) * (text_accuracy) where blur_score measures how blurry the generated patches are, and text_accuracy is the fraction of captions correctly predicted before image generation.

The demo must show that continuous tokens (without quantization) produce sharper patches than a baseline quantized version, and that the flow-matching loss converges smoothly without training instability.
