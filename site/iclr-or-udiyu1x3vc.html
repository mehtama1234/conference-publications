Proper Velocity Neural Networks

Replace constrained hyperbolic spaces with an unconstrained alternative rooted in physics to avoid numerical crashes when learning tree-like structures.

Tree-shaped data appears everywhere: family trees, taxonomies, hierarchies of concepts. Hyperbolic neural networks excel at learning these because hyperbolic space stretches more and more as you move outward, letting the network assign many leaves to the same parent without crowding. But existing hyperbolic networks squeeze all calculations into a bounded ball or surface, which causes numerical instability when data edges push near the boundary. This paper switches to Proper Velocity space, an unconstrained coordinate system from Einstein's special relativity, and shows it stays stable across all four learning tasks: numerical stress tests, image classification, graph structures, and genetic sequences.

Zero assumption version

Hyperbolic networks hit numerical walls

A hyperbolic space is a coordinate system where distances stretch as you move outward—the outer regions expand more and more. This stretching is perfect for tree structures because the outer region can hold exponentially more points than the same distance in flat space. Neural networks have learned to exploit this: put the root of a tree at the center, and let each layer spread outward; thousands of leaves can fit at the edge without overlapping.

Most existing hyperbolic neural networks work inside a bounded surface, like the Poincaré ball (all points squeezed into a ball) or the hyperboloid (points on a curved sheet). These models have neat closed-form formulas for adding lists of numbers and doing matrix multiplies in hyperbolic space, which is convenient. But the boundary acts as a hard limit: when data edges get close to the limit, numerical errors explode. A weight update that overshoots into the boundary, or a calculation that lands exactly on the edge, corrupts all downstream results. A beginner might try to just clip values at the boundary, but that breaks the gradients (which direction to nudge the weights to shrink the error), making training fail silently.

In one sentence. Existing hyperbolic networks crash near boundaries; this paper uses an unconstrained space that never hits a wall.

What it actually does

Step 1: Pick unconstrained coordinates

Represent each point as a list of numbers with no bounds, using velocity vectors (lists of speed values) from special relativity.

Step 2: Define Riemannian operators

Build the full toolkit for adding, multiplying, and taking derivatives in this space—the same operations that Poincaré needs.

Step 3: Implement standard layer types

Rewrite fully connected layers (weighted sums), local feature detectors (pixel neighborhoods), nonlinearities (thresholds), and batch normalization to work in Proper Velocity coordinates.

Step 4: Train without instability

All calculations stay numerically well-behaved; weights never hit a boundary where errors blow up.

Step 5: Verify on four benchmarks

Show that the network learns trees, images, graphs, and sequences as accurately as Poincaré but without crashes.

Why it works

Why unconstrained coordinates stay stable

A hyperbolic space can be built many ways—as a curved surface in higher-dimensional flat space, or in coordinates with a special distance metric. The Poincaré ball uses coordinates bounded to a ball, then measures hyperbolic distance as a formula applied to those bounded coordinates. As coordinates approach the boundary, the distance formula's denominator shrinks, and the distance explodes. Gradients (which way to nudge) blow up proportionally, inverting the learning signal.

Proper Velocity space uses unbounded coordinates with a different distance formula—one rooted in special relativity, where the distance is computed using velocity magnitudes rather than a bounded region. Because there is no boundary, no denominator goes to zero. A weight that doubles in magnitude simply moves twice as far in the curved space; the gradient scales proportionally and stays finite. The network can push weights as large as needed without triggering numerical explosion. The curvature that makes hyperbolic space perfect for trees is still there (the metric still curves), so the network still prefers to spread leaves into the outer region—but now the outer region is infinite and always stable.

Math after the picture

Distance in Proper Velocity space

First in words:

hyperbolic distance equals the inverse hyperbolic cosine of one plus half the squared difference between velocity vectors, divided by the product of their speed magnitudes

Then the compact form:

d_h(u, v) = arccosh(1 + (|u - v|^2) / (2 * sqrt(1 + |u|^2) * sqrt(1 + |v|^2)))

Every symbol:

d_h(u, v)hyperbolic distance between two points u and v in Proper Velocity coordinates
arccosh(x)inverse hyperbolic cosine function
|u|, |v|the speed magnitude (length of the velocity vector)
|u - v|the ordinary Euclidean distance between the two velocity vectors
1 + |u|^2, 1 + |v|^2speed-dependent normalization factors from relativity
A tiny worked example
Say u = [0.5, 0.3] and v = [0.6, 0.4]. Then |u - v| = sqrt((0.1)^2 + (0.1)^2) = 0.141.
|u|^2 = 0.25 + 0.09 = 0.34, so sqrt(1 + |u|^2) = sqrt(1.34) ≈ 1.158.
|v|^2 = 0.36 + 0.16 = 0.52, so sqrt(1 + |v|^2) = sqrt(1.52) ≈ 1.232.
Numerator: (0.141)^2 / 2 ≈ 0.00995. Denominator: 1.158 * 1.232 ≈ 1.428.
Fraction: 0.00995 / 1.428 ≈ 0.007. Argument to arccosh: 1 + 0.007 = 1.007.
d_h ≈ arccosh(1.007) ≈ 0.084. The distance is small and well-defined, with no explosion.

Unlike the Poincaré ball, where this fraction explodes near the boundary, every calculation here stays stable no matter how large the velocity vectors grow.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If you forget to compute the relativity normalization factors. The speed-dependent factors in the denominator matter; omit them and the distance formula breaks its hyperbolic property, and you lose the tree advantage.
If you apply layer normalizations designed for flat space. Batch normalization in ordinary flat space (unbounded coordinates, equal spacing) assumes the mean and variance are computed in flat coordinates. In hyperbolic space, the mean is meaningless; the paper rewrites it using the proper hyperbolic average.
If you treat this as a magic stability cure for any network. Proper Velocity space has curvature that favors certain structures (trees and hierarchies). On flat datasets, the overhead of hyperbolic math offers no benefit and may slow convergence.
Done right: compute all layer operations using the hyperbolic toolkit. Fully connected (weighted sums), local feature detectors (neighborhood products), thresholds (squashing functions), and normalization all get Proper Velocity versions. Train with standard gradient descent; no special tricks needed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train two hyperbolic neural networks on a benchmark hierarchical graph (e.g., a taxonomy with thousands of concepts). One uses Poincaré ball coordinates; the other uses Proper Velocity. Run both for 100 iterations with a fixed learning rate. Log the norm (magnitude) of the largest weight and the loss at each step. Watch the loss curve: Poincaré should show sudden jumps or NaNs (not a number) when weights approach the boundary; Proper Velocity should show smooth convergence. Record the final accuracy on a held-out test set of nodes the network has never seen.

stability score = 1 - (number of NaN or Inf steps / total steps); accuracy = fraction of test nodes classified correctly; smooth training = loss curve has no jumps larger than 10% of the loss magnitude.

A successful demo proves that Proper Velocity networks train without crashes and achieve the same accuracy as Poincaré—the stability gain comes for free, with no accuracy loss.
