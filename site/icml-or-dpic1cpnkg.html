The Implicit Bias of Adam and Muon on Smooth Homogeneous Neural Networks

Momentum-based optimizers like Adam and Muon automatically push toward classifying data by maximizing the gap between the groups, not just fitting the data.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When you train a neural network with Adam or Muon, the optimizer does not just memorize the training examples. Instead, momentum-based optimizers have a hidden bias: they naturally steer the model toward solutions that create the largest possible gap (margin) between the two groups in your data. This paper proves why that happens and which gap each optimizer maximizes.

Zero assumption version

Optimizers do their job, but why do they generalize?

A naive way to train a classifier is simple: adjust the numbers (weights) in the network so that the training examples get classified correctly. If you do that, the model memorizes the data. But in practice, models trained this way still work well on new data they have never seen. This is surprising. Fitting training data should produce models that memorize it and fail on new cases, but they often generalize instead—because the optimizer does not just fit the data, it shapes the decision boundary to be far from the training points, creating a wide safety margin for new points to land on the correct side.

The mystery deepens when you pick an optimizer. If you use plain error-reduction (the most direct path to lower error), your model follows one path. If you use momentum (which remembers the direction you were going), you follow a different path. If you use Adam (which adjusts the step size for each number separately), you follow yet another path. Do they all reach the same place? No. Do they all generalize equally? Also no. So the optimizer matters, but we do not know why.

The puzzle. Training to fit the data should produce models that memorize it. Yet momentum-based optimizers produce models that generalize. Why?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper proves that momentum-based optimizers do one specific thing: they shift the trajectory toward solutions where the gap between the two classes is maximized. They do this naturally, without being told to do it. The width of the gap depends on which norm (distance measure) the optimizer uses—spectral norm for Muon, L2 norm for MomentumGD, L-infinity norm for Adam. Different norms produce different margins.

the object it moves  =  The final margin (gap between groups)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Margin under different norms

measured quantity  =  Margin relative to a chosen norm

The margin is the minimum distance from any training point to the decision boundary, measured in a specific way (a norm). It is not the physical distance; it is distance as defined by the optimizer's choice of which direction to measure along. For a binary classifier, if the points are at distance D from the boundary in the L2 norm but distance D-prime in the spectral norm, different optimizers will push toward different solutions.

This paper measures the margin by running the optimizer and seeing which margin grows largest as the model trains. If Muon grows the spectral-norm margin fastest, that tells you what Muon cares about. If Adam grows the L-infinity margin fastest, that is Adam's hidden preference.

Why it works

Why momentum optimizers maximize the margin

Momentum-based optimizers are approximate steepest descent algorithms under a decaying learning rate. Steepest descent along a given norm inherently pushes toward the largest margin in that norm. When the learning rate decays, the trajectory slows down and eventually stops at a point where no more progress can be made in maximizing the margin.

Think of it like this. The steepest downhill direction at each point is the direction that increases the gap the fastest. Momentum carries you along that direction. Over time, as you slow down, you converge to the point where the gap is as wide as it can be. This happens automatically because of how steepest descent is defined, not because the optimizer was told to maximize margin.

Math after the picture

The margin maximization equation

First in words:

Loss equals the sum of individual sample losses, each penalizing misclassification by an amount that depends on how far the sample sits from the decision boundary. The margin maximization problem aims to maximize the minimum distance from any point to the boundary, subject to the constraint that the parameters have a bounded norm. The two are linked: as the loss-based trajectory decays, it approaches a point that is stationary (no direction gives more margin) with respect to the dual norm of the parameters.

Then the compact form:

max_{theta} gamma subject to ||theta|| <= c and margin(theta) >= gamma for all i

Every symbol:

gammathe margin (minimum distance from any point to the boundary)
thetathe weights of the neural network
||theta||the size of the weights measured in a specific norm
cthe maximum allowed size of the weights
margin(theta)the distance from point i to the decision boundary at weights theta
A tiny worked example with two points
Suppose two training examples: a red point at position 2.0 and a blue point at position 5.0.
The boundary starts at position 3.5 (the middle). Gap = min(3.5 - 2.0, 5.0 - 3.5) = 1.5.
After momentum carries the weights, the boundary moves to position 2.2. Gap = min(2.2 - 2.0, 5.0 - 2.2) = 0.2 (bad).
Momentum keeps pushing in the direction that grows the gap. Boundary moves to position 1.5. Gap = min(1.5 - 2.0, 5.0 - 1.5) is now negative (the boundary crossed the red point—wrong).
Eventually momentum settles at position 2.5. Gap = min(2.5 - 2.0, 5.0 - 2.5) = 0.5.
Then 3.0: Gap = 1.0. Then 3.2: Gap = 1.2. Momentum slows as learning rate decays.
Final: boundary at 3.5, gap = 1.5. No further movement increases the gap equally on both sides.

The optimizer converges when both classes are equally far from the boundary—the margin cannot grow wider.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Non-smooth or non-homogeneous models. The theory applies to smooth homogeneous networks—where the loss surface behaves nicely and the model output scales evenly with the weights. Real networks with ReLU (a piecewise function that amplifies positive values and silences negatives), batch normalization (rescaling the numbers flowing through the network to keep them stable), or other add-ons do not fit this frame perfectly. The implicit bias may be weaker or different.
The gap cannot be arbitrarily large. If the data is inherently messy (one point from each class very close together), no optimizer can make a huge margin. The maximum margin is limited by how the points are physically arranged in your data.
Different norms, different margins. Adam maximizes L-infinity margin. Muon maximizes spectral norm margin. MomentumGD maximizes L2 norm margin. If you need a different margin (a straight-line distance between groups), the implicit bias will not help you directly—because the optimizer's trajectory is set by which norm it uses, and if you need a margin that does not match the optimizer's norm, the optimizer will not naturally steer toward it, so you would have to add an explicit constraint to redirect the training.
Done right. On smooth homogeneous models with exponential or logistic loss, momentum optimizers provably converge to margin-maximizing solutions. The gap grows, and new points are classified correctly more often.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a two-layer homogeneous neural network and train it on a simple two-class dataset (e.g., even vs odd MNIST digits). Use three optimizers: vanilla gradient descent, momentum gradient descent, and Adam. Measure the margin (minimum distance from any training point to the decision boundary) after training under each optimizer. Plot how the margin grows over training iterations.

margin = min_i distance(point_i, boundary)

Vanilla descent produces a small margin. Momentum and Adam produce larger, nearly equal margins. The margin should grow monotonically as training progresses, showing that the optimizer is indeed pushing toward margin maximization.
