Theory-of-Mind–Guided Emotion Reasoning

Make models reason about emotions the same way humans do: by first figuring out what someone is thinking, then connecting that to what they feel.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

When you see someone smile, you do not just memorize that smiles mean happy — you first imagine what they are thinking about, then decide if that thought matches the smile. Today's AI emotion models skip the imagining part and just learn patterns from images and text. This paper teaches them to reason like people do, by guessing the mental state first (Theory of Mind), then using that guess to figure out the emotion. It trains them with a method called TMPO that gives rewards for getting the thinking part right, not just the final answer.

Zero assumption version

Emotion models memorize patterns, not reasoning

Today's multimodal models (systems that read images, text, and audio together) are good at spotting simple cues: a red face plus angry words equals anger. But real emotions are mental — they come from thoughts. A person can smile while sad if they are pretending, or frown while happy if they are frustrated with themselves. Current models do not even try to model this mental layer; they just memorize correlations between cues and labels.

If you train a naive model by showing it images paired with emotion labels, it learns to be a pattern matcher. When it sees a new image, it asks 'which pattern does this match?' It never asks 'what is this person thinking about?' So it fails on cases where the visible cues lie, or when the emotion is shaped by what the person believes, not what we see. The paper calls this shallow 'fact retrieval' — and it breaks on complex, cognitively demanding cases.

The core idea. Teach the model to build a mental picture (Theory of Mind) of what the person is thinking, then use that mental picture to infer the emotion. Reward the model for getting the thinking part right, not just the final answer.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Learn structured reasoning.

Train the model on examples where it must show its thinking in a clear step-by-step chain.

Step 2: Build intermediate mental state labels.

Manually annotate what the person is thinking (joy, confusion, belief, etc.) for each example.

Step 3: Sample candidate reasoning chains.

Generate multiple different chains from the model and score each one on four dimensions.

Step 4: Reward process, not just the answer.

Give bonus points for clear structure, correct final answer, thoughtful mental language, and logical consistency.

Step 5: Update the model using reinforcement learning.

Nudge the model to imitate the high-scoring chains and avoid the low-scoring ones.

What the paper changes

The model's reasoning chain

The paper changes how the model generates its answer. Instead of jumping straight from image-and-text to emotion label, it now outputs two things: a reasoning chain (the mental states it infers) and then the emotion. The reasoning chain is written in plain steps inside special tags so the model is forced to show its work, because when a model writes out each step it becomes harder to hide shortcuts or make lucky guesses without a real reason.

the object it moves  =  the reasoning chain
The hidden quantity

Mental state attribution quality

measured quantity  =  how well the model captures what the person is thinking

The paper introduces a new hidden quantity: the quality of the intermediate mental states the model infers. This is not the emotion itself (that is the final answer), but the chain of thoughts that lead to it. A good mental state attribution means the model correctly identified that the person was confused, then relieved, then proud — in the right order, for the right reasons. A bad one guesses random thoughts.

The paper measures this by training a separate reward function that evaluates four things: (1) Does the reasoning follow a logical structure? (2) Is the final emotion correct? (3) Does the language sound like genuine mental reasoning, not generic phrases? (4) Are there no contradictions in the chain? These four scores combine into a single reward that guides the model during training.

Why it works

How supervising the thinking improves the emotion

Here is the key mechanism: when you train a model to predict only the final emotion, it learns correlation (certain patterns predict sadness). But when you force it to explain its thinking AND reward the thinking, it learns causation (sadness makes sense given what the person is thinking). The model must now commit to a mental narrative, and that narrative must be coherent. A bad narrative — one that contradicts itself or ignores the audio — gets penalized by the reward function. A good one gets reinforced.

This works because the four-part reward function creates pressure on multiple dimensions at once. If the model tries to cheat by generating a nonsensical thought chain that happens to predict the right emotion, the structure and consistency rewards punish it. If it ignores part of the evidence (text or audio), the content reward punishes it. So the model must find a chain of reasoning that is simultaneously plausible (structure reward), grounded in evidence (content reward), logically consistent (consistency reward), and emotion-accurate (content reward on the final answer). Because a chain must satisfy all four constraints at once, the model cannot pass by pattern matching alone — it must actually reason about the mental states.

The reinforcement learning algorithm (GRPO) then updates the model by making it more likely to output chains that score high and less likely to output chains that score low. Over iterations, the model learns to produce reasoning chains that are all four things at once — a much harder task than just memorizing which visual patterns predict emotions, but one that transfers better to new cases, because explicit reasoning generalizes beyond the training examples (a person with a new expression but the same mental state still has the same emotion), while pattern matching fails (a new expression you have not seen before confuses it).

The reinforcement learning update

First in words:

The model is updated to maximize a weighted sum of four reward functions (structure, content, process, consistency) minus a penalty for straying too far from its initial training, scaled by how well each proposed reasoning beats the average.

Then the compact form:

max_πθ E[π_θ(y) / π_old(y) * A_i - β * D_KL(π_θ || π_ref)]

Every symbol:

π_θ(y)probability that the updated model generates reasoning chain y
π_old(y)probability that the old model generated reasoning chain y
A_iadvantage score: how much better this chain is than average
D_KL(π_θ || π_ref)a penalty that keeps the model from changing too much
βhow strong the penalty is
A tiny worked example
Chain A (good mental reasoning): 'He felt confused by the email, then angry at himself' — Reward = 4 (structure) + 3 (content) + 4 (process) + 3.5 (consistency) = 14.5 / 4 = 3.625, Advantage = 3.625 - 2.5 (average) = 1.125.
Chain B (lazy): 'Emotion is anger because red face' — Reward = 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 1, Advantage = 1 - 2.5 = -1.5.
The update nudges π_θ to output Chain A 1.125 times more often and Chain B 1.5 times less. Over many iterations, the model converges to generating only thoughtful chains.

The advantage score tells the model which chains are better than its current average, because by comparing each chain to the average rather than to a fixed target, the algorithm nudges the model to gradually improve instead of suddenly flipping to a different strategy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward hacking. The model learns to write plausible-sounding mental states that do not match the actual evidence, just to get high rewards. This breaks if the four-part reward is not well calibrated. Fix: include real annotated data for at least one of the four reward components so the model learns what genuine reasoning looks like, because a model trained purely on reward signals can optimize the reward without solving the real problem, but if it sees real human examples for even one component (e.g., real human mental-state annotations), it learns the shape of correct reasoning and applies that shape to the other components.
Mental state mismatch. The model generates a coherent reasoning chain that is logically consistent but arrives at the wrong emotion because it misunderstood the context. This happens when the initial mental state attribution is incorrect. Fix: explicitly train the model on first-order mental state mapping before doing reinforcement learning.
Modality collapse. The model learns to ignore audio or video and reason only from text, since text is often clearer. Then it fails when given only audio, or when audio carries critical emotional nuance. Fix: make sure the reward function explicitly scores use of all modalities, not just final accuracy.
Done right. The four-part reward is well calibrated; the model is trained on real annotated mental states first; and the KL penalty is tuned so the model changes gradually, not suddenly. In this regime the method outperforms fact-retrieval baselines, because gradual changes preserve what the model learned during its original training while steering it toward better reasoning, and transfers to unseen emotion categories, because the reasoning path is compositional (you can combine learned mental states in new ways even if the exact combination is new).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of 20 images or short videos, each with an emotion label and a manually written mental state (what the person is thinking). Train a multimodal model with just supervised learning (learning from labeled examples) to output emotion labels, then again with TMPO. Compare three things: (1) final emotion accuracy on held-out cases, (2) the quality of the reasoning chains (do humans rate them as thoughtful?, which tests whether the model is truly reasoning or just guessing), and (3) transfer to a new emotion category the model has never seen (does the reasoning method transfer better than pattern matching?, because a model that understands mental states should generalize to new emotions, while a pattern-matching model cannot).

transfer_gain = (TMPO accuracy on new emotion) - (SFT accuracy on new emotion)

The demo must show that the transfer gain is positive — that reasoning about the mind, even on small data, beats pure pattern matching when the world throws a new case at you.
