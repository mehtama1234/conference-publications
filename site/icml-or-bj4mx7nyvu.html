IMPACT: Influence Modeling for Open-Set Time Series Anomaly Detection

The paper learns which training examples most strongly shape the anomaly detector, then uses those rankings to generate realistic fake anomalies and clean up contaminated training data.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Anomaly detection means finding the broken or unexpected thing in a data stream: a sensor reading a factory temperature that spikes way too high, or a heartbeat rhythm that goes irregular. The hard case is when you have to spot both kinds of anomalies you saw before (seen) and completely new kinds you never trained on (unseen). Most methods try fake data to practice on both, but for time series—sequences where each measurement follows the last—simple made-up anomalies break the sequence pattern itself, making them useless. This paper learns to rank which real training examples matter most, then uses that ranking to create realistic fake anomalies and filter out the accidental broken examples mixed into your training data.

Zero assumption version

Why simple fake data fails for time series anomalies

In time series anomaly detection, you watch a stream of numbers over time and flag when something goes wrong. You want to catch anomalies you saw during training and also spot brand new kinds you have no examples of. The naive approach is to invent fake anomalies to train on, the same way you might photoshop a strange image to teach a vision model to spot weird pictures. But time series has a hidden rule: each number depends on the ones before it. If you just randomly flip or shift a value in the sequence, you break that dependency, creating noise that real anomalies never have.

Worse, your actual training data often contains mistakes: unlabeled anomalies mixed in with the normal examples. You thought you were training on pure normal data, but some of it was already broken. Training on corrupted data teaches the model to tolerate problems it should actually catch.

In one sentence. The paper ranks which training examples most influence the detector, then generates realistic fake anomalies and uses influential examples to clean up contaminated training data.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: influence scores and training data

The paper computes an influence score for each training example—a number that says how much that example pushes the detector's behavior. It then uses those scores to flag corrupted training examples and to guide which patterns to use when inventing new fake anomalies. The detector itself stays the same kind of model (a neural network), but it trains on different data: cleaner and with realistic fakes.

the object it moves  =  Training data selection and synthetic anomaly generation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: influence score

measured quantity  =  Influence of a training example

Influence measures how strongly one training example shapes the detector's behavior. Imagine the detector is a garden that grows a certain way when watered with your full training set. Remove one example and retrain: the detector changes slightly. That change is the influence of that one example. Examples with high influence steer the model more; low-influence examples are almost passengers. The paper computes this using calculus without actually retraining dozens of times—a trick called the influence function.

The paper uses influence to flag two things: high-influence examples are likely hiding anomalies (because weird examples bend the model more), and high-influence anomalies are the best patterns to copy when inventing fake unseen anomalies (because they teach the model the most about what an anomaly actually looks like).

Why it works

Why it works: use influence scores to clean and generate

Time series anomalies have structure—a spike is not random noise, it follows from the history. Simple augmentation ignores this and creates unrealistic fakes. The paper's insight is to use influence scores as a guide: since high-influence examples shape the detector most, they are either key real anomalies (worth copying) or hidden anomalies (worth removing). By computing which examples matter most, you can tell them apart from passengers and either promote them or scrutinize them.

When generating new fake anomalies, the paper does not randomly perturb; it extracts the pattern of high-influence known anomalies and modulates it—shift the timing, scale the magnitude, shorten the duration—while keeping the sequential structure intact. The result is a fake anomaly that looks like it could belong to the real-world stream, not random noise. Training on such realistic fakes teaches the detector to generalize to unseen anomalies it has never met, not just memorize the seen ones. Simultaneously, flagging and removing the high-influence normal-labeled examples (which are actually hidden anomalies) means the detector learns what normal truly looks like.

The equation: influence-weighted loss

First in words:

For each training example, compute how much removing it would change the model, then use high-influence examples to guide cleaning and augmentation, finally train a new detector on the cleaned and augmented data.

Then the compact form:

influence_i = - (dL_test / dtheta) · (d2L_train / dtheta2)^-1 · (dL_train_i / dtheta)

Every symbol:

influence_ihow much training example i pushes the detector
L_testthe detector error on held-out test data
L_trainthe detector error on training data
L_train_ithe error contribution of example i alone
dL / dthetagradient: which way to nudge weights to shrink error
d2L / dtheta2Hessian: the curvature of how error changes with weight nudges
thetathe detector's weights (the numbers inside the model)
Tiny worked example
Example A (normal): gradient 0.1, Hessian inverse 2.0, test gradient -0.05 → influence = -(-0.05) × 2.0 × 0.1 = 0.01 (low).
Example B (anomaly): gradient 5.0, Hessian inverse 2.0, test gradient -0.05 → influence = -(-0.05) × 2.0 × 5.0 = 0.50 (high).
Example B matters 50x more than A. Include it when generating fakes, check it for contamination.

The influence function tells you which training examples the detector's brain depends on most; use those to repair training data and create realistic fakes.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Influence function assumes the Hessian is invertible. If the model is badly conditioned (changes chaotically with tiny weight nudges), the inverse fails. The paper gently dampen the Hessian by adding a small constant to its diagonal, or use a pseudo-inverse that handles singular matrices, but extreme cases still break.
Contaminated examples might not be high-influence. An outlier that sits far from the detector's decision boundary might not push the weights much. Influence finds examples the model learns from; some contamination is quiet.
Generated anomalies might still be unrealistic. Even if the pattern is borrowed from real anomalies, the modulation (scale, timing, duration) can still create fakes that no real anomaly would match. Testing is essential.
Done right. Validate that generated anomalies actually belong to the real unseen classes; use domain experts or real held-out unseen anomalies if available to check realism. Verify that removing flagged contamination does not accidentally delete important normal examples.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple time series anomaly detector (a network that learns to flag spikes or dips) on a clean dataset with some known anomalies, then intentionally corrupt the training data by mislabeling a few anomalies as normal. Run the IMPACT pipeline: compute influence scores, flag high-influence examples, generate new fake anomalies by borrowing patterns from real anomalies (e.g., shift a real spike by a few time steps and scale it), remove the flagged contamination, and retrain. Finally, test the detector on a held-out set with both known and synthetic unseen anomalies.

Seen accuracy = (true positives on known anomalies) / (all known anomalies); Unseen accuracy = (true positives on new anomalies) / (all new anomalies); Contamination tolerance = accuracy on held-out normal data without retraining

The demo must show that the influence-cleaned detector catches both known and unseen anomalies better than simple augmentation because realistic fakes preserve sequence patterns, and that it does not mistake contaminated normal examples for real negatives because removing high-influence hidden anomalies teaches the true boundary between normal and broken.
