Unveiling the Visual Counting Bottleneck in Vision-Language Models

The main failure in out-of-range visual counting is not seeing the objects; it is turning a valid quantity representation into the correct number word.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Large vision-language models can often compare quantities and describe scenes, yet they fail sharply when asked to count beyond the range they saw in training. This paper decomposes counting into three stages: individuate objects, form an abstract magnitude representation, and decode that magnitude into a number word or numeral. Using controlled Go-board scenes, the authors show that the visual backbone still contains usable quantity information beyond the training range. The collapse happens later, when the model must map that magnitude to a discrete symbolic output.

Zero assumption version

Why counting is a stronger test than ordinary recognition

Interpolation, meaning success on quantities within the training range, does not prove that a model learned counting as a transferable rule. A model can memorize frequent pairings between visual patterns and number words without learning how to extend that mapping to unseen counts.

Visual counting requires at least three things to work together. The model must separate the target objects from background, preserve a representation of how many objects are present, and then output the correct symbol. If any stage breaks, the final answer is wrong. Earlier work often blamed perception, but that explanation was incomplete because models sometimes compare larger and smaller quantities correctly even when they cannot name the exact count.

The paper's move in one sentence. Probe each stage of counting separately and show that extrapolation failure is concentrated in symbolic decoding rather than in visual object representation.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the diagnosis of counting failure. It moves the blame from early vision to the final mapping between a quantity representation and a discrete number word.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  the internal magnitude representation before the model commits to a number word

The main hidden quantity is the model's pre-symbolic magnitude state. This is the internal representation that carries how many objects are present before the decoder chooses a numeral or word. If this state is intact, the model may still know that one scene has more objects than another even when it cannot name the exact count.

The paper finds evidence that this hidden magnitude state survives farther than the final output suggests. That matters because it changes what kind of fix is needed. If perception were broken, more visual detail would be the obvious remedy. If symbolic decoding is broken, the remedy must instead strengthen the mapping from magnitude representations to number symbols because the quantity information is already present but not being named correctly.

Why it works

Why symbolic decoding becomes the bottleneck

A model can preserve quantity information without learning a universal mapping from any quantity to the correct number word. During training it may see many examples of counts up to some limit and learn local associations such as specific patch-density patterns to specific number words. That is enough for interpolation but not for extrapolation.

The probe results matter because a linear readout, meaning a simple weighted sum followed by a class choice, can still recover quantity from hidden states outside the training range. That indicates the visual system has not collapsed completely. The comparative tasks matter for the same reason. If the model can often say which of two scenes has more objects, then it still carries an ordered magnitude notion even when exact number naming fails.

The paper's fractured magnitude hypothesis says that visual quantity and symbolic number live in partially disconnected spaces. The model learns some shared regularities but not a truly unified number system. Once it is asked for unseen visual-symbol pairings, the decoder falls back to familiar word statistics instead of extending the magnitude rule.

Math after the picture

A simple decomposition of counting accuracy

First in words:

A useful abstraction is to treat exact counting success as the product of success at each stage: finding the objects, representing quantity, and decoding the right symbol.

Then the compact form:

P(correct count) = P(individuation) * P(magnitude | individuation) * P(symbol | magnitude)

Every symbol:

P(correct count)probability that the final exact count is correct
P(individuation)probability that the model separates the relevant objects from the scene correctly
P(magnitude | individuation)probability that the internal state represents the quantity correctly once objects are separated
P(symbol | magnitude)probability that the model outputs the correct numeral once it has the right quantity state
A tiny worked example
Suppose object individuation is correct 0.95 of the time.
Suppose the internal magnitude state is correct 0.85 of the time once objects are separated.
Suppose symbolic decoding succeeds only 0.30 of the time once the magnitude state is correct.
Then exact counting accuracy is 0.95 times 0.85 times 0.30, which is about 0.24, so the final bottleneck is clearly the decoding term.

The paper does not claim these factors are literally independent. The decomposition is a first-principles way to express why a late-stage failure can dominate the final result.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Synthetic scenes can simplify early vision. Controlled Go boards reduce clutter and make individuation easier, so the diagnosis may understate perception failures that would appear in natural images.
Linear probes may miss nonlinear defects. A successful simple readout shows quantity information is present in some usable form, but it does not prove every downstream decoder can access that information equally well.
Comparison tasks are easier than exact counting. Relative judgments can succeed with coarse ordered magnitude alone, so they do not prove the model has a full symbolic number system.
Done right. The paper is valuable because it distinguishes representational presence from symbolic accessibility and shows that the two can diverge sharply.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create synthetic scenes with repeated shapes and train or evaluate a vision-language model on counts up to a limit such as 10. Then test it on higher counts such as 11 through 20. Add two evaluations: a linear probe on hidden states to predict quantity and a pairwise comparison task to decide which image has more objects.

The demo should show that exact number output falls first, while probe accuracy and comparative judgments stay materially higher. That gap is the paper's core diagnosis.

decode_gap = probe_quantity_acc - exact_count_acc

The main goal is to show that quantity information can remain inside the model after exact number naming has already failed.
