Latent Laplace Diffusion for Irregular Multivariate Time Series

Forecast long sequences of unevenly-sampled time series by diffusing in a compressed latent space and generating the full horizon at once instead of step-by-step.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Real-world data arrives at random times: stock prices tick when trades happen, patient sensors record when nurses check, rainfall only gets logged when it rains. Forecasting these irregular time series is stuck: either squash the uneven times onto a regular grid (which loses the true timing structure), or use models that predict one step at a time (which drift and accumulate errors over long forecasts). This paper bridges that trap by compressing the data into a small set of hidden numbers, then generating the full forecast all at once using a stable mathematical model instead of step-by-step integration.

Zero assumption version

The irregular time series trap

A time series is a sequence of measurements over time. Real data is almost never evenly spaced: a patient monitor samples at irregular intervals (some readings miss because a nurse is busy, others cluster when the patient is critical), stock prices only tick when trades happen, rainfall events don't follow a calendar. Forecasting this irregular data means predicting what happens next, even weeks ahead.

One way to handle irregularity: squash the data onto a regular grid. Pick a time interval (say, one hour), and fill in gaps by guessing what the missing values should be. This grid lets you use standard models. But the original timing information is lost—the model doesn't know that two consecutive measurements were actually five seconds apart, not an hour apart. That timing structure matters: if your patient's heart rate jumped in five seconds, that's a medical emergency; if it drifted over an hour, that's routine.

Another way: use a model that respects time directly and predicts one step at a time, always looking at the current state and the time step to the next observation. For one or two steps this works fine. But forecasting weeks ahead means predicting 1000+ steps, each one building on guesses from the previous step. Errors compound: a small drift in step 1 amplifies through step 2, step 3, and by step 100 the forecast is completely wrong.

The paper's move: don't predict step-by-step. Instead, compress the irregular data into a tiny latent space (a short list of compressed numbers), then generate the entire forecast all at once from that space, using a stable mathematical model that doesn't need sequential integration.

In one sentence. Use a diffusion model (a generative model that gradually adds structure to noise) in a compressed latent space, conditioned on how big the gaps are, so the whole forecast comes out at once without step-by-step drift.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compress via VAE

Feed the irregular time series (past observations, with gaps marked) into a pretrained compressor that squeezes it into 5-10 latent numbers.

Summarize gaps

Measure how big each gap is (time between consecutive observations) and encode those gaps into a separate summary, because large gaps affect which latent code you'll need.

Reverse-diffuse in latent space

Start from pure noise in latent space and gradually add structure (reverse diffusion), guided by the gap summary and the compressed history.

Use stable Laplace-domain poles

Instead of solving an ODE step-by-step, use learnable complex numbers (poles) in a mathematical transform domain to shape the diffusion stably, without drift.

Decompress to forecast

Take the final latent code and run it through the decompressor to get the full forecast—weeks of predictions at the original timestamps.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes the latent representation: instead of trying to predict the actual time series values step-by-step (or squashing them onto a grid), it learns a compressed latent trajectory—a tiny list of numbers that captures the essence of what happens next over the entire forecast window.

The hidden quantity

Pole damping: the hidden quantity the paper measures

measured quantity  =  pole damping

Pole damping is a number that controls stability in the diffusion model. In control theory and dynamics, a pole is a location in a mathematical plane that describes how a system evolves: poles far to the left on the complex plane mean the system decays stably; poles on the right mean the system explodes. Damping is how much the system is slowed down or stabilized. The paper learns pole pairs (pairs of mathematical locations that mirror each other) that guide how the diffusion model adds structure to noise. Larger damping means slower, more stable denoising; weak damping means fast but potentially unstable changes.

The paper estimates pole damping by conditioning the poles on the gap summary: when observations are far apart, the model learns to use poles with more damping (more stable). When observations are close together, it can use less damping (faster changes). Large time gaps are harder to cross without error, so the model automatically becomes more cautious by linking the gap sizes to pole damping. This is the core trick: gap size directly controls how conservatively the model evolves.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

The root problem is step-by-step drift: each prediction is made assuming the previous prediction was perfect, but it wasn't. After 1000 predictions, the compounded errors are huge. The paper's insight is to work in a compressed latent space and generate the entire forecast all at once, which sidesteps sequential error accumulation entirely.

Step 1: compress. The irregular time series (with gaps marked) is fed through a learned compressor (a VAE encoder) that squeezes the whole history down to 5-10 latent numbers. This compression captures the essential structure: trend, seasonality, volatility, whatever matters for forecasting. Working in latent space is cheaper and faster because the diffusion model only needs to generate 5-10 numbers instead of thousands of raw sensor readings, cutting computation time and memory by orders of magnitude.

Step 2: condition on gaps. The model measures how long each gap is and encodes that as a separate signal. A one-week gap in a patient's sensor data is a big red flag, very different from a one-second gap—and this matters deeply because large gaps mean less certainty about what happens between observations. The renewal-averaging analysis used here is a mathematical tool from probability theory that measures what information a gap carries about future dynamics.

Step 3: generate all-at-once. Instead of predicting step 1, then step 2, then step 3... the model uses reverse diffusion in latent space: it starts from noise and gradually adds structure, guided by the compressed history and the gap signal. The whole forecast emerges in one go.

Step 4: use stable poles instead of ODE solving. Most continuous-time models solve a differential equation (an ODE) to move forward in time, step-by-tiny-step. That accumulates numerical errors. This paper instead uses learnable poles in the Laplace domain—a different mathematical representation where evolution is described by pole locations, not by integration. Poles with enough damping (conditioned on gaps) guarantee stability: no matter how far you evolve forward, the state won't explode. This avoids drift without numerical integration.

Step 5: decompress. The final latent code is run through the decompressor (VAE decoder) to produce the actual forecast values at the original irregular timestamps. Because the latent forecast was made stably all-at-once, there's no accumulated drift.

Math after the picture

The equation and worked example

First in words:

The model generates samples by reversing a diffusion process where the evolution at each latent coordinate is governed by learnable complex-conjugate poles conditioned on the gap summary. The mean trajectory is parameterized via Laplace-domain modal decomposition rather than step-by-step ODE solving.

Then the compact form:

z_t = sum_k residue_k * exp(pole_k * t) + conditioning(gap_summary)

Every symbol:

z_tlatent state at time t in the forecast
pole_klearnable complex number (k-th pole pair), conditioned on gaps
residue_kamplitude of the k-th mode
exp(pole_k * t)exponential growth or decay shaped by the pole
gap_summaryvector encoding how big each observation gap is
Tiny worked example: a two-mode latent forecast
Suppose latent space has 2 modes. Pole 1 has real part -0.5 (slow decay), Pole 2 has real part -2.0 (fast decay).
Residue 1 = 1.0 (strong), Residue 2 = 0.3 (weak).
At t=0 (start of forecast): z_0 = 1.0 * exp(-0.5*0) + 0.3 * exp(-2.0*0) = 1.0 + 0.3 = 1.3.
At t=5 (days ahead): z_5 = 1.0 * exp(-0.5*5) + 0.3 * exp(-2.0*5) = 1.0*0.082 + 0.3*0.00005 ≈ 0.082.
The forecast smoothly decays from 1.3 to 0.082 over 5 days—no explosion, no oscillation, just smooth damped evolution.
If gaps are huge, the model learns higher damping (more negative pole real parts), so decay is even faster: stronger suppression of error over distant horizons.

The poles replace ODE solving: instead of numerically integrating forward step-by-step, the model jumps directly to any future time using the exponential formula, which is exact and stable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poles learned poorly. If the training signal is weak or the dataset is too small, the model might learn poles that are too far to the right on the complex plane (positive real part), causing the forecast to explode instead of decay. This defeats the point of using poles for stability.
Gap conditioning misses structure. If the relationship between gap sizes and dynamics is nonlinear or time-varying, encoding gaps as a simple list of numbers might not capture it. The model might assign the same pole damping to a one-week gap and a one-month gap, both of which need very different caution levels.
Latent compressor obscures interpretability. The forecast is generated in a 5-10 dimensional latent space that humans can't directly read. A bug in the diffusion model might produce a latent code that looks good statistically but decodes to a physically impossible forecast (e.g., negative rainfall). The decoder has no constraint to prevent that.
Done right. The paper learns poles with strictly negative real parts (enforced during training), conditions poles on gaps using a learned summarizer that focuses on the important parts of the gap signal, and validates on held-out test sets spanning missing-value imputation and long-horizon forecasting to catch failure modes early.
