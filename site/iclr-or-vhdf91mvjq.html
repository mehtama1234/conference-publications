On Measuring Influence in Avoiding Undesired Future

To avoid a bad future, you need to know which levers actually work—not just which ones predict it.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

When a predictive model sees a bad outcome coming, we naturally want to prevent it. But knowing that a factor predicts disaster is not the same as knowing how to fix it. This paper introduces a new measurement: actionable influence, which answers which variables can we actually change to improve our chances of success. The reason this question matters is that the answer differs from asking which factors have a strong cause-and-effect link to the bad outcome—a variable can have weak causal power but high actionability if changing it reliably cascades through the system to prevent the outcome. The answer is surprisingly different and more useful for decision-making.

Zero assumption version

When prediction is not enough

A predictive model can tell you: if this person eats fast food five times a week, they have a 70% chance of heart disease. That is correlation—one thing predicting another. But if someone asks, what can I actually change to avoid the disease, the model says nothing useful. Should I stop the fast food? Maybe. But what if stopping fast food only reduces risk to 68%? What if changing their sleep schedule reduces it to 40%? The model never tells you which lever actually works.

A naive approach is to look at which variables have the strongest causal effect—which changes produce the biggest shift in the outcome. But this breaks in two ways. First, a variable might have a weak direct causal link, yet changing it early enough might trigger a cascade that flattens the whole bad outcome. Second, a variable might not be a causal factor at all; it might be a sign that something else is going wrong. But if that sign tells you when the problem is most vulnerable to intervention, acting on the sign is the right move. Causation alone misses this.

In one sentence. The paper measures influence as the actual improvement you can gain by changing a variable, not by how much that variable causes the bad outcome.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

You state the goal

I want to avoid this bad outcome. Here are all my variables.

The framework asks: what is my payoff?

Define success clearly as a number from 0 to 1, where 0 means failure and 1 means the goal achieved.

For each variable, measure the counterfactual

If I could freely change this one thing, how much would my payoff improve? The reason you measure this for each variable separately is that some levers interact—pulling one can amplify or cancel the effects of others—and you need to account for that.

Account for what you already know

Use the data to estimate: if I change this variable, what other things stay the same, and what shift?

Rank by actual influence

The variables that raise your payoff the most are the ones that genuinely matter for avoiding the bad future.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces a new way to score variables: instead of asking how much they cause the outcome, it asks how much changing them improves your chances of success. The reason for this shift is that causation only tells you what happened in the past (if X increased, Y changed by so much), but it does not tell you what will happen if you intervene (if you actively change X, what actually occurs next?). Shifting from a causal question (what is the strength of the cause) to a decision question (what action will help most) makes the framework useful for someone who wants to avoid a bad future, not just understand what caused it, because it ranks variables by their real power to change the outcome when you act on them, not just by their historical predictive weight.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Actionable influence

measured quantity  =  How much a variable actually improves the outcome when changed, based on actual data observations, because that is what matters in practice

Actionable influence is a number that measures: if I can change this variable, by how much will my chances of success improve? The reason you need this number is that knowing how much a variable pushes an outcome (causation strength) tells you what happened in the past, but not what will happen if you actively change it. Influence accounts for what else might shift when you pull one lever. The reason influence works better is that it asks the right question: not which factor caused the bad outcome, but which lever can I actually pull to avoid it, given the complex way outcomes arise in the real world.

The paper estimates this from observed data by reasoning backwards: given the pattern of how the outcome and the variables co-occur, if someone were to change variable X, what would their success rate actually be? This is measured using decision theory, a framework for reasoning about choices and their payoffs (how much you win or lose). The paper uses the principle of maximum expected utility: the best action is the one that maximizes your expected payoff, and influence measures which variables unlock the largest payoff gains. It does this by looking at the joint distribution (the pattern of how often each combination of variables and outcomes occurs), conditioning on observable factors. The reason this method works is that it grounds influence in real observations of what happened across many situations, not in abstract causal concepts.

Plain reading. Think of influence as the answer to: if I change this one thing and hold everything else at its typical level, how much better off am I? A variable with high influence gives you a big win when you pull that lever because the paper grounds the ranking in what actually happened across many real situations where that variable was changed. The reason this works is that in real decisions, pulling one lever often cascades through other choices: changing sleep shifts your whole daily rhythm, which shifts stress, which shifts other behaviors. Measuring influence from data captures all of that cascade, while a pure causal test misses it because it only measures passive observation (what occurred naturally), not active intervention (what happens when you deliberately change something).

Why it works

Why measuring actionable influence works

Causation tells you: if X goes up by one unit, Y changes by some amount on average. But it does not tell you what happens when you actively intervene. Intervention is different because when you change a variable, you are breaking its normal correlations with other things. Sleep duration normally correlates with stress—people who sleep more are less stressed. So if the model learned to predict disease from stress, that prediction bakes in the sleep connection. When you intervene to increase sleep, you break that correlation, and all the benefits flow through. The reason causation fails is that it measures passive observation (how things move together naturally), but you need to know what happens when you actively push a lever (and that is a different question).

The paper measures influence by asking: if I take the decision-maker's perspective and think about changing one variable at a time, maximizing my expected payoff, which variable gives me the biggest improvement? This uses decision theory to reason about: given what I know about how outcomes happen, what actually works? A weak causal factor can have high influence if the data shows that changing it consistently leads to success, because the data already captures the cascading effects. The reason this works is that you are basing your ranking on observed outcomes following real changes, not on abstract causal strength. A non-causal signal can have high influence if it marks a moment when other interventions become effective; the framework finds these by looking at conditional probabilities: among cases where I can move this variable, what is my actual success rate across all the different scenarios? The reason this ranking is reliable is that it emerges from how outcomes actually distribute in the data.

Math after the picture

The influence equation

First in words:

Influence of variable X equals the expected payoff if you intervene on X (choosing its best value) minus the expected payoff if you do nothing.

Then the compact form:

Influence(X) = max_x E[Payoff | do(X = x)] - E[Payoff | baseline]

Every symbol:

Influence(X)How much better off you are if you change variable X
max_x E[Payoff | do(X = x)]The best payoff you can expect by choosing the best value for X and letting other things follow their normal patterns
do(X = x)The intervention: you actively set X to value x (not just observe X at value x)
E[Payoff | baseline]Your expected payoff if you do nothing and things unfold as they normally do
PayoffA number measuring success: 1 for the goal achieved, 0 for the goal missed, or anything in between
A tiny worked example
Scenario: Baseline payoff (if you do nothing) = 0.3 (30% chance of health).
If you improve sleep: best achievable payoff = 0.75 (75% chance of health).
Influence of sleep = 0.75 - 0.3 = 0.45. A big win.
If you quit fast food: best achievable payoff = 0.35 (only 35% chance).
Influence of fast food = 0.35 - 0.3 = 0.05. Barely helps.
If you change stress (which has weak direct causal link): best achievable payoff = 0.32.
Influence of stress = 0.32 - 0.3 = 0.02. Almost nothing.
Decision: rank sleep first (0.45), then fast food (0.05), then stress (0.02), even though stress looked like a causal risk factor.

The equation computes influence by treating each variable as something you can control and measuring the payoff gain versus doing nothing. The data tells you what payoff you would actually see in different scenarios.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

You cannot change what you cannot observe. If the data never shows you someone changing a variable, the method cannot estimate influence for that variable. The framework works backward from observed patterns; if the pattern is missing, it is blind.
Hidden confounders still break everything. If there is an invisible variable that causes both your choice and the outcome, the method will misattribute influence to your choice. The framework assumes you have observed all the important factors; if you have not, conclusions are wrong.
Small sample sizes lead to noisy estimates. If you only have 50 data points and 20 variables, the influence scores will bounce around randomly. You need enough data to see clear patterns in conditional probabilities.
Done right. When you have good data, few hidden confounders, and enough samples, the method ranks variables by their true actionable impact. You get a reliable guide to which levers actually work.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic dataset where you know the ground truth: some variables have strong causal effects but weak actionability (changing them is hard or changes other things that cancel the benefit), and some have weak causal effects but strong actionability (changing them is reliable and works). Train a predictive model and use the framework to measure influence of each variable. Then, for a held-out test set, actually intervene on the highest-influence variables and measure the success rate. Compare to intervening on the highest-causation variables. The demo succeeds if the high-influence group achieves better outcomes than the high-causation group because the influence measure correctly identified which changes actually work in practice, whereas causation strength ranked weak-causal-but-actionable variables as unimportant when they were actually the most effective.

Success improvement = (Outcome when you intervene on top-influence variables) minus (Outcome when you intervene on top-causation variables)

The demo must make it impossible to miss that choosing variables by influence beats choosing by causation when your goal is to actually avoid the bad future.
