ManiSoft: Soft Robotic Manipulation via Hierarchical Planning

Breaking soft-arm control into semantic waypoints, then training a policy to track them with torques, solves the problem that vision-language models cannot directly handle soft deformability.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Soft robotic arms bend and twist in ways that rigid arms cannot, but their flexibility makes control harder. Vision-language models (systems trained to understand images and text instructions) work well with rigid arms because the math is simple. For soft arms, the control is distributed across the entire deformable body, making it impossible to write down exact motion plans. This paper solves that by splitting soft-arm control into two parts: first, semantic waypoints (intermediate goals like approach and grasp), then a learned policy that figures out the torques to hit each waypoint.

Zero assumption version

Why direct control fails for soft arms

Rigid robot arms have fixed joints that move in predictable ways. Given a target position, you can write down the exact torques needed because the kinematics (the relationship between joints and end position) are simple and deterministic. Vision-language models trained on rigid arms have learned this simple pipeline: see the scene, read the instruction, predict the joint angles or torques.

Soft arms deform across their entire length. The relationship between the torques you apply at the base and where the tip ends up depends on contact forces, material properties, and the shape of the arm itself. This is a high-dimensional, nonlinear problem. No vision-language model trained end-to-end on images and text can directly output the correct torques for a soft arm because the continuous deformation has no closed-form solution.

A beginner might try to train a model to predict torques directly from images and language. But the output space is huge (torques across many segments), the dynamics are chaotic, and the model has no semantic understanding of what grasping or approaching even means for a deformable object.

The paper's move in one sentence. Instead of predicting torques directly, decompose the task into two layers: a high-level planner that outputs semantic waypoints (hand-designed per task), and a low-level RL policy trained to reach those waypoints using torques.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

High-level plan

Task-specific rules generate waypoints (6-DOF poses: approach, grasp, lift, retract) from the language instruction and object locations in the scene.

Waypoint tracking RL

Train an RL executor on a reward that encourages reaching each waypoint via torques while penalizing excessive deformation or oscillation.

Elastic coupling

Soft body and end-effector are connected via elastic force constraints (Hooke's law), so torques transmit as restoring forces and moments throughout the arm.

Execute full trajectory

At runtime, the planner outputs waypoint sequence, the RL policy hits each one with learned torques, the arm deforms and reaches the target.

Measure success

Task completion counted as success rate and efficiency (steps to completion). Clean scenes show promise; randomized scenes (clutter, lighting, object variety) reveal brittleness.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of asking the vision-language model to predict torques, the paper splits the problem. The model (or a rule-based planner) outputs waypoints (symbolic intermediate goals), and a separate RL-trained executor learns the mapping from waypoints to torques. The object being changed is the control architecture: from end-to-end prediction to a two-layer hierarchy.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Waypoint-tracking error

measured quantity  =  Distance from current arm pose to target waypoint

The RL executor tries to minimize the gap between its current end-effector pose (position and orientation) and the next waypoint. This gap (measured as the straight-line distance in 3D space plus rotational difference) is the quantity the paper measures and rewards heavily. When the executor tracks waypoints accurately, trajectories are stable and repeatable.

The paper estimates this by a dense reward function that penalizes pose difference at every timestep. During training, the policy learns which torques shrink this error fastest without overshooting or wobbling. The test-time metric (success rate under randomization) reveals when waypoint tracking fails: inaccurate vision estimates of arm state cause cumulative errors across waypoints.

Why it works

Why splitting into waypoints makes soft-arm control tractable

Predicting raw torques for a soft arm is intractable because the dynamics are nonlinear and high-dimensional. But waypoints compress the problem into semantic chunks. Each chunk (approach the object, grasp it, lift it) has a much smaller state space and a clearer relationship between torque and outcome within short time horizons.

The RL executor learns that certain torque patterns reliably move the arm from one waypoint to the next. Because the waypoints are close (10-20 steps apart), the executor avoids long-horizon credit assignment problems. The elastic force constraint couples the torques at the base to forces throughout the soft body, so the RL policy learns to use the arm's deformability as a feature, not fight against it.

Vision-language models that learn the high-level planner can be trained on rigid-arm datasets and transferred to soft arms without modification; only the low-level RL executor is soft-specific. This separation also lets you swap in better planners or executors independently, because changes at one layer do not cascade to the other: a new planner produces different waypoints, but the executor reuses the same learned torque mappings; a faster executor learns new torques but the planner interface stays identical.

The reward function that trains the executor

First in words:

Total reward is pose-tracking reward plus penalties for excess deformation and oscillation, summed over each timestep toward a waypoint.

Then the compact form:

R(t) = -||p_current - p_waypoint||_2 - lambda_deform * sum_i ||strain_i|| - lambda_osc * ||torque_rate||

Every symbol:

p_currentcurrent end-effector position and orientation
p_waypointtarget waypoint position and orientation
||...||_2Euclidean distance (straight-line gap)
strain_ideformation amount at segment i along the arm
lambda_deformweight on deformation penalty (keeps arm stiff when not needed)
lambda_oscweight on oscillation penalty (smooths torque changes)
torque_ratehow fast torque is changing (high rate = jerky)
A tiny worked example
At timestep t, target waypoint is (x=1.0, y=0.5, z=2.0), current arm tip is at (x=0.95, y=0.48, z=1.95).
Pose distance = sqrt((0.95-1.0)^2 + (0.48-0.5)^2 + (1.95-2.0)^2) = sqrt(0.0025 + 0.0004 + 0.0025) = sqrt(0.0054) ≈ 0.073.
Pose reward = -0.073 (penalty for missing target).
Arm segments 2-5 have total strain of 0.15 units; with lambda_deform=5.0, deformation penalty = -5.0 * 0.15 = -0.75.
Torque rate is 0.05; with lambda_osc=2.0, oscillation penalty = -2.0 * 0.05 = -0.1.
Total reward R = -0.073 - 0.75 - 0.1 = -0.923 (negative; the executor learns to make it less negative by tracking waypoints tighter and keeping the arm calmer).

The three terms teach the executor to reach waypoints accurately, deform only when necessary, and move smoothly without oscillation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Inaccurate proprioception from vision. If the model misestimates where the arm is (because of visual occlusion or lighting change), waypoint tracking errors compound across a multi-step trajectory. The executor was trained assuming it always knows its exact state.
Waypoint sequence does not adapt to unexpected obstacles. The high-level planner generates waypoints from task-specific rules, not from real-time obstacle sensing. If an obstacle is in a different place than training examples, the waypoint sequence is invalid and the arm collides.
RL executor overfits to training waypoint patterns. If test-time waypoints are slightly different (different object size, different starting pose), the executor may fail to track them because it learned torque patterns that work only for the exact conditions seen during training.
Done right. Retrain the executor on diverse waypoint spacings; add online vision refinement to correct proprioception estimates; learn waypoint sequences from data instead of hand-coded rules.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal soft-arm simulator (or use PyBullet's soft-body mode). Generate three tasks: (1) grasp a block in a clear workspace, (2) reach around a fixed obstacle, (3) place an object in a confined corner. For each, hand-code the waypoints (or learn them from a small dataset of successful trajectories). Train an RL executor to track waypoints on task 1 only. Then evaluate on all three tasks and on randomized object placements.

Success rate = (number of completed tasks / total tasks) per scene distribution. Generalization gap = success_on_train_distribution - success_on_randomized_distribution.

The demo succeeds if success rate is >80% on clean scenes and the generalization gap is <20%, proving that hierarchical decomposition lets a single RL executor handle new task variations without retraining.
