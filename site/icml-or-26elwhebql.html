Anomaly-Preference Image Generation

Generate realistic and diverse anomalous images by treating anomaly synthesis as a preference learning problem, learning from real anomalies instead of human labels.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Creating unusual or defective image samples is critical for training systems to recognize broken or unusual things. But when you have only a few real anomalies to work from, existing methods struggle: they either generate images that look fake (low realism) or all look similar (low diversity). This paper reformulates the problem as learning which deviations from the normal image people prefer, using the real anomalies you do have as positive examples.

Zero assumption version

The anomaly generation trap

When a factory wants to detect broken parts, or a hospital wants to spot tumors, the AI model must learn what normal and unusual look like. But the unusual is rare—maybe one bad part per thousand. A naive approach: take the few real anomalies you have and let a model copy their exact patterns. This works until the model just memorizes them, unable to imagine new anomalies it has never seen.

More complex methods use generative models (image generators trained to create any image) to synthesize anomalies by learning from the few you have. They face a hard choice: push hard to match the real anomalies exactly (high realism), and the generated samples become repetitive and dull (low diversity). Relax that constraint, and generated images start to look artificial or drift toward normal (low realism).

The core problem. Existing generative methods force you to choose between samples that look real but repeat, or samples that vary but look artificial.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Measure preference from real anomalies

When the model generates a candidate anomaly, compare its intermediate noisy versions (the trajectory) against real anomalies' trajectories at each step.

Step 2: Extract the learning signal without labels

The difference in trajectories directly tells the model which direction to nudge—no human annotations needed.

Step 3: Allocate model capacity by diffusion stage

Early phases (high noise) need capacity for structural variety; later phases need precision for fine detail—the method dynamically shifts emphasis.

Step 4: Trade off coherence and alignment at generation time

When generating a new image, a hierarchical sampler lets you dial up diversity or realism as needed.

Step 5: Measure both realism and diversity

Validate that generated samples fool detectors (realism) and cover the space of anomalies (diversity).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The core move is reformulating anomaly synthesis as a preference-learning problem. Instead of training the model to produce images close to a static target, the model learns from how real anomalies differ at every step of the generation process (the denoising trajectory in a diffusion model).

The hidden quantity

Trajectory deviation: where real and generated diverge

measured quantity  =  Denoising trajectory deviation

The hidden quantity is how far the generated image's trajectory strays from a real anomaly's trajectory at each time step. Imagine generating an image by progressively removing noise—real anomalies follow a specific path through noise-space, and generated samples follow a different path. The paper measures the deviation at each step to extract a learning signal.

To estimate it, the model runs the real anomalies and candidate generations through the same noisy progression, then compares positions frame-by-frame. This comparison reveals whether the candidate is on track or drifting toward generic or artificial directions.

Why it works

Why trajectory-based learning avoids the realism-diversity trap

The classic anomaly generation dilemma arises because the model optimizes a single global distance metric: how close the final image is to a real anomaly. This one number forces a compromise—chase realism and lose diversity, or gain diversity and sacrifice realism.

The paper's insight: the generation process happens in stages (from noisy to sharp), and different stages need different goals. In the noisy phases (high-noise early steps), the model should explore diverse structural variations—different object shapes, placements, sizes. In the sharp phases (low-noise late steps), it should refine fine-grained details—textures, lighting, subtle defects. By measuring trajectory deviation (how the intermediate images diverge at each step), the paper can assign different importance weights to different phases.

The Time-Aware Capacity Allocation module learns to allocate model resources (layers, computations) according to this logic: preserve more capacity for structural variation in early phases, concentrate capacity on detail refinement in late phases. This means the same final model can generate both realistic and diverse anomalies—realistic because late phases are tuned for fidelity, diverse because early phases emphasize structural variety.

Math after the picture

The trajectory-alignment loss

First in words:

preference loss equals the sum over all time steps of the squared deviation between the candidate trajectory and the real trajectory, with weights that are higher in early phases and lower in late phases to emphasize structural diversity and detail fidelity respectively

Then the compact form:

L_pref = sum_t w_t * || x_t_cand - x_t_real ||^2 + lambda_div * entropy(x_final)

Every symbol:

L_prefThe preference loss (lower is better; tells the model how to improve)
x_t_candThe candidate anomaly image at denoising step t
x_t_realA real anomaly image at denoising step t
w_tThe capacity weight at step t (higher in early steps for structure, lower in late steps for detail)
entropy(x_final)A diversity term (how much variation in final samples)
lambda_divHow much to prioritize diversity versus realism
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 3 denoising steps (high noise, medium noise, low noise).
At step 1 (high noise): candidate trajectory [100, 95, 110], real trajectory [100, 98, 105]. Difference-squared: (95-98)^2 + (110-105)^2 = 9 + 25 = 34. Weight w_1 = 3.0 for structural variety. Contribution: 3.0 * 34 = 102.
At step 2 (medium): candidate [50, 52, 48], real [50, 51, 49]. Difference-squared: (52-51)^2 + (48-49)^2 = 1 + 1 = 2. Weight w_2 = 1.5. Contribution: 1.5 * 2 = 3.
At step 3 (low noise): candidate [10, 10.5, 9.8], real [10, 10.2, 9.9]. Difference-squared: (10.5-10.2)^2 + (9.8-9.9)^2 = 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.1. Weight w_3 = 0.5 (penalize structure change, focus on detail). Contribution: 0.5 * 0.1 = 0.05.
Total preference loss = 102 + 3 + 0.05 = 105.05. The model learns that high-noise mismatches are most costly, so it prioritizes staying close to real anomaly patterns in structure.

The time-dependent weights encode the insight that structural fidelity (early phases) and detail quality (late phases) require different emphasis, so the same model can deliver both realism and diversity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too few real anomalies. If the real anomalies you provide are themselves not diverse (all the same type of defect), trajectory deviation will learn a narrow pattern. The method inherits the bias of its reference examples.
Capacity allocation gone wrong. If the learned weights at each time-step flip the priorities (e.g., allocate high weight to late steps instead of early), the model might generate diverse garbage instead of realistic diversity. Early-phase structure matters most.
Generation-time tuning misused. The hierarchical sampler lets you trade coherence for alignment, but wrong settings can push too far—either drift toward nonsense (all alignment, no coherence) or collapse to a single mode (all coherence, no diversity).
Used correctly. Start with a representative set of real anomalies, validate that learned time-step weights emphasize early steps, and calibrate the generation-time sampler on a validation split of real anomalies.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A concrete demo would generate synthetic anomalies from a limited real set (e.g., 20 real defects from a factory line), then measure both realism (can an anomaly detector trained only on normal images flag them as unusual?) and diversity (do the generated anomalies cover different types of defects—cracks, discoloration, misalignment—or cluster on one type?). Realism = (anomaly_detector_accuracy_on_generated) / (anomaly_detector_accuracy_on_real), Diversity = (number_of_distinct_defect_types_found_in_generated) / (number_in_real)

The payoff: high realism and high diversity should both be non-zero in the same run, without forcing you to choose one or the other.
