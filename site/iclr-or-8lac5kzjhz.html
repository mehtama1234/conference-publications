Spatial CAPTCHA: A Geometric Reasoning Test That Stops AI

A procedurally-generated visual puzzle system that tests spatial understanding—a skill humans have but modern AI models lack—to catch bots while keeping humans in.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Online security relies on CAPTCHAs (puzzles that only humans are supposed to solve). But modern AI models got so good at reading text and understanding images that old-fashioned CAPTCHAs no longer work. This paper asks: what can AI still not do that humans find easy? The answer: spatial reasoning—imagining how shapes move in 3D space, rotating objects mentally, understanding perspective, and handling occlusions (things hidden behind other things). Spatial CAPTCHA is a procedural generation system that automatically creates unlimited spatial puzzles testing exactly these abilities. Humans pass; current AI fails.

Zero assumption version

Text and images are not secure anymore

A CAPTCHA is a challenge given to prove you are human: read this squiggly text, or click the images with crosswalks. These worked for years because machines could not read or see. But multi-modal AI (models that read text and images at the same time, like GPT-4o) got too good. They now pass text CAPTCHAs and image CAPTCHAs as well as or better than humans do—because the AI has learned from billions of examples of text and images on the internet.

A beginner would try: make the text more squiggly, or hide more of the objects in images. But AI just gets better at parsing scrambled text and inferring hidden things. You are in an arms race you will lose, because the bottleneck has moved. Machines learned perception—reading pixels—a long time ago. The wall today is spatial understanding: how do objects move in 3D space? Can you imagine rotating a shape? Can you tell which piece is in front?

In one sentence. The system generates unlimited spatial puzzles that ask: if this shape rotates 90 degrees, or if you step sideways and look, what do you see now?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define spatial tasks

Seven task types: reference systems (which direction is up?), perspective-taking (what if you move sideways?), mental rotation (spin this shape), occlusion (what is hidden?), multi-step visualization (combine multiple rotations).

Procedurally generate scenes

A random algorithm creates the scene: place objects, set rotation angles, define which spatial relation answers correctly. Every puzzle is different; no memorization is possible.

Control difficulty with knobs

Adjust how many objects, how complex the layout, how many steps of rotation. Easy has 2 objects and one rotation; hard has 5 objects and three spatial transformations.

Verify answers automatically

The algorithm has the true answer baked in from the scene definition. It also generates near-miss distractors (wrong rotations, wrong perspectives) and checks they fail.

Test humans and AI side by side

Humans see: simple puzzles, 30 seconds per puzzle. AI (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini) gets the same puzzle, measured on how often it picks correctly within 100 seconds.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of text recognition or 2D image tagging, the system measures spatial reasoning: the ability to mentally manipulate 3D shapes and rotations. The paper constrains and randomizes the challenge domain to spatial tasks only—asking what is invisible to pure perception.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spatial capability

measured quantity  =  spatial reasoning gap

Spatial reasoning is the mental skill to rotate objects, understand perspective, handle occlusion, and work with reference frames. It is hidden because AI does not report it; you have to measure it by asking questions. For each puzzle, the system has the ground-truth answer (encoded in the scene definition). It compares human and AI responses against that truth.

The paper estimates this gap by running the same spatial puzzles on humans and 10 leading models (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral, and others). Humans averaged 97% pass rate on the easy subset; the best model reached only 31%. The gap—66 percentage points—is the reason Spatial CAPTCHA works as security. Spatial reasoning is what has not yet saturated.

Why it works

Why it works: the three-stage pipeline

Spatial CAPTCHA works because it extracts the exact gap. The system has three hardened components: (1) procedural generation ensures infinite puzzle diversity, killing memorization; (2) constraint-based difficulty control targets the gap precisely—easy puzzles have just enough spatial load to be solvable, hard puzzles pile on occlusions and multi-step rotations; (3) ground-truth certification ensures the answer is mathematically verified, not a guess. Each puzzle's correctness is computed from the scene definition itself (object positions and rotations), so distractors are provably wrong.

Here is why AI fails while humans pass: humans have intuitive spatial understanding from living in a 3D world. When they see a rotated cube, they immediately picture where each face moved. AI models were trained on the internet—lots of text and 2D images, but relatively few 3D-reasoning scenarios and hardly any procedurally-generated spatial puzzles. So when asked to rotate a shape or account for an occlusion, the AI model is guessing a pattern from its training, not actually reasoning spatially.

How difficulty is controlled

First in words:

difficulty_score = weighted_sum_of_knobs

Then the compact form:

d(Θ) = w_T * ϕ(Θ)

Every symbol:

d(Θ)difficulty score for the scene parameters
Θthe input knobs: object count, rotation steps, occlusion fraction, layout complexity
ϕ(Θ)features extracted from those knobs, e.g. (count, max_rotations, occlusion_depth)
wlearned weights: importance of each feature, fitted to human response times
A tiny worked example
Scene A: 2 objects, 1 rotation, no occlusion -> ϕ(A) = (2, 1, 0) -> d(A) = 0.3*2 + 0.5*1 + 0.2*0 = 0.6 + 0.5 = 1.1 (easy)
Scene B: 5 objects, 3 rotations, 50% occluded -> ϕ(B) = (5, 3, 2.5) -> d(B) = 0.3*5 + 0.5*3 + 0.2*2.5 = 1.5 + 1.5 + 0.5 = 3.5 (hard)
The weights are fitted so that d=1.1 correlates with ~90% human pass rate and d=3.5 with ~60%. Difficulty controls the spatial load, not the visual noise.

Difficulty is a linear combination of scene features, tuned so that easy puzzles have one transformation and hard puzzles layer spatial operations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Procedural generation creates visible artifacts. If the random placement algorithm leaves obvious visual patterns (all objects in a line, or all at the same depth), an AI could learn those shortcuts instead of doing spatial reasoning. The paper prevents this with constraint-based generation: objects must be spread, occlusions must vary, rendering must avoid consistent bias.
Distractors are too easy to rule out. If the wrong answers are obviously wrong (a cube clearly in the wrong orientation), the AI does not have to reason spatially; it just eliminates noise. The paper controls this by generating near-miss distractors: the wrong perspective is plausible, the mismatched rotation is subtle, the hidden piece count is off by one.
AI trains on spatial reasoning data. If a new model is trained on millions of 3D scenes, procedurally-generated spatial puzzles, and multi-step rotations, the gap will shrink. The paper is a benchmark today; it will be a regression test tomorrow. The system must evolve.
Done right: humans validate ground truth. The paper includes human-in-the-loop validation: 60 human raters review puzzles to ensure the ground truth is actually correct and the distractors are plausible. This guards against bugs in the procedural generator.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a procedural generator that creates three spatial task types (mental rotation, perspective-taking, occlusion). For each task, generate 20 random scenes at three difficulty levels. Render each as an image plus four multiple-choice answers. Then: ask 10 humans (or yourself) to solve them, recording pass rate and response time. Next, submit the same image and options to an AI model (like Claude or GPT-4o) and log its answers. Compare side by side.
