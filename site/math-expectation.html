In plain terms

Imagine a game: you roll a die and win a prize that depends on the result. Roll a 1 and you get one dollar. Roll a 2 and you get two dollars. Roll a 3 through 6 and you get ten dollars each. Before you roll, what should you expect to earn on average? The fair answer isn't just 'somewhere between one and ten dollars'—it depends on how likely each outcome is. Since a fair die gives each number a one-sixth chance, the six-dollar outcome is rarer than winning ten dollars. The expected value is your fair guess: the weighted average of all possible winnings, where the weight is how often each shows up.

The expectation is that fair guess. It answers the question: if this random process happened a thousand times, what would the average come out to? It's not the most likely single outcome—it's the long-run average if you could repeat the situation forever. Machines use expectation to think about uncertain futures: how good will this policy (strategy) be on average, what precision score will the model reach on new data, how much signal does this noisy sensor hold on average. Whenever you average something out over all the ways it might vary, you're computing an expectation.

The equation
E[f(x)]=\sum_x p(x) f(x)
E[f(x)]the expected value of f(x)—the long-run average outcome you get from applying function f to a random input
f(x)a result or measurement that depends on the random input x—could be a reward, a loss, or any outcome
p(x)the probability of outcome x—how often x shows up
∑_xthe sum over all possible values of x—add them all up
p(x) f(x)the product of probability and outcome—the contribution of this single possibility
Worked example
Rolling a die for money
Three outcomes: roll 1 get $1; roll 2 get $2; roll 3-6 get $10 each.
Probabilities: p(1)=1/6, p(2)=1/6, p(3..6)=1/6 each.
Contributions: 1·(1/6) + 2·(1/6) + 10·(1/6) + 10·(1/6) + 10·(1/6) + 10·(1/6)
Sum: 1/6 + 2/6 + 40/6 = 43/6 ≈ $7.17
Average over many rolls: $7.17 per roll (long-run average).
Why it shows up

Expectation is everywhere in machine learning because predictions and decisions always deal with uncertainty. When a model outputs probabilities over classes, you compute the expected accuracy (how correct will it be on average). When reinforcement learning decides which action to take, it picks the one with the highest expected reward (not the one that got lucky once, but the best on average). When Bayesian methods infer hidden parameters (the tunable knobs inside a model) from noisy data, they work by averaging over all plausible possibilities weighted by how likely each is given the observed evidence. When generative models are trained, the training process minimizes expected loss (the average mistake across many examples), not the worst-case mistake. In optimization, gradient descent (the method that adjusts those knobs by following the slope of the error surface) follows the expected gradient direction, not the direction from a single noisy update. In every paper that touches probabilities, noise, sampling, or future uncertainty—from safety alignment to reinforcement learning to representation learning—expectation is the tool that says 'here's what should happen on average, not on this one lucky roll.'

Expectation lets machines reason about the future and about uncertainty without picking a single guess. Instead of predicting 'the temperature tomorrow will be 72 degrees,' a weather model can say 'the expected temperature is 72 degrees, accounting for all the rain and wind scenarios.' Instead of guessing 'this image is a cat,' a vision system can say 'on images like this, we expect 97 percent accuracy, after summing over all images we trained on.' This average is what matters for tuning systems and making reliable predictions.
