Decoupling Positional and Symbolic Attention in Transformers

Attention heads in Transformers split into positional and symbolic handlers because of how Rotary Positional Encoding's frequency components work.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Transformers must handle two kinds of information at once: where a word sits in the sentence (positional) and what it means (symbolic). This paper shows that Rotary Positional Encoding achieves this split automatically by using high frequencies for position and low frequencies for meaning, and that individual reading mechanisms (small neural networks that decide which words to focus on) naturally divide themselves into pure positional handlers, pure symbolic handlers, or hybrids based on which frequencies they use.

Zero assumption version

Transformers must split two signals at once

A language model must answer two separate questions to understand a sentence: Is this word near the start or near the end (position)? And what does this word mean (symbol)? The model stores this information in its weights and activations (the numbers that get computed and passed along when the finished model answers a new question).

In a Transformer, positional information is fed in via a Positional Encoding — a mathematical transformation added to each word before the model processes it. Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE), a popular choice, encodes position as a rotation using high and low frequencies (essentially, fast and slow sine waves). But for decades, nobody knew exactly how a single encoding pulled apart position from meaning, or which parts of the model were reading which signal.

The question. Do different attention heads (the reading mechanisms in each layer) naturally specialize into some reading position and some reading meaning, and if so, why?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define the split

A head is positional if attention stays in one place regardless of word content; symbolic if it moves with meaning.

Measure both signals in one head

Score each head on a positional scale and a symbolic scale using two separate metrics.

Connect to frequencies

Decompose each head by RoPE frequency and show high-frequency heads score high on positional, low-frequency heads score high on symbolic.

Causality test

Train tiny Transformers on pure-positional and pure-symbolic tasks to verify heads can learn these roles.

Block and measure

Block access to certain frequencies and watch performance collapse on the tasks that need them.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper does not propose a new method; instead it reveals something hidden: the connection between which frequencies an attention head can see (via RoPE) and what job that head ends up doing (positional vs. symbolic). By formalizing this connection, the paper shows that the frequency components of RoPE naturally partition the model into specialist readers.

the object it moves  =  Frequency access in attention heads
The hidden quantity

Positional and symbolic scores

measured quantity  =  positional and symbolic behavior scores

Each attention head has two scores: a positional score (how much does this head concentrate on word location, ignoring content?) and a symbolic score (how much does this head move its focus when word identity changes, independent of position?). The paper defines these mathematically by looking at how attention weights cluster into blocks and how they shift when you permute the words.

The paper measures both by partitioning each sentence into chunks and checking: Does average attention stay constant across chunks (high positional), or does it chase the actual words (high symbolic)? A head can score high on one, high on the other, or both. The key finding: heads that score high on positional use only high-frequency RoPE components, and symbolic heads use only low frequencies. This correspondence is not random — it emerges from how RoPE was designed.

Why it works

How frequency components enable the split

Rotary Positional Encoding works by rotating each word representation using different sine and cosine frequencies. High frequencies rotate fast (they change direction many times within a sentence), encoding fine position details. Low frequencies rotate slowly (they complete few cycles), encoding global position but mixing easily with semantic content.

Attention heads are not forced to use any particular frequency. Instead, each head learns which frequencies to attend to during training. Heads that learn to weight high-frequency rotations end up reading pure position: the rotation state tells them everything about where they are in the sequence. Heads that weight low frequencies do something different: the low-frequency rotation is almost constant across a sentence, so these heads must rely on the word embeddings themselves (the learned semantic numbers, a representation of what each word means) to distinguish words. This is why low-frequency heads naturally become symbolic readers. The causality is direct: the frequencies a head can access determine which kind of information it has available, and thus what job it can do. Block the high frequencies from an early layer and it cannot compute position; block the low frequencies from a late layer and it loses meaning.

Math after the picture

Measuring the split

First in words:

positional_score = concentration of attention over position blocks; symbolic_score = shift in attention when content changes

Then the compact form:

P(H) = 1/m * sum_i (min_j max_k attention[j,k within block i]); S(H) = KL_divergence(attention_on_x, attention_on_permuted_x)

Every symbol:

P(H)positional score of head H (higher = more positional)
S(H)symbolic score of head H (higher = more symbolic)
mnumber of contiguous blocks the input is divided into
attention[j,k]attention weight from position j to position k
KL_divergencea measure of how different two distributions are (0 if identical)
permuted_xthe same input but with word order shuffled
A tiny worked example
Input: 4-word sentence divided into 2 blocks (words 1-2, words 3-4).
Head A attention: [0.8, 0.7, 0.05, 0.08] — mostly on block 1 regardless of content.
P(Head A) = high (attention concentrated in early block).
Shuffle words: same positions get new words; Head A still attends to same positions.
KL(original, shuffled) near zero → S(Head A) = low (symbolic score drops).
Conclusion: Head A is highly positional.
Head B attention: [0.15, 0.05, 0.7, 0.8] — moves to follow the actual words present, not their positions.
P(Head B) = low (spread across blocks); S(Head B) = high (attention shifts with shuffle).
Conclusion: Head B is highly symbolic.

The positional score measures how fixed the attention location is; the symbolic score measures how much it chases content. Together they characterize which frequencies a head uses.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Circular frequency coupling. This paper shows correlation between frequency use and head behavior. If a head must do both position and symbol (a mix head), the two requirements can conflict — high frequencies aid position but hurt meaning, and vice versa. The paper does not solve this; it identifies it.
RoPE-only insight. The frequency decomposition is specific to Rotary Positional Encoding. Other positional encodings (like absolute positions or learned embeddings) do not have a natural frequency structure, so this analysis does not transfer.
Canonical tasks are artificial. The paper uses pure positional (find index) and pure symbolic (match a word by meaning) tasks. Real language understanding mixes both constantly. These canonical tasks are not representative of real workloads.
Frequency control does not reorder layers. Blocking frequencies breaks a head's job but does not change how layers stack. Early layers still run before late layers, so architectural constraints remain even after controlling frequency access.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a 1-layer Transformer (just one stack of attention heads plus a small neural network) on two canonical tasks: (1) Given a list of words, return the position of a target word (pure positional task). (2) Given a list with multiple copies of the same word at different positions, return the position of the last occurrence (pure symbolic task). For each task, train multiple times with different RoPE configurations: no frequency restrictions, only high frequencies allowed, only low frequencies allowed. Measure accuracy on a held-out set of positions (e.g., target at position 7 of 10).

accuracy_positional_task_high_freq_only / accuracy_positional_task_full_freq; accuracy_symbolic_task_low_freq_only / accuracy_symbolic_task_full_freq

If the theory holds, blocking low frequencies should kill symbolic performance while preserving positional; blocking high frequencies should kill positional while preserving symbolic. You will see causality: remove the frequencies a task needs, and the head fails to solve it.
