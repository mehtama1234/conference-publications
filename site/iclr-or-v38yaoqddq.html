Decoupling Positional and Symbolic Attention in Transformers

Transformer attention heads split into two separate jobs: one tracks where words sit in a sentence, the other figures out what they mean.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

A Transformer reads words by doing two things at once: tracking their position in the sentence and understanding what they represent. Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE), a popular technique, handles both using different parts of its signals. This paper shows exactly how RoPE separates these two jobs and proves that attention heads either do position-tracking or meaning-tracking, but not both.

The two-job problem

Transformers read a sentence by putting each word into a list of numbers called an embedding (just a list of numbers that encodes what the word is). But a list alone does not tell you WHERE each word sits in the sentence. A naive approach is to add the position directly to the list: embedding of word 3 gets +3, word 4 gets +4. The problem: this mixes position and meaning together in the same numbers, so the model cannot tell them apart because when the head tries to use a number, it gets both signals mixed together—like hearing a voice and music through the same speaker. When it needs to know position (e.g., to count how far two words are apart), it has to filter out the meaning information first, which takes extra computational steps and introduces errors. When it needs to know meaning, it has to filter out the position. This confusion makes the model slower and less accurate.

Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE) tries to fix this by using a trick: it rotates the number-list differently at each position, like spinning a wheel by different amounts. The question the paper tackles is: does RoPE actually separate the two jobs? And if so, how?

In one sentence. The paper shows that RoPE naturally decouples position-tracking from meaning-tracking by using large rotations (large frequencies) for position and small rotations (small frequencies) for meaning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define the two jobs

Positional head: knows where word sits. Symbolic head: knows what word means.

Measure which frequencies each head uses

Inspect the rotation patterns and compute a score for how much each head relies on large vs. small rotations.

Prove they are mutually exclusive

Heads that use large rotations do not use small ones, and vice versa. No head does both.

Test with pure tasks

Create tasks that ONLY need position (e.g., find the 5th word) or ONLY need meaning (e.g., find the noun). Measure performance.

Block frequencies and control performance

Restrict which frequencies the model can access; performance drops only on the matching task.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change Rotary Position Encoding itself. Instead, it reveals what RoPE is already doing: encoding position in large-rotation signals and meaning in small-rotation signals. It then shows the causal proof: by blocking access to certain rotations, you control which jobs the heads can do. The object that moves is frequency access: which rotation sizes the attention head can use.

the object it moves  =  Frequency access for each attention head
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frequency dependence

measured quantity  =  How much a head trusts each rotation size

The paper introduces a metric that measures how strongly each attention head relies on large rotations versus small rotations. Think of it as a dial: turn it toward large rotations (high frequencies) and the head gets better at position-tracking tasks because large rotations create different signals at each word position, making location differences obvious to the head. Turn it toward small rotations (low frequencies) and the head gets better at meaning-tracking tasks because small rotations change slowly across positions and preserve the semantic (meaning-related) structure within the list. This metric is the hidden knob the paper discovers and then turns experimentally.

The paper estimates this by looking at which frequency signals the head's attention scores actually use. It computes a score that quantifies the 'temperature' of each frequency component in the head's learned weights: hot frequencies (position) versus cold frequencies (meaning).

Why it works

Why it works: the frequency separation trick

RoPE rotates the embedding list by different amounts at each position. Position 1 gets a small rotation (like 1 degree), position 2 gets 2 degrees, position 100 gets 100 degrees. But here is the key: RoPE does not rotate every number in the list by the same amount. Instead, it rotates different parts (different frequency bands) at different rates. The high-frequency parts (like bands 1-10) rotate very fast: position 1 gets a small rotation, position 2 gets a much bigger rotation, position 100 gets a huge rotation. The low-frequency parts (like bands 100-128) rotate very slowly: position 1 gets a tiny rotation, position 100 gets only a slightly bigger rotation. Because the rotations are so different, the model can tell them apart. High-frequency motion (big jumps) says where the word is. Low-frequency motion (tiny, smooth changes) carries what the word means. The model learns this automatically: heads that need to know position grab the high-frequency parts. Heads that need meaning grab the low-frequency parts.

The paper then proves this by blocking access: if you hide the high-frequency parts from all heads, they lose position information but keep meaning. Hide the low-frequency parts, and they lose meaning but keep position. This proves the separation is not an accident; it is baked into how RoPE rotates.

inputWord embedding (list of numbers) + position in sentence
stepApply RoPE: rotate embedding by amount that depends on position
stepHigh-frequency parts rotate fast (encodes position), low-frequency parts rotate slow (encodes meaning)
stepAttention head selects which frequencies to pay attention to
outputHead attends to position or meaning, but not both
Math after the picture

The equation: position rotation

First in words:

For each position m, rotate the embedding by an angle that grows with the frequency band. Low bands (meaning) rotate slowly. High bands (position) rotate fast. The rotation angle is frequency times position.

Then the compact form:

theta_m = m * base_frequency

Every symbol:

theta_mThe rotation angle (in degrees or radians) applied at position m
mThe position of the word in the sentence (1, 2, 3, ...)
base_frequencyHow fast this frequency band rotates (small for meaning, large for position)
A tiny worked example
Position 1, low frequency band (meaning): theta = 1 * 0.001 = 0.001 radians (almost no rotation)
Position 100, low frequency band (meaning): theta = 100 * 0.001 = 0.1 radians (tiny slow rotation)
Position 1, high frequency band (position): theta = 1 * 10 = 10 radians (big rotation)
Position 100, high frequency band (position): theta = 100 * 10 = 1000 radians (huge rotation)
Result: high-frequency parts show huge differences across positions (says where). Low-frequency parts show tiny differences (says what).

The model can separate location (where the word sits) from identity (what the word is) because the rotation speeds are so different.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mixed frequencies in a head If a head learns to use both high and low frequencies, it ends up trying to do both jobs at once and does neither well. The model can avoid this during training because specialization is more efficient, but in principle it is possible.
A task that actually needs both Some tasks (e.g., 'find the verb in the 5th word position') genuinely need both position and meaning. In these cases, one head cannot do the job alone; two heads must cooperate. The framework still holds, but the story is more complex.
Long sequences where RoPE rotations wrap around RoPE rotations are periodic: they repeat after 2 pi radians. For very long sequences, high frequencies can wrap around so much that distant positions look similar. This confuses the head about true position.
Done right: the frequency boundary is learnable The model does not hardcode which frequencies mean position versus meaning. It learns which frequencies to trust for each head. This works across many different model sizes and languages.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny Transformer (just 2 attention heads) on a mini language task. First, run the model normally and measure which head uses high frequencies and which uses low. Second, add a 'position-only' synthetic task: given a sequence of random tokens, predict the position of a specific marker. Third, add a 'meaning-only' task: given a sequence, find and classify the noun. Finally, mask out high frequencies in one test and low frequencies in another, and measure performance on each task. The demo should show that masking high frequencies breaks position-only but not meaning-only, and vice versa.

task_accuracy = (correct_predictions / total_predictions) * 100; frequency_importance = task_accuracy_with_full_frequencies - task_accuracy_with_blocked_frequencies

If the model truly separates position and meaning via frequency, blocking one frequency band breaks only one task, not both.
