SophiaVL-R1: Thinking Rewards for Multimodal Reasoning

A multimodal model learns to reason better by getting feedback on both its thinking process and its final answer, but only trusts the thinking feedback when it reliably points to correct reasoning.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Multimodal models (models that read both images and text) trained with reinforcement learning learn to find correct answers, but they do not get direct feedback on whether they are thinking through problems well. This lets them develop bad thinking habits that work for the training problems but fail on new ones. SophiaVL-R1 fixes this by adding a separate reward signal that judges the reasoning steps themselves, then carefully decides when to trust that signal by checking if it gives high marks only to reasoning that leads to right answers.

Zero assumption version

The thinking process has no teacher

When a multimodal model learns from a set of problems with right and wrong answers, the training only says whether the final answer is correct. It does not say anything about whether the path to that answer was logical. So a model can stumble onto the right answer by bad luck or by learning tricks that happen to work on that particular test set.

A beginner might try: just give the model more training examples. But that does not help, because the model still sees only right-and-wrong outcomes, not feedback about the quality of its reasoning. Whether you train on 100 examples or 10,000, if none of them say anything about how good the reasoning is, the model still learns to find any shortcut that works—guessing the most common answer type, or following patterns that work on this data but break on new data.

The problem in one sentence. Outcome-only reward trains models to find correct answers, not to reason well.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train a thinking reward model

Collect reasoning chains from initial training. Train a separate model to score the logical quality of entire thinking processes.

Compute trustworthiness weights

For each thinking reward score, compare it to outcomes: if high score goes to wrong answers, the score is unreliable; give it a lower weight.

Combine outcome and thinking rewards

Blend two signals: rule-based right-or-wrong outcome score, plus the weighted thinking score, into one final reward for training.

Use annealing to fade the thinking signal

Over training steps, gradually lower the strength of the thinking reward, because the thinking reward model is only trained on a limited set of reasoning patterns early on and can mislead late-stage training. The outcome signal (right or wrong) is always truthful, so shift weight toward it over time.

Train the main model on the combined reward

The multimodal model learns to maximize both reasoning quality and getting the answer right, updating all its weights accordingly.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the training signal: instead of feeding the model a single outcome reward (right or wrong), it feeds a blend of two rewards: one for the final answer, one for the reasoning process. The blend is weighted by trustworthiness and fades over time.

The hidden quantity

Trustworthiness: when to believe the thinking reward

measured quantity  =  Trustworthiness weight

The paper introduces a trustworthiness weight, a number between 0 and 1, that tells how much to believe the thinking reward for any particular answer. It is low when the thinking reward gives high marks to answers that turn out to be wrong, indicating the thinking reward is being fooled.

The paper estimates it by looking at groups: it compares the average thinking reward score given to answers with correct outcomes versus answers with incorrect outcomes. If these two averages are close, the thinking reward is not reliably pointing to right reasoning, so trustworthiness goes down. If correct answers consistently get higher thinking scores, the thinking reward is useful, so trustworthiness goes up.

Paper reading. The weight is computed from the contrast between mean thinking-reward scores on correct-outcome samples versus incorrect-outcome samples, following the principle that a reliable reward signal should rank correct reasoning higher.
Why it works

Why it works: honest feedback on both the process and the answer

The core reason this works is separation of concerns. The outcome reward (right or wrong) is objective and reliable but says nothing about the reasoning path. The thinking reward looks at reasoning quality but can be tricked by a model that learns to say the right words without sound logic. By weighting the thinking reward by trustworthiness, the method uses the thinking score as a guide only when it actually correlates with correct outcomes. This fixes the gap: the model now has explicit feedback on whether its reasoning steps are sensible, but only follows that feedback when it proves reliable.

The annealing schedule further improves this by starting with both signals equally weighted, so early training gets the benefit of process-level guidance. As training proceeds, the schedule fades out the thinking reward, so late training relies increasingly on the objective outcome signal alone. This prevents the model from over-fitting to a thinking reward model that was trained on limited data early in training.

Math after the picture

The reward formula with annealing

First in words:

The training reward for each sample equals the outcome reward plus (trustworthiness weight times annealing decay factor) times the thinking reward.

Then the compact form:

R_i = R^o_i + (w_i * exp(-steps / T)) * R^t_i

Every symbol:

R_ifinal training reward for sample i
R^o_iobjective outcome reward (1 for correct, 0 or negative for wrong)
w_itrustworthiness weight for sample i (how much the thinking reward correlates with correctness)
stepscurrent training step number
Ttotal number of training steps (controls how fast annealing happens)
R^t_ithinking reward: score of how logically sound the reasoning is
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have two samples at step 500 of 1500 total steps (T = 1500).
Sample A: outcome reward = 1 (correct), thinking reward = 0.8 (good reasoning), trustworthiness weight = 0.9 (this model usually scores right). Annealing factor = exp(-500 / 1500) = exp(-0.333) = 0.717.
R_A = 1 + (0.9 * 0.717) * 0.8 = 1 + 0.516 = 1.516
Sample B: outcome reward = 0 (wrong), thinking reward = 0.6 (mediocre reasoning), trustworthiness weight = 0.5 (unreliable). Annealing factor = 0.717.
R_B = 0 + (0.5 * 0.717) * 0.6 = 0 + 0.215 = 0.215
At step 500, Sample A gets reward 1.516 (thinking reward boosted it), Sample B gets 0.215 (thinking reward suppressed a bad signal). By step 1500, annealing factor = exp(-1500 / 1500) = exp(-1) = 0.368, so thinking signal is nearly gone and only outcomes drive learning.

The exponential decay exp(-steps / T) smoothly shrinks the thinking reward's influence, letting early training benefit from process feedback and late training rely on objective outcomes.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The thinking reward model is trained on biased data. If the initial training examples come from a model using shortcuts, the thinking reward model learns to score those shortcuts as good. Trustworthiness weighting helps catch this, because it looks for cases where the thinking reward gives high marks but the answer is wrong—this mismatch signals that the thinking reward is being fooled. When such mismatches are frequent, the weight is lowered, so the thinking reward contributes less to training. But if all the initial samples are biased the same way (all shortcuts that happen to work), this contradiction never appears, and the thinking reward stays unreliable.
Thinking reward overfits to the training set. The thinking reward model is trained on reasoning from the main model itself. If it sees only a narrow set of reasoning styles, it may penalize reasoning that looks different but is actually correct. Annealing mitigates this by letting outcome rewards take over, but early training may be misdirected.
The trustworthiness weight is computed from the same batch of outcomes. If a batch has only easy or only hard problems, the correlation between thinking scores and outcomes may be misleading. A larger, more balanced dataset for computing trustworthiness reduces this risk.
Done right: multi-scale dataset and trustworthiness audit. Compute trustworthiness weights on a held-out validation set with diverse problem difficulty. Monitor whether thinking-reward correlations stay stable across training. Adjust annealing schedule if the thinking reward stays too strong too long.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny multimodal reasoning task (e.g., a set of 50 image-math problems with right and wrong answers marked). Train a small multimodal model in two ways: (1) with only outcome rewards, (2) with SophiaVL-R1 using thinking rewards. Measure how each strategy generalizes to a held-out test set of 20 new problems with the same difficulty distribution. Track the learning curve: does outcome-only training plateau early from over-fitting to shortcuts, while SophiaVL-R1 keeps improving? Also manually inspect a sample of reasoning chains from each: does the outcome-only model learn nonsensical shortcuts, while SophiaVL-R1 produces more systematic reasoning steps?

Generalization gap = (accuracy on training set) - (accuracy on held-out test set). Lower is better: outcome-only should show 20+ point gap; SophiaVL-R1 should show <10 point gap.

The demo must make obvious that thinking rewards steer the model toward reasoning that generalizes, not just toward answers that memorize.
