SophiaVL-R1: Training MLLMs to Reason Out Loud

A smaller multimodal model learns to reason as well as a much larger one by rewarding not just right answers, but the quality of its thinking process.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Multimodal large language models that can see images and reason about them are typically trained by giving them rewards for correct final answers. But they never get direct feedback on whether their thinking — the steps they take to arrive at an answer — is any good. SophiaVL-R1 changes this by adding a second reward signal that judges the reasoning process itself, then carefully learns which thinking rewards to trust.

Zero assumption version

The model learns to fake its way to the right answer

Today's multimodal models (ones that read images and write) are trained by showing them a puzzle and a reward: plus 1 if your final answer is correct, zero otherwise. The model learns to hunt for any reasoning path that lands on the right answer, even a sloppy or roundabout one. A student cram-studying for a test might get 95 percent correct but never internalize the logic — they memorize the answers. When the test changes slightly, they crash.

The naive fix is to hope that by seeing enough images and puzzles, the model will accidentally learn clean reasoning. But that is slow and unreliable. Many reasoning chains that lead to the right answer are brittle: they work only on that exact problem shape, not on a family of related ones.

The real issue. Reward only the final answer, and the model learns which answers to output, but not how to think.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train the thinking judge

Build a second model that scores entire reasoning chains by comparing good and bad examples.

Run the main model

The multimodal model now sees the puzzle and generates a reasoning chain plus a final answer.

Score both halves

The thinking judge scores the reasoning; an outcome judge scores whether the answer is correct.

Assign trust to thinking

For each sample, measure: thinking reward for correct chains minus thinking reward for wrong chains.

Update with weighted signals

Nudge the model toward thinking that the judge trusts, while always respecting the ground-truth answer reward.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The object is the reasoning chain itself — the intermediate steps, explanations, and working that the model generates before the final answer. Earlier work trained by nudging only the final output. This paper adds direct learning signals on the chain in the middle.

the object it moves  =  The reasoning chain
The hidden quantity

Thinking trustworthiness: how much to believe the thinking reward

measured quantity  =  Trustworthiness of each thinking signal

Trustworthiness is a simple quantity: for any problem sample, compute the thinking reward given to correct reasoning minus the thinking reward given to incorrect reasoning. If the thinking judge gives high scores to both right and wrong paths, that sample's thinking reward is unreliable, and trustworthiness is low. If it sharply praises only correct thinking, trustworthiness is high.

This quantity matters because the thinking reward model (the judge) is learned, not perfect. On some samples it may hallucinate and rate sloppy chains highly. Trustworthiness tells the main model which samples it should listen to the thinking judge, and on which samples it should ignore the thinking reward and trust only the outcome.

Plain reading. Some problems are easy to judge. On those, the thinking reward should be trusted. Other problems are fuzzy, and the reward model disagrees with itself. On fuzzy cases, only the right-answer reward should count.
Paper reading. Trust-GRPO assigns a trustworthiness weight by measuring how much higher the thinking judge scores correct chains than incorrect chains on each sample, bounding it in a range from zero to one, and using it to scale the thinking reward contribution to the model update.
Why it works

The causal chain: why judging thinking works

Without thinking rewards, the model only sees a binary signal (correct or incorrect) and may luck into the right answer by memorizing coincidences. When you add a thinking reward that praises good reasoning steps, the model now has a finer-grained signal: it can learn which intermediate moves are sensible, not just which answers win.

But that thinking reward is learned, not ground truth. It will sometimes praise sloppy reasoning that happens to lead to a correct answer on that one sample. The core move is to use the answer itself as an audit: on samples where the thinking judge gives high scores to both right and wrong chains, the thinking reward is unreliable. Scaling it down (low trustworthiness) on those samples prevents the model from learning garbage. On samples where the thinking judge clearly praises correct thinking and dismisses incorrect thinking, the model can safely follow the thinking reward.

Over time, as the model gets better, this thinking reward becomes more useful because correct and incorrect chains become clearer. So the paper also slowly reduces the weight of the thinking reward in the training formula, letting the model rely more on the outcome reward. This prevents the model from freezing to an imperfect thinking judge and never improving further.

Math after the picture

The reward formula with trustworthiness weighting

First in words:

Training signal equals the outcome reward (is the answer correct) plus a trustworthiness-weighted thinking reward (how good is the reasoning, but only if the judge seems honest on this sample) minus an annealing coefficient times the thinking reward (gradually fade it out).

Then the compact form:

L = - (R_outcome + alpha_t * w_i * R_thinking)

Every symbol:

R_outcomeoutcome reward: plus 1 if answer correct, zero otherwise
R_thinkingthinking reward: score from the judge on the reasoning chain quality
w_itrustworthiness on sample i: difference in thinking reward between correct and incorrect chains
alpha_tannealing weight at training step t: starts high, decreases to zero
A tiny worked example
Sample A: correct answer, thinking judge gives 0.9 to correct chains and 0.85 to incorrect. Trustworthiness w_A = 0.9 - 0.85 = 0.05 (low, fuzzy). alpha_t = 0.5. Signal = 1.0 (correct answer) + 0.5 * 0.05 * 0.8 = 1.02 (mostly the answer, small thinking help).
Sample B: correct answer, thinking judge gives 0.9 to correct chains and 0.1 to incorrect. Trustworthiness w_B = 0.9 - 0.1 = 0.8 (high, clear). Signal = 1.0 (correct answer) + 0.5 * 0.8 * 0.8 = 1.32 (answer plus strong thinking help).
Same outcome on both (correct), but on Sample B the model learns the reasoning harder because the thinking reward is honest.

The formula ensures the model learns clean reasoning on samples where the thinking judge is reliable, and ignores noisy thinking rewards on fuzzy samples.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The thinking judge itself is broken. If the judge gives high scores to both correct and incorrect chains equally, trustworthiness stays low, and the thinking reward becomes useless. The model learns only from outcome, falling back to memorization.
Annealing reduces thinking weight too early. If alpha_t drops to near zero before the model has internalized good reasoning patterns, it stops learning thinking and relies only on answers again.
Trustworthiness misses reward hacking on complex problems. On a very hard reasoning chain, the judge might give high scores to any plausible chain, whether it is truly sound or just sounds right. Trustworthiness assumes the judge can tell; on very hard problems, it might not.
Done right. The thinking judge is trained on a diverse, well-labeled dataset of reasoning chains. Trustworthiness is computed per-sample, allowing the model to be selective. Annealing is tuned so that thinking rewards matter most in the early stage when they help, then fade as outcome reward becomes sufficient.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple multimodal task (e.g., visual math problems where the model must show working before giving a numerical answer) and two models: a baseline trained only on outcome rewards, and SophiaVL-R1. Train both on a set of problems. Then test both on a held-out set with a different visual style or problem difficulty. Measure both accuracy and how often the reasoning is correct (i.e., even if the answer is right, is the chain sensible).

Reasoning quality = (# problems with both correct answer AND sound reasoning) / (# total problems); Generalization = accuracy on new-style problems / accuracy on training-style problems

SophiaVL-R1 should maintain high accuracy on new-style problems while baseline drops. SophiaVL-R1 should also show more human-readable reasoning chains in its outputs.
