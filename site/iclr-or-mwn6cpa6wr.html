Hourglass Persistence for Graphs

Graph neural networks can read more of the graph's topology by tracking holes and features as edges are both added and removed, not just added.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graph neural networks usually understand graphs by looking at local neighborhoods—each node's immediate neighbors. But graphs have deeper geometric structure: loops, tunnels, and regions of high connectivity that a neighborhood view misses. This paper measures that structure using topological persistence, which is like zooming in and out of the graph to count how many connected chunks, loops, and higher-order holes appear and disappear. Prior methods only zoom in (add edges); this paper zooms in and out (add and remove edges) to see more patterns.

Zero assumption version

Graphs hide their shape

A graph is just nodes (dots) and edges (lines connecting them). A neural network trained on graphs learns by passing information from each node to its neighbors, over and over. But this local view misses the graph's global shape: How many separate clusters are there? Are there loops? Do loops form tunnels or cavities? These large-scale patterns are invisible to neighborhood gossip.

A beginner might try to measure shape by counting connected components or finding the longest cycle. But this is binary—each feature is either present or absent. Real graphs are mixed: a loop might be important in one region but strangled by shortcuts elsewhere. The old way (inclusion-based persistent homology) draws increasingly large subgraphs and counts features each time, but only growing; it never shrinks to see what was hiding.

So the graph's full story—which features are strong, which are fragile, which are just noise—goes unrecorded. The model misses it.

The method. Track what topological features (connected chunks, loops, cavities) stay alive as the graph shrinks and grows, not just grows.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build two sequences

Grow the graph by adding edges; shrink it by removing edges. Each is a sequence of stages.

At each stage, count features

For each grown or shrunk graph, count connected components, loops, cavities, etc.

Record birth and death

When does each feature appear (birth) and when does it merge or vanish (death)?

Compare the two sequences

A feature's lifetime depends on whether it was born during growth or during contraction.

Encode all lifetimes as numbers

Feed the birth-death pairs into the neural network so it sees the full topological story.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a second sequence to the topological measurement. Standard persistent homology only grows the graph. This paper also shrinks it, and interleaves both. The graph still ends up the same, but the model sees it two ways.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: topological features and their lifetimes

measured quantity  =  Birth-death pairs

Topological features are the connected chunks, loops, and higher-dimensional cavities in a graph. Each feature is born when it first appears (as edges are added) and dies when it merges with something else or vanishes (as edges are removed or as we zoom out). The birth stage and death stage are a pair of numbers—that pair encodes how real and important the feature is. A long lifetime (death far from birth) means the feature stays alive through many changes; a short lifetime means it disappears quickly when edges are nudged.

This paper measures birth-death pairs not just during growth but also during contraction. As the graph shrinks, different features become visible; a loop that looked permanent during growth might actually have been a shortcut away from becoming a tunnel. By contracting, the paper sees which features are truly central to the graph's shape.

Why it works

Why both growth and contraction see more

Consider a star-shaped graph: a central hub node with spokes radiating outward, plus an outer ring connecting the spoke ends. If you only add edges (growth), you might add the ring first, forming a large loop. Then you add the spokes. The ring's lifetime is short because the ring closed quickly, before the interior structure mattered. But each spoke is a separate component initially, and they stay separate longer. The growth sequence says: spokes are important, the ring is not.

Now shrink the graph (contraction). Remove edges from the outer ring first. The big loop disappears immediately. Remove more edges—eventually the spokes disconnect from the hub. The contraction sequence sees the ring die right away, but the hub-spoke connection stays strong. This shows the model: the ring was surface-level, the hub is the true structural heart.

By interleaving both sequences, the model sees the graph in many intermediate states. Add some edges, remove some, add more, remove more. Each intermediate state highlights different features. Some loops are byproducts of the edge order; some are truly central to the shape. The contraction sequence reveals which is which, giving the neural network a much clearer view of the graph's topology.

A tiny worked example
Graph: 4 nodes A, B, C, D with edges A-B, B-C, C-D, D-A (a square).
Growth order: add A-B (1 component: {A,B}), add B-C (still 1), add C-D (still 1), add D-A (forms a loop).
Feature: loop born at stage 4 (when edge D-A closes the square).
Contraction: remove D-A. The loop dies at stage 1 (counting backwards). Birth=4, death=1 (in contraction time). Lifetime=3.
Growth alone: loop appears at stage 4, stays forever (death at infinity). Lifetime=infinite.
Interleaved: two viewpoints—growth says permanent, contraction says fragile. Model sees both lifetimes.

The extended diagram (growth and contraction together) is richer than growth alone because it shows which features are load-bearing versus contingent on the particular edge order.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If both orderings are the same, no new information. The growth sequence and contraction sequence must be chosen to reveal different perspectives on the graph. If you add edges in the same order as you remove them, you get redundant information. The two functions parametrizing the sequences must be chosen carefully.
Small graphs have no interesting topology. If the graph is a tree (no loops), persistent homology reports almost nothing—no cycles, no cavities, just a chain. The method works best on graphs with many cycles, cavities, and intricate connectivity.
Computational cost grows quickly. Computing homology at every stage of growth and contraction is expensive. The paper needs efficient algorithms; naive implementations become slow on large graphs.
Done right: both sequences are complementary. When the growth order and contraction order are chosen to expose different subgraphs, the model sees a detailed topological fingerprint that generic neural networks otherwise miss.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small graph with a clear topology: for example, a grid graph with a hole in the middle (making a loop around the hole), plus a tail sticking off the side. Train a graph neural network on a simple task (e.g., classify the graph as having a hole or not). Compare two versions: one that uses only standard graph convolutions (neighborhoods), one that also feeds in birth-death pairs from the hourglass method. The second should learn faster and generalize better to new graphs, because it sees the hole explicitly.

accuracy_improvement = accuracy_with_hourglass - accuracy_without_hourglass_on_held_out_graphs

The demo makes clear why it works: adding topology lets the model see the hole explicitly, so it learns graph structure without waiting for neighborhoods to gossip about it.
