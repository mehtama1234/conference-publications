InnoEval: On Research Idea Evaluation as a Knowledge-Grounded, Multi-Perspective Reasoning Problem

Evaluate research ideas by gathering outside evidence and letting reviewers with different backgrounds score the idea on separate criteria before seeking consensus.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Generating research ideas has become easy for large language models. Judging which ideas are actually promising is harder, because good evaluation needs outside knowledge, multiple criteria, and disagreement between reviewers who notice different weaknesses. This paper builds an idea-evaluation pipeline that retrieves evidence from external sources and organizes several reviewer roles into a board that debates before producing a consensus judgment.

Zero assumption version

Why idea evaluation is harder than idea generation

A raw idea score from one language model is easy to produce but hard to trust. The model may know only a narrow slice of the relevant literature, may collapse all evaluation criteria into one vague impression, and may carry its own judging bias from the prompt or training data.

Human reviewers do not evaluate ideas this way. They check what is already known, inspect the proposed contribution from different disciplinary angles, and weigh several criteria separately such as originality, feasibility, and likely impact. The paper argues that an idea-evaluation system should imitate that structure, because a single ungrounded score hides too much of the actual reasoning.

The move. Retrieve external evidence, assign several reviewer perspectives, score the idea on separate criteria, and then form a consensus judgment from those grounded partial views.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Retrieve evidence.

The system gathers outside sources that bear on the proposed idea, such as related methods, benchmarks, or earlier findings.

Instantiate reviewer roles.

Several reviewer personas with different academic backgrounds are assigned so that each inspects the idea from a different angle.

Score separate criteria.

Each reviewer evaluates dimensions such as originality, feasibility, and likely value instead of collapsing everything into one immediate score.

Deliberate across perspectives.

The board compares disagreements and evidence support so that unsupported enthusiasm or unsupported skepticism is challenged.

Produce consensus.

A final judgment is issued only after the evidence-grounded reviewer scores have been reconciled.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes idea evaluation from a single-pass judgment into a structured decision process. The object is no longer one model's global score. It is a board-style evaluation made of external evidence, role-specific judgments, and explicit criteria.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: evidence-backed consensus quality

measured quantity  =  Evidence-backed consensus quality

The hidden quantity is how trustworthy the final judgment is after combining knowledge coverage, criterion coverage, and reviewer agreement. High agreement alone is not enough, because reviewers can all agree for the wrong reason. High evidence coverage alone is not enough, because the evidence must still be interpreted correctly across criteria.

InnoEval aims to raise this quantity by grounding each reviewer in retrieved evidence and by forcing the board to compare several criteria explicitly. That reduces single-judge bias because a reviewer who is too optimistic on one dimension can be corrected by another reviewer with different evidence or a different evaluative focus.

Why it works

Why it works

The mechanism works because each failure mode of naive judging is addressed by a corresponding structural component. Outside retrieval widens the knowledge base, so the evaluator is less likely to miss earlier related work or obvious feasibility obstacles. Multiple reviewer roles widen perspective, so one style of reasoning does not dominate every decision. Criterion-level scoring widens the decision surface, so a flashy idea cannot hide weak feasibility behind a strong originality impression.

Consensus comes last rather than first. That matters because early averaging often erases informative disagreement. By comparing evidence-backed partial judgments before merging them, the system can preserve useful reviewer differences long enough to identify what the idea is strong at, what it is weak at, and why the final decision should lean one way.

Math after the picture

The consensus score

First in words:

A final idea score can be written as a weighted sum of criterion scores, then adjusted by how well those scores are supported by retrieved evidence and reviewer agreement.

Then the compact form:

final_score = sum over criteria of weight(c) * score(c) * evidence_support(c) * agreement(c)

Every symbol:

weight(c)importance assigned to criterion c
score(c)board judgment on criterion c such as originality or feasibility
evidence_support(c)how strongly retrieved evidence supports that criterion judgment
agreement(c)how aligned the reviewers are on criterion c after deliberation
final_scoreoverall evaluation after combining all criteria
A tiny worked example
Suppose the idea has two criteria for a toy example: originality and feasibility.
Originality: weight 0.5, score 0.9, evidence support 0.8, agreement 0.9.
Contribution from originality = 0.5 * 0.9 * 0.8 * 0.9 = 0.324.
Feasibility: weight 0.5, score 0.6, evidence support 0.9, agreement 0.8.
Contribution from feasibility = 0.5 * 0.6 * 0.9 * 0.8 = 0.216.
Final score = 0.324 + 0.216 = 0.54.
The score is held back by feasibility because the board found weaker practical support there.

The exact aggregation can vary, but the key principle is to combine criterion value with evidence support instead of trusting an unsupported headline score.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Evidence retrieval misses key earlier work. If the search stage omits key papers or benchmarks, the board can still reach a polished but weak conclusion because it is reasoning from incomplete evidence.
Reviewer roles are too similar. If all reviewer personas reason the same way, the board adds little beyond repetition. Consensus then rises for the wrong reason because diversity of viewpoint is missing.
Consensus is forced too early. If disagreement is averaged away before the supporting evidence is inspected, the system loses the very signal that could have revealed an important weakness.
Done right. Use broad evidence retrieval, define genuinely different reviewer perspectives, and delay consensus until criterion-level disagreements have been examined with the supporting sources.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Assemble a small benchmark of research proposals with expert judgments. Compare three evaluators: one single-judge language model, one multi-reviewer board without outside retrieval, and one full system with retrieval plus criterion-level board review. Score them on pointwise grading, pairwise preference between ideas, and groupwise ranking across a proposal set.

The demo should show that the full board aligns better with experts because it grounds each criterion in retrieved evidence before asking the reviewers to agree.
