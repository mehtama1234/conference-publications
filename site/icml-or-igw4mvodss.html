AnomSeer: Reasoning over Time Series

Teach a vision-language model to explain what is normal and abnormal in time-series data by grounding its thinking in precise statistical details.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Multimodal large language models (models trained to read images and text at the same time) can answer questions about pictures, but they struggle when you ask them to find unusual patterns in charts or sensor readings. AnomSeer addresses this by forcing the model to base its explanations on precise mathematical measures—like how spread-out the numbers are, or what frequencies show up in the data—rather than loose, vague descriptions.

Zero assumption version

Vision-language models see the picture but miss the numbers

A chart shows temperature readings from a factory sensor over a month. Most days the line bounces between 70 and 75 degrees. Then one day it shoots up to 95. A model trained to describe images can see there is a spike, but it cannot easily say whether 95 is actually abnormal or just normal noise—it has to guess based on patterns it memorized, without doing real math. When the data has multiple dimensions (five sensors at once, or a weekly pattern buried in daily noise), this guesswork fails completely.

A human engineer, by contrast, would compute how far 95 sits from the average, check whether it violates the normal frequency spectrum, and then write down exactly what makes it anomalous, because doing math anchors the verdict to measurable facts rather than guesses. The model needs to learn to do that work too, not just eyeball the chart.

The paper's core move in one sentence. AnomSeer trains the model to always justify its anomaly verdict by computing and naming precise statistics—like mean, variance, and dominant frequencies—before declaring something abnormal.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Human expert traces

A human engineer analyzes each anomaly by hand, computing mean, standard deviation, frequency content. This trace becomes ground truth.

Step 2: Model learns the reasoning

The model reads the chart and the expert trace together, learning to output similar step-by-step explanations with real numbers.

Step 3: Reward signal from transport distance

A mathematical measure (optimal transport) grades how well the model's statistical explanation matches the expert one.

Step 4: Training adjustment

The model gets nudged to match the reward (reinforcement learning), but an orthogonal projection ensures the granular reward does not accidentally break the main detection accuracy.

Step 5: Final answer

The model outputs both a yes/no anomaly verdict and a chain of reasoning anchored in precise statistics.

What the paper changes

What changes: the reasoning trace

The paper changes what the model outputs. Instead of just saying 'this is anomalous' or 'this is normal', it now must produce a step-by-step written reasoning that includes computed statistics before the final label. This turns a black-box guess into a transparent chain of thought.

the object it moves  =  reasoning trace
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: statistical grounding

measured quantity  =  quality of the reasoning chain

A reasoning chain's quality is how closely it matches an expert's chain. An expert has computed the mean, the deviation from mean, maybe the frequency spectrum. If the model outputs similar statements with similar numbers, the expert and model are aligned. If the model makes up numbers or skips steps, they drift apart.

The paper estimates this alignment using optimal transport—a mathematical measure of how much 'cost' it would take to warp one statistical description into the other. Smaller cost means the model's reasoning is closer to the expert's reasoning.

Why it works

Why grounding in statistics makes the model reason better

When a model is trained only to say yes/no, it can hide whatever it wants inside its hidden layers—it might learn a shortcut that looks good on test data but breaks on new data. When you force it to spell out its math first, you create a bottleneck: it must compute statistics, and those numbers either match reality or they do not. If the model says 'mean is 73' and the actual mean is 50, the expert will catch it immediately.

The reinforcement learning part (reward based on transport distance) pushes the model toward expert-style reasoning, but reinforcement learning has its own pitfall: over-optimizing for the reward signal can degrade the main task (finding anomalies correctly). The paper's solution is orthogonal projection—a geometric trick that splits the push-toward-better-reasoning from the push-toward-better-detection, because when two objectives compete, forcing them to remain perpendicular ensures neither can steal resources from the other. This prevents the reward for good explanations from accidentally breaking the detector.

Math after the picture

The grounding formula and worked example

First in words:

The reward for good reasoning is the negative transport distance from the model's statistics to the expert's statistics, scaled by a factor. The total training loss combines the anomaly detection loss and this grounded reward, separated by orthogonal projection so they do not interfere.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_detect + lambda * proj_ortho(W_transport(stats_model, stats_expert))

Every symbol:

L_detecthow wrong the anomaly verdict is (classification or localization error)
L_totalthe combined training loss to minimize
lambdathe strength of the grounding reward
W_transportoptimal transport distance (Wasserstein) between two statistical descriptions
stats_modelthe statistics the model computed
stats_expertthe statistics the human expert computed
proj_orthoorthogonal projection that removes any component pointing toward the detection loss gradient
A tiny worked example
Sensor reads: [72, 73, 72, 74, 95]. Expert says: mean=73.2, std=8.9, threshold=90.
Model says: mean=72, std=9, threshold=90. Transport distance between (73.2,8.9) and (72,9) is 1.5.
Grounding reward = 0.1 * (-1.5) = -0.15 (smaller distance = bigger reward, so negative distance is positive reward).
Detection loss for the 95 verdict = 0.2. Total loss = 0.2 - 0.15 = 0.05 (the grounding helped reduce it).
Without projection, pushing to minimize loss might break detection accuracy; projection keeps the two objectives aligned.

The model learns to output statistics that match an expert's, without sacrificing its core job of correctly spotting anomalies.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Expert traces are themselves flawed. If the human engineer who traces the ground truth makes a systematic error, the model will learn it. The method is only as good as the expert traces it copies.
The transport distance metric misses important dimensions. Optimal transport measures closeness between sets of statistics. If the true reason for an anomaly is something the statistics do not capture (e.g., a rare event that has no historical precedent), the grounding reward will be blind to it.
Orthogonal projection is imperfect in high dimensions. Orthogonal projection works well when the detection and grounding signals are nearly uncorrelated. If the model absolutely cannot do both simultaneously (a hard trade-off), the projection may not resolve it cleanly.
Done right: careful validation of expert traces and testing on unseen anomalies. Before training, audit the expert's reasoning. After training, validate the model on held-out anomalies that the expert did not see, to ensure the model learned to reason, not merely copy.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time-series dataset with known anomalies—e.g., five temperature sensors over one month, with three manually-injected anomalies (a spike, a persistent shift, a frequency corruption). Train AnomSeer on half, validate on the other half. Collect two kinds of output: (1) the final anomaly verdict (yes/no), and (2) the reasoning chain (mean, standard deviation, frequency observed, threshold crossed). Compare the model's reasoning to a human's reasoning on the same data.

accuracy = (correct verdicts) / (total verdicts); reasoning alignment = transport distance between model chain and expert chain

The demo succeeds when the model's reasoning closely mirrors the expert's reasoning, not just because it guesses right, but because it computed the same statistics.
