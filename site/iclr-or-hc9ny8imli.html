SAGA: Multi-View Graph Domain Adaptation

When a graph model trained on one labeled dataset must work on a different unlabeled one, pick which view and hop distance matter most at each training step instead of treating all equally.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Suppose you have a graph (a network of nodes and connections, such as a social network or knowledge base) with multiple types of connections. You trained a model on a fully labeled version of that graph. Now you need that model to work on a different graph with the same connection types but different labels and node data. The challenge is that the two graphs do not have the same patterns — a connection type that matters a lot in one graph might matter less in the other. This paper says: instead of assuming all connection types and all hop distances are equally important, watch which ones actually matter most during training and focus there.

Zero assumption version

Multiple graph views with unknown importance

In the real world, graphs often have many types of edges. A social network has mentions, retweets, and direct messages. A knowledge base has entities linked by different relation types. When you have a fully labeled training graph, you can learn how much each edge type matters. But when you switch to a new graph with the same edge types but different data, the importance order changes. A relation that was critical in the training graph might be noise in the new one.

The naive approach: treat all edge types and all distance levels the same. Use the same weight for the mention-edge contribution as the retweet-edge contribution, even though the new graph's structure has shifted. The beginner assumes: if a method worked for all edges equally on the training graph, it will work the same way on the new graph. This breaks because the hidden patterns are different.

In one sentence. Pick which view and neighborhood distance matter most at each training step, letting the important ones shift dynamically.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure each pair

For each combination of edge type and hop distance, compute a mismatch score.

Find the worst mismatch

Identify which view-distance pair has the biggest discrepancy between the two graphs.

Align that pair

Steer training to fix that bottleneck first, ignoring stable pairs.

Shift focus as training progresses

As that pair improves, re-measure and move focus to the next worst one, because solving the largest problem first makes the remaining problems smaller and easier.

Converge on all mismatches

By handling one dominant mismatch at a time, all discrepancies shrink together.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes which view-distance pair gets the most training signal. Instead of weighting all edge types and all neighbor distances equally, it dynamically boosts the one that diverges most between the training graph and the new graph, because fixing the pair with the largest mismatch first reduces the total loss most efficiently. This is the knob: which connection type and which hop level to emphasize in the alignment loss at each step.

the object it moves  =  View-order weighting
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Structural Aggregation Distance

measured quantity  =  Structural Aggregation Distance (SAD)

Structural Aggregation Distance is a number that measures how much a single view-distance pair has shifted between the two graphs. A view is one edge type (like mention-edges). A distance (or hop) is how many steps away you look at neighbors — direct neighbors are hop 1, neighbors of neighbors are hop 2, and so on. SAD captures how differently a particular combination (say, blue-edges-at-hop-3) behaves in the training graph versus the new graph. The paper estimates SAD by comparing the local neighborhoods in both graphs: if the pattern of nodes reachable via blue-edges at exactly 3 steps differs wildly, SAD is high.

The paper uses SAD to rank which view-distance pair matters most. At each training step, the pair with the highest SAD becomes the focus: the model aligns on that pair first, pulling the others along. SAD evolves as training proceeds — once the top pair improves, a different pair may become the new bottleneck, because focusing on one pair at a time means earlier focus pulls the aligned structure around it.

Why it works

Why it works: the dynamic bottleneck fix

The core insight is that when two graphs differ, they do not differ uniformly. Some edge types are stable across the shift, others are not. Some neighborhood distances are reliable, others are not. By measuring Structural Aggregation Distance for all combinations, SAGA identifies which particular view-distance pair drives the largest mismatch. By focusing the alignment loss on that pair first, the model uses its limited training signal where it has the biggest gap to close. Once that pair is aligned, SAD re-measures, and a different view-distance pair may become the new bottleneck — but now the previously focused pair is stable enough to maintain its alignment. This greedy, dynamic approach avoids wasting effort on stable pairs and concentrates on the true barriers. As training progresses, the evolving SAD scores shift focus automatically, ensuring all pairs eventually align without explicit scheduling.

The mechanism is simple but effective: focus on the hardest problem first. In a graph, the hardest problem is the view-distance pair whose structure has drifted most. By identifying and fixing that first, you pull all other pairs along — they become easier targets as the foundational structure solidifies.

Math after the picture

The alignment equation

First in words:

The alignment loss sums over all view-distance pairs, but weights each pair by its Structural Aggregation Distance so the worst-mismatched pair gets pushed harder.

Then the compact form:

Loss = sum over (v,h) of SAD(v,h) * divergence(source_v_h, target_v_h)

Every symbol:

Losstotal training penalty
vthe view (edge type)
hthe hop distance (neighborhood depth)
SAD(v,h)Structural Aggregation Distance between graphs for that view-distance pair
divergence(source_v_h, target_v_h)mismatch between the patterns in the source graph and target graph at that view and hop
sum over (v,h)add up the weighted divergences for all view-distance combinations
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 2 views (red, blue) and 2 hops (1, 2).
Measured SAD values: red-hop1 = 0.8, red-hop2 = 0.3, blue-hop1 = 0.2, blue-hop2 = 0.9.
Divergence in source vs target: red-hop1 = 0.5, red-hop2 = 0.5, blue-hop1 = 0.5, blue-hop2 = 0.5.
Weighted penalties: (0.8 × 0.5) + (0.3 × 0.5) + (0.2 × 0.5) + (0.9 × 0.5) = 0.4 + 0.15 + 0.1 + 0.45 = 1.1.
Red-hop1 (SAD=0.8) contributed 0.4; blue-hop2 (SAD=0.9) contributed 0.45. These two dominated the loss, so training pushed hardest on them, even though all divergences were equal.

Pairs with high Structural Aggregation Distance get pushed harder during training (larger updates), so the model fixes the biggest mismatches first, which means the training budget is spent on the bottlenecks that matter most.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The dominant pair is wrong. If Structural Aggregation Distance is computed poorly or if the mismatch in the first hop is actually noise, SAGA focuses on the wrong pair early. Training then spends steps fixing a red herring.
Multiple pairs matter equally. If many view-distance pairs are similarly misaligned, SAGA's greedy focus on a single top pair may ignore important interactions. It fixes one bottleneck but leaves others unchanged, and they reinforce each other in ways the model cannot overcome.
The target graph is too different. If the target graph has fundamentally different structure (not just shifted edge weights but missing entire edge types or disconnected components), SAD may not detect the difference cleanly. The method assumes the same view-distance structure exists in both graphs.
Done right. SAGA succeeds when: the two graphs have the same structural vocabulary (same edge types and reachable hop distances), the dominant view-distance pair truly does drive the discrepancy, and the training budget is large enough to converge after refocusing. In typical domain adaptation settings this holds.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple graph with two edge types (say, social mentions and direct follows) at two hops. Create a source graph where mentions dominate at hop 1, and a target graph where follows dominate at hop 2. Train a node classifier with equal weighting on both views and both hops, and measure final accuracy on both source and target. Then train with SAGA: compute SAD, pick the dominant pair, weight it higher in the loss. Plot how SAD evolves (does the dominant pair shift?), plot training loss on each pair separately, and measure source and target accuracy again. The demo must show that SAGA's dynamic selection focuses on the right pair at the right time and achieves better balance between source and target than naive equal-weighting, because focusing training effort on the bottleneck pair leaves training bandwidth free for the others to align through the way their training nudges pull on each other.
