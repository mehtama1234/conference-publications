CiteGuard: Conformal False-Discovery Control for Faithful Retrieval-Augmented Generation

CiteGuard treats citation faithfulness as a statistical multiple-testing problem to keep only claims the evidence genuinely supports.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models now answer questions by looking up information from outside documents, a technique called retrieval-augmented generation. The problem: even with good retrievers, models still write claims that the documents do not actually support, and they cite evidence that does not back those claims. CiteGuard solves this by treating each claim as a statistical test—like a jury deciding guilt beyond a reasonable doubt—and rejects claims where the evidence is too weak, weaker than we can afford.

Zero assumption version

The citation crisis: falsely backed claims

When a language model generates text grounded in external documents, each claim needs evidence from those documents to be trusted. A naive approach retrieves documents and lets the model write freely—the result is statements that contradict the retrieved text or get citations that do not match the claim. The root problem: the model treats citations as decoration, not as verification. A beginner might try filtering obviously unsupported claims by hand, but that does not scale and misses subtle mismatches where the document says something close-but-different from the claim.

In one sentence. CiteGuard replaces binary pass-fail judgment with a statistical test—it measures how likely each claim would appear by accident in random evidence, rejects claims too-risky to trust, and keeps only those with proof beyond a statistical threshold.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Score each claim

For every sentence the model generates, measure how much the retrieved documents support it.

Convert to probability

Use conformal calibration (a method from statistics) to turn the support score into a probability that the claim is false.

Adjust for multiple testing

Because you are testing many claims at once, the risk of false alarms adds up—use the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to lower thresholds for claims that are clearly supported.

Accept or abstain

Keep claims with p-values below your chosen threshold, and abstain on weaker ones (return I don't know instead of a false citation).

User controls the risk

The threshold parameter lets practitioners choose: stricter thresholds mean fewer false claims but more abstentions; looser thresholds mean more claims but higher false-alarm risk.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

CiteGuard intercepts the model's output at the RAG decoding stage and filters claims before they are presented to the user. It does not retrain the model or change how retrieval works.

the object it moves  =  RAG output filtering
The hidden quantity

The false-discovery rate: how many false claims slip through

measured quantity  =  False-discovery rate

In any system that accepts or rejects many claims, some acceptances are wrong—those are the false discoveries. The false-discovery rate is the fraction of accepted claims that are actually wrong. In naive RAG, 28-31% of kept claims are unsupported; CiteGuard reduces this below 10%. This quantity is hidden because the system never knows ground truth for every claim in deployment, but statistical testing lets you put an upper bound on it before you deploy.

The paper estimates this rate by converting each claim-evidence score into a p-value via conformal calibration, then using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction—a procedure from statistics that controls how many false positives you will see when testing many hypotheses together.

Plain reading. Imagine you ask for 5 facts from a source. You get all 5, but one is made up. That is a 20% error rate. Instead, you ask for 5 facts but agree to settle for maybe only 3 if they are guaranteed right—now the system rejects 1 risky claim, gives you 4 solid ones, and 0% are wrong. That is false-discovery-rate control.
Paper reading. The paper computes the false-discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure: p-values are sorted, a cutoff line is drawn, and all hypotheses below the line are rejected.
Why it works

Why treating claims as statistical tests works

Every claim-evidence pair comes with a support score from the model. If that score were generated randomly by a model with no real evidence, how likely would we see a score that high or higher? Conformal calibration answers this by learning, from training data, the mapping from score to probability. Once you have a probability that the claim is wrong, you can set a threshold: reject any claim where the probability of being wrong exceeds your risk budget. But here is the trick: if you test 100 claims and use a threshold that rejects 5% of them by chance, you will reject 5 claims. Some will be genuinely unsupported, but some will be correct claims that got unlucky. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure lowers the threshold systematically so that, on average across all 100 tests, the false-discovery rate stays below your chosen target. This is the same logic used in medical trials when testing many drugs: you cannot just test each one independently, or you will declare too many drugs effective by random noise.

The mechanism works because it treats the decoding step as a gate: the model generates claims freely, but only claims with statistical evidence of being true pass through. This separates two jobs—generation and verification—instead of asking the model to self-censor while generating.

Math after the picture

The false-discovery-rate control equation

First in words:

For each claim, compute a p-value measuring how likely its evidence support score would occur by accident. Sort p-values. For false-discovery-rate target alpha, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure sets a cutoff: reject claims with p-value below (k/m) * alpha, where k is the rank of the p-value and m is the total number of claims.

Then the compact form:

Reject if p_k < (k / m) * alpha; otherwise accept. Guarantees: E[false discoveries / total discoveries] <= alpha.

Every symbol:

p_kthe probability that claim k is wrong (from conformal calibration)
kthe rank of this claim among all sorted p-values (1 = most likely false)
mtotal number of claims tested
alphauser-chosen false-discovery-rate target, typically 0.05 or 0.10
A tiny worked example
Suppose 5 claims, alpha = 0.10.
Sort p-values: 0.01, 0.04, 0.08, 0.15, 0.30.
Compute thresholds: k=1 threshold = (1/5)*0.10 = 0.02; k=2 threshold = (2/5)*0.10 = 0.04; k=3 threshold = (3/5)*0.10 = 0.06; k=4 threshold = (4/5)*0.10 = 0.08; k=5 threshold = (5/5)*0.10 = 0.10.
Reject if p < threshold: claim 1 (0.01 < 0.02, reject); claim 2 (0.04 = 0.04, borderline, but BH says keep); claim 3 (0.08 > 0.06, keep); claim 4 (0.15 > 0.08, keep); claim 5 (0.30 > 0.10, keep).
Result: abstain on claim 1, keep 4 claims. Expected false discoveries: at most 0.10 * 4 = 0.4, or roughly 10% of the 4 you kept.

The threshold increases for claims with higher p-values because they are more likely to be true, so you use a stricter bar only for the risky ones.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Conformal calibration assumes exchangeability. If test claims differ systematically from training claims (for example, training on open-domain questions, testing on domain-specific), the p-values will be miscalibrated and guarantees fail. The paper assumes that the pattern of claims does not change between training and deployment.
Evidence score must be reliable. If the underlying model's claim-evidence score is nonsense, no amount of statistical filtering will help, because CiteGuard cannot distinguish true from false claims without a reliable score to work from. If the score is random noise, the p-values are uniformly scattered—some true claims get high p-values (look false), some false claims get low p-values (look true)—and no threshold can separate them. Weak retrievers or broken evidence scorers make the problem unfixable.
Abstention reduces coverage. As alpha gets stricter (lower false-discovery rate), more claims are rejected, and the system says I don't know more often. You must trade off honesty for coverage.
Done right. With a well-trained evidence scorer and held-out calibration data, CiteGuard provides finite-sample guarantees: you choose alpha up front, deploy, and are mathematically assured the false-discovery rate stays below your bound.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small retrieval-augmented system: take 50 questions, retrieve top-3 documents for each, generate claims with citations, score each claim against its documents, and apply CiteGuard with a false-discovery-rate target of 0.10. Measure the false-discovery rate (fraction of accepted claims that are actually unsupported) and coverage (fraction of generated claims that were kept).

False-discovery rate = unsupported claims accepted / total claims accepted. Coverage = claims accepted / total claims generated.

The demo must show that setting the false-discovery-rate target to 0.10 keeps at least 80 percent of supported claims while rejecting enough unsupported claims that the false-discovery rate drops below 10 percent of what it was without filtering.
