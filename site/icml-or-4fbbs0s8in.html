Zero assumption version

Standard physics simulators miss the quirks of real materials

A physics simulator tells a robot how a cloth or foam will move when pushed. It assumes materials are homogeneous (the same throughout) and isotropic (the same in every direction). A real rubber band does not work that way — it stretches differently along its length than across its width.

If you start with a good isotropic model, the remaining error comes from these directional quirks. A naive approach would throw away the physics model and train a neural network to predict raw motion from scratch. But neural networks then waste data learning obvious physics laws instead of learning the weird residual effects, and they overfit to the training videos.

The method in one sentence. Keep the standard isotropic simulator, but train a small neural network to learn and add the missing directional stress corrections.

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the stress operator — the mapping from how much a material has stretched (strain) to how much it pushes back (stress). Normally this is fixed by physics. Here, the paper learns small corrections to it that capture real-world anisotropy.

The hidden quantity

Residual anisotropy: how directional is the material

measured quantity  =  residual anisotropy

Anisotropy means a material behaves differently in different directions. A perfect isotropic material has zero anisotropy. Real materials have small but measurable anisotropy — a rubber band is stiffer along its length than across.

The paper measures this by looking at what the standard isotropic model got wrong. After training, the learned residual stress operators encode exactly this anisotropy. The paper validates that the model truly learned the material's directional properties by checking whether the learned corrections transfer to new materials and by verifying the corrections match expected physics patterns.

Why it works

Why learning residual stress fixes the real-to-sim gap

A standard physics model assumes isotropy because it is mathematically simple. Most of the real behavior is isotropic, so this assumption is almost right. The gap comes from the small directional and spatial quirks. Trying to fit all of this from scratch with a neural network wastes data and overfits because the model has to re-learn all the isotropic physics from videos.

Instead, the paper keeps the isotropic model as a scaffold. Small neural networks then learn ONLY the corrections on top. This is much more efficient because the heavy lifting (isotropy) is already done. The motion constraints (supervising the deformation field's time and space derivatives) push the corrections toward physically realistic patterns instead of memorizing rendering accidents. The cascade structure (applying corrections step-by-step) forces the model to decompose the anisotropy and heterogeneity into interpretable pieces, so the learned corrections actually represent real material properties instead of noise.

Math after the picture

Equation: corrected stress from isotropic base

First in words:

Final stress equals: isotropic stress, then apply a learned pre-correction, then redistribute across directions (microplane), then apply a learned post-correction and rescaling. Optionally add a learned heterogeneity term.

Then the compact form:

σ_learned = C^post * (L^post * (Redistribute(L^pre * σ_iso)) + H_residual

Every symbol:

σ_isothe stress from the standard isotropic model
L^prelearned pre-correction network (adjusts magnitude before redistribution)
Redistributemicroplane-based redistribution (changes how stress points in different directions)
L^postlearned post-correction network (fine-tunes after redistribution)
C^postlearned scaling factors (how much each direction's correction matters)
H_residuallearned heterogeneity term (spatial non-uniformity within the material)
σ_learnedthe final stress with residual anisotropy accounted for
A tiny worked example
Say isotropic model predicts stress = 50 (in some direction).
Pre-correction learns: multiply by 0.8 -> 40.
Microplane redistribution rotates stress ellipse: some directions get +10, others get -5.
One direction now: 40 + 10 = 50; another: 40 - 5 = 35 (anisotropic).
Post-correction learns to scale by 1.1 and add 2 -> first direction 57, second 40.5.
Heterogeneity adds local spatial variation: +3 in this zone -> first direction 60, second 43.5.
Result: isotropic base corrected with learned directional quirks.

The cascade of corrections (pre, redistribute, post, heterogeneity) decomposes the residual error into interpretable pieces so each part learns real material properties.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too much freedom in the correction networks. If the neural networks are too large, they can overfit to the training video instead of learning real material properties. Motion constraints prevent this because they directly supervise how the material moves through time and space — rendering artifacts do not obey real motion laws, so enforcing physics on the motion derivatives rules out overfitting solutions. The cascade structure prevents overfitting because it forces the corrections to decompose into stages, so memorizing one piece breaks the whole pipeline unless that piece corresponds to real physical behavior.
Material changes between training and deployment. If you learn residual stress on rubber but then deploy on leather, the learned corrections do not transfer. The paper validates transfer by testing on different materials and shows good generalization when the materials are similar.
Bad motion constraints. If the deformation video is blurry or low-res, the motion constraint supervision (tracking spatial and temporal derivatives) becomes noisy and the model learns spurious corrections.
Done right. With proper supervision from high-quality video, a good starting isotropic physics model, and motion constraints, the learned residuals capture genuine material anisotropy and transfer to new scenarios.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulator that learns dynamics from video. First, train it on 20 frames of cloth or foam deforming. Then predict the next 10 frames without seeing them. Measure how closely the predictions match the actual video (pixel-by-pixel similarity). Show that the isotropic model makes smooth but directionally wrong predictions, and that MoSA's learned residuals fix the directional errors. Finally, take the learned residuals from one material and try applying them to a different material — show that they do not blindly transfer, proving the model learned real properties, not rendering artifacts.

prediction_accuracy = average pixel similarity over 10 unseen frames; stability = how much prediction error grows from frame 5 to frame 10

The demo must show that isotropic-plus-residuals outperforms pure isotropic on directional accuracy because the residuals capture real anisotropy that isotropy misses, and outperforms pure neural networks on generalization because learning only the residuals (not re-deriving isotropy from scratch) forces the network to find real material properties instead of memorizing training patterns.
