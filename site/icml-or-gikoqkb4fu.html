LAST: Bridging Vision-Language and Action via Lie-Algebraic Alignment

To align vision-language embeddings with robotic actions, remap actions into a flat additive space that matches how language embeddings are spread.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot learning from images and text requires aligning two incompatible spaces. Language embeddings are built to be isotropic (points spread uniformly in all directions), while robot trajectories are anisotropic (motion clusters along constrained directions). This paper introduces LAST, which remaps actions into a Lie-algebraic tangent space — a flat plane where motion becomes additive — to match how language spreads its points, enabling discrete-symbol alignment.

Zero assumption version

Two incompatible spaces that cannot align directly

A vision-language model (a system that understands images and text) represents concepts as vectors (lists of numbers). These vectors are trained to sit on a hypersphere (an N-dimensional ball surface), where all directions matter equally — each dimension carries roughly equal weight.

Robot motion trajectories live in a very different space. The laws of physics and mechanics constrain where actions can go. Valid movements cluster tightly along certain dimensions (like arm joints moving together in coordinated ways) and ignore others. This means true neighbors of a motion pattern cluster along the strong directions, not spread evenly. Some directions are much more important than others.

When you try to mix these two spaces naively, nearest-neighbor retrieval fails: the system finds false neighbors along the weak directions of motion while missing true neighbors along the strong directions. Worse, linear mixing of rotations creates physically invalid poses — mixing two valid rotations with standard averaging can produce a rotation outside the feasible set of valid poses.

The naive approach breaks because isotropic and anisotropic geometries cannot align directly. LAST remaps actions into a flat, balanced tangent space, then converts them into discrete symbols (like words in language) that align with language embeddings.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Remap actions to tangent space

Convert variable-length robot trajectories into fixed-length representations in the Lie-algebra tangent space of SE(3), making motion residuals additive instead of curved.

Stage 2: Apply B-spline basis

Represent trajectories as a small set of control points using B-splines, compressing motion into a fixed-length form.

Stage 3: Whiten residuals

Apply covariance-aware whitening: rescale each dimension by the inverse square root of its variance, removing anisotropy.

Stage 4: Quantize into tokens

Hierarchically cluster the whitened residuals into discrete action tokens using vector quantization, creating a vocabulary like language models use.

Stage 5: Train dual-head policy

Use a single language encoder to drive both a discrete token-prediction head and a continuous diffusion refinement head, aligning semantic and action spaces end-to-end.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

LAST changes how continuous robot trajectories are represented for learning. Instead of trying to mix them directly with language embeddings in their original curved, imbalanced space, LAST first remaps them into a flat, balanced tangent space, then tokenizes them (converts them into discrete symbols, like words in language).

The hidden quantity

The anisotropy problem and how it is measured

measured quantity  =  Directional balance (anisotropy ratio)

The hidden quantity is how uniformly important each direction is in the action space. In a balanced space, all directions have equal spread. In an imbalanced space, a few directions dominate while others are nearly zero. This imbalance breaks nearest-neighbor retrieval and causes training instability.

The paper measures imbalance by looking at the covariance matrix (a table showing how much each dimension varies) of motion patterns. It computes the ratio of largest spread to smallest spread across dimensions. A high ratio means the space is severely imbalanced. By whitening (rescaling each dimension by the inverse of how much it varies), the paper forces all directions to have equal spread, achieving balance.

Why it works

Why the method works: from curved space to flat alignment

The core insight is that rotations in 3D space are curved — they do not form a flat, additive space. When you try to average or linearly mix two rotations in standard (Euclidean) space, the result can fall outside the valid set of possible rotations. This breaks token-based learning, because tokenization assumes you can move smoothly between nearby points.

LAST solves this by mapping rotations to their tangent space (the flat plane tangent to the rotation surface at the starting point), where motion becomes additive and flat. In this tangent space, a small motion from the starting point is representable as a simple vector. The paper uses B-splines (smooth curves controlled by a small set of points) to represent a trajectory as a fixed sequence of control points in this space, turning variable-length motion into a fixed-length representation.

Next, LAST applies whitening: it computes how much each dimension varies across many training trajectories, then rescales each dimension by the inverse of that spread. This removes imbalance — all dimensions now have equal importance, matching how language embeddings spread their points evenly.

Finally, LAST quantizes the whitened vectors into discrete tokens using vector quantization (grouping similar vectors into clusters), exactly like language tokenization. Now both vision-language and action spaces have balanced, discrete structure. A vision-language encoder can drive both: a discrete head predicts action tokens for immediate execution, and a continuous diffusion head refines the token into a smooth action trajectory. Both heads share the same language-encoded context, aligning the two domains.

The whitening and quantization equation

First in words:

Whitened representation equals (control-point vector minus mean) times (inverse square root of covariance). Quantization rounds to the nearest token in the codebook.

Then the compact form:

z_whitened = (z - mu) * Sigma^{-1/2} ; z_token = argmin_k ||z_whitened - c_k||^2

Every symbol:

zthe B-spline control point vector (fixed-length representation of a trajectory)
mumean control point across the training dataset
Sigmacovariance matrix of control points (how each dimension varies)
Sigma^{-1/2}inverse square root of covariance (rescales dimensions to equal variance)
c_kthe k-th codebook vector (a learned prototype token)
z_whitenedthe rescaled representation with equal variance in all directions
z_tokenthe nearest codebook vector (the discrete token assigned to this trajectory)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a trajectory has control points [1.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.5] in tangent space.
Mean control point (across 100 trajectories): mu = [1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0].
Spread per dimension: [0.04, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01] (dimension 1 varies 4x more than the others).
Whitening rescaling: inverse square root is [1/0.2, 1/0.1, 1/0.1, 1/0.1] = [5, 10, 10, 10].
Center the trajectory: [1.2-1.0, 0.4-0.0, 0.3-0.0, 0.5-0.0] = [0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.5].
Apply whitening: [0.2*5, 0.4*10, 0.3*10, 0.5*10] = [1.0, 4.0, 3.0, 5.0].
Now all dimensions have equal spread. Dimension 1 is no longer 4x noisier.
Tokenization finds the nearest prototype. With balanced directions, search finds true neighbors, not false ones.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Insufficient whitening. If some dimensions have zero spread in training data, whitening becomes ill-conditioned and amplifies noise in those directions. The paper avoids this by using a hierarchical tokenizer that learns codebooks that are stable even with sparse data.
Codebook collapse. If the commitment loss (which steers whitened vectors toward token prototypes) is too weak, all trajectories might collapse to a single token. If too strong, discrete tokens become rigid and cannot represent motion variety. The paper balances this with lambda_1 set to 0.5, meaning the two loss terms get equal weight.
Action statistics shift at test time. If test tasks have very different action patterns than training (e.g., a robot learning to manipulate heavier objects), the whitening rescaling computed from training no longer matches test actions, breaking alignment. The model assumes test actions stay close to training patterns.
Done right. The method works when whitening is stable, codebook training balances reconstruction and token selection, and test-time actions follow similar patterns as training. The paper achieves 96.1% success on spatial manipulation and 86% on long-horizon tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo should show that whitening reshapes action space from anisotropic to isotropic. Start with a set of real robot trajectories (e.g., 100 pick-and-place moves). Compute the covariance matrix before and after whitening. Measure the ratio of the largest variance to the smallest. Without whitening, this ratio should be large (e.g., 10:1 or more); after whitening, it should be close to 1:1. Then, sample a random language embedding and find its nearest neighbor in the raw action space versus the whitened action space. Measure retrieval accuracy on a held-out task.

anisotropy_ratio_before = max(diag(Sigma)) / min(diag(Sigma)) ; anisotropy_ratio_after = 1.0 (all dimensions whitened to unit variance) ; neighbor_accuracy_after = frac of correct k-nearest-neighbors recovered

The demo must show that whitening balances all directions, making true motion neighbors equally findable in all dimensions — which is why matching with language embeddings now works.
