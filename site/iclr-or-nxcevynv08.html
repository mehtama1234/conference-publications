Jumbo Token: Making Vision Transformers Fast and General

Add one extra-wide token to the transformer, process it with a shared feed-forward layer, and get faster models that still work with all the same ViT tricks.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

A vision transformer (a model that reads an image as patches and compares them) is slow because it processes many tiny pieces through many layers. Existing speed-ups shrink patches or swap in specialized layers, but then lose compatibility with popular training methods. This paper keeps the standard transformer structure, adds one oversized piece that acts like a global workspace, and processes it efficiently with a shared bottleneck.

Zero assumption version

Vision models are slow; shortcuts break compatibility

A plain vision transformer reads an image as about 200 small patches, each a compressed piece of data. The model then feeds all 200 pieces through many layers (24 for large models), and every layer does attention (each piece looks at every other piece to find what matters). The cost grows quadratically: double the patches, and you quadruple the attention comparisons in every layer.

Researchers have tried two paths: (1) shrink the pieces to fewer numbers—this makes the model faster because it has less to compare, but the smaller pieces lose detail and the model becomes less accurate. (2) Drop some layers or add specialized operations like local convolutions (pixel-level filters that see a small neighborhood, not the whole image) or collapse the pieces into pyramids. This second approach also gets faster but breaks: models like EfficientViT add these specialized operations and pyramids, which confuse modern training tricks like masked autoencoders (a self-supervised learning method where you hide parts of an image and predict them) because the training code expects a flat structure. They also cannot handle video or time series as easily. They no longer work with the whole ecosystem of tools and methods that have been built for plain vision transformers.

The dilemma: Make vision models fast without losing their simple structure, their ability to handle any input shape, and their compatibility with all the modern training tricks.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Split the Jumbo into heads

One oversized piece becomes J separate pieces for multi-headed attention.

Process patches normally

Regular patch pieces go through standard self-attention, comparing to each other and to Jumbo.

Merge Jumbo back together

After attention, the J Jumbo pieces are concatenated into one wide piece again.

Run through shared bottleneck

The merged Jumbo passes through a single feed-forward layer (a bottleneck), not a full transformer block.

Reuse that bottleneck across all layers

The same feed-forward weights are used for Jumbo in layer 2, layer 3, etc., saving memory.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a new Jumbo piece alongside the regular patch pieces. This piece is deliberately wider than the patch pieces—it holds J times as many numbers. Regular pieces stay small; the Jumbo is thick and gathers a compressed global view of the image. The method does not shrink patches, does not add local convolutions (pixel-level filters), does not rearrange pieces into pyramids—it simply adds one more piece with special processing.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Global context bottleneck capacity

measured quantity  =  How much information Jumbo can carry

The Jumbo piece acts like a bottleneck—a narrow passage through which global information must flow. Its width (measured in numbers inside the piece) determines how much context it can hold. A thin Jumbo loses information; a thick Jumbo costs computation. The paper measures this by building models with different widths and checking which ones give the best accuracy and speed.

The method estimates the right Jumbo width by comparing models with different widths (ranging from 1 to 12 times as wide as a patch piece) and measuring both accuracy and wall-clock time. The paper finds that moderate widths (around 4 to 8 times as wide) give the sweet spot.

Why it works

Why it works: compressed global context without quadratic cost

The core idea is bottleneck compression. With 200 patch pieces, the model creates a 200-by-200 interaction map (each piece looks at each other piece), and every one of the 24 layers repeats this. Adding a Jumbo piece does not cost much because the Jumbo is processed by its own dedicated, lightweight feed-forward layer, not by the full transformer logic. This feed-forward layer sees only one input piece (the merged Jumbo) and outputs one piece, so its cost does not grow when you add more patch pieces.

The Jumbo acts as a global workspace: patches write into it via attention (all patches can see the Jumbo and each other), and the Jumbo can read and summarize via its feed-forward bottleneck. This is faster than having all 200 patches compare to all 200 patches in every layer, because the Jumbo feed-forward is much cheaper—it processes one piece instead of 200. The paper also reuses the Jumbo feed-forward weights across all 24 layers, so a single learned bottleneck handles global context throughout the network, which saves memory because you do not have to store 24 separate copies of those weights.

Importantly, the architecture stays simple and flat—no pyramids, no convolutions. The Jumbo is just another piece in the transformer, not a special downsampling layer. This means all existing training tricks (masked autoencoding, which hides parts of an image and predicts them; rotation, which applies learned transformations; DINOv2 pre-training, which learns without labels) work out of the box.

The equation

First in words:

For each layer, the Jumbo token gets split into J tokens for attention, then merged and processed by a feed-forward bottleneck. The feed-forward is applied to the one merged token and reused across all layers.

Then the compact form:

A tiny worked example
Suppose patch width D=64 numbers per patch, and Jumbo width J*D=4*64=256 numbers.
Patch attention costs about O(N^2 * D) where N=200: about 2.56 million operations per layer.
Jumbo feed-forward costs O(D^2) on one token: about 4096 operations, a tiny fraction.
Sharing the feed-forward across 24 layers saves 23 sets of parameters (23*256*256 fewer numbers).
Net result: 10-15% faster wall-clock time than plain ViT, same accuracy or better.

The Jumbo token splits for attention but is processed by a single lightweight feed-forward, cutting both computation and memory.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Jumbo width too small. If J is too small, the Jumbo cannot hold enough global context. The model becomes starved of high-level information and accuracy drops below the baseline.
Jumbo width too large. If J is too large, the feed-forward bottleneck becomes expensive, and you lose the speed advantage. The trade-off curve flattens.
Misaligned layer sharing. If the shared feed-forward is poorly initialized or has different norms across layers, the Jumbo signal can degrade as it flows deeper. Careful layer normalization is needed.
Done right. Tune J for your target latency, initialize the shared feed-forward carefully, and the Jumbo stays compatible with masked autoencoders, fine-tuning, and time-series tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small plain ViT on CIFAR-10 (1000 images, 10 classes). Train one baseline ViT and one ViT+Jumbo for 100 epochs. Measure top-1 accuracy and wall-clock time per epoch. Add Jumbo with width 4 times the patch width. Measure latency with a timing profiler (not just accuracy).

Speedup = (plain ViT time per epoch) divided by (ViT+Jumbo time per epoch); Accuracy delta = (ViT+Jumbo accuracy) minus (baseline accuracy)

The demo must show that ViT+Jumbo trains faster (1.1x to 1.3x speedup) and matches or beats baseline accuracy. This proves the bottleneck does not starve the model of global information, because if the Jumbo were too thin, the model would lose accuracy.
