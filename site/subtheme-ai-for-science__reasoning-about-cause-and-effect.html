Reasoning about cause and effect

Helping AI understand not just correlation but causation—which gene or drug actually causes a disease—through causal inference and interpretable reasoning.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A person gets sick. A genome-wide scan finds a thousand genes that correlate with the disease. Which gene actually causes it? A treatment works in mice but fails in humans. Was it something unobserved that was different between the two? A study finds that people who take vitamins live longer. Is it the vitamin, or are they healthier for other reasons? These are questions about causation — what actually causes what — and they are notoriously hard to answer from data alone.

The challenge is that correlation is easy but causation is invisible. Two things can correlate because one causes the other, because the other causes the first, because both are caused by something hidden, or by pure accident. Machine learning usually finds correlations — patterns that predict one thing from another. It does not naturally distinguish between 'A causes B' and 'C causes both A and B.' In medicine and biology, this is critical: if you make a treatment based on a correlation that is actually due to confounding, it might be useless or harmful.

This cluster uses causal inference — techniques that try to untangle causation from correlation. Some use the structure of known causal relationships (a gene causes a protein, a protein causes a disease, but not the other way), then test whether that structure fits the data. Others use experimental tricks: if we can intervene on one variable (give people a medicine, edit a gene) and see what changes, that teaches us causation directly. Still others use reasoning about counterfactuals: what would happen if we changed this one thing and nothing else? Papers here combine machine learning with causal reasoning, building models that explicitly track what causes what, so they can predict the effect of an intervention, not just correlation with past data.

How the work breaks down

Understanding causation in science breaks into five directions because different domains ask different causal questions: discovering the actual equations that govern a system (what rule produced this data?), figuring out which medical treatments actually help specific patients (which drug helps this person?), predicting how molecules will behave (does this structure make this property?), reading signals directly from the brain (which neural activity causes that behavior?), and understanding which proteins interact and why (which amino acids cause binding?).

Discovering equations from data

Scientists often know some data but not the underlying equation. A biologist measures how cells behave under different conditions and wants to write down the rule. A physicist has sensor readings from an experiment and needs the formula. The challenge is that there are infinitely many possible equations, and most don't work. Traditional methods require guessing the form first. These papers train machine learning to search through millions of candidate equations and find the simplest one that actually fits the observations.

EGG-SR uses a graph representation to avoid exploring the same equation twice, discovering more accurate formulas faster. Breaking the Simplification Bottleneck improves amortized symbolic regression by learning to simplify intermediate results, recovering expressions that are both accurate and concise. DecAEvolve decomposes equations into parts, adapts each part separately, then evolves the whole thing to reduce errors by up to ten times. Discovering Ordinary Differential Equations uses language models to evaluate candidate equations both logically and numerically, ranking the ones most likely to match the data. Neural-Evolutionary Symbolic Regression with Global Constraints ensures that discovered equations obey physical laws (conservation of energy, symmetry) by shaping the search reward. SR-Scientist treats equation discovery like a research agent that picks which experiments to run, selects equations to test, and refines them—outperforming single-shot methods across biology, physics, and engineering. These six papers compete on speed and accuracy but all aim at the same goal: converting data into human-readable equations that scientists can understand and trust.

Personalized treatment effect prediction

A doctor has a treatment and needs to know: will it help this particular patient, because treatment response differs across people? A drug that works for one person might fail for another due to genetics, age, or other medications, so population averages are useless for individuals. And the doctor can only observe what happened in one timeline—they cannot rewind time and see what would have happened if the patient had taken a different drug, which creates the counterfactual gap: how do you infer what would happen under a treatment you never actually tried?

DeepBlip is the first neural network framework for sequential nested mean models, handling treatments that change over time and learning unbiased estimates even if intermediate models are wrong. Foundation Models for Causal Inference recipes foundation models (pre-trained on real data) for causal inference by freezing their weights and fitting only a small adapter, achieving strong results on personalized treatment effect benchmarks. IGC-Net for conditional average potential outcome estimation handles the case where treatments shift over time (like medication adjustment in an ICU) by using regression-based G-computation, moving toward medical decisions tailored to each patient's history. All three papers solve the same problem differently: given what happened to many patients under different treatments, infer the most likely outcome for one patient if you tried something new.

Molecular properties and design

Chemists need to know: how will this molecule behave? Does it dissolve in water or oil? Will it fold correctly as a protein? Will it fit into a receptor pocket and activate it? Traditional methods require building or synthesizing the molecule first. Neural networks can predict these properties from the molecular structure alone, but they often fail on molecules they have never seen before, especially when slight changes in structure matter drastically.

DISSOLVR predicts aqueous and organic solubility by learning structural invariance—capturing which parts of the molecule always matter the same way, even in new contexts—achieving the theoretical limits of experimental uncertainty without needing opaque architectures. CalPro builds confidence intervals around protein structure predictions by using evidential methods and structure-aware sensitivity bounds, improving downstream docking success from 52% to 75%, which matters because false predictions waste weeks of lab work. GenUnfold models protein mechanical unfolding—how a protein stretches and breaks under force—by guiding a diffusion model with physics principles, reducing error by 30-54% compared to earlier methods. Each paper tackles a different molecular question but all share one strategy: encode the physical invariants (atoms and their bonds) so that the learned pattern stays the same when applied to unseen molecules, because the underlying chemistry does not change.

Reading causation from brain signals

The brain produces data: electrical activity in the cortex, blood flow patterns, spiking neurons. Researchers measure these signals and ask: which regions caused this behavior? Which neurons control that thought? The signals are noisy, the brain is hugely complex, and most methods hide which parts mattered by using opaque black boxes.

GARLIC uses a graph neural network that predicts patient outcomes from ICU time series by showing exactly which measurements matter for which prediction (using feature-removal trials to verify importance), working across three large medical datasets while staying interpretable. The second paper reconstructs what a person sees from their fMRI brain activity (neural signal patterns from their cortex) by tracking how each brain region influences the visual output, providing transparent accountability for which brain areas contribute. Both papers combine neural prediction with clear tracing of which inputs caused which outputs, solving the causation problem by making the reasoning visible.

Protein interactions and sequences

Proteins find each other in cells and bind together—a T-cell receptor docking with a viral peptide, a transcription factor latching onto DNA. Understanding these interactions tells you how immune systems work, why a mutation causes disease, and how to design new drugs. Sequences and structures give clues, but predicting which pairs actually bind is hard, and predicting which amino acids matter most is even harder.

The TCR-pMHC Binding paper estimates which amino acid residues actually cause the binding with high accuracy, grounding the importance scores in physical interaction regions rather than hidden neural weights. Extending Sequence Length is Not All You Need shows that combining DNA sequence with epigenomic signals (chemical tags on the DNA) beats just reading longer sequences, because causation requires seeing both the code and the chemical context. PPI Candidate Ranking evaluates a pipeline that uses domain knowledge to rank protein-protein interactions, achieving large improvements over black-box methods on a massive real dataset from STRING. All three papers show that predicting molecular causation requires encoding not just sequence but structure, chemical context, and actual physical interactions.
