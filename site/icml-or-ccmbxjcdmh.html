PhoStream: When Should an AI Assistant Speak?

A benchmark shows that multimodal AI models know what to say about phone videos, but crash at deciding when to speak—especially when guessing the future.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Your phone plays a video. A smart assistant watches live alongside you, and must speak up at just the right moment. But today's models excel at answering questions looking backward at what already happened, and flatline when forced to predict what is coming next. This paper introduces a real-world streaming test that catches this weakness, revealing a gap that no benchmark had measured before.

Zero assumption version

The phone plays a live stream; your assistant watches

A multimodal model (one that sees video and hears audio at the same time) is good at offline tasks: you show it a video, ask a question, and it answers. But real mobile assistants must work live, watching a stream tick forward moment by moment. When should it interrupt? If you ask 'what is the user tapping?', the assistant must wait until the tap appears on screen. If you ask 'will the next button be red?', it must guess the future. Today's benchmarks are all offline—they give the model the whole video, then quiz it. None measure what happens when the model must act in real time.

A beginner might think: 'Just send the model what I have seen so far, and let it answer whenever it is ready.' That breaks because the model does not know where the video is going. With the full video available offline, it is easy to answer. In a live stream, the model cheats: it answers too early, before the required visual evidence appears on screen.

The core problem. Models excel at offline multimodal understanding, but streaming evaluation—where the model answers live without seeing the future—has never been measured at scale.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect real mobile videos

Gather phone use videos (app tutorials, live streams, first-person footage) from YouTube and custom recording.

Resample and split for streaming

Process video at 2 frames per second and split into small segments so streaming evaluation does not leak future frames.

Auto-generate questions with a cutoff timestamp

Use a large language model to write questions, marking the moment in the video when the question is asked—no peeking ahead.

Send each query once at the right time

Query the model only once, at the question's timestamp, with only the video seen so far. Model must decide if and when to reply.

Judge answers by comparing to a reference

A language model grades each answer (0 to 5 points) by comparing it to a human-written reference, rewarding well-timed, precise, assistant-style responses.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the evaluation setup: instead of giving the model a complete video offline, it forces the model to watch a live stream and decides when to speak, with no access to the future. This single shift reveals a hidden weakness.

the object it moves  =  Evaluation timing (offline to live)
The hidden quantity

The temporal reasoning asymmetry

measured quantity  =  Response timing error

The paper measures a quantity the field had not tracked: how well does a model know when to speak? It is not about the answer text. It is about the decision to reply at all, and the moment chosen. A response is scored 0 if it comes too early (before the visual cue appears) or too late (the model refuses to speak). Only responses timed right, with correct content, score high.

The paper estimates this by testing three temporal modes: Backward (answer about a past event), Instant (answer about the current moment), and Forward (predict the immediate future). The model's score collapses from Instant to Forward, exposing a stark asymmetry. Models that have seen the answer in the video (Backward) or are looking at the evidence now (Instant) perform well above 80 out of 100. When forced to predict what comes next, scores plummet to 16. This asymmetry is the hidden quantity—it measures not what the model knows, but when the model is brave enough to commit to an answer.

Paper reading. The paper defines a response window for each question type: Backward questions have an infinite window (the answer is already onscreen); Instant questions must be answered within the current frame; Forward questions have a narrow window (the model must wait for the cue to arrive and respond within a tight bound). Scores collapse outside these windows.
Why it works

Why the timing gap appears

Here is the mechanical reason: when you train a model on offline video understanding, it learns to predict by ingesting the entire video at once. The gradient (the learning signal) points the model toward patterns that are true for the whole video. In a live stream, the model receives video chunks over time. It must decide at each frame: should I answer now? The model was never trained to say 'not yet'—it was trained only on complete videos. So it either guesses too early or waits too long, unable to calibrate.

For Backward and Instant tasks, the model can be confident: the visual evidence is already in its input. For Forward tasks, the model has two bad choices: (a) gamble and answer early, risking that the proof has not yet appeared (it will be marked wrong), or (b) wait in silence, hoping the right moment will come (also marked wrong if it stays silent too long). Neither strategy works reliably, because the model has no training signal that says 'this is the right moment to commit.' The streaming constraint—knowing only the past—locks the model into this trap.

Math after the picture

Scoring the response timing

First in words:

response_score = LLM_judge(answer_text, reference, response_time, question_timestamp)

Then the compact form:

score = 0 if response_before_cutoff OR no_response; else LLM_grade(answer, reference) in range 0--5

Every symbol:

response_timethe moment the model output its answer
question_timestampthe moment in the video when the question was asked
cutoffthe earliest frame where the required visual or audio cue appears
LLM_gradea language model that scores the answer text from 0 to 5
response_before_cutoffboolean: true if the model answered before the proof was visible
A tiny worked example
Question at 1:20: 'What does the user tap?' (the tap happens visibly at 1:45).
Model A answers at 1:25 (before the cutoff 1:45): response_before_cutoff = true, score = 0.
Model B answers at 1:50 (after the cutoff 1:45): response_before_cutoff = false; LLM grades the text 'tap the Settings icon' as 4 out of 5; score = 4.
Model C does not answer by 2:00: no_response = true, score = 0.
Same video, same question, same models—only the streaming timing changed the outcome.

The equation is asymmetric: a correct answer at the wrong time scores 0; a correct answer at the right time scores high. This is why Forward tasks are so hard.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Early response trap. The model is trained on complete videos, so it rushes to answer as soon as it guesses the right answer. It does not know how long to wait. Streaming evaluation catches this; offline benchmarks do not.
No-response collapse. Smaller models like MMDuet2 (a model with 3 billion internal numbers) refuse to answer 59% of the time on forward tasks, trading early-answer errors for silence. This is also marked wrong. The model has learned no strategy for predicting the future.
Multimodal confusion. Richer video and audio signals help Instant and Backward tasks, but they also make the model more aggressive at guessing forward, because more visible evidence makes the model confident that it already knows what will happen next. More data does not fix the timing problem—it makes the model rush, because confidence about the answer is different from knowing when the answer is visible.
Done right. A model trained with streaming examples, where it learns to defer commitment until the evidence is on screen, should close the Forward-Backward gap, because the model will have a training signal that says 'wait until the visual cue arrives.' The benchmark makes this clear: the path to improvement is not better video understanding, but better temporal reasoning, because the bottleneck is not the model's ability to see and understand, but its ability to know when the answer has become visible.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple mobile video QA system where you control the playback timeline. Implement three test cases: (1) Backward: pause the video at 2:00, ask 'what happened at 1:20?', let the model answer—it should score high. (2) Instant: pause at 1:20 right when the action occurs, ask 'what is happening now?', the model should score high. (3) Forward: pause at 1:00, ask 'what will happen at 1:30?', give the model only the first second of video, measure how often it answers too early (before 1:30 in the actual timeline) or gives up. Log the response timestamps and compare the three modes.

temporal_gap = score_backward - score_forward

The demo should make it impossible to miss: the same model, the same video, the same language, fails catastrophically when forced to predict instead of remember. That gap is the problem this paper measures.
