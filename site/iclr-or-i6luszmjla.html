HumanPCR: Probing Multimodal LLM Abilities in Human-Centric Scenes

A benchmark that measures how well multimodal language models understand humans in real-world scenarios at three levels: seeing details, grasping what is happening, and reasoning about why.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Today's multimodal models (AI systems that process both images and text) claim to understand the visual world, but they stumble on a core human skill: making sense of people. HumanPCR is a test suite with over 6,000 questions designed to probe this weakness across three levels of understanding. The paper's key finding is surprising: models fail not because they cannot see details, but because they wait passively for the question to tell them which parts of a scene matter, instead of gathering evidence on their own like a human observer would.

Zero assumption version

Models think they see humans, but they do not understand the full scene

Multimodal language models (systems that read images and answer questions about them) are trained on billions of examples from the internet. They can name objects and describe what is visible. But humans do not just passively receive information from their eyes. When you watch someone work, you notice details they do not talk about: their posture, the sequence of their actions, what they are thinking. A model tested only on surface-level questions never learns to gather this kind of evidence.

A beginner might build a benchmark that simply asks 'what is in this image?' These tests measure whether models can name objects and colors. But they miss the deeper failure: when a scene grows complex—multiple people, sequences of actions, implicit reasoning about intent—models look only where the question points. They do not proactively search for clues the way a human would, scanning the scene to build a complete mental picture. The result is brittleness: models perform well on test sets designed around obvious features but break on scenes where understanding requires gathering hidden context.

The method in one sentence. Build a benchmark with 6,000+ human-annotated questions across perception, comprehension, and reasoning, then show that models fail not on seeing but on gathering evidence they were never asked about.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Layer 1: Perception

6,000+ multiple-choice questions on seeing humans—pose, contact, movement, expression. Tests pixel-level visual detail.

Layer 2: Comprehension

6,000+ questions on understanding what people are doing—sequences of actions, procedures, relationships between people.

Layer 3: Reasoning

Manually written video reasoning: watch sequences of human activity, then answer questions that demand gathering scattered clues about intent, causality, and unseen assumptions.

Proactive vs. query-guided evidence

Annotators mark which parts of the video are essential to answer the question but are not mentioned in the question text. This measure exposes how often models ignore evidence they were never told to seek.

Evaluation of 30+ models

Test leading multimodal models and measure failure patterns. Show that advanced techniques (like asking the model to think step-by-step) do not close the gap on Human-R, revealing that the bottleneck is evidence gathering, not reasoning power.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: how evidence gets selected

The benchmark changes the way evidence is selected in a reasoning task. Normally, a model receives a question and attends to features the question mentions by name. HumanPCR forces models to confront a scene where the question is vague or incomplete. The object here is the selection mechanism: the method by which a model decides which parts of the visual input to process. Instead of waiting for the question to guide attention, the benchmark demands proactive gathering—a model must learn to extract clues regardless of whether the question hints at them.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Proactive Visual Evidence Ratio

measured quantity  =  Proactive vs. query-guided evidence

Every question in Human-R (the reasoning level) has been manually annotated with two kinds of visual evidence: referred evidence (pieces the question explicitly names or asks about) and proactive evidence (clues hidden in the video that a human observer would gather without being told, but that are essential to understand the full answer). For example, a question might ask 'Why did she stop?' but never mention the phone on the table. A human sees the phone before the question and knows it is relevant. A model trained on query-guided attention would miss the phone entirely.

The paper measures this ratio for every question. Then it evaluates 30+ models and scores them separately on questions where proactive evidence is abundant versus questions where most evidence is referred. Models trained on standard data perform much worse when forced to gather evidence without cues, revealing the gap.

Why it works

Why this benchmark works: forcing genuine evidence gathering

The benchmark works by constructing scenes where a model cannot cheat. If you ask a model 'What color is the car?' it searches for the word 'car' in the image and reports the color. It never learned to scan the whole scene first. But HumanPCR's reasoning questions do not name their subjects up front. A question might ask 'What is she thinking?' and a human must first gather evidence—her expression, what she is looking at, what just happened. Only then can they infer her thoughts. A model trained on query-guided retrieval fails because it has never practiced the act of gathering evidence independently.

The mechanism is exposure. By measuring performance separately on proactive versus referred evidence, the benchmark makes the failure mode visible and measurable. Models score high when the question tells them where to look (referred). They score low when they must find the evidence themselves (proactive). This gap is not an accident of data; it reflects a genuine difference in capability. The paper shows that advanced techniques—like asking the model to explain its reasoning step-by-step—do improve referred-evidence questions but leave proactive performance flat, because these techniques ask the model to think harder about what it already found, but do not help the model search for clues that were never found in the first place. This proves the bottleneck is not reasoning power but the ability to search for unseen clues.

Math after the picture

The measurement: accuracy on questions split by evidence type

First in words:

accuracy_proactive = ( questions_with_high_proactive_evidence_answered_correctly ) / ( total_questions_with_high_proactive_evidence ) versus accuracy_referred = ( questions_with_high_referred_evidence_answered_correctly ) / ( total_questions_with_high_referred_evidence ) ; the gap is accuracy_referred - accuracy_proactive

Then the compact form:

Gap = Accuracy_Referred - Accuracy_Proactive

Every symbol:

Accuracy_Referredfraction of questions correct when the question text names the critical visual evidence
Accuracy_Proactivefraction of questions correct when the critical visual evidence is not mentioned in the question but must be gathered from the scene
Gapthe performance drop that reveals how much models rely on text-guided attention versus true scene understanding
A tiny worked example
On 100 referred-evidence questions (where the question says 'look at the phone'), a model answers 80 correctly. Accuracy_Referred = 80 / 100 = 0.80.
On 100 proactive-evidence questions (where the phone is critical but the question never names it), the same model answers 42 correctly. Accuracy_Proactive = 42 / 100 = 0.42.
Gap = 0.80 - 0.42 = 0.38. A 38-percentage-point drop exposes that the model's strength is pattern-matching text, not gathering evidence independently.

The gap between referred and proactive accuracy is the yardstick. It measures how much a model relies on the question to guide its attention versus its ability to search for evidence on its own.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Model relies on question keywords. If the question says 'show me the phone,' the model looks for 'phone' in the image. When the question is vague ('Why did this happen?'), the model has no guidance and fails.
Advanced reasoning techniques do not solve this. The paper tests step-by-step reasoning prompts (asking the model to explain its thinking). These help on referred-evidence questions but not on proactive ones, because the model only reasons about evidence it has already gathered; step-by-step thinking does not create new evidence, so it cannot overcome an attention bottleneck, proving the bottleneck is attention, not logic.
Context extraction methods miss the point. Recent papers propose techniques like 'select only the most relevant frames' or 'rank parts of the visual input by importance.' These work on standard datasets but fail on Human-R because they still rely on text-guided scoring rather than true scene understanding.
Correct approach: learn to gather before you reason. Models that learn to extract a dense, scene-centric representation first (gathering all relevant evidence regardless of what the question will ask) then perform reasoning on top of that representation. This breaks the dependency on query-guided attention.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple video clip of two people working together (e.g., one person setting the table while another pours drinks). Write ten reasoning questions: five where the question explicitly mentions critical actors or objects (referred), five where the question is vague and the critical evidence (a gesture, a glance, a change in posture) is never named (proactive). Run a standard multimodal model on both sets. Measure accuracy separately for each set and compute the gap. A gap of 0.30 or larger is typical of modern models.

Gap = Accuracy_Referred - Accuracy_Proactive

The demo makes it impossible to miss: the model performs well when told where to look, because the question text acts as a beacon that directs the model's limited attention, and poorly when forced to gather clues independently, because no query-guided cue narrows the search space. This gap—between guided and self-directed evidence gathering—is the benchmark's core finding.
