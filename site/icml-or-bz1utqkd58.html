Motion Planning in Compressed Representation Spaces

Plan robot motions by building a compact code for trajectories, then searching through trajectory codes instead of raw positions.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Planning a robot's motion normally means searching through millions of possible positions and angles. This paper learns a compact numerical code for trajectories (a compressed version that packs motion into a few numbers) and then does planning by searching through codes instead of raw positions. The payoff: flexible, efficient planning that works without task-specific training.

Zero assumption version

Planning in a huge space is slow, and learning often locks you into one task

Motion planning for robots is stuck between two worlds. Learning-based methods learn from data and work well, but they bake in task-specific goals at training time: if you train a planner to reach point A, it is awkward to repoint it at point B without retraining. Model-based planning methods (like search or optimization) are flexible — you can swap in any goal at test time — but searching through millions of candidate arm positions or vehicle poses is slow and memory-hungry.

A naive approach tries to learn a task and then swap the goal. This breaks because the model's internal layers assume a specific objective during training; changing the goal at test time does not retarget the learned features.

In one sentence: Learn a zipfile-like code for trajectories, then plan by searching through codes with any test-time goal, without retraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build a compact code

Train an autoencoder: encode real trajectories into a sequence of discrete numerical tokens; decode them back to motion.

Order the tokens hierarchically

Arrange tokens so early tokens pick the broad shape, later tokens refine details — coarse-to-fine structure.

Search token-by-token at test time

For each token position, try all discrete choices; decode each; score via your chosen objective; keep the best.

Greedy selection

Pick the best token at each step without looking ahead. This is fast and works because the autoencoder learned structure.

Decode the chosen tokens

Pass the selected code through the decoder to get the final realistic motion sequence.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the search space

Normally, motion planning searches through the raw space of positions, angles, and velocities — millions of points. This paper changes the search space to a compact numerical code made of discrete symbols (called tokens: individual numerical codes that encode part of a motion). Instead of trying every raw pose, the planner tries a much smaller set of token combinations, and the autoencoder decoder turns tokens back into realistic motion.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: latent token order

measured quantity  =  hierarchical structure in token sequence

The paper measures and exploits the hierarchical structure the autoencoder learns: a ranking of token importance where early tokens capture broad trajectory shape and later tokens add fine details. This is not explicitly computed; it emerges from training with a nested dropout technique.

The planner uses this order during search: it locks in early tokens (coarse shape) before searching over later tokens (refinements). This breaks the search problem into smaller, sequential steps instead of one giant combinatorial explosion.

Plain reading. When a motion sequence is squeezed into codes, some codes matter more than others. Early codes set the overall direction and shape; later codes tweak the path. The autoencoder naturally learns this ranking.
Paper reading. Nested dropout during training enforces an ordering: during autoencoder training, the paper randomly drops the later tokens, forcing the model to reconstruct well from just the early tokens. This incentive makes early tokens carry broad information and late tokens refine.
Why it works

Why it works: trading search breadth for autoencoder structure

Motion planning is normally hard because the space is enormous: an arm with 6 joints is 6 continuous dimensions, a vehicle has at least 3 (x, y, heading). Searching all combinations explodes exponentially.

This paper uses an autoencoder to buy structure. The autoencoder is trained to squeeze trajectories into a much smaller set of discrete tokens; this compression is a bet that most 'realistic' motions cluster in a smaller subspace. The decoder's job is to faithfully unzip tokens back to motion, so if you search only in the token space, every path you find is automatically realistic because the autoencoder learned only realistic codes.

At test time, greedy search picks one token at a time, always choosing the token that optimizes your current objective (reach this goal, change lanes, avoid obstacles). Because tokens are ordered hierarchically (early tokens do coarse work, late tokens do fine detail), the search does not explode: fixing token 1 narrows token 2's choices, and so on. By the time you reach the last token, you have already committed to a coarse shape, so the final refinement is fast.

The rounding and search equation

First in words:

To compress trajectories, the autoencoder rounds continuous learned codes into discrete symbols. At test time, greedy search picks each token by maximizing the score under the chosen objective, decoded and evaluated.

Then the compact form:

z_i* = argmax_{z_i} Obj(Dec(z_1:i)), where z_1:i means tokens 1 through i

Every symbol:

z_i*the best choice for token at position i
z_ia discrete token value (from a learned vocabulary)
Obj(·)the objective function at test time (reach goal, avoid collisions, change lanes)
Dec(·)the decoder that reconstructs motion from token codes
z_1:ithe sequence of tokens from position 1 up to i, already committed
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 4 discrete tokens available for each position and a goal-reaching objective.
Step 1 (coarse): Try all 4 codes for token 1. Decode each. Score each by distance to goal. Token 1 = code A (score 85, best). Lock it in.
Step 2 (refine shape): Fix token 1 = A. Try all 4 codes for token 2 given A. Decode each full path (A + new token). Score by distance to goal. Token 2 = code C (score 92, best). Lock it in.
Step 3 and 4: Repeat. By step 4, we have committed to A-C-D-B, which the decoder turns into a smooth trajectory.
Greedy pick at each step: 4 choices × 4 steps = 16 evaluations. Brute force over all codes: 4^4 = 256. Greedy is 16x faster.

Each token locks in a decision, shrinking the search tree exponentially because tokens are hierarchical.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor autoencoder compression. If the autoencoder is too lossy (codes do not capture realistic motion well), greedy search will pick codes that decode to jerky or implausible motion. The search is only as good as the decoder.
Broken hierarchical structure. If early tokens do not capture coarse motion, the search will not be efficient. Nested dropout during training is essential to enforce the hierarchy; skipping it ruins the ordering.
Objective not aligned with learned structure. If you optimize an objective the autoencoder never saw (e.g., a safety constraint not in training data), greedy token selection may find codes that technically score well but decode to motion that is unsafe or violates domain constraints.
Done right. Train the autoencoder on diverse motions from your domain (autonomous driving data). Enforce hierarchical structure via nested dropout. At test time, use objectives that are evaluable on decoded trajectories (distance to goal, collision checking, smoothness).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a small demo with a 2D or 3D mock environment (e.g., a goal point and obstacles). Train an autoencoder on a dataset of recorded motions (or synthetic trajectories). Then run greedy latent search with two different test-time objectives: reach Goal A, then swap to Goal B without retraining. Measure planning time (should be fast), success rate (trajectory reaches goal without collision), and realism (trajectory is smooth, not jittery). The demo must show that the same trained model handles both objectives without task-specific training.

success_rate = number of plans reaching goal without collision / total attempts

The demo must make it impossible to miss: drop a new objective in at test time, and the same autoencoder re-plans instantly.
