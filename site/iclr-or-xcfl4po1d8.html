Best-of-three-worlds: Dueling Bandits with Borda Winner

One algorithm learns from pairwise comparisons in chaos, noise, and sabotage without knowing which one you are in.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

When you learn from pairwise comparisons (one arm beats another), you need different algorithms for three worlds: chaos (adversary picks), noise (nature picks randomly), and sabotage (adversary corrupts some rounds). This paper unifies all three into one algorithm that works equally well in each, using a voting trick that ranks all arms fairly instead of picking just one winner.

Zero assumption version

One algorithm cannot rule all three worlds

Imagine you are choosing between job offers. You get feedback only by comparing two offers at a time: does offer A beat offer B? Over many rounds, you want to find the best offer. The rules change depending on your luck. In the chaos world, an adversary (bad luck) can pick which pairs you compare. In the noise world, nature flips a coin to decide each comparison. In the sabotage world, someone corrupt most rounds. Most algorithms are tuned for only one world and fail in the others.

The old standard was to assume one winner (Condorcet winner: someone who beats everyone else). But in many real settings, no single winner exists. So researchers tried ranking all options fairly (Borda voting: count how often each option wins), but the old algorithms do not work for all three worlds at once. A beginner's fix would be to build three different algorithms and switch between them, but you do not know which world you are in until you are done learning.

The paper's move in one sentence. Use follow-the-regularized-leader (a general tuning algorithm) with negative entropy (a fairness brake) to make one algorithm that works in chaos, noise, and sabotage equally well.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Count wins per arm

Keep a tally of how often each arm wins a match.

Compute a fairness cost

Penalize if any arm is too far behind its natural win-rate, using negative entropy.

Choose an arm with entropy regularizer

Pick the next pair by trying to lower the total cost (wins plus fairness penalty).

Get binary feedback

Observe which arm won the comparison.

Update and repeat

Update the win-count tally and run the algorithm again.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the decision-making rule: instead of picking arms one at a time to maximize a single winner, it tallies how often each arm wins and uses that vote count to rank all arms. The regularizer (a fairness brake using negative entropy) keeps the algorithm from becoming too confident about any arm being mediocre.

the object it moves  =  voting tally + fairness brake
The hidden quantity

The regret: how much worse you did than if you had known the best answer upfront

measured quantity  =  Regret (rounds of suboptimal choices)

Regret is a measure of how long it took to find the best offer. If the best offer wins, say, 100 times out of 1000 rounds, but you only picked it 800 times (and picked worse offers 200 times), your regret is roughly 200 rounds of lost opportunity. Different worlds have different regret formulas: chaos (adversary picks) grows like K^(1/3) times T^(2/3), noise (nature picks) grows like K log T squared divided by the gap, sabotage (corruption plus nature) grows like K log T squared divided by the gap plus a corruption term.

The paper estimates regret by computing the expected cost of its algorithm's arm-picking process, then bounding how many rounds it takes to reduce that cost below the optimal solution.

Why it works

Why one algorithm works in all three worlds

Voting (Borda) works better across all three worlds than picking a single winner, because averaging everyone's ranked choices cancels out any one judge's blind spot. In chaos, an adversary can prevent any single arm from being the clear winner, but they cannot prevent you from counting wins accurately. In noise, a random coin-flip gives a fair estimate of win-rates over time. In sabotage, you tally all the honest rounds (and the corrupted rounds dilute the signal but do not flip the count completely). Counting votes survives because it treats all rounds equally rather than trusting any single comparison.

The fairness brake (negative entropy regularizer) prevents the algorithm from overcommitting to any arm. Early in learning, you do not know which offers are good, so the brake keeps you sampling all offers roughly equally. As the win-counts diverge, you gradually drift toward high-win arms, but the brake keeps you cautious and prevents you from abandoning good arms too early. This cautious drift ensures the algorithm performs well whether the world is adversarial (you have to hedge against surprises) or random (you must explore thoroughly to estimate win-rates).

Math after the picture

The tuning rule with fairness brake

First in words:

The algorithm picks the next arm to query (or the pair to compare) by minimizing the sum of the negative win-count loss and the entropy penalty. The entropy term is scaled by a tuning factor (inverse temperature, often called beta). Higher beta makes the penalty weaker and allows the algorithm to pick greedily; lower beta makes the penalty stronger and keeps exploration uniform.

Then the compact form:

x_{t+1} = argmin_x [ -sum_i w_i log(p_i) + (1/beta) * sum_i p_i log(p_i) ]

Every symbol:

w_irunning win-count for arm i
p_iprobability or weight assigned to arm i
betainverse temperature (tuning factor for fairness brake strength)
sum_i p_i log(p_i)negative entropy (diversity penalty)
A tiny worked example
Suppose there are 3 arms with win-counts: w1=8, w2=4, w3=2 (arm 1 is winning more often).
Uniform exploration would assign p1=0.33, p2=0.33, p3=0.33, entropy = -3*(0.33*log(0.33)) = 1.10.
Greedy (no fairness) would assign p1=1.0, p2=0, p3=0, entropy = 0.
With fairness brake (beta=2): the algorithm trades off win-counts (preferring arm 1) against entropy (wanting all arms equal).
Result: p1=0.6, p2=0.25, p3=0.15, entropy = 0.89. Arm 1 gets more picks, but all three stay alive.
Regret grows slowly because the algorithm does not abandon arm 3 too early, even though it is losing.

The entropy regularizer acts as a fairness brake: it penalizes any allocation that is too concentrated on one arm, forcing the algorithm to keep exploring.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Borda ranking assumes all arms are comparable. If some arms are never paired against each other (sparse comparison graph), the win-counts become incomparable and voting breaks down. The algorithm assumes a fully connected pairwise comparison structure.
Tuning the fairness brake (beta) is necessary. If beta is too high, the algorithm ignores win-counts and explores all arms equally forever. If beta is too low, the algorithm converges too fast to a wrong answer. The paper derives beta values that work for each world, but they differ.
Corruption level must be known in advance. In the sabotage world, the regret bound assumes you know how many rounds the adversary corrupts. If corruption is higher than assumed, regret can be much worse.
Done right. The paper shows that with the right beta values for each world (or a simple scaling that adapts), one algorithm achieves regret that matches the best known single-world algorithm, proving the theory is tight.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a dueling bandits simulator with 4 arms of varying quality. Run the FTRL voting algorithm with a fixed beta across three environments: (1) adversary picks pairs greedily to confuse the algorithm, (2) pairs drawn uniformly at random, (3) pairs drawn randomly but 20% of results are flipped by an adversary. Plot the Borda win-rank of each arm over time, and measure cumulative regret. The demo should show that all three curves (regret in each world) stay close together, proving the unified algorithm does not sacrifice performance in any single world to gain the others.

regret_t = T * (value of optimal Borda winner) - sum_{s=1}^{t} (actual value observed by algorithm at round s)

The three regret curves should be flat and overlapping, showing the algorithm learns equally well whether the world is adversarial, noisy, or corrupted.
