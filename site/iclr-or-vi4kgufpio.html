VideoMathQA: Reasoning Through Time and Sound

A benchmark that tests whether AI models can follow a full math lecture—watching the board, reading handwritten text, and listening to explanations—all happening at different times.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Math in video is harder than math in text. When a teacher explains on a whiteboard over 10 minutes or 1 hour, you must catch details scattered across frames, read scribbled equations, hear spoken words, and track when each piece appears. VideoMathQA is a benchmark (a standardized test) that measures whether AI models can do this. It includes videos from 10 seconds to over 1 hour, covering 10 math domains, with careful expert annotations of every reasoning step.

Zero assumption version

Old tests miss the time problem

Today's AI benchmarks (standardized tests) for math live on static pictures or flat text. A model reads an image with an equation or reads a paragraph of words, then answers. But real math teaching is a movie. A calculus instructor draws on a board for 5 minutes—the final answer appears only after step 4 of 6. A student cannot see the full problem at once; it unfolds in time. They must glue together pieces from different moments, listening as the teacher speaks, watching as symbols appear, reading when text flashes onscreen. Old benchmarks never test this.

A naive approach: take a video and turn it into a single screenshot by stacking frames, or just play it and hope the model picks up everything passively. Both fail. The model can miss a detail, forget what was said 30 seconds ago, or confuse which equation is the real question and which was just an example.

In one sentence. The benchmark tests how well AI models can reason through math explained over time, integrating visual details, text, and spoken words.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect diverse math videos

10 mathematical domains (arithmetic, shapes and space, calculus, statistics, graph theory, etc.); durations 10 seconds to over 1 hour.

Experts craft questions

Graduate-level math experts write a question for each video, tied to real content in the video; 30 minutes to find the right video, 40 minutes to write a clear question.

Experts trace the steps

A separate annotator writes out every reasoning step needed to solve the problem, 4 to 10 steps per question; 1 hour per sample.

Run the models

Test 30 different AI models (small, large, old, new) on the benchmark to see how many get the right answer and trace valid reasoning.

Publish the results

Report which model sizes and architectures excel; measure how much each feature (video length, reasoning type, math domain) affects performance.

What the paper changes

What the benchmark changes

VideoMathQA shifts the test surface from static images to full videos, from instant perception to temporal reasoning. It forces models to process information that arrives over seconds or minutes, not all at once.

the object it moves  =  the evaluation surface (static to temporal)
The hidden quantity

Temporal cross-modal reasoning ability

measured quantity  =  ability to integrate scattered visual, audio, and text cues over time

This is the hidden quantity the benchmark measures: Can the model follow a lecture? Can it fuse details from different modalities (what it sees, what it hears, what it reads) and different time points? A model with high ability catches the equation that flashes on-screen, remembers it when the teacher speaks 20 seconds later, and applies it correctly even though the problem was not fully revealed until frame 1500 out of 2000.

The paper estimates this by grading two things: (1) does the model arrive at the final correct answer? and (2) does the model's step-by-step explanation match the expert's reasoning trace? If a model guesses the right answer but for the wrong reasons, the step-level grades catch that.

Why it works

Why a video benchmark is harder than a static one

A static image benchmark can work because all relevant details are on screen at once. You can scan left, then right, then up, then down, and find everything. But a video unfolds in time. Frame 1 shows the setup, frame 100 shows a key equation, frame 200 shows the data, frame 300 shows the working. A model must hold each piece in memory, update its understanding as new frames arrive, and then retrieve the right pieces when needed to solve the problem.

Second, a lecture is multimodal. The board shows one thing (the formula), the narrator says another (the interpretation), and subtitles show yet a third (a clarification). A model must align these three streams: when the teacher says 'integrate from 0 to 1' and the board writes the integral sign, those refer to the same idea even though one is audio and one is visual. A model that processes video frames alone misses the narration; one that reads only transcripts misses the visual flow.

Third, questions in VideoMathQA come in three flavors, each requiring a different kind of temporal reasoning. Direct problem-solving means the answer is explicitly stated in the video (the teacher says 'the answer is 42'). Conceptual transfer means the video teaches a method, and the question asks the model to apply that method to a new, slightly different problem—the model must bridge from 'here is how to solve equation type X' to 'now solve this equation type Y.' Deep instructional comprehension means the question requires tracing a multi-step explanation unfolded over a long video, with the setup at the start and the question hidden until the middle. Each type rewards models that truly understand temporal structure, not ones that guess.

How the paper measures performance

First in words:

answer_accuracy = (number of correct final answers) / (total questions); step_coverage = (reasoning steps model writes that match expert steps) / (total expert steps); score = answer_accuracy * 0.5 + step_coverage * 0.5

Then the compact form:

score = 0.5 * (correct_answers / total_questions) + 0.5 * (matched_steps / total_expert_steps)

Every symbol:

correct_answershow many questions the model answered right
total_questionshow many questions are in the benchmark
matched_stepshow many reasoning steps the model wrote match the expert trace
total_expert_stepstotal steps the experts wrote across all questions
scoreoverall grade for the model (0 to 1, where 1 is perfect)
A tiny worked example
A benchmark has 100 questions. A model answers 64 correctly: answer_accuracy = 64 / 100 = 0.64.
The experts wrote 300 reasoning steps total. The model's traces match 225 of them: step_coverage = 225 / 300 = 0.75.
Combined score = 0.5 * 0.64 + 0.5 * 0.75 = 0.32 + 0.375 = 0.695 (strong performance; a model guessing randomly would score near 0.2).

The benchmark blends two forms of accuracy: getting the final answer right and explaining the path truthfully. A model cannot hide a lucky guess.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forgetting earlier frames. A model processes frames sequentially but discards early details; when the answer requires a fact from frame 50 of a 300-frame video, the model has already forgotten it.
Missing the modality alignment. The board shows an equation, the teacher says 'this is why,' but the model reads only the visual or only the audio; it fails to connect the two.
Hallucinating steps. A model invents reasoning steps that sound plausible but never appeared in the video; it gets the right answer by accident, not understanding.
Done right. A model attends to every modality, holds a persistent memory of key details, explicitly traces its reasoning against the expert steps, and re-reads the question before answering to avoid mistaking an example for the real problem.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test: curate 5 short videos (each 30 seconds to 2 minutes) from real lectures or educational content, each with a single clear math question (e.g., 'Using the method shown, solve for x'). Annotate each video with the 3–5 key reasoning steps. Run an AI model on each video and record the final answer (correct or wrong) and the model's step-by-step explanation. Compare the model's steps to the expert steps; mark which steps the model got right, which it hallucinated, and which it skipped.

demo_score = (correct_answers + valid_steps_traced) / (total_questions + total_steps)

The demo makes impossible to miss: a model that truly follows a lecture will give the right answer AND explain it via the right intermediate steps. A model that guesses will get some right but fail to trace its reasoning honestly. This separates real reasoning from luck.
