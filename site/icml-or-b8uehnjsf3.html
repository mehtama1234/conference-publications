Hugging Carbon: Quantifying the Training Carbon Emissions of AI Models at Scale

Estimate how much carbon open-source AI training has emitted by combining partial public records, engineering metadata, and error-checked fallback rules instead of retraining every model from scratch.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Training a large model consumes electricity, and electricity use creates carbon emissions that depend on where and how the compute ran. The problem is that most public model pages do not report the full chain from hardware time to energy use to carbon output. This paper builds a large-scale accounting method for Hugging Face models that works even when disclosures are incomplete. It uses direct reports when they exist, falls back to compute and hardware clues when they do not, and checks each fallback with regression models so the final totals are not just rough guesses.

Zero assumption version

Carbon accounting for AI training breaks when disclosures are incomplete

If you want the carbon cost of training one model, you need at least three pieces: how much computation was run, how much electricity that computation consumed, and the carbon rate of the electricity source. Public model cards often omit one or two of those pieces. Some list emissions directly, some list hardware and training hours, and many list only model size or a vague training description.

That missing data turns a simple bookkeeping task into an estimation problem. A large platform may host thousands of models, so manually reconstructing each training run is not practical. The paper asks a narrower and more useful question: given the metadata that platforms already expose, how far can we go toward a platform-wide estimate with explicit fallback rules and explicit error checks?

The paper's move. Treat carbon accounting as a tiered estimation pipeline: use direct emissions when available, infer missing pieces from energy and compute metadata when needed, and quantify how trustworthy each fallback is.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect public model metadata

Read model cards, hardware fields, training notes, energy reports, and download counts from the platform.

Assign each model to an estimation tier

Use direct emissions if present, otherwise infer from energy, then from compute, then from metadata-based prediction.

Convert compute or energy into carbon

Map hardware use to electricity use and electricity use to carbon output by using explicit conversion rules.

Check fallback quality

Fit regression models on better-labeled cases so each fallback path can be tested against known outcomes.

Aggregate and compare

Add model-level estimates into platform totals and compute AI training carbon intensity, which is emissions per unit of training compute.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the accounting procedure. Instead of requiring full disclosure from every model, it builds a hierarchy of increasingly indirect estimates and validates each indirect step on the subset where stronger labels exist, because that overlap is the only way to tell whether a weaker fallback preserves the scale of a stronger measurement.

the object it moves  =  the carbon estimation pipeline
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  AI training carbon intensity

The hidden quantity is AI training carbon intensity, which means carbon emissions per unit of training compute. Total emissions alone answer how large the problem already is, but they do not tell you whether one training run used unusually dirty electricity, unusually inefficient hardware, or simply a very large amount of compute.

This intensity measure matters because it separates scale from efficiency. Two models can emit the same amount of carbon for different reasons: one may be small but trained with carbon-heavy electricity, while another may be very large but trained with cleaner power. By tracking emissions relative to compute, the paper can ask whether training practice is becoming cleaner or only becoming larger.

Why it works

Why the tiered method works

The main mechanism is error containment. If the authors used a single formula for every model, missing metadata would force them to make the same weak assumptions everywhere. The tiered design avoids that because each model uses the strongest evidence available. A direct emissions report stays direct. A model with energy records uses those records. Only the least documented cases rely on more indirect prediction.

The second mechanism is calibration on overlapping cases. Some models expose both a strong label and the weaker clues that would have been used if that label were missing. Those overlapping cases let the authors test whether a weaker path reproduces the stronger one closely enough to keep. This matters because a fallback is useful only if it preserves the ordering and rough scale of the true emissions, not merely because it produces a number for every row.

The final mechanism is aggregation across many imperfect records. One model-level estimate may be noisy, but the platform total can still be informative if the errors are bounded and the estimation rules are consistent. That is why the paper invests in validation at the tier level before summing across the corpus.

Math after the picture

The accounting equation

First in words:

Carbon emissions equal electricity use times the carbon emitted per unit of electricity. The paper also tracks emissions per unit of compute to compare training efficiency across models.

Then the compact form:

emissions = energy_used * carbon_intensity_of_electricity

Every symbol:

emissionsestimated carbon output from one training run
energy_usedelectricity consumed during training
carbon_intensity_of_electricitycarbon emitted for each unit of electricity supplied
A tiny worked example
Suppose a training run used 12,000 units of electricity.
Suppose the electricity source emitted 0.4 units of carbon for each electricity unit.
Then the estimated training emissions are 12,000 times 0.4, which gives 4,800 carbon units.
Now compare that run with a second run that used the same compute but cleaner electricity at 0.2.
Its emissions would be 12,000 times 0.2, which gives 2,400 carbon units.
The difference shows why the paper tracks both scale and carbon intensity.

The exact engineering conversion from hardware time to energy can vary by tier, but the accounting logic always runs through electricity use and then through the carbon rate of that electricity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Metadata can be wrong or strategically vague. If training hours, hardware type, or emissions fields are incorrect, the pipeline inherits those errors because no public estimator can repair hidden false reporting.
Fallback quality can differ by model family. A regression calibrated on one slice of the platform may transfer poorly to another slice if their hardware or reporting habits differ, so tier validation has to be checked carefully.
Training is only one part of lifecycle impact. The paper measures training emissions, not deployment, repeated fine-tuning, or hardware manufacturing, so the total environmental cost of AI systems can still be larger than the reported number.
Done right. The framework is useful when the goal is platform-scale training accounting because it turns partial public records into auditable estimates instead of either discarding most models or treating every undocumented case as unknowable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a table of synthetic model cards with four tiers of detail: direct emissions, energy only, compute only, and sparse metadata only. Hide the direct labels for part of the table, then ask the demo to reconstruct them through the same fallback order as the paper.

The demo should report two things. First, how close each fallback tier comes to the held-out direct labels. Second, how much the estimated platform total changes when you keep incomplete rows instead of dropping them. That comparison shows whether the tiered method expands coverage without letting weak estimates dominate the total, because the held-out labels reveal how much error each fallback path adds.

tier_error = absolute(estimated_emissions - held_out_direct_emissions)

The central test is whether the platform total stays anchored to validated cases while coverage expands to the many models that would otherwise disappear from the accounting.
