COGS: Composition-Grounded Instruction Synthesis

COGS breaks down questions into simple reasoning steps, then mixes and recombines them with new images to grow a huge training dataset from a tiny seed.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Charts and webpages have no large question-answering datasets, so multimodal models (systems that read both text and pictures) struggle with tricky reasoning tasks. COGS solves this by taking a handful of example questions, breaking each one into primitive perception and reasoning pieces, then systematically recombining those pieces with new images to create thousands of synthetic question-answer pairs that teach the model to reason step-by-step.

Zero assumption version

The reasoning bottleneck in artificial images

Multimodal models (systems trained to understand pictures and text together) excel at simple recognition tasks—spotting objects, reading text, labeling parts. But they stumble on harder questions that require multiple reasoning steps, like interpreting a chart's trend, comparing values across rows, or extracting a fact from a rendered document. The core problem is simple: huge question-answering datasets exist for natural photographs, but almost none exist for artificial images (charts, diagrams, webpages, rendered documents), so models never learn to reason over them.

A naive approach would be to hire people to annotate thousands of questions manually for every image domain. That is slow and expensive. So researchers need a way to generate question-answer pairs automatically, without humans writing each one by hand.

The challenge. How do you create a large reasoning dataset for charts and documents when no humans are annotating them?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Collect seed questions

Start with a small set of hand-written question-answer pairs for one chart domain.

2. Extract factors

Analyze each seed question and label its perception steps (what you see) and reasoning steps (what you conclude).

3. Generate new pairs

Take those factors and recombine them with new images to create thousands of synthetic questions.

4. Add reasoning scaffolds

For each synthetic question, include the intermediate answers to each reasoning step, not just the final answer.

5. Train with step-level rewards

Use reinforcement learning to reward correct reasoning at each step: when the model scores each intermediate answer right, weights shift in a way that reinforces that reasoning pattern, because the learning algorithm directly credits the step that produced the correct intermediate result.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves the training signal from just the final answer to every reasoning step in between. Instead of telling the model only whether it got the question right, it gives feedback on each intermediate conclusion—did you read the axis label correctly? Did you compare the two values fairly? This step-by-step signal teaches the model the reasoning process itself, not just the answer pattern.

The hidden quantity

The factor structure

measured quantity  =  Question decomposition into perception and reasoning factors

A factor is one irreducible piece of a reasoning question—a single perception act (reading a label, spotting a number) or a single reasoning act (comparing two values, detecting a trend). The hidden insight is that these factors are reusable: if you know how to read a chart's y-axis in one question, you can slot that same perception factor into dozens of other questions paired with different images.

The paper estimates factors by having a person label each seed question's components, then uses those labels to guide automatic generation of new questions. The factor-level structure makes it possible to mix-and-match: perception factors with reasoning factors, factors from one image domain (charts) with factors from another (webpages), because the reasoning step (e.g., compare these two numbers) works the same way whether the numbers came from a bar chart or a line graph, so retraining the model on both types simultaneously exposes that invariant and prevents overfitting to one visual style.

Why it works

Why factor reuse teaches better reasoning

The mechanism has three parts. First, breaking a question into factors forces you to identify the actual reasoning steps humans follow—not just the final answer, but the path to it. Second, reusing factors across thousands of new images exposes the model to the same reasoning pattern applied in different contexts, which teaches the abstraction: the model learns that read-the-y-axis is a portable skill, not specific to one chart. Third, giving rewards (in reinforcement learning—nudging the model weights to favor correct patterns) at the factor level means the model's learning signal points directly at each reasoning step, so errors in intermediate reasoning get corrected immediately, not buried inside a final-answer loss.

By contrast, if you train on final answers only, the model can accidentally memorize spurious patterns (e.g., red bars always mean higher values) or skip reasoning steps when they lead to the right answer by luck. Step-level rewards close that loophole.

The reinforcement learning update

First in words:

Expected reward equals baseline plus the sum of factor-level rewards for each step, each weighted by how well the model commits to that step.

Then the compact form:

R = b + sum_j (w_j * r_j * c_j)

Every symbol:

Rthe total reward signal for one question-answer pair
bbaseline reward (what would a random answer score)
w_jthe commitment weight at step j (how confident the model is in that step's answer)
r_jcorrectness reward for step j (1 if right, 0 if wrong)
c_ja constant balancing factor j's contribution
sum_jadd up the rewards for every step in the question
A tiny worked example
Question: A chart has bars 30, 45, 60. Is the trend increasing?
Step 1 (read y-values): model confidence 0.9, answer correct, reward = 0.9 × 1 × 1.0 = 0.9
Step 2 (compare values): model confidence 0.85, answer correct, reward = 0.85 × 1 × 1.0 = 0.85
Step 3 (conclude trend): model confidence 0.7, answer correct, reward = 0.7 × 1 × 1.0 = 0.7
Total reward = 1.0 (baseline) + 0.9 + 0.85 + 0.7 = 3.45. If step 2 had been wrong (0 instead of 1), total would drop to 2.6—the model learns to nail intermediate steps.

The key is that each step's reward depends on both correctness and the model's confidence, so the learning signal flows directly to improving intermediate reasoning, not just final answers.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incomplete factor decomposition. If the person labeling seed questions misses a reasoning step or marks factors too coarsely (e.g., grouping read-axis and compare-values into one factor), the generated synthetic questions will reuse that error across thousands of pairs, teaching the model a broken skill.
Domain shift in synthetic images. If the automatic image generation or factor recombination creates unrealistic combinations (e.g., mislabeled axes, impossible value ranges), the model trains on plausible-looking nonsense and learns spurious patterns instead of genuine reasoning.
Factor brittleness. A factor decomposed for one chart type may not transfer to a very different visual style (e.g., a horizontal bar chart vs. a line plot). The paper shows this can be mitigated by mixing factors from multiple seed domains, but the limit is real.
Done right. With careful factor annotation and diverse image generation, COGS trains models that apply reasoning across new questions and chart types—the paper demonstrates this via ablations disabling each component and showing performance drops when factors are not reused or step-level rewards are removed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small chart dataset with just five hand-written question-answer pairs. Implement the factor decomposition (mark which words in each question refer to read-values, compare-values, detect-trend). Write a recombination script that generates ten new questions by mixing factors with new chart images (you can draw simple bars and line plots programmatically). Train a multimodal model on both the original five pairs and the 50 generated pairs, with process rewards at each reasoning step. Test on held-out charts with unseen questions that reuse factors in new combinations. The demo should show that factor-level training improves on reasoning-heavy questions more than final-answer-only training, because the model receives a learning signal (a reward) for each reasoning step independently, so it cannot skip a step and still get credit for reaching the right answer by luck—every step must be correct to accumulate high reward.
