PMDformer: Patch-Mean Decoupling for Long-term Forecasting

Separate the trend from the shape in time series patches so attention sees true similarities, not just scale noise.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you try to forecast weeks ahead in electricity demand or stock prices, you break the time series into chunks called patches. But patches at different scales (a 100-unit spike versus a 10-unit spike) look different to the model's attention mechanism (the part that decides which past pieces matter most) even if their shapes are identical. PMDformer fixes this by removing the average from each patch before the model looks at it, letting the mechanism focus on actual shape patterns instead of getting distracted by magnitude differences.

Zero assumption version

The scale confusion problem

Long-term forecasting means predicting many steps into the future—weeks or months of electricity use, traffic, or stock prices. Models break the time series into overlapping or non-overlapping chunks called patches, similar to how a vision model reads an image as small tiles.

The attention mechanism (the part that learns which past patterns matter most) compares patches to decide which ones influence the forecast. But here's the trap: two patches with identical shapes—same rises and falls—get different attention scores if one is scaled up or down. A peak of 100 and a peak of 10 are treated as completely different, even if the pattern is identical. Meanwhile, a low-magnitude patch might get high attention just because the numbers happen to be close to another patch, not because the shape is truly similar. The attention mechanism sees scale noise instead of real patterns.

In one sentence. The model confuses scale changes with shape changes, so it learns the wrong historical patterns.

What the paper changes

The single change

The paper modifies what the attention mechanism sees: instead of the raw patch values, it sees each patch with its mean subtracted. This tiny change has a big effect because the model now learns shape patterns directly, without being fooled by different magnitudes.

the object it moves  =  the input to attention
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: shape alignment

measured quantity  =  shape alignment score

Shape alignment is a measure of how similar two patches are in their up-and-down pattern, ignoring overall scale. Normally, the attention mechanism computes a rough proxy for this (dot product of the values), which is corrupted by scale differences. PMDformer computes it cleanly by comparing the mean-removed versions of the patches.

The paper does not measure this explicitly as a number, but you can infer it from the attention weights after decoupling: high attention between two patches means they have aligned shapes. This is how the model learns which historical patterns predict the future.

Why it works

Why it works: The causal chain

Here is the mechanical reason the method works. When you compute attention, the model scores each pair of patches by how similar they are. The scoring method (called a dot product) adds up products of paired numbers. For patch A (large scale) and patch B (small scale but identical shape), this sum stays small because the numbers are small. For patch A and patch C (medium scale but different shape), the sum gets large because big numbers multiplied by medium numbers produce large products. The biggest terms dominate the dot product, because multiplication amplifies large values; so the model ranks C as more similar to A than B, because the numbers matter more than the pattern.

PMDformer subtracts the mean. For A, the mean is about 100.3, so after subtracting you get residuals near -0.3, 1.7, -1.7. For B, the mean is about 10.3, so you also get residuals near -0.3, 1.7, -1.7. For C, the mean is about 51, so you get residuals near -1, 4, -3. Now when you compute the dot product of A and B residuals, all three terms are tiny; the total is around 6. When you compute A and C residuals, you get a bigger number around 15. The ranking has flipped slightly, but more importantly, the model now has a clean signal of shape match independent of scale.

Second, after attention computes which historical patches matter, the model needs to make a forecast. But the residuals (mean-removed patches) no longer contain the long-range trend information—the overall drift up or down over weeks. PMDformer stores the means separately and adds them back before the final prediction, so the forecast captures both the shape patterns and the underlying trend.

The equation

First in words:

For each patch j, subtract its mean from each element to get the residual; then the attention score between two residual patches is their dot product.

Then the compact form:

P_j^residual = P_j - mean(P_j) ; score(i,j) = dot(P_i^residual, P_j^residual) ; forecast = attended_sum(P^residual) + sum(means)

Every symbol:

P_jthe j-th patch, a sequence of C values (one per variable)
mean(P_j)the average value across the S time steps in patch j
P_j^residualthe patch after subtracting the mean
score(i,j)the attention weight between patch i and patch j
dot(...,...)multiply corresponding elements and add them up
attended_sumweighted average of the residual patches, using the attention weights
forecastthe predicted values for the next T time steps
A tiny worked example
Patch A: [100, 102, 99], mean = 100.33, residuals = [-0.33, 1.67, -1.67]
Patch B: [10, 12, 9], mean = 10.33, residuals = [-0.33, 1.67, -1.67]
Dot product of raw values: 100*10 + 102*12 + 99*9 = 2327 (confuses scale)
Dot product of residuals: (-0.33)*(-0.33) + 1.67*1.67 + (-1.67)*(-1.67) = 6.07 (pure shape match)
Forecast for A: attended_residual + 100.33 (restores the trend magnitude)

Mean subtraction removes scale noise from the dot product, so attention sees true shape similarity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Non-stationary trends. If the long-range trend is not flat (for example, electricity demand growing year over year), storing and re-adding means separately can lose information about how the trend changes. The model assumes each patch's mean is independent, which breaks down when the trend itself is changing.
Tiny patches lose signal. If patches are very short (only 3–5 values), the mean is noisy and the residuals do not carry enough information. The method needs patches large enough that the mean is stable.
Outliers in residuals. A single spike in one patch creates a large residual that dominates the attention score, even though it may not be a true pattern. The method does not handle outliers specially.
Done right. When patches are large enough, trends are locally stable, and outliers are rare, PMDformer cleanly separates shape from scale and learns the right historical dependencies.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate synthetic time series with known repeating shapes at different scales (for instance, 10 cycles of a sine wave at amplitude 50, then 10 cycles at amplitude 200, then 10 cycles at amplitude 75). Train a baseline attention-based forecasting model (a model that learns to focus on relevant past patterns) with and without mean decoupling to forecast the next cycle. Measure which model better predicts the next cycle's amplitude and shape. The baseline should fail to generalize shape to new scales; PMDformer should succeed because it has learned the shape pattern, not the scale.

shape_match_accuracy = fraction of predicted cycles whose pattern matches the held-out shape signature (correlation of residuals), not just magnitude

The demo proves that decoupling mean from shape forces the model to learn the real pattern, not scale tricks.
