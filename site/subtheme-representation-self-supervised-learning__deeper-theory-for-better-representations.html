Deeper theory for better representations

Papers provide mathematical foundations using variational inference, Riemannian geometry, and information theory to understand why certain representation learning methods work and how to improve them.

What this is

Why does contrastive learning work? What guarantees does a representation satisfy? How many unlabeled examples do you need before representations converge to the truth? These questions live in theory. Papers here provide mathematical foundations: probabilistic frameworks explain why certain training goals lead to good representations, the mathematics of curved spaces shows how space shape affects what patterns can be encoded, information theory bounds how much data you need and predicts which methods should work best. Theory does not always lead to practical improvements, but it builds intuition and can reveal failure modes.

Theoretical work is hard because neural networks are nonconvex and complex — most analysis requires approximations or focuses on simpler settings (linear networks, infinite width limits, or highly structured data). Researchers study what properties a 'good' representation should have: sufficient information (capturing everything relevant for downstream tasks), disentanglement (different factors of variation are separable), or invariance (insensitivity to irrelevant transformations). Some papers prove that certain algorithms converge to true solutions or characterize the sample complexity: how the number of examples needed scales with dimension, number of classes, or noise level.

Across 11 papers, researchers build theoretical foundations for contrastive learning, self-supervised clustering, multimodal alignment, and continual learning. Some papers study loss surfaces: which optimization algorithms avoid bad local minima? Others study generalization: proving that models that fit training data well will transfer to new data. The work is often technical (heavy math) but yields insights: contrastive methods work because they maximize information between views so the model must capture what is stable across views, clustering works because it approximates the true density structure so categories emerge naturally, and transfer learning works because upstream and downstream tasks share underlying factors of variation. These insights guide practitioners in designing better methods and setting hyperparameters because they understand which design choices matter theoretically.

How the work breaks down

The work splits into four directions: understanding what structures representations must preserve (like causal patterns), building probabilistic frameworks to explain why certain training goals work, adapting representations across domains and tasks, and studying geometric properties that affect what can be encoded.

Preserving causal and independence structure

When you learn a representation, you want it to capture relationships that actually matter in the data. A key question: if two things are causally independent in the true world, does your representation keep them independent? This is hard because the representation must separate out what is truly independent from what just looks independent by accident in your training set.

CARL directly tackles this. It learns representations that preserve conditional independence patterns — the 'true' independence structure that would hold if you could see the full causal graph. By testing on data with known ground truth, CARL shows that explicitly optimizing for independence patterns leads to better performance than methods that ignore structure, because the learned representation captures what actually matters causally. The Spectral Representations paper takes a different angle: it proves that when you use representation learning to make independence testing possible, you can still get statistical guarantees (asymptotically valid and high-power tests) if you connect ideas from similarity-based mathematics with modern learned representations, which means the representation's accuracy directly translates to reliable test results. Together, these papers show that representations are better when they respect independence structure because downstream tasks depend on these structural relationships, and that theory can guide us toward methods with formal guarantees because the theory reveals which design choices preserve what matters.

Probabilistic and variational foundations

Why does a particular training objective lead to good representations? Probabilistic theory says: frame learning as turning raw data into a structured summary using a probabilistic model, and the loss functions that emerge tell you what the model is trying to optimize. The challenge is that neural networks are complex, nonconvex optimization problems — you cannot easily prove that the weights converge to a true solution, only that certain loss surfaces or approximations behave well.

Variational Deep Learning uses implicit regularization — it shows that certain training procedures with variational objectives give you strong performance without tuning extra regularization hyperparameters. The mechanism: the variational framework itself constrains the solution space so you do not overfit, even on new unseen data. The Amortized Variational paper on Partial-Label Learning applies this to the partial label problem, where each example can have multiple possible true labels. It discovers the true label by treating it as a hidden unknown, reasoning about it probabilistically through computational shortcuts. The method automatically handles ambiguity without explicit label disambiguation rules. Both papers show that when you build representations through a probabilistic lens, the theory guides algorithm design and the guarantees reduce the need for ad-hoc tuning.

Space shape and specialized domains

Representations live in a space where distances and angles follow certain rules. In flat space, distances work one way. In hyperbolic space (curved inward), they work differently — hyperbolic space naturally captures hierarchy because distances grow exponentially with depth. The question becomes: for a given domain, which space shape fits the underlying structure?

Batch Normalization for Neural Networks on Complex Domains extends the standard normalization recipe to complex-valued data (used in signal processing and radar). Normalizing complex activations correctly requires accounting for the structure of complex numbers (both magnitude and angle), which matters because ignoring it leads to unstable weight updates and poor learning. The experiments on radar clutter and node classification show that domain-appropriate normalization boosts performance because the representation computation respects the right space structure and can therefore separate signals that matter. Hyperbolic RQ-VAE uses hyperbolic space inside a quantized autoencoder (a method that discretizes activations to a codebook of values) for recommendation systems. By placing the codebook in hyperbolic space and optimizing reconstruction there, the method captures hierarchical item relationships naturally and gets fewer collisions and better codebook coverage because hyperbolic distances naturally expand to fit hierarchies. Together, these papers show that choosing the right space deliberately — whether complex-valued for signal data or hyperbolic for hierarchies — allows representations to encode domain-specific structures more efficiently because the space's shape (its rules for distance and angle) matches what the data naturally contains.

Transfer and adaptation across tasks

A representation trained on one task or dataset may fail on another. Why? What is stable enough to transfer, and what needs to adapt? This requires understanding which parts of a learned representation encode universal structure (shared across tasks) and which are task-specific. The challenge: disentangling these components so you can reuse the universal part while adapting the task-specific part efficiently.

Fine-Tune Once, Reuse Across Models treats fine-tuning as a probabilistic update: the base model is an initial assumption, and the task update is a change you can apply to multiple base models without changing the assumption itself. This separates what is learned from the original task from what is learned during adaptation, making updates reusable and model-independent. Local Covariate Selection for Causal Effect Estimation sidesteps the need for global causal structure — it selects the minimal set of confounders needed to estimate treatment effects under local assumptions, which is practical because learning global causal graphs from data is extremely hard. Von Mises-Fisher Mixture Model with Dynamic Shrinkage addresses test-time adaptation: when you apply a trained model to new data it may encounter unfamiliar examples or classes it has never seen. The method learns a probability distribution over classes (Von Mises-Fisher, a circular distribution) and dynamically downweights unreliable predictions so adaptation does not go wrong. All three papers target the same problem from different angles: representations that reliably transfer when you move between tasks, datasets, or time periods because they isolate what generalizes from what is specific.
