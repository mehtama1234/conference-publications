SpeechJudge: Toward Human-Level Judgment for Speech Naturalness

To align speech synthesis models with human preferences, the paper builds a large dataset of human speech judgments and trains a reward model that learns to predict which synthesized speech sounds more natural.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

When people generate speech with AI, we know some sounds natural and some don't. But no one had collected the thousands of human preferences needed to train a model to reliably judge naturalness the way humans do. This paper fixes that: 99 thousand examples of humans comparing two AI-synthesized speeches, plus a model trained on those comparisons. The result is a judge that agrees with people 77% of the time, compared to 69% for the best prior method—better because it learned from real human judgments instead of guessing from rules or generic audio metrics.

Zero assumption version

Aligning speech synthesis with human taste, the hard part

Modern AI models can generate speech from text. But do they sound natural? We know what we prefer when we hear it—a well-paced sentence with honest emotion beats a robotic drone. Yet no model had learned this preference by studying thousands of real human judgments. Without that training data, engineers had no way to fine-tune the synthesis models toward the sound people actually want.

A naive approach: measure how often the AI mispronounces words, or how clear the sound is. But naturalness is bigger—it lives in prosody (the rise and fall of pitch), in rhythm (the pacing), in how each syllable articulates (sounds sharp or blurry). These things don't have obvious metrics. You need human ears, and you need them to say which of two options they prefer.

The core bottleneck. No large collection of human preferences for speech naturalness existed, so reward models (the systems trained to predict good from bad) were left guessing.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Build the data.

Create a thousand pairs of synthesized speeches by running six different AI models on the same text, then ask humans which sounds more natural.

Step 2: Add reasoning.

For each pair, use Gemini to write out step-by-step why one is more natural (Prosody, Pacing, Articulation—the three pillars of naturalness).

Step 3: Train on explanations.

Fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter speech-aware model (Qwen2.5-Omni) on the data and reasoning, teaching it to predict preference and explain.

Step 4: Focus on the hard stuff.

Find cases where basic methods (like Gemini-2.5-Flash) guess wrong, then use reinforcement learning to make the reward model nail those.

Step 5: Deploy and measure.

Test the trained judge against human raters. Measure: how often does it predict the same preference? (77% vs. 73% for the baseline Bradley-Terry model.)

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes one thing: it provides humans who can judge speech naturalness, and a model trained to mimic those humans. Before, no such dataset or trained judge existed. After, engineers have both.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: naturalness alignment

measured quantity  =  How often the model agrees with humans

Naturalness is not a formula—it lives in human ears. So the paper's core hidden quantity is alignment: the fraction of times the model predicts the same winner as a human judge. This is measured by showing the model and a human the same pair of speeches, having each pick the more natural one, then checking if they agree.

The paper estimates this alignment by training on a subset of the 99 thousand human judgments, then testing on a held-out set of fresh pairs and comparing model predictions to human annotations. They call this agreement rate accuracy.

Why it works

Why training on human pairs teaches alignment

The mechanism is straightforward. The model learns by seeing thousands of choices: for each pair (Speech A, Speech B, Human said A is better), the model tries to predict which one humans prefer. When it guesses wrong, the training algorithm nudges its weights. After 99 thousand examples, it has internalized what humans listen for.

The trick is using two stages. In Stage 1 (Supervised Fine-Tuning), the model learns to not just pick a winner, but to explain why in terms of the three dimensions humans care about: Prosody (pitch patterns), Pacing (rhythm and speed), and Articulation (how clear each sound is). Writing out reasons forces the model to attend to the same features humans do. In Stage 2 (Reinforcement Learning on challenging cases), the paper focuses training on examples where simpler methods fail—cases where the choice is subtle. That forces the model to refine its understanding on the edge cases where alignment is hardest to achieve.

Because the model is speech-aware (Qwen2.5-Omni is trained on audio), it can perceive audio features directly instead of trying to infer them from metadata. This head start accelerates learning.

Math after the picture

The preference prediction equation

First in words:

preference_score_A minus preference_score_B equals the model's prediction of how much humans prefer A over B; the loss penalizes the model if its prediction disagrees with the human label

Then the compact form:

loss = -log( sigmoid( score_A - score_B ) ) if human chose A, else -log( 1 - sigmoid( score_A - score_B ) )

Every symbol:

score_Athe model's estimated naturalness of speech A (higher means more natural)
score_Bthe model's estimated naturalness of speech B
sigmoida smooth function that squeezes any number into a probability between 0 and 1
logthe logarithm (a slow-growing function that penalizes confident wrong predictions more)
human chose Aground truth: annotator labeled A as more natural
A tiny worked example
Suppose score_A = 0.8 (model thinks A is quite natural) and score_B = 0.3 (model thinks B is less natural).
Difference: 0.8 - 0.3 = 0.5.
sigmoid(0.5) ≈ 0.622 (about 62% probability the model assigns to A being better).
If the human indeed chose A, the loss is -log(0.622) ≈ 0.476.
If the human chose B instead (human disagrees), the loss is -log(1 - 0.622) = -log(0.378) ≈ 0.971 (about twice as large, punishing the mistake harder).
Over 99 thousand pairs, the model adjusts its weights to shrink the loss on the next batch.

The equation compares two scores. If the model thinks A is more natural and the human agrees, the loss is small. If they disagree, the loss is large, and training pushes the weights to fix the mistake.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The data reflects only the languages in the dataset. SpeechJudge covers English, Chinese, and a few others. If you ask it to judge naturalness in a language it never saw during training, it will guess poorly, because it never learned what naturalness means in that language's phonemes (unique sounds).
Naturalness is not everything. The model learns to rank by naturalness specifically—how human-like the speech sounds. But a speech system might also care about being intelligible (clear), emotive (sounding happy or sad), or matching a specific voice. The SpeechJudge model ignores those other goals.
Annotator disagreement is baked in. Some humans have different taste. Maybe 60% of annotators prefer the warm-toned voice, 40% prefer the clear-articulate one. The model averages across all opinions, so it will be confident in a pick that only 60% of humans agree with. That is a fundamental limit.
Done right: collect diverse voices, multiple languages, and retrain. When the method works, it works because the human data is broad: six different TTS models with different architectures, multiple languages, and annotations from many humans. That diversity teaches the model to recognize naturalness in many contexts.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset: generate 200 speech pairs using two different TTS models on a variety of texts (short sentences, longer passages, different topics). Recruit 10 human annotators to label which one sounds more natural. Split into train (140 pairs) and test (60 pairs). Fine-tune a speech-aware model on the 140 pairs, then measure accuracy on the 60. Compare against a baseline (e.g., predict the first speech always wins, which should get around 50%). The demo should show that training on even 140 human judgments pulls accuracy from 50% to at least 65%.

accuracy = (number of test pairs where model prediction matches human preference) / (total test pairs)

The demo makes impossible to miss: humans have a clear preference for naturalness, and a model trained to predict that preference learns patterns that generalize to new speeches.
