Extending LLM Context by Dropping Positional Embeddings

You can remove positional embeddings after pretraining and the model still works on longer sequences without any special retraining.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Pretrained language models hit a wall: they were trained on sequences of a fixed length (say, 4,000 words) and cannot handle much longer ones without expensive retraining. DroPE (Dropping Positional Embeddings) breaks this bottleneck by removing positional embeddings — the numbers that tell the model where each word sits in the sequence — and doing a tiny bit of recalibration. The result: the same pretrained model suddenly handles sequences twice as long, with no special training.

Zero assumption version

Why pretrained models choke on long sequences

A language model is trained on sequences of a specific length — say, 4,000 words. During training, positional embeddings (numbers attached to each word that tell the model its position in the sequence) help the model learn that word order matters, because without explicit position cues, the model has to infer position purely from statistical patterns in the text, which is slow. Explicit positions make the training curve smooth and fast. The model gets very good at using these numbers to understand the structure of 4,000-word texts.

If you then feed the model a 8,000-word document at test time, the positional embeddings are now outside their training range. The model has never seen a word at position 6,000 before. So it makes poor guesses about how to use the attention mechanism (the part that lets the model look back at earlier words). The traditional solution is to finetune the model on long sequences — expensive, slow, and requires careful tuning.

The naive approach and why it fails. Retrain the model on longer sequences. But this is costly and loses the clean understanding the model learned during pretraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Identify the problem

Positional embeddings lock the model into one sequence length.

Step 2: Remove embeddings

Delete the positional embeddings from a trained model. Attention mechanism now has no explicit position signal.

Step 3: Recalibrate briefly

Train for a short time (e.g., 2000 steps on normal sequences) so the model re-learns how to extract position from word context, because attention heads already know how to find patterns; they just need to repoint their position-sensitive behavior from the explicit embeddings to learned patterns in the text.

Step 4: Test on longer sequences

Feed the model texts longer than anything it saw during pretraining. Attention now generalizes without retraining.

Step 5: Preserve original capabilities

The model still works perfectly on the original sequence length, because recalibration kept the learned knowledge intact.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

DroPE removes the positional embeddings — the explicit numeric labels that tell the attention mechanism where each word sits — after pretraining is complete. The weights of the model stay; only the positional signal is stripped away and then rebuilt implicitly through a brief recalibration phase.

the object it moves  =  positional embeddings
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: positional bias in attention heads

measured quantity  =  positional attention bias

Not all attention heads care equally about position. Some heads are purely positional — they look back at the word exactly five places earlier, or at words in a certain span. Others are semantic — they look for words that are similar in meaning, regardless of distance. The paper measures the positional attention bias: the average tendency of each head to focus on words at specific relative distances.

During pretraining with positional embeddings, certain heads learn strong positional biases quickly — they use the explicit position numbers to organize attention. After removing embeddings, these heads must reconstruct their positional behavior from the words themselves, without the shortcut. The recalibration phase teaches them to encode position implicitly by observing patterns in the text.

Plain reading. Think of a room full of people chatting. Some people (positional heads) always turn to look at whoever is exactly to their left. Others (semantic heads) turn to look at whoever said something interesting, regardless of where they sit. Positional embeddings hand a name tag to every seat. When you remove the name tags, positional heads have to learn to turn to their left without being told which seat that is.
Paper reading. The paper defines positional attention bias as the magnitude of attention gradients in the Fourier domain. High gradient norm means the head encodes position explicitly. Heads with low gradient norms learn position implicitly from their learned patterns.
Why it works

Why removing embeddings and retraining works

During pretraining, positional embeddings serve two jobs: (1) they help the model learn quickly, because explicit position hints make the training curve smoother; (2) they lock the model's attention behavior into the training sequence length. The model learns that position is whatever the embedding says it is.

When you remove embeddings, the model loses the shortcut. Attention must now learn to encode position from the data — from patterns in the text itself. Positions in language are not arbitrary: punctuation marks tend to come in familiar patterns, sentence structures repeat, and paragraph breaks happen at regular intervals. With recalibration, the model re-learns positional awareness by observing these patterns, not by reading explicit position numbers.

Recalibration is fast because the model's general understanding of language is already learned. You are not retraining from scratch; you are only teaching the attention heads how to extract position from the context they already understand. Since position is learnable from data (as the paper proves theoretically), a brief tuning phase is enough.

The recalibration objective

First in words:

Loss equals the next-word prediction error, with standard language-model weighting. The model adjusts its attention weights to minimize this error, given that positional embeddings are gone.

Then the compact form:

Loss = mean(cross_entropy(predicted_next_token, actual_next_token))

Every symbol:

predicted_next_tokenthe model's probability distribution over the next word
actual_next_tokenthe true next word in the sequence
cross_entropya measure of how far the probabilities are from the truth, scaled logarithmically
A tiny worked example
Recalibration step 1: Model sees words 'the', 'quick', 'brown'. Predicts 'fox' with 60% confidence. Actual word is 'fox'. Loss = -log(0.60) = 0.51.
Recalibration step 2: Model sees 'the', 'quick', 'brown', 'fox'. Predicts 'jumps' with 45% confidence. Actual word is 'jumps'. Loss = -log(0.45) = 0.80.
Over 2000 steps like this, the model adjusts weights so that its attention heads gradually learn to track position through redundancy in the data, until the loss stabilizes and position is implicit.

The model is not learning a new task; it is learning the same task (predict next word) but with a new constraint (no positional embeddings). Position emerges as a side effect of minimizing prediction error.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Recalibration is too short. If you only train for a handful of steps, the model does not have time to learn position implicitly. Attention stays confused. Longer sequences fail. Solution: use at least 2000 steps, monitor validation loss.
Recalibration data is too different from pretraining. If the recalibration text is in a completely different language or domain, the model may not recognize positional patterns it saw during pretraining. Position encoding fails. Solution: use the same kinds of text, domain, and language as pretraining.
Model was trained with a positional scheme that is non-learnable. Some positional methods (like certain sinusoidal encodings) may embed position in ways that are hard to recover from data. The paper uses rotary positional embeddings, which are learnable. Solution: use learnable or data-friendly positional encodings during pretraining.
Done right. Use the same data and language for recalibration. Train until validation loss stabilizes. Test on sequences at 2x, 4x, and 8x the original length to confirm generalization.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Download a small pretrained model (e.g., a 1.3B-size LLM trained on 4K-word sequences). Remove its positional embeddings. Recalibrate on 2000 random steps of text from the same corpus. Then construct a needle-in-a-haystack test: insert a random sentence in the middle of a 8K-word document and ask the model to retrieve it. Compare the model's retrieval accuracy before and after extension. Also test on the original 4K-word context to ensure you did not lose accuracy.

context_extension_score = (needle_retrieval_at_8K / needle_retrieval_at_4K) * 100

If the score is above 80%, DroPE works: the model generalizes to unseen sequence lengths without expensive retraining.
