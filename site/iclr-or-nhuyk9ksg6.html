Horseshoe Splatting: Gaussian 3D Rendering with Calibrated Uncertainty

A Bayesian upgrade to 3D Gaussian Splatting that knows what it doesn't know—giving sharp 3D images plus honest uncertainty maps by automatically ignoring noise-dominated directions in each splat's shape.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting renders 3D scenes as fast, photorealistic images by placing millions of ellipses (Gaussians) in space and projecting them onto camera views. But it does not know which directions in each ellipse are actually useful and which ones just capture noise. This paper adds two things: a smart prior that automatically shrinks pointless directions, and calibrated uncertainty—pixel-by-pixel confidence maps so downstream tasks know when to trust the image.

Zero assumption version

The old 3D Gaussian Splatting does not know what is noise

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) renders a 3D scene by placing small ellipses in space, each described by its position, shape, and opacity. The shape is stored as covariance—a matrix describing how stretched or squashed the ellipse is in each direction. The naive approach: treat every direction equally important. But in reality, if a direction points through empty air or captures random camera sensor noise, it adds nothing. It just wastes computation and confuses downstream uses (like robotics or physics simulation) that need to know which parts of the rendered image are trustworthy.

A beginner might try: just delete the noisy directions. But which ones? You would have to look at every one of millions of ellipses and guess. There is no automatic signal.

In one sentence. The paper detects and shrinks noisy directions in each ellipse's shape, then reports pixel-wise confidence so users know when to trust the image.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Place a Horseshoe prior

Before training, declare that most directions in each ellipse's shape should be near zero (harmless noise).

Train by fitting to views

Adjust the weights to maximize how well renders match the training images, while penalizing unnecessary directions.

Learned weight per direction

Each direction gets its own learned magnitude. Large means important; tiny means noise.

Render by sampling shapes

Draw multiple ellipse shapes from the learned range of possibilities, creating varied renders for each pixel.

Output image and confidence

Return both the sharp 3D render and a map showing which pixels are trustworthy.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the shape of each ellipse

Standard 3DGS stores the covariance (the shape matrix) directly. Horseshoe Splatting adds structure: each direction gets a learned weight (a single number) that controls how much that direction contributes. The prior pushes most weights toward zero unless the training data insists otherwise.

the object it moves  =  per-direction importance weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: per-direction importance (scale)

measured quantity  =  direction importance weight

The Horseshoe prior places a spike at zero, meaning it expects most directions to be useless. It also has heavy tails, meaning if a direction is truly important it can grow tall. The model learns a scale (a positive number) for each direction in each ellipse. Scales near zero mean that direction carries noise. Scales far from zero mean that direction carries real scene information.

The paper estimates this by factorized variational inference: a technique that approximates the posterior (what we believe after seeing the training data) by decomposing it into independent factors, one per scale. This lets the model compute uncertainty and ignore noise efficiently.

Why it works

Why it works: aggressive shrinkage leaves only true signal

The Horseshoe prior has a magic property: it places a spike of probability density exactly at zero. This means the model starts with a strong expectation that each direction is useless. Only if the training data (the input views) provides strong evidence that a direction is important does the model keep it large.

Why does this work? Because overfitting to noise is easy—every random fluctuation in the training images looks like a real direction if you try hard enough. But if you penalize complexity (the prior) and reward predictive accuracy (the likelihood), the model naturally trades off: it keeps directions that predict all the training views consistently, and shrinks directions that only explain one view or capture a sensor artifact. The Horseshoe does this automatically—no manual threshold needed.

Uncertainty arises from the learned range of covariances: the model does not commit to a single shape per ellipse. Instead, it maintains a probability distribution (a range of possibilities with different likelihoods) over plausible covariances. When rendering, it samples different shapes from this range, producing different pixel values on different render passes. The spread of those samples gives the uncertainty: high spread in pixel values means low confidence; tight spread means high confidence.

Math after the picture

The equation: Bayesian penalty for unnecessary directions

First in words:

Loss equals how badly the render matches the training image, plus a Horseshoe penalty that shrinks every direction's scale toward zero unless the render quality improves. Scales that matter stay large; scales that add only noise get pushed down.

Then the compact form:

loss = render_error(theta) + sum_i Horseshoe_penalty(scale_i)

Every symbol:

render_errorhow far the rendered pixels are from the training images
scale_ithe learned importance of direction i across all ellipses
Horseshoe_penaltya prior that expects scale_i near zero but allows large values if data insists
A tiny worked example with three directions
Setup: Suppose one ellipse has three directions (x, y, z) and training images vote on how important each is.
Direction x: appears in all 8 training views consistently -> model learns scale_x = 2.8.
Direction y: appears in 6 training views -> model learns scale_y = 1.2.
Direction z: appears randomly in 2 views -> model learns scale_z = 0.1 (Horseshoe prior pushed it down).
At render time, sample from posterior: if scale_z is only 0.1, that direction wobbles (high uncertainty). If scale_x is 2.8, that direction is stable (low uncertainty).
Result: x dominates the image; z contributes only uncertainty; y is mixed. Pixel confidence reflects this.

The Horseshoe penalty automatically selects the few truly important directions and shrinks the rest, so the final image carries high fidelity and trustworthy uncertainty.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Variational approximation is approximate. The paper uses factorized variational inference (treating each scale's posterior as independent). In reality, scales interact; this approximation may underestimate uncertainty in entangled regions.
Prior hyperparameters must be tuned. The Horseshoe prior has shape parameters (the spike steepness and tail heaviness). If you misset them, the model may over-shrink important signal or keep too much noise.
Monte Carlo rendering adds overhead. Sampling from the posterior many times to get a good uncertainty estimate costs computation; you must balance speed against confidence quality.
Done right: theory and practice agree. The paper proves that with enough training data, the posterior shrinks around the true scales. In experiments, uncertainty maps align with actual reconstruction error, so users can trust the confidence scores.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train Horseshoe Splatting on a 3D scene (a simple CAD model or real scene with clean training views). Render a held-out view. Side-by-side show three outputs: (1) the rendered image, (2) the uncertainty map (darker means more confident), (3) the reconstruction error (how far pixels are from ground truth). The uncertainty map should align with error: high uncertainty where error is high, low uncertainty where the image is accurate. Then corrupt some training views (add noise or blur), retrain, and show that the uncertainty map expands in affected regions—the model is honest about what it no longer trusts.

correlation_of_uncertainty_with_error = sum_pixels (uncertainty - mean_uncertainty) * (error - mean_error) / (std_uncertainty * std_error)

Uncertainty is calibrated: it predicts where the model will be wrong, proving the Horseshoe prior did not just shrink noise, it learned what is real.
