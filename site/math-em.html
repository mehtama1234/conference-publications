Expectation-Maximization

Guess hidden parts, estimate what they should be, repeat until stable.

In plain terms

Imagine you have a jar of mixed coins but cannot see inside. You shake it and hear the clinks, but you don't know how many dimes versus pennies are in there. The sound tells you something useful, but not everything. You want to figure out the mix. One approach: first guess there are maybe forty percent dimes and sixty percent pennies. Then listen to the sounds and ask: if that mix were true, what sounds would we expect? Compare that prediction to the actual sounds. Now adjust your guess—maybe it's really forty-five percent dimes. Try again. Keep refining your guess until it matches reality so well that you stop needing to change it.

Expectation-Maximization is exactly this: a two-step dance. First, assume some hidden thing is true (the coin mix, the cluster each point belongs to, the hidden state of a system), and work out what that would predict about what you see. Second, look at what you actually saw, and update your assumption to make it more likely you would have seen exactly that. Repeat. The math is built to guarantee that each round makes your guess better—you never backslide. Eventually your guess locks in because it explains the data so well that changing it would only make things worse.

The equation
E-step then M-step
Xthe data you can actually observe (the sounds from the jar, the points you measure)
Zthe hidden thing you want to figure out (how many dimes, which cluster each point belongs to)
θthe guess or best-estimate values (the coin mix ratio, cluster means, hidden state parameters)
p(X | Z, θ)the probability of seeing your data if the hidden thing and guess were both true
p(Z | X, θ)how likely each possible hidden thing is, given what you saw and your current guess
Q(θ | θ_old)a lower-bound approximation—easier math than the real problem—built from your current guess
Worked example
Guess a coin mix from sounds
Start: guess 50% dimes, 50% pennies.
E-step: if true, a dime drops with 0.50 chance per sound.
M-step: count sounds. Got 7 dime-like, 3 penny-like.
Update: new guess is 70% dimes, 30% pennies.
E-step: now expect dime-sounds with 0.70 chance.
Compare: predicted 7 dime-sounds matches observed 7.
Done: guess is stable (70% dimes).
Why it shows up

Any time a model must figure out something it cannot directly see—where did this sentence come from? What cluster is this image in? What behavior did the agent actually choose?—EM is there. It breaks the problem into two: what the hidden thing probably is (E-step), and what hidden thing would best explain the data (M-step). This two-step is simple enough to compute and guaranteed to climb toward better answers because each M-step finds the guess that maximizes how likely your actual data is—so your next E-step will predict better, and you never undo progress. Reinforcement learning papers use it to infer the agent's real goal. Time-series work uses it to find the hidden states a system passed through. Clustering methods lean on it to assign points to clusters and update the cluster centers. Generative models use it when they need to split the learning into easier pieces.

The reason EM shows up everywhere is that it trades hard problems for easy ones. Instead of solving one nasty optimization in one shot, you do two simple operations repeatedly. The math guarantees each cycle gets you closer to the best answer because every M-step improves the fit to your data, so repeated E and M steps converge toward the best explanation. You don't need to know the global optimum in advance—just keep iterating until your guess stops changing. This is why papers on learning latent-variable models (that is, models with hidden things), reinforcement learning agents, and time-series systems all reach for EM when the direct approach is too tangled to solve.
