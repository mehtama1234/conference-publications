Riemannian Networks over Full-Rank Correlation Matrices

Use correlation matrices as a space to represent data, and adapt neural networks to work inside that space by following its mathematical rules.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Most neural networks treat numbers as if they live in flat, ordinary space. But correlation matrices (which measure how related things are to each other) live on a curved space with special rules. This paper shows how to build neural networks that work directly inside that curved space instead of trying to force correlations into flat math. Flat-space operations on correlations produce invalid results (numbers that violate correlation rules), forcing the model to learn to undo this damage before learning the actual task. Curved-space networks keep every intermediate result valid by respecting the space's rules; because no learning effort goes to fixing broken math, the network can dedicate more capacity to learning the actual patterns, so predictions are more accurate on tasks where relationships matter.

Zero assumption version

Why flat space breaks for correlations

A correlation matrix (a grid of numbers showing how strongly related different things are to each other) captures relationships. If you have time-series data (like stock prices or weather readings), a correlation matrix tells you which things move together. The problem: these matrices live on a curved surface with special rules — you cannot just add two of them, rotate them, or average them the way you would with ordinary numbers.

A beginner would try to treat a correlation matrix like any other list of numbers and feed it into a standard neural network. But when you do arithmetic with correlations on flat space, you get nonsense: the result might not be a valid correlation matrix anymore (it might claim two things are 1.5 correlated, which is impossible, or break the symmetry that correlations require). This breaks the network's ability to learn actual patterns from the data.

The paper's move in one sentence. Build neural network layers that do arithmetic inside the curved space of correlation matrices, so every step respects the rules of how correlations actually work.

The hidden quantity

The rules of correlation space

measured quantity  =  the internal coordinate system

Correlation matrices have a curved shape (called a manifold). When you move around inside this space, the rules change — distances work differently, angles are measured differently, and there are certain directions you cannot go without leaving the space entirely (because you would create an invalid correlation).

The paper measures this by using five different sets of rules (called metrics), each defining distances and directions differently inside the correlation space. The network learns which set of rules best suits the data by seeing which one gives the most accurate predictions.

Why it works

Why curved-space layers work better

In flat space, adding two correlation matrices can produce invalid results because the rules of correlation do not hold. Imagine two three-by-three correlation matrices: if you naively add them entry-by-entry, the result might claim that two variables are 1.5 correlated (impossible; maximum is 1.0) or break the symmetry that correlations require.

By doing arithmetic inside the curved space, the paper builds layers that automatically respect these rules. A fully-connected layer in flat space multiplies by a weight matrix and adds a bias; in curved space, it multiplies (using the space's multiplication rules) and adds (using the space's addition rules). Every operation is constrained to keep results valid. Backpropagation — the training algorithm that nudges weights to reduce error — also respects the space's rules, so weight updates do not accidentally push the network outside the valid correlation space.

This is more than cosmetic. When the network learns representations that live inside valid correlation space, it can actually understand and use the structure of relationships. A model working on flat corrupted data wastes learning capacity trying to undo the damage; a curved-space model can spend all its learning power on the task itself.

The core equation

First in words:

In flat space: output = weight matrix times input plus bias. In curved space: output equals the curved-space combination of the weighted input and the bias, where both the multiplication and addition follow the space's own rules, not flat arithmetic.

Then the compact form:

y = Exp_c ( Weight * Log_c(x) + bias )

Every symbol:

A tiny worked example
Input correlation matrix x has three variables with correlations 0.8, 0.3, and -0.2.
Log_c maps the matrix to a direction in curved space: direction = -0.1, -0.5, and 0.3.
Multiply by weights: weighted = 0.2, -0.3, and 0.1.
Add bias in curved space: biased = 0.4, -0.2, and 0.2.
Exp_c maps back to correlation space: output = 0.75, 0.1, and -0.05 (still a valid correlation).
Flat arithmetic would give 1.1, -0.1, and 0.15 after naive add — invalid because 1.1 exceeds 1.

The exponential and logarithmic maps are the key trick: they let the network do normal arithmetic (multiply, add) in a flat local neighborhood, then fold the result back to the curved surface where correlations actually live.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Picking the wrong rule-set for the data. The paper gives five different sets of rules. If the data matches one rule-set and you use another, you lose the benefit of respecting structure. The model must learn to find the right one.
High-dimensional correlations blow up. With many variables, computing correlations becomes expensive. Curved-space arithmetic is slower than flat arithmetic, so very large correlation matrices can strain computation.
Singular or rank-deficient data. The method requires full-rank correlations. If data is near-singular (some variables are perfectly dependent), the operations in curved space may fail or produce numerical instability.
Done right. Choose the rule-set to match your data domain (stock correlations respond to affine-invariant rules; medical time series to Fisher-Rao rules). Check that data is full-rank, and use moderate correlation sizes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a three-variable time series (e.g., temperature, pressure, humidity from a weather station). Compute a rolling correlation matrix over a window. Train both a flat-space network and a curved-space network to forecast the next day's correlations. Measure two things: (1) the fraction of predictions that violate correlation properties (are invalid), and (2) prediction accuracy on held-out days. The curved-space version should have zero invalid predictions; the flat version should have many. The curved-space accuracy should be higher.

validity = count(valid predictions) / count(all predictions); accuracy = mean(squared error between predicted and true correlation)

The one thing the demo makes impossible to miss: a flat network produces broken correlations that fail basic math checks, while curved-space learning respects the space's rules and guarantees valid output.
