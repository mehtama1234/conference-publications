Newton Method Revisited: Fast Convergence Rates

A new stepsize rule makes Newton's method arrive at answers three times faster than before, by treating it as a third-order process instead of a second-order one.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Newton's method is the workhorse of fast optimization. But its speed depends on knowing how bumpy the loss surface is in advance. This paper shows that with a simple new stepsize schedule—a rule for how big each step should be—Newton's method can converge up to three times faster, because measuring the bumpiness directly (the third derivative) lets you set each step's size exactly right instead of guessing safely.

Zero assumption version

Newton's method needs to know how bumpy the loss surface is

Imagine you're at the top of a foggy hill, trying to find the lowest point. Newton's method is like feeling the ground's tilt and curve under your feet and taking a smart step toward the bottom.

But here's the trap: the size of each step depends on knowing HOW much the curve changes where you are. If the surface is very bumpy, you need tiny steps or you'll overshoot. If it's smooth, bigger steps work fine. The old stepsize rules had to play it safe: they assumed the worst-case bumpiness, making steps smaller than needed, so convergence slowed down.

The real problem: even the best old stepsize schedules (the rules that say how much to adjust the step size on each iteration) only gave you a constant improvement factor per iteration. After 300 iterations, you've applied that factor 300 times. But here is what nobody tried: as you get closer to the answer, the loss surface gets smoother (less bumpy), so why not take bigger steps then? The old rules didn't adapt to this smoothness.

In one sentence. We measure how fast the curve's bend changes (the third derivative) and use that to set stepsize smartly—big steps where smooth predictions hold longer, small steps where they decay fast—making Newton converge much faster because each step is sized exactly for what the curve allows.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper adjusts is the stepsize—the multiplier that says how far to move along Newton's direction at each iteration. Old schedules were conservative: they guessed a worst-case bound on how bumpy the loss surface could be, then set stepsize to stay safe everywhere. This paper's formula directly uses the measured third derivative (how fast the curve's bend changes), tightening the stepsize to exactly fit the current spot, not a worst-case phantom.

The hidden quantity

The third derivative: how fast the curve bends

measured quantity  =  Third derivative of the loss

The hidden quantity is the third derivative of the loss function—a number measuring how fast the curve's bend itself changes. A smooth valley has a small third derivative; a jagged rocky chasm has a large one. This quantity is the key: it tells you exactly how bold the Newton step can safely be.

The paper estimates this quantity from the slope measurements (gradients) during the algorithm's run. Each iteration, you check how the slope changes; that tells you the curve's bend (second derivative). By comparing how the bend changes across iterations, you infer the third derivative. The stepsize formula then uses this inferred bend-change to set each step's size precisely.

Why it works

Why the new stepsize makes Newton faster

Newton's method at each iteration solves a locally-accurate curved-surface model of the loss. The step size tells you how far along the Newton direction to move. Take a step too large and you overshoot the good region where the model is correct; too small and you waste iterations.

The old theory assumed the worst-case curve-shape everywhere and set stepsize to guarantee safety. But most loss functions are smoother than worst-case. The new insight is this: if you measure how fast the curve-shape itself changes (the third derivative), you can predict how far the curved model stays accurate. A fast-changing curve shape means the model decays fast, so take a small step. A slowly-changing curve shape means predictable loss surface, so take a bigger step. This adaptive logic lets Newton travel much faster because each step is aggressive when it's safe, rather than uniformly cautious.

The old method converged at a constant rate each iteration: after many steps, the error divides by a fixed factor. The new method achieves faster convergence because the rate itself improves as you get closer to the answer—the curve flattens and the new formula detects this via the third derivative, allowing bolder steps with each iteration. This is why three times faster is not hyperbole: convergence accelerates over time because the compounding effect of increasingly-bold steps beats a constant improvement factor.

Math after the picture

The stepsize formula

First in words:

new position = current position minus (stepsize times the Newton direction). The stepsize adapts based on how much the curve bends.

Then the compact form:

x_new = x_old - alpha * [Hessian]^(-1) * gradient, where alpha = 2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 2 * bend_change * ||gradient||))

Every symbol:

x_oldcurrent position in the search space
x_newnew position after the step
alphastepsize (the schedule we set)
Hessianthe curve-shape matrix (second derivative of loss)
gradientthe slope vector (first derivative of loss)
bend_changeestimated third-derivative roughness constant
||gradient||length of the slope vector (steepness of the hill)
A tiny worked example
Step 0 (far from answer): slope is steep (length 10), bend-change 1. Stepsize = 2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 2*1*10)) = 2 / (1 + sqrt(21)) = 2 / 5.58 = 0.36.
Step 1 (closer): slope is shallower (length 2), so stepsize = 2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 2*1*2)) = 2 / (1 + sqrt(5)) = 2 / 3.24 = 0.62.
Step 2 (near answer): slope nearly flat (length 0.1), so stepsize = 2 / (1 + sqrt(1 + 0.2)) = 2 / 2.10 = 0.95.
The stepsize grows: 0.36 to 0.62 to 0.95. Near the answer, Newton takes increasingly bold steps because the curve is smooth there.
This accelerating boldness is why convergence speeds up over time instead of crawling forward at constant speed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unknown bend-change. If you don't know how fast the curve's bend changes, the stepsize formula will guess wrong. The paper addresses this with linesearch and backtracking procedures that automatically adapt, requiring no advance knowledge.
Computing the curve-shape is expensive. On large neural networks, computing the Hessian (the curve-shape information) and using it is prohibitively slow. This method is practical for smaller convex problems or where Hessian-free approximations exist.
Non-convex loss functions behave differently. The analysis assumes loss functions that are convex (one valley everywhere). On non-convex neural network losses, the third-derivative assumptions may not hold and the speedup may disappear.
Done right: use linesearch. When the bend-change is truly unknown, use the paper's linesearch variant (GRLS), which automatically finds a safe stepsize without needing advance knowledge of the problem.

What a runnable demo should prove

Implement three versions: standard Newton with a fixed stepsize, Newton with the old constant-rate schedule, and Newton with the new adaptive schedule (using the real formula above). On each iteration, record the error (how far the current point is from the true optimum). Plot error versus iteration count on a log scale (so accelerating convergence shows as a curve bending downward, not a straight line).
