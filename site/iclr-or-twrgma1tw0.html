Rating Quality of Diverse Time Series Data by Meta-learning from LLM Judgment

Instead of retraining a quality-rater for every new time series domain, train one rater across nine diverse domains so it works on any new time series without retraining.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you have a new pile of time series data (sensor readings, stock prices, weather patterns), you need to know which samples are trustworthy before training a model on them. But existing methods train a separate rater for each domain, wasting time and computation. This paper trains one meta-learned rater across nine different domains using LLM judgments, so it works on any new time series with just a few quick tuning steps.

Zero assumption version

Domain-specific raters are wasteful

Today, when you want to rate time series data quality, you train a separate scoring model (a rater) for each type of data. If you move to a new domain (say, from electricity prices to weather data), you have to start over and retrain the entire rater from scratch. This takes weeks of computation and you never reuse the knowledge from the old domain.

A beginner might think: just use a fixed scoring rule (like influence functions or Shapley values, which measure how much each sample contributes to the model). But these break for time series because they ignore the temporal patterns that make data trustworthy or noisy. Apply them to electricity data, switch to weather, and they fail silently.

The core move. Train one rater on quality comparisons from nine domains at once (meta-learning), then adapt it to any new domain with just a few tuning steps.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: LLM as judge

Prompt an LLM to compare pairs of time series blocks and judge which is higher quality on criteria like trend stability, frequency patterns, and noise.

Step 2: Train cross-domain

Meta-learning: train one rater on all nine domains together so it learns shared quality signals, not just domain-specific quirks.

Step 3: Efficient amortization

The meta-trained rater's inner weights are already tuned to time series. New domains need only lightweight few-shot fine-tuning, not full retraining.

Step 4: Score and select

Apply the adapted rater to assign quality scores to all blocks in the new dataset, then train models on the high-quality subset.

Step 5: Faster models, better accuracy

Training on high-quality data means fewer training steps and higher final accuracy on all downstream tasks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of training a separate rater for each new time series domain, the paper learns ONE rater across multiple domains that transfers to any new domain with a few tuning steps.

the object it moves  =  The rater weights and architecture
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: cross-domain transferability

measured quantity  =  How much the rater generalizes across domains

The hidden quantity is how well a rater trained on nine source domains can predict quality in a completely different target domain without full retraining. This is the core property that makes the paper work: a generic rater that captures what makes any time series good, not just one specific type.

The paper estimates this by meta-learning: train the rater to minimize loss on held-out quality judgments from all nine domains at once, using a technique called MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning) that forces the rater to find weights that can quickly adapt to any domain with just a few small tuning steps (each step nudges the weights by a tiny amount based on how far off the predictions are).

Plain reading. A good quality rater should know what clean patterns and low noise look like, regardless of what physical thing is being measured (stock prices, electricity, weather). Meta-learning forces the rater to learn these universal signals by exposing it to nine different domains.
Paper reading. The paper formulates this as a meta-objective: minimize the sum of query losses across all tasks (domains), with each task adapted via support-set inner loop. Cross-domain knowledge transfer happens because the outer loop computes a direction-to-move (a gradient, which is just which way nudges the rater to improve on all domains simultaneously) and steers the initial weights toward a point from which any domain can be reached with one inner tuning step.
Why it works

Why training across domains beats training one domain at a time

Without meta-learning: A rater trained only on electricity data learns electricity-specific shortcuts. When you apply it to weather, those shortcuts fail because weather has different seasonal patterns, sensor noise profiles, and timescale artifacts. You must retrain from scratch, discarding all the learned patterns.

With meta-learning: A rater exposed to nine domains at once learns what makes ANY time series trustworthy: smooth long-term trends, no sudden jumps, consistent amplitude, stable frequencies. These signals are universal. The rater's inner weights become a launch pad: any new domain can reach high accuracy with just one small fine-tuning step (think of it as tuning the last few dials after the main rater is already calibrated). This is why the paper only needs 55 seconds of fine-tuning per new domain instead of hours of retraining.

Concretely: the meta-learning algorithm trains on support sets (a few labeled examples from each of the nine domains) and query sets (held-out examples from those same domains). The outer loop updates the rater's weights based on how well it can quickly adapt to each domain's query set. This forces the rater to learn transferable features, not overfitting to one domain's idiosyncrasies.

Math after the picture

The meta-learning formula

First in words:

minimize the sum of query losses across all training domains, where each domain's rater is first adapted on its support set, then evaluated on its query set

Then the compact form:

L_meta = sum_i L_query_i(theta' - alpha * gradient(L_support_i))

Every symbol:

thetathe rater's initial weights shared across all domains
alphathe step size for inner-loop adaptation (how fast the rater tweaks itself to a new domain)
L_support_ithe quality-rating loss on labeled examples from domain i
L_query_ithe quality-rating loss on held-out examples from domain i
theta'the rater's weights after one fine-tuning step on domain i
A tiny worked example with three domains
Start: initial rater theta = [0.5, 0.3, 0.8] (simplified 3-weight model).
Domain A (electricity): support set loss gradient = [+0.1, -0.05, +0.02]. Adapt: theta_A' = [0.5 - 0.1*0.1, 0.3 - 0.1*(-0.05), 0.8 - 0.1*0.02] = [0.49, 0.305, 0.798]. Query loss on electricity holdout = 0.12.
Domain B (weather): support gradient = [-0.08, +0.06, -0.04]. Adapt: theta_B' = [0.508, 0.294, 0.804]. Query loss = 0.10.
Domain C (traffic): support gradient = [+0.04, -0.02, +0.03]. Adapt: theta_C' = [0.496, 0.302, 0.797]. Query loss = 0.11.
Meta-loss = 0.12 + 0.10 + 0.11 = 0.33. Update theta in the direction that lowers this across all three domains. After many iterations, theta becomes a universal entry point: any new domain can reach good performance by applying the same one-step adaptation process.

Instead of optimizing for one domain, the outer loop optimizes for rapid adaptability across domains. The rater learns to be a starting point that can reach any domain's optimum in one inner step.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

LLM judgment noise. If the LLM's quality comparisons are inconsistent or wrong on some criterion (e.g., the LLM conflates trend with noise), the rater learns incorrect quality signals. Check that LLM agreement is high across multiple models (the paper uses GPT-4o-mini, Claude-3.5, Gemini-2.0) and validate a sample of judgments against human experts.
Overfitting to the nine training domains. If the nine domains are too similar (e.g., all electricity-like), the rater may not transfer to very different domains (e.g., medical time series with completely different noise patterns and timescales). The meta-learned weights become tuned to those nine, not to universal quality signals.
Fine-tuning instability. If the new domain is very different from the training domains, a single fine-tuning step may not be enough to adapt the rater. You may need more than 55 seconds or more labeled examples (the paper claims few-shot suffices, but pathological domains may break this).
Done right. Train on diverse domains with consistent LLM judgments, validate transfer on held-out domains before deployment, and monitor rater performance on new data to catch when a domain is too different to handle.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a script that: (1) pretends you have a meta-trained rater (initialize it with random weights, because this is a proof-of-concept); (2) generates 100 synthetic time series blocks of mixed quality (some with clean sine waves, some with noise, some with sudden jumps); (3) runs the rater on the blocks to assign quality scores; (4) selects the top 50 samples by score; (5) trains a simple forecasting model on just those 50 high-quality samples; (6) measures forecasting accuracy on held-out clean data vs. held-out noisy data. The demo proves that filtering by rater score before training improves final accuracy because a model trained on high-quality data sees consistent patterns that generalize well, whereas a model trained on noisy data wastes capacity learning to ignore the noise rather than capturing true trends.

accuracy_gain = (accuracy_with_filtered_data - accuracy_with_all_data) / accuracy_with_all_data * 100 percent

The demo must show that even with a randomly initialized rater, the filtering signal is directionally correct because cleaner samples have stable, repeatable structure that any rater (even untrained) can learn to recognize via scoring patterns, while noisy samples are harder to assign consistent scores to; models trained on high-quality samples converge faster and to higher accuracy than models trained on all data because they are not wasting training effort on noise.
