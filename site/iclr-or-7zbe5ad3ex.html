Convergent Privacy Bounds for Federated Learning

A new way to measure privacy loss in federated learning that stays tight even after many rounds of training, instead of growing arbitrarily loose.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

When many people train a model together across separate computers (federated learning), each person adds noise to their data to hide it from attackers. The old way to measure how much privacy that noise gives was like looking at a fence: each day the fence gets a little worse, and after a thousand days you can see the whole yard. This paper replaces that fence-counting with a new measurement that actually stays honest no matter how many days pass, proving that the noise protects people reliably over the entire training.

Zero assumption version

Privacy bounds that lie the longer training goes

In federated learning, each person's computer keeps data private by adding noise (random garbage) to the information it sends to the central server. Attackers can still try to steal that information by running reverse-inference attacks: repeatedly requesting model updates and trying to guess what data produced them.

The old way to measure privacy safety used something called the composition theorem: after every communication round (called a round), you add up the privacy leakage. One round might leak almost nothing, so you think you are safe. But the theorem says: add them all together. This creates a problem: as rounds increase from 10 to 100 to 1000, your privacy safety gets worse and worse without bound. The math predicts the noise becomes completely useless after enough training, yet the experiments show the noise still protects people. This gap between theory and reality meant nobody could trust whether federated learning with noise actually worked.

The core issue: Old privacy analysis grows weaker the longer you train, even though the noise protects equally well every step.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the composition theorem (which stacks privacy loss) with a new analysis based on f-divergence differential privacy. This is a different lens for measuring privacy: instead of adding up leakage, it treats privacy as the error rate of an adversary running a hypothesis test: Can you distinguish whether a single person was in the training data or not? This shift lets you avoid the stack-it-up logic entirely.

the object it moves  =  privacy-measurement framework
The hidden quantity

Data sensitivity: how much the model varies with different data

measured quantity  =  data sensitivity

Data sensitivity measures the maximum distance the model travels after training on one dataset versus a slightly different dataset (where one person was removed). Small sensitivity means the model is stable: your private data does not leave a huge mark on the final model. Large sensitivity means the model yells your data's presence through its final weights.

The paper estimates data sensitivity by running forward passes and tracking how weight nudges (directions in which the loss pushes to change the model) propagate through local training on each client. For Noisy-FedAvg, they measure the gap between global updates on the real client dataset and updates on an adjacent dataset with one person removed. For Noisy-FedProx (a variant with a gently-steering term), the proxy term actually reduces sensitivity by anchoring the model to a previous version, creating built-in stability.

Paper reading: Data sensitivity is formalized as the gap in model outputs phi(w) on datasets C and C' (differing by one person), measured after t local steps from the same initialization. The paper bounds it using L-smoothness of the loss and bounds on the direction and size of weight nudges.
Why it works

Why measuring privacy differently stops the divergence

The composition theorem adds privacy leakage sequentially: imagine you lose 1 dollar of privacy each round, so after 1000 rounds you have lost 1000 dollars. That mathematics is sound if leakage truly accumulates. But it does not: the noise in each round is drawn fresh and independent. The same noise level protects equally well no matter which round you are in.

The new approach uses f-divergence (a tool from information theory that measures distinguishability). Instead of stacking loss, it asks: Given the noisy outputs from many rounds, what is the success rate of an attack that tries to guess whether person X was in the training data? This is a single hypothesis test over all rounds at once, not separate tests added together. As you add more rounds with the same noise level, the attack gets more chances to gather evidence, but the noise is protecting equally hard every time. The analysis finds the right balance point where these two forces meet.

To prove this, the paper uses shifted interpolation: imagine two parallel training runs (one on the real data, one on fake data) and measure their divergence. This measurement naturally converges to a stable bound as rounds increase, because after enough rounds the repeated noise creates a statistical regime where the attacker hits a hard wall—they cannot distinguish any better no matter how many more rounds they get.

Math after the picture

The privacy bound equation

First in words:

Privacy loss is bounded by a term that grows with data sensitivity but shrinks as noise gets stronger. After enough rounds, this bound stops growing and converges to a stable level.

Then the compact form:

epsilon_DP = min_lambda [ lambda * D_KL(C || C') + log(1 / delta) / lambda ]

Every symbol:

epsilon_DPthe privacy leakage measure (smaller is more private)
lambdaa tuning parameter that trades off the two terms
D_KL(C || C')the KL divergence (information distance) between distributions over models trained on real data C and adjacent data C'
deltafailure probability for the privacy guarantee
log(1/delta)the tail-risk term (how sure we are)
Tiny worked example: two rounds of training
Imagine two people each have one piece of data (binary digits). Round 1: noise level is 0.5, data sensitivity is 2.0. First term (data risk): 0.5 * 2.0 = 1.0.
Round 2: same noise 0.5, same sensitivity 2.0. Under old composition: total risk = 1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0 (leaks double).
Under new f-DP: after two rounds, the attacker has twice as much noisy data but the noise is twice as strong. The KL divergence stays near 1.5 (converged). We pick lambda = 1.5, privacy loss stays roughly 1.5 + 0.5 = 2.0 (nearly same, not doubled).
Key point: composition says 2.0, new analysis says roughly 2.0. At round 100, composition predicts 100.0, new analysis stays near 2.5. The gap grows because new measurement captures the real protection.

The minimum over lambda finds the best trade-off; shifted interpolation proves D_KL stays bounded as rounds grow.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Heterogeneous local data breaks tightness. If clients have wildly different data (some people with obvious patterns, others with random data), the data sensitivity rises unpredictably. The tight bound assumes reasonably uniform data across clients. Real federated scenarios have high heterogeneity, so practitioners must measure their own sensitivity by running tests on their actual data.
Noisy-FedProx requires tuning the proximal coefficient. The proximal term (which anchors to a previous model) does reduce data sensitivity and stabilize privacy, but only if the coefficient alpha is set correctly. Too weak and you lose the benefit; too strong and training becomes slow or unstable.
Non-convex objectives make analysis harder. The paper proves results for non-convex smooth loss functions (the realistic case for deep learning), but the constants in the bounds are loose because the analysis must account for the fact that the loss surface has many valleys and peaks, not just one bottom. The loose constants mean the predicted privacy guarantee might be more conservative (worse) than what actually happens in practice, but it is still a valid guarantee.
Done right: use measured data sensitivity. Measure how much your models change on your own data by training on subsets, then use that in the privacy calculation. This gives you a true privacy certificate specific to your federated setup.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small federated learning simulation: 5 clients each with their own dataset, train a simple neural network (linear model or shallow MLP) for 1000 rounds with noise added to the weight updates (direction and size of nudges to the model). On each round, measure data sensitivity by training the same model on a version of each client's data with one record removed, then measure the model distance. Track the privacy loss using both the old composition method and the new f-DP method. Plot privacy loss over rounds for both. The demo proves the claim by showing: (1) composition loss diverges upward, (2) f-DP loss converges to a stable plateau, (3) experiments match the new analysis, not the old one.

privacy_loss_old(t) = t * noise_leakage_per_round; privacy_loss_new(t) = f_divergence(real_path, fake_path, t)

The core insight is visual: plot privacy_loss over training rounds. Old analysis is a line going up forever; new analysis is a curve that flattens and stays flat. That flatness is the entire paper.
