Geometry and structure in representation spaces

Papers exploit geometric properties like symmetries and manifold structures to build better models, using techniques like hyperbolic space and polar embeddings to capture hierarchical relationships.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When a model learns representations, they live in some mathematical space. That space has structure: some points are close together, others far apart, and there may be natural directions or symmetries. A classic observation is that word encodings (dense vectors representing words) have structure — the direction from 'king' to 'queen' mirrors the direction from 'man' to 'woman,' which means the vector space has absorbed linguistic relationships. This is true in learned representations of images and other data too. Rather than treating representations as unstructured high-dimensional numbers, some papers exploit their structured properties to build better models because the structure encodes information the model has already learned about relationships in the data.

Real-world data has hidden structure: hierarchies (animals, then mammals, then dogs), symmetries (rotating an image should correspond to rotating its representation by the same amount), or continuous change (moving through representation space smoothly transitions between related objects). Standard Euclidean space (where distance is straight-line) may not be the best fit. Hyperbolic space (like the curved surface of a saddle) naturally encodes hierarchies — it has room in the center and expands exponentially at the edges. Manifolds (curved surfaces where the intrinsic structure is not flat) offer another option. Using these structured spaces, models can enforce natural relationships: a dog representation stays close to other dogs but far from cats, and small animals are hierarchically nested inside larger categories.

Across 16 papers, researchers explore hyperbolic number-spaces, manifold-based representations, circular coordinates for angular relationships, and other structured constructions. Some papers study symmetry groups and how networks learn them. Others apply these ideas to link prediction in knowledge graphs or hierarchical clustering. The payoff is that these structures often improve generalization and interpretability because the model respects the natural structure of the data instead of trying to force it into arbitrary geometric arrangements, which means the learned patterns transfer better to new data that has the same structure. Applications span hierarchical data (protein families, organizational hierarchies, image taxonomies) and domains where symmetry matters (physics-informed learning, equivariant neural networks).

How the work breaks down

The papers in this subtheme split into five natural directions: hyperbolic space for hierarchies, curved surfaces that capture data structure, symmetries and transformations, choosing the right distance measurement for different data types, and controlling how the learned space changes during training. These directions matter because real data has hidden structure—hierarchies, symmetries, and curved surfaces—and using the right space helps models capture those patterns without forcing them into arbitrary arrangements.

Hyperbolic space for hierarchical data

When data has a hierarchy—animals grouped into mammals, then specific species—it naturally fits into hyperbolic space because hyperbolic space (a curved space like a saddle) expands exponentially at the edges. This gives more room for many fine distinctions without forcing them to spread out in flat standard space. Three papers directly tackle this.

The Klein Hyperbolic Metric Learning paper introduces the Klein model as a hyperbolic alternative. It performs well on fine-grained image classification where visual categories have hierarchical structure, and it does this without adding extra parameters compared to the older Poincaré model, which means it is actually simpler to use in practice. Hyperbolic Associative Memory Networks takes a different angle by putting associative memory (a mechanism where you store and retrieve patterns) into hyperbolic space for the first time. On data with deep hierarchies—like biological taxonomies or organizational structures—these hyperbolic memory networks outperform their flat-space versions, but they stay just as good on shallow, non-hierarchical data, which means the framework is flexible. Path-Decoupled Hyperbolic Flow Matching goes further by using hyperbolic space with flow matching, a modern technique for learning distributions (probability mappings over data). It wins on multiple cross-modal few-shot benchmarks because hyperbolic space naturally encodes hierarchical relationships, so when you have limited examples and need to match across different data types like images and text, the model can use the inherent structure that already exists in the data.

Curved surfaces and shortest paths

A curved surface is one where nearby points represent similar data items, even though the surface is embedded in high-dimensional space. The hard problem is: how do you preserve this curved-surface structure in learned representations while handling real data that is sparse, noisy, and may shift distribution? And how do you compute distances and paths on these curved surfaces once you learn them?

The Bi-Lipschitz Autoencoder paper ensures that the mapping between the high-dimensional data space and the low-dimensional representation respects distances in a controlled way. This matters because when data points get further apart in the original space, their representations also stay far apart, which preserves the underlying manifold structure. It handles sparsity—where you only see some combinations of data—and distribution shifts without breaking this property. Geometric Flow Grounding takes manifolds further by decoupling different sources of variation. In a gene expression system, flow (the change over time) lives on one direction while other structure lives elsewhere, and by separating them you can recover the true biological motifs without numerical confusion. It even enables detecting fake videos by measuring how well real video dynamics fit the learned manifold. Geodesic Calculus on Implicitly Defined Latent Manifolds solves a practical problem: once you learn a manifold inside an autoencoder, how do you compute geodesics (shortest paths on the curved surface)? This framework makes that computation stable and accurate across different autoencoder types, so you can measure distances between data points the way they naturally flow on the manifold.

Symmetry and equivariance

Many data domains have symmetries: rotating an image rotates its representation by the same amount, or permuting elements in a sequence should permute the representation similarly. Exploiting these symmetries makes models more sample-efficient and interpretable because the network learns to respect what the data itself respects.

Flow Equivariant World Models build a memory for video prediction that respects symmetries in the underlying physical or visual dynamics. Instead of memorizing arbitrary patterns, the model learns to represent transformations equivariantly, which means symmetries in the input flow through to symmetries in the learned state. This helps even on partially observed video where you can't see everything because the model can infer hidden parts based on the symmetries it learned. Recurrent Equivariant Constraint Modulation takes a more flexible approach: it learns symmetry layer by layer. On data that has full symmetries, the model recovers full equivariance automatically. But when data only has approximate symmetries—real data often does—the network can relax the constraint, gaining expressiveness while still respecting what symmetry is present. This balances structure and flexibility.

Distance metrics and structured representations

Sometimes the structure you want to capture is not hierarchy or smoothness but more abstract relationships: permutations (orderings of elements), probability distributions, or aligned representations of multiple data views. These methods handle these by choosing the right space and distance metric for the problem.

Learning Distributions over Permutations and Rankings tackles a challenge where the output space is permutations—orderings of objects—not vectors. Classical vector representations break down here because permutations have their own structure. This paper learns factorized representations that respect permutation structure, winning on jigsaw puzzle assembly and ranking tasks. Wasserstein Geodesic Principal Component Analysis takes probability distributions as the objects to analyze. Instead of treating distributions as flat vectors, it works in Wasserstein space where distance measures how much you need to transport one distribution to match another. PCA in this space discovers the main directions of variation in distributions themselves, revealing structure invisible in standard PCA. Prompt Tuning for CLIP on the Pretrained Manifold keeps large vision models on their learned curved surface—the surface where the pretrained CLIP model lives. When you tune prompts for new tasks, keeping adjustments small and aligned with what CLIP already knows reuses what the pretrained model learned, which means transfers to unseen classes and across domains work better because the model still remembers its original knowledge.

Properties of learned representation spaces

As networks train and learn representations, the representation space itself changes—points can collapse to the same location, curvature changes, and structure emerges. Understanding and controlling these properties improves learning and generalization because you can deliberately design spaces where the network's learned patterns are more stable and transfer better to new tasks.

Beyond Point-wise Neural Collapse adds topology awareness to hierarchical classifiers when learning continuously. Neural collapse is when different class representations squeeze to the same point—normally bad for learning. This paper adds structure that respects hierarchies: similar classes stay closer while distant classes stay far apart, which means the model learns distinct patterns for each class even when new ones arrive. This lets it generalize to new classes without forgetting old ones because the hierarchical structure keeps old classes anchored far from new ones. KromHC uses manifold constraints (curved-surface constraints) and Kronecker products to build deep networks efficiently. By constraining connections to respect curved-surface structure, it cuts parameters dramatically—from cubic to quadratic complexity—while staying competitive or better than standard methods. The Projection Heads and Representation Collapse paper studies how the shape of projection heads affects collapse and curvature in the final layers of self-supervised models. Smooth projection heads create negative curvature, which mathematically makes collapse unstable, so representations naturally spread out. Linear or ReLU heads work differently, relying on batch normalization to prevent collapse, which means the stability mechanism itself depends on the head shape.
