Guidance Without Classifiers for Human-Object Interaction Animation

Instead of training separate classifier networks to guide animation generation, let the denoising speed itself guide which parts of the motion to refine first.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

When an AI generates a person grabbing a cup, it must coordinate hand position, body posture, and object location all at once. Older methods use separate neural networks trained as classifiers to enforce contact rules. This paper shows you do not need them: if you denoise different parts of the motion at different speeds, the cleaner parts automatically guide the noisier ones through a communication layer that lets parts look at each other and borrow information, creating contact-aware behavior without any extra training.

The coordination puzzle

Generating a realistic animation of a person interacting with an object is hard because the AI must control many things at once: the pose of the human body, the motion of the fingers, and the location and rotation of the object. All three must agree at each moment in time, or the result looks broken—fingers pass through the cup, the hand floats above the grab point, or the body twists impossibly.

Older methods train external classifier networks that watch the animation unfold and give feedback, saying things like: is the hand touching the object? Is the grip stable? These classifiers are handcrafted for specific contact rules and require labeling data for each new scenario. A beginner would try the same thing: train a classifier to recognize good contact and use it to steer the generation. But this is fragile—the classifier overfits to one shape of object or one type of grasp, and fails when you try a new object shape or task.

The paper's core idea: Use different noise levels for different parts of the motion (body, hands, object), so the parts that denoise faster (cleaner parts) guide the slower-denoising parts through a communication mechanism that lets parts look at each other. Clearer information dominates this communication, so the body learns to align with the hands because the model's focus always prefers the clearest signal available.

Separate body skeleton, hand joints, and object into different components.

Hands start cleaner (lower noise) than body and object (higher noise).

Denoise in lockstep but at different speeds

Cleaner guides noisier via cross-attention

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of training a separate classifier network to guide diffusion, the paper assigns different noise levels to different motion components (body, hands, object) and lets them denoise asynchronously. The cleaner, faster-denoising parts (hands) naturally guide the noisier, slower parts through the communication mechanism that is already part of the diffusion model, because the model learns to focus on whichever input is clearest and most reliable.

The hidden quantity

The denoising pace—guidance without asking

measured quantity  =  Denoising speed per component

When you denoise a noisy image or animation, you progressively reduce the noise level over many steps. Most methods denoise all parts at the same pace. This paper measures something new: how fast each component (hand, body, object) reduces its noise. If hands denoise faster, they reach a clear state before the body and object do, which means the hand position is reliable and stable while the body position is still uncertain and shifting.

The paper uses this pace difference to create guidance. It does not train a classifier to enforce contact rules. Instead, it relies on the fact that attention—the mechanism by which parts of the motion communicate—will preferentially attend to clearer information. Clearer hands anchor noisier body and object motions to realistic positions.

Why it works

Why cleaner guides noisier

Imagine you have two people trying to draw the same scene: one has glasses (clear view) and one is fog-bound (blurry view). If they stand next to each other and the clear-sighted person points, the blurry person can align their sketch to match. The blurry person's brain does the work, but it is anchored by the clearer information next to them.

In this method, hands are the clear-sighted person and the body is fog-bound. At each denoising step, the model has a communication layer (transformer with a mechanism called cross-attention): it lets body position numbers look at hand position numbers and see what the hand is doing. A hand at position X attracts the body to position X because the transformer learns that bodies should follow hands. This happens not because a classifier said so, but because during training, valid animations show this pattern—bodies naturally follow hands. The transformer simply learns to copy what the clearer part is doing. Since hands are always clearer (less noisy), they dominate this communication and naturally guide body and object positions into contact.

Math after the picture

The math of asynchronous noise

First in words:

Loss = reconstruction error + bone-length penalty + contact penalty + velocity penalty, where each motion component has its own noise coefficient that decreases at its own rate.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_recon + w_bone * L_bone + w_contact * L_contact + w_velocity * L_velocity

Every symbol:

L_reconreconstruction error, how far predicted motion is from ground truth
w_bone, w_contact, w_velocityweight factors controlling how much to penalize bone deviation, contact errors, and jerky motion
lambda_handnoise level for hand component (decreases fast)
lambda_bodynoise level for body component (decreases slow)
lambda_objectnoise level for object component (decreases slowest)
Cross-attentionmechanism that lets one component 'look at' another and borrow its information
A tiny worked example
Hand noise schedule: lambda = [0.9, 0.6, 0.3, 0.0] (goes to zero fast)
Body noise schedule: lambda = [0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6] (decreases slowly)
At step 2: hand lambda = 0.3 (clear), body lambda = 0.7 (blurry)
Body position numbers look at hand position numbers through communication layer; hand's clear information dominates
Body's predicted hand position aligns to actual hand position because attention weight on clear hand is highest
Over iterations, body and object cluster around the clearer hand trajectory

Each motion part has its own noise level; cleaner parts (lower noise) naturally attract the attention of noisier parts (higher noise), creating contact guidance without a separate classifier.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hands too much faster than body. If hand noise drops to zero while body is still very noisy, the body may not have time to converge to the hand's location. The hand is a fixed target and body oscillates around it instead of settling.
All components denoise at the same speed. If you remove the staggered schedules and denoise everything together, cross-attention has no ordering information to follow. The model must learn contact rules from the training data alone, which is fragile on new object geometries.
Text prompt is vague or contradictory. If the prompt says grab and punch, the hand may be pulled by conflicting goals. Staggered noise helps but cannot resolve an impossible request, because the underlying training data does not contain examples of simultaneous grabbing and punching, so no amount of communication between clearer and noisier parts will invent a plausible sequence.
Tuned right: schedule gaps match object size scale. When the schedule gap (hand speed minus body speed) is calibrated to the typical distance from hand to body in the training data, convergence is smooth and contact accuracy is high.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a sequence of 50 frames of a human hand grasping and lifting one of three unseen object shapes: a cylinder, a sphere, or an irregular blob. For each object, sample 10 times using the text prompt grab and lift the object carefully. Measure (1) how often the hand penetrates the object (should be near zero), (2) how often the fingertips are within 2 centimeters of the object surface (contact recall), and (3) how often the hand position drifts more than 3 centimeters between frames (smoothness). Also generate the same 30 sequences using the same model but with all components forced to use the same noise schedule (ablation), and compare.

Contact_fidelity = 100 - penetration_rate_percent, Contact_recall = fingertips_within_2cm / total_frames, Smoothness = 1 - drift_exceeding_3cm

The staggered schedule should show penetration near zero, contact recall above 85%, and smooth motion. The same-schedule ablation should show penetration above 15%, contact recall below 60%, and jerky motion.
