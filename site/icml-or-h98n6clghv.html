DRFusion: Drift-Resilient Video Fusion

Instead of trying to align frames with optical flow, DRFusion learns to predict each infrared-visible frame from its history, avoiding drift by treating fusion as motion generation.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

When you fuse infrared and visible video in real time, small mistakes pile up—a tiny blur in frame 1 becomes a ghostly smear by frame 10. Existing methods either lock frames in place with rigid alignment (which breaks in dynamic scenes) or process each frame alone (losing the motion context that keeps things steady). DRFusion fixes this by learning to generate the fused frame directly from what came before, treating fusion not as alignment but as conditional prediction.

The drift problem: mistakes that grow over time

Fusing infrared (heat) and visible (color) video is hard because the two streams move differently. Infrared sees heat, visible sees edges—they do not naturally align.

Early methods tried optical flow (computing where pixels moved between frames) to keep alignment rigid. But optical flow breaks when motion is fast or objects are hidden—forced alignment creates ghosting artifacts and structural distortions.

Modern diffusion models (generative models that build images step by step) can fuse frames well in isolation, but when run frame-by-frame on a video without explicit temporal wiring, errors creep in. A small blur in frame 1 is not corrected by frame 2; instead, it is carried forward as a seed for the next blur. By frame 10, the video is unusable drift.

The naive approach fails because: Processing each video frame in a generative model without connecting it to the frame before it causes error accumulation—the model can generate beautiful single frames but has no intrinsic way to keep them temporally consistent.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Train a noise-resistant compressed representation

Compress both infrared and visible video to a dense numerical summary (a compressed representation, like reducing a photo to its essential numbers) while enforcing smooth motion between frames using optical flow hints—like a video autofocus that smooths frame-to-frame jitter.

Stage 2: Teach the model to ignore flow, focus on history

Train a lightweight adapter (a small numerical patch) on top of a frozen generative model so it learns to predict the fused frame from a sliding window of frames that came before it.

Soft Temporal Anchoring during generation

Repurpose the model's attention mechanism (the part that decides where to look) to create three overlapping windows of history. Each attention layer uses these windows as soft anchors—like three slightly shifted mirrors reflecting the same scene.

Spectral filtering of motion

The overlapping windows implicitly filter out high-frequency noise (flickering) while preserving low-frequency motion (actual scene changes), without ever computing optical flow explicitly.

Refine structure to match infrared

At inference, slightly nudge the fused image to preserve infrared structure (edges and textures) while keeping the temporal smoothness learned from history.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the architecture used to process video fusion: from independent frame-by-frame processing to history-conditioned prediction. Instead of a 2D model that sees each frame alone, DRFusion uses a 3D temporal model that explicitly encodes the previous frames (frames that came before the current one) as context.

The hidden quantity: motion continuity score

The paper does not name a single hidden quantity but implicitly measures frame-to-frame consistency. DRFusion learns to predict the warping error (how much each pixel should shift between frames) using optical flow computed during Stage 1 training. The paper estimates this via temporal smoothness constraints on the latent space (the compressed representation) and validates it with metrics like structural similarity (SSIM) and temporal coherence scores across benchmark datasets.

Why it works

Why learning motion history beats rigid alignment

Rigid optical flow breaks because it assumes pixels move in straight lines at constant speed—a false assumption when objects rotate, deform, or vanish. Forcing this assumption causes ghosting and structural artifacts.

DRFusion avoids the trap by learning the motion pattern implicitly. By conditioning on history, the generative model learns: when frames shift like this in the past, what does the next frame look like? The model discovers non-linear, scene-aware motion without being told.

The Soft Temporal Anchoring mechanism (three overlapping history windows passed to attention layers) acts as a low-pass filter. High-frequency jitter—tiny flickers that bounce around—gets averaged out across the windows. Low-frequency motion—genuine scene changes—passes through because it is consistent across all three windows. This is spectral filtering without rigid alignment.

Finally, the two-stage training decouples learning: Stage 1 builds a clean latent space (like a noise-free blueprint), and Stage 2 teaches the generative model to use it. This separation prevents the model from memorizing input noise and amplifying it over time.

Math after the picture

The temporal smoothness principle

First in words:

occlusion_aware_latent_warping_loss = sum of (distance between compressed frame T and compressed frame T minus 1, warped by optical flow, weighted by occlusion mask)

Then the compact form:

L_warp = ||z_t - W(z_{t-1}, flow_{t-1->t}) * mask_t||^2

Every symbol:

z_tthe compressed summary of frame t
z_{t-1}the compressed summary of the previous frame
flow_{t-1->t}optical flow: how each pixel moved from frame t-1 to frame t
W(...)warp operation: shift each pixel according to optical flow
mask_tocclusion mask: which pixels are hidden (1 = visible, 0 = hidden)
L_warpthe mismatch penalty (how much the frames differ after flow-based alignment)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the compressed representation encodes each frame as 100 numbers. At time t-1, frame is [0.5, 0.3, ..., 0.2].
At time t, frame is [0.52, 0.29, ..., 0.18]. Optical flow says pixel 1 moved +2 units right.
After warping the frame from before by flow, we get [0.5, 0.3, ..., 0.2]_warped.
Difference: [0.52-0.5, 0.29-0.3, ..., 0.18-0.2] = [0.02, -0.01, ..., -0.02].
Squared: [0.0004, 0.0001, ..., 0.0004]. Sum = 0.0008. This is L_warp—small because motion is smooth.
If the model produces erratic encodings (frame t is [0.9, 0.1, ..., 0.1]), squared difference is [0.16, 0.04, ..., 0.01], sum = 0.21—huge penalty.
During training, the model learns compressed representations that change predictably, avoiding abrupt jumps.

The latent space (compressed representation) is trained to evolve smoothly across frames. This gives the generative model a clean foundation—motion information is already baked in before the model even starts predicting.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stage 1 latent space is noisy. If the initial compression is jerky (high-frequency jitter baked in), the history windows cannot filter it out. The model learns bad motion patterns from corrupted training data. Solution: pre-train the VAE on high-quality video first.
History window size mismatch. If the three overlapping windows are too small (e.g., only 1 frame back), they do not capture genuine motion and over-filter real changes, because a single frame before it is not enough to distinguish between random jitter and true motion. If too large, they include outdated context that contradicts the current frame. Solution: tune window size to scene dynamics (faster scenes need smaller windows because rapid motion changes quickly and old frames become misleading).
Infrared structure loss during refinement. If the refinement step (nudging the fused image to preserve infrared edges) is too aggressive, it undoes the temporal smoothing. If too weak, infrared detail is lost. Solution: adaptive weighting based on local infrared contrast.
Done right. The model learns motion continuity without rigid constraints, attends to history without being locked to it, and preserves both temporal smoothness and structural fidelity.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small video fusion pipeline: download one sequence of aligned infrared and visible frames (e.g., from the M3SVD or HDO dataset), run the pre-trained model on 30 frames, and measure two things: (1) SSIM score between the fused frame and a ground-truth fused reference (structure quality), and (2) temporal stability via optical flow magnitude between consecutive fused frames (flickering metric).

temporal_stability = mean(L1_distance(fused_{t}, fused_{t-1})) over all frames; lower = smoother. structural_quality = SSIM(fused_t, reference_t) over all frames; higher = better.

Run the same 30 frames through a naive frame-by-frame diffusion model (no history). The frame-by-frame version will have high SSIM but low temporal stability (many flickers). DRFusion should show: high SSIM AND high stability, proving that history conditioning filters jitter without sacrificing detail.
