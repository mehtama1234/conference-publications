Common Corpus: Building an Open, Legal LLM Training Dataset

This paper shows how to collect two trillion training tokens from fully open and legally permissible sources—the largest dataset of its kind.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Large language models need enormous amounts of text to learn from. But most training data today comes from copyrighted books, news articles, and proprietary websites, which means the trained models cannot legally be shared as open-source. Common Corpus solves this by assembling two trillion words from sources you can legally use: Wikipedia, open-licensed repositories, government data, academic papers, and creative-commons material. The dataset spans 100+ languages, making it the first truly open and auditable training corpus at this scale.

Why models trained on scraped data can't be open-source

Today's largest language models are trained on trillions of words scraped from the internet. This includes copyrighted books, news archives, academic papers behind paywalls, and proprietary website content—most of which the model creators never actually have permission to use. The legal argument was 'fair use' (that using copyrighted material to train AI counts as transformation), but that defense is collapsing. The New York Times successfully sued OpenAI; publishing houses are blocking scrapers; and researchers are removing datasets because they contain copyright violations or other illegal material.

This creates a trap: a model trained on scraped data is legally risky to release as open-source (you cannot guarantee the training data was licensed). A beginner would try to just scrape more data and hope no one sues. It does not work because the legal risk remains, and the takedown risk is real—entire datasets have vanished overnight.

The real problem. Most training data today is legally uncertain; you cannot release an open model trained on it without legal exposure.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: List all permissible sources

Wikipedia, public code repositories, government databases, open-access academic repositories, creative-commons licensed websites.

Step 2: Crawl and retrieve

Download full text from each source using APIs or respectful HTML crawling (not aggressive scraping).

Step 3: Filter by language and format

Remove non-text files; keep languages from high-resource (English, French) to low-resource (Uyghur, Amharic).

Step 4: Clean and deduplicate

Remove near-duplicate documents, fix encoding errors, strip personal data, check for toxicity and illegal content.

Step 5: Tokenize and validate

Convert text to tokens (short numerical codes); train two small models to verify the dataset works.

What the paper changes

What the paper actually changes

The paper changes the SOURCE of training data. Instead of scraping any website and claiming fair use, it builds a dataset only from legally permissible origins—Wikipedia, open-license repositories, public government data, published open-access papers, and creative-commons text. The legal status is clear by construction: every word in Common Corpus came from a source you can legally use.

the object it moves  =  training data sources
The hidden quantity

The legal surface area of the training data

measured quantity  =  Percentage of legally permissible tokens

The paper's hidden quantity is: how much of the training data comes from sources you are legally allowed to use? In existing datasets, this is often unknown or low (much of it is scraped copyrighted material). In Common Corpus, it is 100 percent—every token has a clear, permissible license or public-domain status.

The paper estimates this by tracking the provenance (origin) of every source: Wikipedia (public domain), GitHub (permissively licensed code), arXiv (open-access papers), Wikidata (CC0 public domain), government websites (public records), and so on. If a source's legal status is ambiguous, it is excluded.

Why it works

Why legal compliance at this scale is now possible

For the first time, three things aligned: (1) there is now enough publicly available, open-licensed text on the internet—Wikipedia alone has grown to billions of words, open-source code repositories host trillions of tokens, government agencies publish vast archives; (2) the legal and ethical pressure is clear—copyright holders are suing and removing datasets, forcing a reckoning; (3) the tools exist to identify and verify licenses at scale—you can now programmatically check that a source is open-access, download its license metadata, and confirm compliance. Before, any one of these was missing. Now all three are true, and it becomes economically and technically possible to build a two-trillion-token dataset from only permissible sources.

The mechanism works because: (a) clear permission removes legal uncertainty (the trained model can be released as open-source without fear of takedowns or lawsuits); (b) scale is achieved by aggregating many permissible sources (Wikipedia + code + government data + academia + creative-commons, not relying on any single risky source); (c) multilingual diversity comes automatically (many permissible sources are not in English—government data spans all nations, open-access repositories are global). The result is a dataset that can be used to train open models that are auditable and legally defensible.

The legal surface area formula

First in words:

legal_coverage = (tokens_from_permissible_sources) / (total_tokens_in_dataset) × 100%

Then the compact form:

L = sum(T_wiki + T_github + T_arxiv + T_gov + T_cc) / T_total × 100%

A tiny worked example
Suppose a dataset has 1000 tokens total.
Wikipedia contributes 250 tokens (permissible).
GitHub contributes 200 tokens (permissible).
arXiv contributes 150 tokens (permissible).
Government data contributes 200 tokens (permissible).
Creative commons contributes 100 tokens (permissible).
Total permissible: 250 + 200 + 150 + 200 + 100 = 900 tokens.
Legal coverage: (900 / 1000) × 100% = 90%.
Common Corpus achieves 100% coverage (only permissible sources, no risky material).

The formula simply asks: what fraction of your training tokens come from sources you have legal permission to use? Common Corpus answers: all of them.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hidden licensing traps. A source claims to be open-access but has restrictions you missed (e.g., a derivative work requires attribution in a way that complicates redistribution). Mitigation: human legal review of major sources, not just automated license parsing.
Toxicity and harmful content in open data. Wikipedia, code repositories, and forums contain slurs, spam, and abusive text. Simply including it exposes users to harm. Mitigation: the paper applies toxicity filters (though it notes perfect accuracy is impossible).
Personal data in government records. Open government data sometimes leaks citizens' names, addresses, or health records. Using it to train models could expose individuals. Mitigation: detect and remove personally identifiable information (PII) before including text.
Imbalance across languages. English Wikipedia and GitHub are vast; Amharic and Uyghur open data are tiny. Simply collecting all permissible sources yields huge imbalance. Mitigation: intentionally oversample low-resource language sources (trade off some scale for equity).
Done right. Track every source's license and legal status; apply automated toxicity and PII detection; balance across languages intentionally; publish the full provenance so anyone can audit it.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should: (1) start with a list of candidate sources (Wikipedia, GitHub, arXiv, government websites); (2) automatically check the license metadata for each (Wikipedia is CC-BY-SA, arXiv is CC-0, GitHub allows permissive licenses, government is public domain), because known licenses guarantee you can use the data; (3) download a sample of text from each; (4) run a toxicity detector and a PII detector to flag harmful or private data, because including such material would harm users or violate their privacy; (5) convert the cleaned text to numerical codes and count total words by source; (6) report the legal coverage percentage (should be 100% if all sources are permissible). Then train a tiny language model (e.g., a 100-million-weight model, where each weight is a number controlling the model's behavior) on the cleaned dataset and measure its accuracy on a standard benchmark (e.g., how well it predicts the next word in a held-out text sample). Finally, show that the legal coverage is fully transparent: print a table showing where every word came from and why it is legally safe to use, because transparency lets anyone verify your claims and trust the model.

legal_coverage % = (permissible_tokens) / (total_tokens) × 100%; model_benchmark = accuracy_on_held_out_text

The demo must make impossible to miss: we collected two trillion legal training tokens, no scraped risky material, and trained models on it that work well. You can release these models open-source without legal fear.
