Bridging Past and Future: Distribution-Aware Alignment for Time Series Forecasting

Fix the mismatch between past data patterns and future ones by teaching the model to reconstruct the past through a lens tuned to the future.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you forecast the next month of sales, your historical data (past years of sales) often has different patterns than what you are trying to predict (next month). Time series forecasters train on this past data but do not explicitly account for the fact that its statistics may differ from the future. TimaAlign corrects this by adding a reconstruction task (learn to rebuild the past) that tunes internal features to match future patterns, then feeds these adjusted features into any existing forecaster.

Zero assumption version

The pattern mismatch in time series: past versus future

In time series forecasting, you train a model on years of historical data (the input) to predict the next few days or weeks (the output). The problem: the statistics of historical data often differ from future data. For instance, sales patterns in a pandemic year look different from normal years. A naive forecaster sees the input and output as drawing from the same underlying pattern, so it uses raw historical data directly. This blind spot means the model spends learning capacity matching historical patterns that do not generalize to the shifted future.

A beginner might try just retraining on more recent data or using heavier-weight samples from recent years — but these are band-aids. The real issue is that the model's internal features (its learned representations) are tuned to past statistics, not future ones.

In one sentence. When historical data and future data have different statistics (different patterns), a standard forecaster wastes learning capacity on patterns that will not match the real target.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Observe the mismatch

Compute statistical properties (frequency content, autocorrelation) of past vs. future.

Add auxiliary reconstruction

Insert a second task: given past data, predict past itself through a shared feature layer.

Share the feature layer

Past-reconstruction task and future-forecasting task both read/write the same internal features.

Joint training

Train both tasks together; reconstruction loss nudges features to bridge the past-future pattern gap.

Deploy the forecaster

At test time, use only the forecasting task; the adjusted features now match future statistics.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The method modifies the internal features (the learned representations sitting between input and output) by routing them through an auxiliary reconstruction task during training. The forecaster itself (the mapping from features to predictions) stays fixed; only the feature layer is tuned.

the object it moves  =  internal representations
The hidden quantity

The frequency mismatch: how much does spectral content shift?

measured quantity  =  frequency alignment gap

A time series' frequency content is the breakdown of how much signal energy lives at each time scale (daily, weekly, yearly, etc.). When historical data comes from one regime and future data from another, their frequency spectra differ. TimaAlign measures this gap by comparing the Fourier spectrum (which scales are strong) of the input and target. Large gaps mean the model must learn a hidden adaptation; small gaps mean the standard approach already mostly works.

The paper estimates this by looking at which frequency components contribute most to reconstruction error. Bands where the past has strong signals but the future does not (or vice versa) define the mismatch. The reconstruction task, by forcing the model to predict past data, implicitly learns to downweight dead frequencies and upweight future ones.

Why it works

Why reconstruction aligns past and future patterns

Here is the causal chain. During training, the forecaster learns to map input features to target forecasts. But the features come raw from the input, which is tuned to past statistics. If past and future differ, this mismatch sits in the features: they carry past-relevant information that is irrelevant or misleading for the future. Adding a reconstruction task says: given these same features, also predict the input itself. Now the features must satisfy two demands: be good for predicting the future AND for reconstructing the past. This dual pressure forces the features to find a representation (a code) that works for both. Since reconstruction pulls the features toward the input's statistics and forecasting pulls toward the output's statistics, the features settle into a neutral ground—or better, a ground tuned to the shared structure that both past and future exhibit. When the future differs only in frequency mix (not in the underlying causal structure), reconstruction naturally de-emphasizes the frequencies that matter only to the past, because those frequencies are not needed to explain both tasks.

Concretely: if the past has strong weekly cycles but the future does not, a feature detector tuned to weekly pulses will be good for reconstructing the past but bad for forecasting the future. Joint training penalizes this detector (high reconstruction loss on the past means the feature was wasted). The model gradually redirects that capacity to features that explain both the past and the future, which means the truly predictive frequencies.

The joint loss that aligns features

First in words:

Total loss is a weighted sum of forecast loss and reconstruction loss. Forecast loss measures how far the prediction is from the real future. Reconstruction loss measures how far the rebuilt past is from the real input. Both losses flow back into the shared feature layer, pulling it in both directions until it settles into features that serve both jobs.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_forecast(output, target_future) + alpha * L_reconstruct(reconstructed_past, input)

Every symbol:

L_forecast(output, target_future)error between predicted future and true future (measured as mean squared error or similar)
L_reconstruct(reconstructed_past, input)error between rebuilt past and original input
alphaweight controlling how hard the reconstruction task pulls (if alpha is high, past-matching dominates; if low, future forecasting dominates)
outputthe model's forecast (the predicted future time series)
target_futurethe real future time series you are trying to predict
reconstructed_pastthe rebuilt input (what the reconstructor outputs)
inputthe original historical time series (what you started with)
A tiny worked example
Suppose on one batch: L_forecast = 0.8 (forecast is off by an average amount), L_reconstruct = 0.4 (past rebuilt reasonably well).
Set alpha = 0.5 (balance both tasks equally after scaling).
L_total = 0.8 + 0.5 * 0.4 = 0.8 + 0.2 = 1.0.
Gradient flows back: forecaster head gets nudged to improve future predictions (from the 0.8 term), reconstructor head gets nudged to improve past reconstruction (from the 0.2 term), and the shared encoder is pulled by both, settling into a middle ground.
As training progresses, if frequency mismatch is causing forecast error, reconstruction loss stays high for past-only frequencies, and training redirects those feature detectors to capture future-relevant frequencies instead.

The shared encoder is pulled by both forecast and reconstruction gradients, naturally discovering features that bridge the past-future gap.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reconstruction weight set too high. If alpha is too large, the model focuses so hard on rebuilding the past that it neglects the future forecast. The shared features become good at capturing historical noise, not future signals.
No actual mismatch between past and future. On datasets where historical and future statistics are already aligned (e.g., stationary, unchanging patterns), the reconstruction task adds overhead without benefit. The paper shows gains vary by dataset—some have high mismatch, some do not.
Reconstruction task is too easy. If the past is trivially predictable (pure white noise or random walk), reconstructing it teaches the model nothing useful about future structure. The reconstruction must capture real structure.
Done right. Tune alpha by validation: pick the weight that balances forecast error and reconstruction error. Check that the dataset has genuine past-future mismatch (e.g., regime shift, seasonal change) before using TimaAlign. On aligned datasets, standard forecasting is simpler and just as good.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a time series dataset where the first half (training input) and second half (target future) have visibly different frequency content. For example, generate a series where the first 100 steps have strong weekly cycles and the second 100 steps have only daily cycles. Train a baseline forecaster and a TimaAlign forecaster on the first 100 steps, then forecast the next 50 steps (drawn from the second regime). Measure forecast error on those future steps.

improvement = (baseline_error - timeAlign_error) / baseline_error × 100 percent

The demo must make it obvious that when past and future differ, reconstruction pulls the model toward the right patterns, cutting error by 20–50 percent.
