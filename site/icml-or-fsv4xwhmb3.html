Training Deep Spiking Neural Networks without Normalization

A smart initialization trick that lets you train very deep spiking neural networks without needing the plumbing that usually stabilizes them.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Spiking neural networks are a class of AI models that mimic how biological neurons work — they fire when their inputs cross a threshold. Deep ones have always needed batch normalization (a heavy preprocessing step that rescales data during training) to keep their signals stable enough to learn. This paper shows that the real problem is not batch normalization itself but the careless way weights are initialized at startup. A better initialization strategy removes the need for batch normalization entirely, even in 1000-layer networks, because if weights start at the scale where charge and its rate of change are naturally stable, the network stays stable throughout training without any rescaling.

Zero assumption version

Deep spiking networks become unstable without heavy preprocessing

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are models where each neuron fires discrete events (spikes) when its accumulated electrical charge crosses a threshold, mimicking biological brains. Deep SNNs should be more expressive than shallow ones, but they have a concrete problem: signals explode or vanish during training. A naive beginner would just initialize the weights randomly and train. The network quickly becomes unstable. Gradients (which-direction nudges for learning) blow up or shrink to zero. Firing rates (how often neurons spike) either jam constant-on or stop firing entirely.

The traditional fix is batch normalization: before each layer processes data, rescale it so it sits in a narrow range. This works, but it is cumbersome. It requires processing many examples at once in a batch. Variable-length inputs (sequences of different lengths) break it. Small batches make it unreliable. None of these constraints should be necessary if the network were just initialized better in the first place, because batch normalization is only a bandage for poor initialization — if weights are set to the scale where signals naturally stay centered, no rescaling is needed.

In one sentence. SpikeInit initializes weights and gradient curves to keep signals stable from the first forward pass onward, eliminating the need for batch normalization.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure the charge curve

For each layer's neuron type, compute how its charge (accumulated input) responds to different weight scales.

Measure the gradient curve

Compute how the gradient (the learning nudge) changes as weights scale, accounting for the surrogate function used during backward training.

Find the stable zone

Find the weight scale that keeps charge centered and gradient magnitude moderate across all layers.

Initialize weights to that scale

Set all weights at startup using the derived formula, not random uniform.

Train without normalization

Forward and backward passes proceed with stable signals. Batch size, sequence length, and batch norm are no longer needed.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The object is the weight initialization formula (how you set the weights when training starts). Instead of drawing weights uniformly at random, SpikeInit derives a specific scale based on the spiking function's response curve. This single change cascades through all downstream training.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Stable charge and stable gradients

measured quantity  =  charge and gradient stability zones

Spiking neurons work by accumulating inputs (charge) until it crosses a threshold and they fire. The 'charge curve' describes how this threshold-crossing behavior responds to different weight scales. If weights are too large, charge explodes and neurons jam firing constant-on. If weights are too small, charge starves and neurons never fire. There is a sweet spot — a weight scale where charge stays centered and firing rate stays healthy. Similarly, during learning, the gradient (the update signal) has a curve: it depends on how weights scale. A gradient that is too large overshoots; one that is too small disappears into randomness. The sweet spot for training stability is the weight scale where gradients flow at healthy magnitude.

SpikeInit estimates both curves by analyzing the spiking function and the surrogate gradient formula (a smooth approximation used during backward learning through discrete spikes). It solves for the weight scale that satisfies both constraints simultaneously. This is the hidden work: the paper measures these invisible response curves and uses them to initialize better than chance, because if you know how the charge and gradient respond to weight scale, you can pick the one initialization value that keeps both healthy from the start instead of hoping randomness works.

Why it works

Why it works: stable signals from the start

Batch normalization works because it rescales data during training whenever signals drift. But this is reactive — it fixes problems after they appear. SpikeInit is proactive: it chooses the initial weight scale so that signals stay stable from the very first forward pass. When weights are initialized to the right scale, the charge curve naturally keeps neuron firing rates centered and moderate. Gradients, which depend on both charge and the surrogate function, also stay at healthy magnitude. Because both the forward signal and the backward gradient signal are stable from the start, training converges smoothly. No rescaling step is needed, and the constraints that batch normalization imposes (large batch, fixed-length sequences) vanish.

The mechanism works because the spiking function and surrogate gradient are deterministic mathematical functions. SpikeInit analyzes their slopes and curvatures and solves for the initialization weight scale that sits in both of their stable zones simultaneously. It is a one-time calculation at the start of training. Once initialized, the network trains normally, but because it started in the right place, it stays on track.

Math after the picture

The initialization formula

First in words:

Weight scale is chosen to keep charge centered and gradient magnitude moderate. For a layer with spiking neurons, the weight initialization standard deviation is set to a value derived from the neuron membrane time constant and the surrogate gradient function slope.

Then the compact form:

sigma_w = sqrt( 1 / (n * C_mem * alpha) )

Every symbol:

sigma_wstandard deviation for initializing weights
nnumber of incoming connections to a neuron (fan-in)
C_memmembrane time constant of the spiking neuron
alphaslope of the surrogate gradient at the threshold
A tiny worked example
Layer 1: n=100 inputs per neuron, C_mem=0.5, alpha=0.25. sigma_w = sqrt(1 / (100 * 0.5 * 0.25)) = sqrt(1 / 12.5) = sqrt(0.08) ≈ 0.28
Layer 2: n=256 inputs, same C_mem=0.5, alpha=0.25. sigma_w = sqrt(1 / (256 * 0.5 * 0.25)) = sqrt(1 / 32) = sqrt(0.03125) ≈ 0.18
Deeper layers (more inputs) get smaller initialization to control charge growth. Charge stays centered instead of exploding.

This formula shows that wider layers need smaller weights to keep charge stable, because wider layers receive more input summed together, so each individual weight contributes less to avoid summing to a huge charge, which would jam neurons on or cause them to stop firing.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Initialized to the wrong scale. If the weight standard deviation does not match the charge and gradient curves, signals become unstable again. SpikeInit must correctly estimate the neuron membrane constant and surrogate gradient slope.
Network topology changes mid-training. SpikeInit optimizes for a specific layer width and depth. If layer widths vary unpredictably (as in dynamic networks), the initialization may become suboptimal for later layers.
Very deep networks with poor residual pathways. SpikeInit stabilizes charge and gradients, but if gradients cannot flow through very deep layers (no shortcuts), training can still stall despite good initialization.
Done right. Use SpikeInit to set the initial weight scale based on your spiking neuron model and surrogate gradient. Monitor early training to confirm signals stay stable. For very deep networks, combine with residual connections or normalization-free designs that have worked before.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple spiking neural network (10 layers, spiking LIF neurons) on a standard benchmark (CIFAR-10 or a neuromorphic dataset like MNIST). Train two versions: one with random initialization, one with SpikeInit initialization. Both without batch normalization. Track: (1) average neuron firing rate per layer over the first epoch (should stay in range 0.1–0.5 with SpikeInit; should explode or vanish with random); (2) average gradient magnitude per layer (should stay moderate with SpikeInit; should diverge with random); (3) convergence speed and final accuracy. The key is to show that with SpikeInit, charge and gradient stay healthy across layers, permitting stable training from the start.

stability = (layers_with_healthy_firing_rate / total_layers) * 100%, converged = (loss_decreases_monotonically == true)
