EMFormer: Forecasting the Far Future Without Forgetting the Recent

A weather model learns to predict months ahead by training on both short- and long-term patterns together, so it does not unlearn what works for now to chase what works later.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Weather forecasting is hard because a model trained to predict far into the future often forgets how to predict the next day well — like a student cramming for an exam next month but bombing today's quiz. This paper introduces EMFormer, a transformer (a model that attends to many past patterns at once) that learns both short and long forecasts in one go, using a clever training recipe that keeps both skills sharp.

The Forgetting Problem

Weather forecasters face a dilemma. To predict what will happen in 30 days, a model needs to learn long-range patterns — big pressure systems that take weeks to form. But those patterns are rare in the training data, buried under daily noise. So trainers add special loss functions that reward predicting far ahead.

Here is what breaks: the moment you start rewarding distant predictions, the model stops learning short-range weather. It unlearns the daily skills it mastered before. This is catastrophic forgetting — the model forgets its old training to chase the new goal. At the same time, predicting 30 days out means 30 chances to make a mistake; each wrong prediction ripples forward, compounding into nonsense. And training the model from scratch each time takes weeks of compute.

In one sentence. EMFormer trains on both short and long forecasts at the same time with a smart loss function, keeping both skills sharp while using a single efficient local pattern detector instead of expensive full-grid pattern-matching.

What it actually does

Multi-scale extraction

One convolution kernel reads the weather grid at multiple scales (coarse and fine details together).

Accumulate short and long context

Keep training examples that span both short (24 hours) and long (weeks) prediction windows in the same batch.

Sinusoidal loss weight over time

A smooth wave function automatically shifts emphasis: early in training, focus on short range; mid-training, balance both; late training, emphasize long range.

Attend to both patterns

The transformer attends to patterns that matter for both timescales, so neither is forgotten.

Predict across horizons

One forward pass outputs predictions for day 1, day 7, day 14, and day 30 all at once, with shared learned skills.

What the paper changes

What Changes

The paper changes two things at once: the model's backbone (how it reads the input weather grid) and the training process (which forecasts to reward). Instead of an expensive transformer that attends to every pixel of every historical day, EMFormer uses a single convolution to capture patterns at multiple scales — coarse (the big fronts) and fine (the local storms) in one forward pass. Then, during training, it uses a composite loss that smoothly swaps emphasis between short and long forecast goals over the course of learning.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Temporal Consistency

measured quantity  =  Prediction stability across forecast horizons

Temporal consistency is a measure of how well a model's prediction for day 30 stays coherent with its prediction for day 1 — not contradicting itself, not suddenly forgetting local patterns when zooming out to the month view. EMFormer measures this by comparing the accuracy of short forecasts before and after training on long forecasts.

The paper estimates it by tracking the accuracy of the 24-hour forecast on the same weather grids, both before finetuning and after finetuning for the 30-day horizon. If the short-range accuracy drops by 5 percent, consistency is poor. If it stays stable (within 1-2 percent), the model has learned to keep both skills.

Why it works

Why Sinusoidal Weighting Keeps Both Skills

The core idea is that the model learns in stages: first, nail the obvious daily patterns (because they have lots of examples); then, carefully layer in the rare long-range patterns (because adding them too fast drowns out short-range learning). A sinusoidal function — a smooth wave that goes up, then down, then up again — automatically manages this. Early in training, the weight on short-range loss is high (the model focuses on day 1); mid-way through, both short and long get equal weight; late in training, long-range gets the emphasis to squeeze out every percent of improvement.

Why does this work? Because when a task shift is gradual (not sudden), the model's shared pattern detectors have time to reorganize. A weight that predicts day 1 starts as a general feature detector (this looks like a cold front). As training gradually shifts emphasis, that detector refines itself to work in both contexts — predicting where the front goes in 24 hours AND in 30 days. If you switched the emphasis suddenly, the weight would be torn between two contradictory goals and forget both. The smooth sine curve prevents the tear.

Math after the picture

The Composite Loss Function

First in words:

Total loss equals short-range loss times a weight that starts high, then dips low, plus long-range loss times a weight that starts low, then rises high; the weights are a smooth wave that oscillates.

Then the compact form:

L_total = w_short(t) * L_short + w_long(t) * L_long, where w_short(t) = (1 + cos(pi * t / T)) / 2 and w_long(t) = 1 - w_short(t)

Every symbol:

L_shorterror in predicting the next 24 hours on test grids
L_longerror in predicting 30 days ahead
w_short(t)weight on short-range loss; 1.0 at start, 0.0 at midpoint, back to 1.0 at end
w_long(t)weight on long-range loss; starts 0, rises to 1.0 at midpoint
tcurrent epoch (training iteration count)
Ttotal number of epochs (usually 100-200)
A tiny worked example
Epoch 0 (start): w_short = 1.0, w_long = 0.0. If L_short = 10 and L_long = 50, then L_total = 1.0 * 10 + 0.0 * 50 = 10.
Epoch 50 (middle): w_short = 0.0, w_long = 1.0. Same losses, but L_total = 0.0 * 10 + 1.0 * 50 = 50. The model now learns long-range.
Epoch 100 (end): w_short = 1.0, w_long = 0.0 again. L_total = 1.0 * 10 + 0.0 * 50 = 10. Back to short-range focus to polish.
Result: By epoch 100, both L_short and L_long are much lower (say 3 and 8) because the model learned both. L_total spirals downward, never gets stuck optimizing one at the cost of the other.

The sine curve works because by giving each forecast horizon a turn, the model's shared pattern detectors (its weights) have time to adapt smoothly instead of being torn between two opposing goals. Early layers learn "is this a cold front" (useful for both); later layers refine that answer for each timescale separately.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong sinusoid period. If the sine wave completes too fast (short period), the model oscillates between short and long without mastering either. If it completes too slow (long period), one task dominates for too long and causes partial forgetting early on.
Imbalanced losses. If the short-range error and long-range error have very different magnitudes (say short is 5 but long is 500), then even with sinusoidal weighting, the larger error drowns the smaller one. Must rescale both errors to the same level first.
Rare long-range patterns still missed. Extreme events (hurricanes, freak heat waves) happen so rarely that even gentle retraining can underfit them. The model may learn the 30-day average well but miss the 1-in-100-year storm.
Done right. Tune the sinusoid period to your dataset size (usually 100-200 epochs for 10 years of history), normalize both losses to unit scale, and validate short-range accuracy at every epoch to catch forgetting early.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny weather forecaster on gridded data (even synthetic 2D Gaussian blobs that drift over time will work). Split into short-range examples (predict 1 step ahead) and long-range examples (predict 10 steps ahead). Train once with a fixed loss that only cares about the long range, and measure short-range accuracy. Train again with the sinusoidal weighting, and measure both. The demo must show that sinusoidal weighting keeps short-range accuracy above 85 percent even after 100 epochs of long-range training, while fixed weighting lets it collapse to 40 percent.
