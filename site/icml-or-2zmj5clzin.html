FocalPolicy: Frequency-Optimized Chunking and Locally Anchored Flow Matching

A robot policy learns to move its arm smoothly by watching its predictions' low-frequency changes and anchoring its training to strong signals at the end of each action sequence.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A visuomotor policy teaches a robot to perform manipulation tasks by imitating expert videos. The challenge is that when the robot produces actions in chunks (multiple steps at once), the boundaries between chunks often have jerky discontinuities that break the smooth flow. FocalPolicy fixes this by monitoring the frequency content (the smooth trends, like how position changes over time) of multi-step action sequences and anchoring the training signal near the end of each chunk where it is strongest.

Zero assumption version

Robot arms produce jerky, inconsistent movement between action chunks

A visuomotor policy learns to control a robot arm by watching human demonstrations. To reduce mistakes that pile up step-by-step, the policy predicts multiple actions at once (called a chunk: say, move for 4 steps). This works, but creates a new problem: each chunk is trained independently to match the expert's example within that 4-step window. The boundary where one chunk ends and the next begins often shows a sudden jump or jerk—the velocity changes abruptly, the position overshoots, or the direction flips.

A beginner might try: train each chunk separately to fit the expert's movements in its window. But this ignores what happens at the seams. If chunk 1 predicts ending at position (5, 3) and chunk 2 predicts starting at (5.1, 2.8), the hand jumps. The arm executing this trajectory looks broken.

In one sentence. FocalPolicy anchors training signals at chunk boundaries and watches the smooth trends of multi-step paths to catch and fix jerky jumps.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Chunk actions into multi-step sequences

Policy predicts 4 steps (or more) of robot movement at once, not 1 step.

Extract frequency content via DCT

For each predicted trajectory of 8+ steps, compute the Discrete Cosine Transform (a way to isolate slow, smooth trends from the raw positions and velocities).

Compare frequency trends to expert

Measure how different the predicted trajectory's smooth trends are from the expert's smooth trends. These trends represent the overall direction and curvature, not noise.

Anchor learning signals near chunk boundaries

During training, anchor one sampled time point to the end of the chunk where learning signals are strongest, ensuring the policy receives clear feedback about how chunks should connect.

Combine time and frequency feedback

Merge short-window time-domain loss (within the first 4 steps) with full-trajectory frequency-domain loss (across 8 steps) into one objective that the policy optimizes.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies two things: what the policy trains to predict (the training objective) and how training signals are routed during learning (the sampling strategy). Specifically, it adds a frequency-domain loss that watches long-term smooth trends, and anchors sampling to the end of action chunks where the learning signal (the weight nudges that training applies) is strongest.

the object it moves  =  training objective and sampling strategy
The hidden quantity

Target-signal propagation efficiency

measured quantity  =  Learning signal strength at chunk boundaries

The paper introduces a named quantity called Target-signal Propagation Efficiency: the strength and accuracy of the learning signal (the direction and magnitude of the weight nudges that training applies) at a given point during training. When you randomly sample time points during flow-matching training, some samples are far from the ground truth, and the learning signal they produce is noisy and weak. Samples near the terminal time (the end of the chunk) have higher fidelity—they point in a direction more aligned with what supervised learning would produce.

The paper estimates this by comparing the consistency gradient (the learning signal computed from two predictions at nearby flow times) to the ideal supervised gradient (what you would get if you had a direct label). By biasing the sampling to the end of the chunk, FocalPolicy strengthens the signal that flows backward through the network during training, making learning more efficient.

Why it works

Why it works: foresight composite training

The core insight is that a robot's smooth motion has two levels: the immediate step-by-step refinement (proximal precision) and the overall arc of the trajectory (distal coherence). A standard training loss only sees the immediate level. FocalPolicy adds a frequency-domain lens that sees the trajectory's long-term trends by extracting its Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) coefficients. These coefficients capture slow, smooth variations—how the hand's position curves over 8 steps, not jittery frame-to-frame noise.

Here is how it works step by step: First, the policy predicts an 8-step action sequence. Second, apply the DCT to extract the smooth trends: the first few DCT coefficients represent overall direction and curvature. Third, compare these coefficients to the expert's DCT coefficients. If the predicted trajectory's curve deviates from the expert's curve, a frequency-domain loss is computed. Fourth, also compute a standard time-domain loss on just the first 4 steps (proximal): this refines immediate precision. Fifth, the policy is updated via both losses combined. This dual feedback means: if your first 4 steps match the expert well but your full 8-step arc deviates, the frequency loss catches that. If your arc is good but steps 1-4 have small errors, the time loss catches that.

Why this solves chunk discontinuity: when two chunks join, the frequency loss looks at the combined 8-step trajectory and sees if the hand's overall curve is smooth. A jerk at the boundary shows up as a discontinuity in the DCT coefficients. By penalizing frequency-domain deviations, the policy learns that ending chunk 1 smoothly flowing into chunk 2 is rewarded. Locally anchored sampling amplifies this signal during training: by biasing the sampled time points toward the end of the chunk, gradients are strongest and most accurate where they matter most—the hand's final position and velocity before the next chunk takes over.

Math after the picture

The foresight composite objective

First in words:

Final loss is a weighted sum of time-domain loss (first H steps) and frequency-domain loss (full L-step trajectory), where L equals N times H. Frequency-domain loss is the squared difference between the discrete cosine transform of the predicted trajectory and the expert trajectory.

Then the compact form:

L_FCO = L_time(1:H) + lambda * L_freq(1:L), where L_freq = ||DCT(predicted) - DCT(expert)||_2^2

Every symbol:

L_FCOthe final composite loss the policy minimizes
L_time(1:H)how much the first H steps deviate from expert in raw positions/velocities
L_freq(1:L)how much the full L-step trajectory's smooth trends deviate from expert's trends
lambdathe weight controlling how much the frequency loss influences training
DCT()discrete cosine transform: converts a trajectory into its smooth trend components
Hlength of one action chunk (e.g. 4 steps)
Llength of full trajectory being evaluated (e.g. 8 or 16 steps)
A tiny worked example
Expert trajectory (4 steps): x = [0, 2, 5, 7]. DCT coefficients: [3.5, 1.8, 0.4, 0.1].
Predicted trajectory (4 steps): x = [0, 2, 4.5, 7.2]. DCT coefficients: [3.4, 1.6, 0.3, 0.15].
Frequency loss: (3.5-3.4)^2 + (1.8-1.6)^2 + (0.4-0.3)^2 + (0.1-0.15)^2 = 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.01 + 0.0025 = 0.0625.
Proximal time-domain loss on steps 1-2: (2-2)^2 + (5-4.5)^2 = 0 + 0.25 = 0.25.
Combined: L_FCO = 0.25 + 0.8 * 0.0625 = 0.25 + 0.05 = 0.30. The policy is nudged to reduce both immediate precision and long-term smoothness.

The two losses work together: time-domain fixes local mistakes in the first chunk, frequency-domain ensures the full trajectory's arc matches the expert's arc.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Frequency loss weight set too high. If lambda is too large, the policy over-corrects for frequency trends and ignores the immediate motion details required for the current chunk. The trajectory becomes artificially smooth but fails to match the expert's exact positions and velocities, degrading task success.
Chunk length H mismatched to task dynamics. If the chunk length is too long, the proximal time-domain loss covers only the first few steps, and the remainder of the chunk receives only frequency supervision. If too short, the full-trajectory frequency loss has little benefit. Optimal H depends on the task's natural rhythm.
Locally anchored sampling breaks on very short trajectories. Anchoring to the terminal time assumes there is sufficient margin between the anchor and early time points. On trajectories shorter than the anchor's concentration, the learning signal may fail to reach early steps, leaving them untrained.
Done right: balance lambda and chunk length. Empirically choose lambda and H by validation. The method is modular: the frequency loss and locally anchored sampling can be applied to any action-chunking policy, so failures in one component do not block the entire approach.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple two-chunk arm trajectory generator. Input: expert demonstration videos of a pick-and-place task (e.g. moving a block from point A to point B in 8 steps, split into two chunks of 4 steps each). Baseline: train the policy with only time-domain loss. FocalPolicy: train with foresight composite objective. Measure smoothness by computing the jerk (the change in acceleration) at the chunk boundary. Measure success by how often the final hand position matches the expert's within a threshold.

Smoothness score = 1 - (jerk_at_boundary / max_jerk), Success rate = (num_successful_trials / total_trials) * 100%

The demo must show that FocalPolicy reduces jerk at boundaries while maintaining task success—the arm moves smoothly from one chunk to the next, not with a visible twitch.
