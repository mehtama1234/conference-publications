Inference of Online Newton Methods with Nesterov's Accelerated Sketching

A fast second-order method for streaming data that estimates its own uncertainty without expensive matrix operations.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When a model learns from data that arrives one sample at a time (streaming), it needs to know not just what to predict, but how confident it is in that prediction. Second-order methods (like Newton's method) give better predictions than first-order methods because they account for the shape of the error surface (how curved or flat it is), not just the slope. But computing that shape usually requires huge expensive grids of numbers that get slower as the problem grows. This paper shows how to get second-order's accuracy and confidence while keeping the cost down to the speed of first-order methods.

Zero assumption version

Confidence is expensive when data arrives one sample at a time

Online learning means new examples show up one by one, and the model updates instantly. But the model also needs to express how sure it is in each prediction. For first-order methods (gradient-based, fast), expressing confidence requires maintaining a covariance matrix—a grid of numbers tracking how the predictions vary with different inputs. That matrix is size d by d, where d is the number of weights. For a medium problem with 10,000 weights, that is 100 million numbers. Updating it takes thousands of arithmetic operations per new sample.

Second-order methods (like Newton's method) give more accurate directions to update weights because they account for the shape of the error surface—imagine adjusting your steering not just by how far off the road you are, but by how curved the road is. But computing a Newton update requires solving a system of equations with the Hessian matrix (the curvature matrix), which costs d cubed operations. For 10,000 weights, that is a quadrillion operations. A beginner might try: use first-order for speed, or use second-order for accuracy and live with the slow confidence estimates. Both fail.

Why it breaks: if you use first-order with expensive confidence estimates, each new sample is cheap but your confidence calculation is a bottleneck. If you use second-order, the confidence is accurate but you stall waiting for the Hessian to solve.

In one sentence. The paper speeds up second-order confidence estimates for streaming data by using a low-rank approximation that keeps Nesterov's fast convergence.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the Newton direction is computed. Instead of solving a full d-by-d system (which takes quadrillions of operations), it projects the Hessian onto a small random subspace and solves a tiny system inside that subspace. This is called sketch-and-project.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: curvature direction

measured quantity  =  Curvature Direction (Newton Step)

Every machine-learning update involves two ingredients: the direction (which way to nudge weights) and the step size (how far). First-order methods find direction using only the gradient (a single slope). Second-order methods find direction using the Hessian—the full curvature map. Think of a hill map: steep slopes get big nudges, gentle slopes get small nudges. The Hessian is that map. The paper measures curvature by averaging the Hessian matrix (a grid of numbers) over many samples, building up an approximate version of the true curvature.

The paper's estimator works online: after each new sample, it updates its belief about what the curvature looks like, and it proves that this belief converges to the true curvature (given enough samples). The speed of convergence comes from Nesterov's acceleration trick, which is a momentum-like nudge that makes convergence rate optimal.

Why it works

Why it works: the three-part trick

The mechanism has three parts. First: a full Newton step requires solving a d-by-d linear system (quadrillions of operations). The paper skips this by projecting the Hessian onto a random k-by-k subspace, where k is much smaller (say 100 instead of 10,000). Inside this tiny space, the system solve is cheap (just a million operations). The Newton direction is still computed, just in a low-rank approximation.

Second: solving even the small system takes many iterations when the conditioning is bad (one direction of the error surface is very steep, another is nearly flat—the solver ping-pongs). Nesterov acceleration adds a momentum term that makes convergence happen in fewer iterations, so the small solve is also faster. It works because momentum prevents the solver from bouncing back and forth; instead it overshoots slightly and corrects in fewer bounces. This is the same trick that makes accelerated gradient descent fast.

Third: the covariance estimator works online. After each sample, the paper updates its running Hessian average and proves that the asymptotic covariance of the final weights matches what you would get from exact Newton (if you had done it). The proof uses a Lyapunov equation, which is a matrix equation that tracks how errors in the covariance estimate shrink over time. Because the Hessian is averaged and Nesterov is used, the shrinking happens as fast as possible without extra terms in the update.

The update equation

First in words:

Weights get updated by the Newton direction approximated in a sketched subspace; covariance is updated by the Hessian average with Nesterov acceleration.

Then the compact form:

theta_{t+1} = theta_t + eta * S * (S^T H_t S + lambda I)^{-1} S^T gradient_t Sigma_{t+1} = Sigma_t + alpha (H_t - Sigma_t) + (1 - beta) (H_t - Sigma_t)_{prev}

Tiny worked example with three weights
Suppose we have 3 weights and project onto 2 dimensions (k=2).
Hessian at step t: H = [[4, 0.5, 0], [0.5, 2, 0.1], [0, 0.1, 3]]
Gradient: [1.0, 0.5, 0.8]
Random projection S (3-by-2): [[0.8, 0.2], [0.1, 0.9], [0.5, 0.1]]
S^T H S (2-by-2): [[3.2, 1.1], [1.1, 2.6]] (much smaller to solve)
Solve (S^T H S)^{-1} S^T gradient in 2D space, get direction (much faster).
Project back: update = S * [direction] (size 3 weights now).
New weights: theta_{t+1} = theta_t + 0.01 * update (tiny step shown).
Covariance update: average the Hessian; momentum term pulls previous update slightly forward.
Cost: O(d^2) per sample (like first-order), not O(d^3) (like full Newton).

The trick is projecting the Hessian down, solving the small system fast with Nesterov, and projecting back. The covariance grows trustworthy over time because it is averaged and accelerated.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad random projection. If the random matrix S is unlucky and aligns poorly with the true Hessian's important directions, the sketched Newton step is weak. Mitigation: use multiple sketches or adaptive sketching (the paper does not explore this).
Poor conditioning of the small system. Even in the tiny k-by-k space, if the Hessian is very ill-conditioned (one direction of curvature is much steeper than others—one eigenvalue much larger), Nesterov does not help much because the solver gets stuck in the narrow ravine. The solve gets stuck.
Noise heterogeneity in the data. If different samples have very different noise levels (some samples are measured cleanly, others are noisy), the Hessian average includes this noise and the covariance estimate does not converge as fast.
Lyapunov equation assumes ergodicity. The proof assumes the data is stationary (the distribution of samples does not change). In a non-stationary stream, the covariance bounds degrade.
Done right. The method works best on smooth regression problems with moderate dimension (k in the range 50-200) and streams where the data distribution is stable. Testing on linear and logistic regression (where the error surface is smooth like a bowl, not jagged), the method delivers on its promises.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a streaming linear regression: 1000 samples arrive one at a time with 100 weights. At each step, apply first-order gradient, full Newton (on a copy of the weights), and sketched Newton with Nesterov (using k=20). Track three quantities: (1) the test error on held-out samples, (2) the confidence interval width (how certain the model claims to be), and (3) wall-clock time per update.

speedup = time_full_newton / time_sketched_newton; confidence_ratio = accuracy_of_sketched_covariance / accuracy_of_true_covariance; final_error = test_loss_at_end_of_stream

The demo must show that sketched Newton finishes in 5x less time than full Newton while keeping confidence accuracy above 85% of the gold standard, and converges to lower test error than first-order alone.
