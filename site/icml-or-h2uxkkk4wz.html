Accelerated Sketching for Online Newton Methods

Speed up Newton's method on streaming data by solving its linear system faster, using momentum to cut sketching steps from ten down to five.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Fitting a model to streaming data (one sample at a time) needs two things: fast weight updates and honest confidence intervals. First-order methods update fast but give uncertain intervals, because they ignore the shape of the error surface. Newton methods give better intervals because they follow the curvature of the error surface, reading the shape to pick better directions. But reading curvature means solving a large linear system every step. This paper solves both problems at once: it keeps Newton's insight about following curvature but accelerates the linear solver using Nesterov momentum, cutting the sketching steps needed by half while keeping the statistics equally honest.

Zero assumption version

Two contradictory needs in online learning

When you fit a model to data coming one sample at a time (streaming data), you face a trade-off: fast methods are cheap but uncertain; accurate methods are expensive. First-order methods (the kind most ML practitioners use) update weights by a small nudge in the direction that shrinks error. They are fast, but the intervals around predictions (how confident you should be) are hard to compute reliably. Newton methods use curvature information (how bent the error surface is) to pick better nudges and give honest confidence intervals, because curvature tells you whether a small change in weights will give you a lot or a little movement in error—that same sensitivity shows up in how certain you should be about predictions. But computing curvature at each step means solving a large linear system: a table of numbers times an unknown list of numbers equals a known list. When the table is d-by-d and d is the number of weights, solving it exactly takes time proportional to d-cubed—prohibitively slow when d is large.

A beginner might think: use a sketch (a random projection) to make the system smaller and solve it faster, since solving smaller systems costs less. But sketching adds randomness: the random projection throws away information, so you need many iterations of the solver to recover an accurate answer. The early work showed the randomness does not break your confidence intervals (because you can track how much information is lost), but it still requires many sketching steps (ten or more) to get accurate answers, which eats away the speedup you hoped to gain.

The core tension. You want Newton's accurate intervals without paying the cubic cost, and you want sketching to be fast without sacrificing statistical honesty.

Sketch the system

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes how you solve the linear system at each step. Instead of solving it exactly (expensive) or with many slow sketching steps, it accelerates the sketched solver using Nesterov momentum. This one algorithmic tweak cuts steps needed from ten to five without breaking confidence intervals.

The hidden quantity

The fourth moment of the error

measured quantity  =  Fourth moment of the iterate error

When you approximate a solution to a linear system, you get an error: the gap between your approximate answer and the true answer. That error propagates through all your future predictions. The paper needs to estimate how this error affects your confidence intervals. Confidence intervals depend on how spread out your estimates are (their variance), but to know that spread reliably without infinite data, you need to measure a stronger signal: how spread out the squared errors are (the fourth moment). This is the fourth power of the gap between your current approximate solution and the true answer, because looking at the fourth power instead of the square amplifies the signal when errors vary a lot.

The paper estimates this fourth-power quantity from the data and uses it to build online confidence-interval estimates that stay honest even when the linear system is only approximately solved. This is the key technical challenge: the sketching adds randomness that does not vanish (unlike exact solves), so the paper must track this residual algorithmic error and weave it into the uncertainty estimate.

Why it works

Why momentum cuts sketching steps in half

When you sketch a matrix, you lose information, so solving the sketched system converges slowly—like moving downhill on a gentle slope, always taking the same cautious step. Nesterov momentum works by overshooting: look ahead to where the gentle slope points, take a bigger step in that direction, then correct. This converges faster because you are using the direction of change to anticipate where the next good step will be, rather than only looking at where you are now. The speed improvement comes from the square root of the condition number appearing in the convergence rate instead of the condition number itself—this roughly halves the number of steps needed to reach the same accuracy.

Why does this not break the statistics? The uncertainty in the final approximate Newton direction comes from two sources: randomness in your data and randomness in the sketching. The paper tracks both and shows they combine in a way that you can estimate online. The momentum just makes you arrive at the approximate answer faster by using lookahead; it does not hide or distort the errors, it just reaches the same destination with fewer steps, so the final accumulated error is the same.

Math after the picture

The sketched solver with momentum

First in words:

For each iteration j within one solver call, compute a weighted average position y_j, then evaluate the sketched gradient direction w_j using sketched Hessian and sketched residual, update momentum state z and velocity v using momentum parameters alpha, beta, gamma.

Then the compact form:

y_j = α·v_j + (1-α)·z_j; w_j = B·S_j·(S_j^T·B^2·S_j)^†·S_j^T·(B·y_j + g); z_{j+1} = β·z_j + (1-β)·y_j + γ·w_j; v_{j+1} = α·v_j + (1-α)·z_{j+1}

Every symbol:

y_jmidpoint between momentum states z_j and v_j; where we look ahead
w_jsketched gradient step; the direction that shrinks the linear system error
α, β, γmomentum parameters; control how much to overshoot and correct
S_jrandom sketch matrix; projects d-by-d down to r-by-r (smaller system)
(S_j^T·B^2·S_j)^†pseudo-inverse; inverts the sketched Hessian (stays tractable, size r-by-r)
τnumber of solver iterations (5 instead of 10)
Tiny example: solving Δx = -g with sketching and momentum
Suppose true Newton would converge in 100 steps without sketching.
With sketching alone (unaccelerated): error decays as (1 - 0.01)^τ. To reach error 0.001, need τ ≈ 690 steps (convergence rate 0.99).
With Nesterov momentum: error decays as (1 - √0.01)^τ = (1 - 0.1)^τ = 0.9^τ. To reach error 0.001, need τ ≈ 69 steps (ten times faster).
In the paper's experiments: unaccelerated sketch needs ≈10 steps per sample; accelerated sketch needs ≈5 steps per sample.

Nesterov momentum speeds convergence by taking the square root of the convergence exponent, cutting required steps roughly in half.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too few sketching steps. If you stop the momentum solver too early (say, only 2 iterations), the approximate Newton direction is so bad that your step makes the error worse, not better. The paper requires the number of steps to satisfy a bound: the multipliers that control momentum must be tuned so each iteration shrinks the error reliably.
Ill-conditioned Hessian. If the Hessian is nearly singular (very flat in some directions), the momentum gains shrink, and you do not see the full speedup. The method still works but loses some of its acceleration advantage.
Non-zero data bias. The fourth-moment estimate assumes the data has finite fourth moments. If some samples are outliers, the covariance estimate can be too optimistic, making confidence intervals too narrow.
Done right. With momentum parameters tuned to your problem's shape (how the error surface curves), the method converges reliably, confidence intervals have correct coverage, and you save half the sketching steps compared to unaccelerated sketching.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple online regression: generate streaming data (y, x pairs) and fit a linear model step by step. At each step, form the Hessian (second-derivative matrix), then solve the Newton system using (a) exact Newton, (b) sketched Newton without momentum, and (c) sketched Newton with Nesterov momentum. Track three signals: time per step, mean absolute prediction error, and the measured coverage of 90-percent confidence intervals (what fraction of new y values truly fall inside the interval).

Coverage = (number of new y inside the interval) / (total new samples). Also measure steps-to-converge and time-per-sample.

The demo must show that accelerated sketching cuts solver iterations in half while keeping coverage within 1-2 percentage points of exact Newton.
