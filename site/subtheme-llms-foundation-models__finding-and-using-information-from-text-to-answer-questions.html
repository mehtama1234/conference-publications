Finding and using information from text to answer questions

These papers help models search through documents and retrieve relevant facts to answer questions more accurately, combining language models with information retrieval.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Models trained on internet text have knowledge baked into their parameters, but that knowledge is limited to what was in the training data and is fixed after training ends. If you ask a model a question about recent events, it will fail because it has not seen them. Papers in this area solve this by giving models access to external documents—they pair a language model with a retrieval system that searches through a large collection of texts, finds relevant passages, and shows them to the model. The model then reads those passages and answers the question based on what it found. This is like letting the model use Google and then reason about the results.

The challenge is making this actually work well. Retrieval systems sometimes find passages that are relevant but do not answer the question, or they miss the right passage and the model has to guess anyway. Papers study how to make retrieval better by ranking search results more carefully, how to decide when retrieval is necessary versus when the model can answer from its own knowledge, and how to teach models to actually use the retrieved passages to build an answer. Some papers also study combining this with reasoning steps, so a model can search for information, reason about it, realize it needs more information, and search again.

The payoff is that models can now answer questions about documents they have never seen before, can access recent information, and can work with private documents without needing to retrain. This opens up applications like question-answering over medical records, legal documents, or a company's internal knowledge base. The technique is also more interpretable than pure memorization—when a model gives an answer, you can point to the exact passage it used, which builds trust.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into four main directions: making the context window bigger so models can read more at once by compressing the information they store in memory, building smarter retrieval systems that search documents the way a reasoning model would instead of simple keyword matching, helping models understand what they do and do not know so they can decide when to retrieve versus when to answer from memory, and creating systems that adapt their retrieval strategy on the fly based on what is working.

Context compression

Large language models have a fixed limit on how much text they can read at once—their context window. But many real applications need to work with documents much longer than that limit. These papers tackle the compression problem: they want to squeeze important information down so a model can hold more text in the same amount of memory, without losing the ability to answer questions about it. The challenge is knowing what to compress. If you throw away the wrong details, the model will fail to answer questions that depend on those details. But if you keep everything, you gain nothing.

Autoencoding-Free Context Compression uses anchors—key concepts that stay fixed—to compress text without needing to train a separate compression model first, which makes it fast and practical because you can apply it immediately to new text. COMI uses marginal information gain to decide what matters: it keeps pieces of text that add the most new information and discards pieces that are redundant. FreqKV extends context to 256K positions by storing key-value pairs (the model's internal memory of what it has seen) in frequency domain instead of full precision, like storing a photo as a compressed JPG instead of raw pixels. Context-Driven Incremental Compression keeps the model's speed constant even over hundreds of dialogue turns—it compresses each turn as the conversation grows. Learned Meta-Tokens treats certain positions as learned summary symbols (pieces the model learns to use as shorthand) that stand in for longer sequences, helping the model learn to compress and then extend the context window beyond training. Rethinking KV Cache Eviction decides which parts of the key-value cache are most important to keep based on information theory—pieces that preserve the most predictive signal stay, others are dropped. Taming the Fragility of KV Cache Eviction makes this safer by preventing the quality collapse that happens when you drop the wrong cache entries.

These papers agree on the core insight: compression is not about throwing things away randomly, but about keeping pieces that carry the most signal for answering questions. They differ in what they compress (the full text versus just the key-value pairs the model has already computed) and how they decide what to keep (by pre-training anchors, by information gain, by frequency patterns, or by information-theoretic importance).

Retrieval with reasoning

Naive retrieval just matches keywords in the question to keywords in documents. But the papers here ask: what if retrieval could think like a reasoning engine? If you are trying to answer a complex question, you might need to search for one piece of information, reason about it, then realize you need a different piece, then search again. Or the information you need is scattered across many documents, and you have to piece it together. These papers rethink retrieval as a reasoning loop.

DeepRAG breaks retrieval into steps. Instead of searching once and answering, it thinks step-by-step: what do I need to find first? It searches, reads, reasons about what it found, and if it realizes it needs more, it searches again. From Conflict to Consensus (MA-RAG) uses a key idea: when multiple candidate answers disagree with each other, that disagreement is a signal to search more carefully. It runs multiple reasoning paths, sees where they conflict, and uses that conflict as a trigger for deeper multi-round retrieval. CausalRAG2 builds a causal knowledge graph—a structured map of how concepts cause and influence each other—instead of just a flat list of documents. When you search that causal graph, you find not just relevant documents but documents that explain the causal chains needed to answer the question. From Retrieval to Translation converts your question into graph clues—not just keywords but structural hints about what kind of relationship you are looking for. Hierarchical Abstract Tree builds a tree of increasingly abstract summaries: top-level summaries of whole document clusters, then summaries of subclusters, then individual passages. When you search, the model can first find the right cluster at the abstract level, then zoom in. Q-RAG trains embeddings to understand value: which documents are most useful for long-context multi-step questions, not just which are most similar to the question. LinearRAG uses a linear graph structure—computationally cheaper than dense graphs because you store fewer connections—to map how documents connect, so retrieval can follow those connections efficiently.

All of these papers agree that retrieval should not be one shot but iterative, and that structure matters—so that you can traverse relationships between documents efficiently. They differ in the structure: causal graphs versus hierarchical trees versus value-aware embeddings versus explicitly learned reasoning steps. The common thread is treating retrieval as part of a reasoning loop, not a separate lookup task, which means you can adapt your search based on what you have learned so far.

Reliability and trust

A model might confidently answer a question completely wrong. It might cite a passage that does not actually support its answer. It might have learned something from training that is now outdated or false. These papers address a core reliability problem: how do you know when a model's answer is trustworthy, and how do you fix a model that has learned incorrect information without breaking everything else it has learned?

Query-Level Uncertainty in Large Language Models detects when a model is unsure—when it does not have confidence in its own answer. This matters because it signals when retrieval should kick in: if the model admits uncertainty, search for an answer. Revisiting the Past (data unlearning) fixes the opposite problem: sometimes a model has learned something you want it to forget, like private data or outdated information. Rather than retraining from scratch, this approach saves checkpoints during training and reverts to earlier states strategically, keeping the knowledge you want while removing what you do not. Copy-Paste to Mitigate Hallucinations ensures the model copies text directly from the retrieved passage when answering, which prevents hallucination because the answer is grounded in actual text. Guidance uses a decoding-time technique to enforce sentence-level citations: as the model generates each sentence, it is constrained to cite which passage backs it up, which makes hallucination much harder. RAG without Forgetting solves a different problem: as a retrieval system learns from new queries, it sometimes forgets how to handle old queries. This paper proves that expanding queries and expanding keys in the memory are theoretically equivalent, and uses that insight to keep the system from degrading over time. Linguistic Nepotism reveals a subtle bias: multilingual models prefer to cite documents in the same language as the query, even when documents in other languages are more relevant. This means you cannot always trust retrieval rankings in multilingual settings. Revisiting Parameter-Based Knowledge Editing (editing the model's internal numbers that carry what it has learned) shows that most methods for modifying stored knowledge this way actually break the model's core abilities on standard tasks because you can accidentally erase important information the model needs—a simple retrieval-based approach does much better because it keeps the model untouched.

These papers form a reliability chain: uncertainty detection tells you when to retrieve, citations ground answers in real text, knowledge editing keeps the model accurate, and avoiding parameter editing prevents collateral damage. Together, they answer the question: how do you keep a retrieval-augmented system trustworthy?

Adaptive retrieval strategies

Not every question needs retrieval. Simple factual questions that are in the model's training might not need a search because the model already knows them. Complex questions might need multiple rounds. And different retrieval strategies work better for different types of questions because some questions are answered by keyword match and others by semantic meaning. These papers build systems that adapt: they learn which queries need retrieval, which retriever to use, and when to stop searching.

GAM-RAG uses a gain metric: it tracks whether each retrieval round is actually helping the answer quality, and stops retrieving if gains are diminishing, which means you save time while staying accurate. This cuts the time cost of generating an answer by 61% while improving accuracy. JADE bridges strategy and operations by jointly optimizing what retrieval should do (get the right documents) and how efficiently to do it (with minimal delay), rather than treating these as separate problems, so the system does not waste time getting irrelevant documents fast. Retriever Portfolios acknowledges that different retrievers are better for different questions—semantic search works well for some queries because it finds meaning-based matches, keyword search for others because it is fast, graph-based retrieval for others because it follows relationships. Instead of picking one retriever, this approach learns a portfolio with a router that selects which retriever to use per query. Panini continuously learns: as new contexts and questions come in over time, it learns to compress and represent them in memory more efficiently, getting smarter with each new query. From Volume to Value compresses the retrieval database itself for on-device systems: it learns which passages matter for which types of queries and drops the rest, reducing memory by 2,404x while actually improving accuracy because it removes noise. In-Context Learning as Rate-Distortion Optimization reframes in-context learning as an information trade-off: you have a fixed position budget, and the system learns to pack the most useful examples into that budget, trading off diversity for quality.

These papers share the insight that one-size-fits-all systems are wasteful. Some questions need complex multi-step retrieval; others just need a keyword search. Some databases are noisy and need aggressive compression; others are clean. By adapting the retrieval strategy to the question and the data, you can be both more accurate and much more efficient.

Specialized domains

General retrieval systems work for open-domain questions, but specialized fields have their own challenges. Medical records use domain-specific terminology. Legal documents have strict structure and precedent chains. Code repositories mix human-written code with auto-generated tests. These papers apply retrieval-augmented systems to these hard domains and show what breaks.

Addressing Semantic Blind Spots in Text-to-SQL translates natural language questions into SQL queries. The challenge is that SQL has semantics the model might miss—certain column combinations are invalid, certain joins do not make sense. This paper pre-generates valid components and rewards correct AST structures, which prevents the model from generating nonsense SQL because it constrains the space of possible outputs. CerebraGloss applies instruction tuning to medical EEG signals with a large vision-language model. The specialized challenge is that clinicians need fine-grained interpretation (which specific milliseconds show abnormality), and the model needs to learn medical terminology. Cyber-Zero trains cybersecurity agents on CTF (Capture The Flag) challenges without running them—the model learns to plan attacks through description alone, which is faster than simulation because it skips execution overhead. KORE injects new knowledge into multimodal models for scenarios like a company training an AI on recent product launches. The problem: injecting new knowledge often breaks old knowledge (catastrophic forgetting), which means the model forgets how to do tasks it previously could do. KORE prevents this by using knowledge-oriented control mechanisms. Linguistic Nepotism's finding applies here: in multilingual healthcare, retrieval systems prefer documents in the query language over more relevant documents in other languages, which is dangerous because you miss better answers. ML-Embed builds embeddings specifically for multilingual retrieval that work well in low-resource languages—the retrieval system has to work across 100+ languages with limited training data in many of them. TG-RAG ensures faithful adherence to domain standard operating procedures: in medicine or law, you cannot just answer any way; you have to follow specific procedures, which means answers must comply with guidelines. This paper uses domain SOPs to guide retrieval and reasoning. Token-Free Hierarchical Indexing handles complex multi-hop reasoning over legal or scientific documents—cases often cite other cases, which cite other precedents, forming long chains. This indexing structure lets you follow those chains without needing to summarize everything as tokens.

These applications show that retrieval-augmented generation is not just about answering Jeopardy questions. It is about accessing specialized knowledge that changes constantly (medical advances, legal precedent, product launches) while respecting domain constraints (medical terminology, legal procedure, cybersecurity rules).
