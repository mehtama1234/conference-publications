MMReD: Cross-Modal Dense Context Reasoning Benchmark

A benchmark that measures how well language and vision models can reason over dense, packed contexts where simple searching does not work.

Current language and vision models can read long documents, but they struggle to understand patterns across all the information. This benchmark tests whether models can truly reason over dense contexts — situations where every piece of information matters and you need to see how things connect globally, not just pluck out one needle from a haystack.

Zero assumption version

Why context length alone is not enough

Today's language models can handle very long input texts — thousands or even tens of thousands of words. But long input does not mean they understand the whole picture. The classic test, Needle-in-a-Haystack, just hides one fact in a pile of words and asks the model to find it. This is easy retrieval: scan the pile, spot the needle.

Real reasoning over dense contexts is different. It means noticing patterns that emerge only when you look at ALL the pieces together — how three pieces of information contradict each other, where a concept repeats across different sections, how a trend evolves as you move through the data. A naive model just tries to use its usual retrieval tricks and misses the global picture.

In one sentence. The benchmark tests whether models can detect patterns and relationships across all the information, not just retrieve isolated facts.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Design 24 reasoning tasks

Tasks range from simple retrieval to reasoning that requires the model to focus on all chunks.

Vary complexity systematically

Some tasks need to focus on key chunks only; others demand equal focus across all sections.

Increase context length gradually

Test each task with 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 chunks of dense information.

Run all models through the gauntlet

Evaluate language models, vision-language models, reasoning specialists, code models.

Measure the collapse

Record how accuracy drops as context grows. Identify which tasks break first.

What the paper changes

What changes

The benchmark itself — the measurement tool — changes what we test. Instead of asking only "can the model find one needle?", it asks "can the model reason across all the context at once?".

The hidden quantity

Dense reasoning capacity: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  How well a model reasons over all information at once

Dense reasoning capacity is not the same as context window length. A model might accept 100,000 individual numbers from its input but fail to reason over patterns that require looking at all of them together. This benchmark measures the gap between input capacity and actual reasoning ability — how well can a model reason when it sees the full picture?

The paper estimates this by running tasks of increasing complexity at increasing context lengths and watching where accuracy falls. When a model gets 95% on a task with 4 chunks but 5% with 128 chunks, that gap is the limit of its dense reasoning. The benchmark also separates two types: selective reasoning (attending to only key chunks) versus uniform reasoning (needing to weigh everything equally).

Why it works

Why it reveals the hidden limit

Models are built to do retrieval well: find the query, scan the context, pluck out the answer. When context is short, this works even if the task is not really retrieval — the model gets lucky by accident. But when all 128 chunks matter and no simple keywords pinpoint the answer, the model has to hold and weigh information across the entire input. It collapses because its architecture was never trained to maintain coherent reasoning over such dense global patterns.

The benchmark forces a choice: either the model has learned to reason globally (and succeeds even at 128 chunks) or it has only learned retrieval (and fails as context grows). The 24 tasks are designed to rule out shortcuts — each one demands that the model actually process and integrate information from many chunks at once, not just spot a surface signal.

Quantifying the reasoning cliff

First in words:

accuracy_drop = accuracy_at_4_chunks minus accuracy_at_128_chunks

Then the compact form:

dense_reasoning_gap = acc(4) - acc(128)

Every symbol:

acc(4)accuracy on the same task with 4 chunks
acc(128)accuracy on the same task with 128 chunks
dense_reasoning_gapthe size of the cliff — how much ability the model loses
A tiny worked example
Model A on a reasoning task: 95% accuracy with 4 chunks, 50% with 128 chunks. Gap = 95 - 50 = 45 percentage points.
Model B on the same task: 88% accuracy with 4 chunks, 15% with 128 chunks. Gap = 88 - 15 = 73 percentage points.
Model B loses more ability as context grows, so it has weaker dense reasoning capacity than Model A.
The benchmark reveals which models have learned true reasoning (small gap) versus clever retrieval only (large gap).

The bigger the accuracy drop, the less the model can reason over the full dense context.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Retrieval shortcuts fool early tests. At 4-8 chunks, a model can succeed by pure surface matching even if it has no real reasoning ability. The benchmark designs tasks with no retrieval shortcuts — you must attend to the full context.
Focus dilution breaks reasoning. When a model must weigh 128 chunks equally, its ability to focus gets spread too thin. Each chunk receives minimal focus, and global patterns are lost in noise.
Fine-tuning does not fix the gap. Even when trained on data with longer contexts, models do not generalize to tasks they have not seen before. The underlying architecture cannot handle dense reasoning.
Reasoning specialists still fail. Even models trained to reason do not escape the benchmark. This shows the problem is architectural, not just a matter of training technique.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dense reasoning task: give a language model a set of 4, 8, 16, and 32 text chunks. Each chunk describes one person's opinions on three topics (e.g., climate, energy, economy). The task is to answer a question that requires detecting a pattern across all chunks — for example, "Who favors economic growth in the most topics?" Track accuracy as the number of chunks increases.

reasoning_degradation = accuracy_at_4_chunks minus accuracy_at_max_chunks

The demo will show that as context grows, even advanced models fall from high accuracy to near-random guessing, proving that they are not truly reasoning over the full context.
