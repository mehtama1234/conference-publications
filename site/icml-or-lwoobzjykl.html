LaST: Latent Spatio-Temporal Chain-of-Thought for Robot Manipulation

A robot model that thinks in hidden numbers before moving, not in words, so it reasons fast and captures physics that words cannot.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a robot picks up objects, it needs to plan its moves carefully. Most robot models today think out loud: they generate words describing their reasoning step-by-step. But words are slow to generate, and they cannot express the detailed physical shapes and forces that matter for manipulation. This paper proposes a smarter approach: a robot model that reasons silently, in a compressed numerical space that captures 3D shape, visual changes, and the robot's own body position. This silent reasoning is faster because generating dense numbers takes fewer computational steps than generating words one at a time, and it captures more information because the numbers directly encode what objects look like in 3D (their exact shape, size, and where they sit in space) and visual details that matter for manipulation, rather than forcing them into words.

Zero assumption version

Robot models today reason out loud, and it costs time and expressiveness

A robot arm needs to decide: should I reach here or there? Most modern robot models do what humans do—they reason aloud. The model generates words describing the plan: 'The cup is on the left. I should rotate my wrist. The cup rim faces up.' Each word takes time to generate. For a robot that needs to act many times per second, this word generation is a bottleneck. The finished reasoning comes too late for the arm to move smoothly.

Worse, words cannot express fine details. A robot needs to know the exact 3D shape of the cup, how light plays across its surface, the exact position of its own fingers. These are ineffable—they cannot be captured well in words. A word-based model is forced to squeeze detailed physical reality into words, losing information that matters for smooth, precise manipulation.

The trap: slow reasoning that misses physics. Reason aloud in words first, then act—but words take time and cannot express the physical details of 3D shape, visual texture, and robot state.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the scene

Extract visual features (what the scene looks like now), 3D point cloud (exact shape of objects), and proprioception (where the robot's arm is) into three dense vectors.

Predict future latent states

The reasoning expert generates a short sequence of future hidden states—what the scene and robot will look like after 1, 2, 3, 4 steps—all in compressed number form.

Share reasoning with the action expert

Both experts live in the same model. They share a lookup mechanism (the action expert can read the latent reasoning tokens) so the action expert knows what the reasoning expert predicts.

Generate low-latency actions

The action expert generates actual motor commands (joint angles, gripper opening) at high frequency, using both the linguistic goal and the latent reasoning.

Adapt the update frequency during deployment

The reasoning expert can update every 10 steps (low frequency) while the action expert updates every step (high frequency), so reasoning is not a bottleneck.

What the paper changes

What changes: from words to hidden numbers

The core change is where the model puts its reasoning. Instead of generating text tokens that describe the plan, the model generates latent tokens (compressed vectors of numbers). These latent tokens encode what the robot needs to know: the 3D shape of nearby objects, the visual texture and lighting, the robot's current joint position, and how the scene will evolve over the next few steps. A latent token is roughly 1000 numbers; a word token is roughly 10,000 numbers. Fewer numbers means fewer parameters to generate, which takes less computation. Fewer computation steps mean less latency. So latent reasoning is faster, because there is less to compute.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spatio-temporal coherence

measured quantity  =  spatio-temporal coherence in latent space

The paper introduces a quantity that previous VLA models did not measure: how well does the model's latent reasoning remain consistent across time and space? A latent token at step k should faithfully represent the 3D shape and visual state of the environment at that step. If the latent representations jump erratically (the shape suddenly looks different, the robot's position contradicts the last step), the action expert loses the thread and makes mistakes. The paper measures this coherence by training the reasoning expert to predict the next latent state accurately.

To estimate this quantity, the paper computes a regression loss that compares the predicted latent state against the actual encoded state (from the point cloud encoder, vision encoder, and proprioception encoder). If this loss is high, the latent reasoning is incoherent. If it is low, the latent reasoning maintains a stable, self-consistent picture of the world.

Why it works

Why splitting reasoning and action into two experts solves the latency problem

The core insight is a clean separation of labor. The reasoning expert (slow) generates latent predictions once every N steps. The action expert (fast) runs every step, using the most recent latent reasoning. By decoupling these two loops, neither one slows down the other.

Why does this work? Because the latent space is compact. A latent token is 1000 numbers; a word is 10,000 numbers. The action expert can read the latent reasoning in 1–2 milliseconds. If reasoning happened in words, the same read step would cost 10–20 milliseconds. Over a 1-second manipulation horizon, that is 100-200 milliseconds of latency you cannot spend moving the arm. The compression solves the bottleneck.

A second reason: the latent space captures the detailed physical reality that words cannot. Suppose a robot needs to adjust its fingers around a wine glass stem. The stem is thin, fragile, and transparent—details that defeat language. 'Be careful around the thin part' is useless. But a latent representation trained on real 3D point clouds learns to encode the exact diameter, material, and fragility directly as numbers. The action expert reads these numbers and adjusts finger position correctly, without ever translating to words.

The latent prediction loss

First in words:

The reasoning expert minimizes the error when predicting latent states. For each future step k, the expert predicts a latent code; the true latent code comes from encoding the actual future observation. The loss is the squared distance between prediction and true code.

Then the compact form:

L_latent = sum_k ||Z_pred_k - Z_true_k||^2

Every symbol:

Z_pred_kthe latent code predicted for step k steps ahead
Z_true_kthe actual latent code from encoding the real future observation at step k
||·||^2squared Euclidean distance (how far apart two vectors are)
sum_ksum over all predicted future steps
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model predicts a latent code for 1 step ahead: Z_pred_1 = [0.5, 1.2, -0.3, 0.8].
The true latent code (from the encoders) is: Z_true_1 = [0.6, 1.0, -0.2, 0.7].
Differences: [0.1, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1]. Squared: [0.01, 0.04, 0.01, 0.01].
Loss for step 1: 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.07.
Do this for steps 2, 3, 4. Total loss = 0.07 + 0.06 + 0.05 + 0.04 = 0.22.
Lower total loss means the reasoning expert's predictions are stable and consistent across all future steps.

This regression loss teaches the slow reasoning expert to generate a stable, physically coherent sequence of hidden predictions, so the fast action expert can trust them.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Latent space collapse. If the reasoning expert is not trained carefully, all future latent predictions can converge to the same average vector, losing information. The robot then cannot distinguish step-1 futures from step-4 futures. Train on diverse manipulation data and monitor coherence loss.
Action expert ignores latent reasoning. Without explicit loss coupling between the reasoning and action experts, the action expert may learn to ignore the latent tokens and rely only on the current observation. Use joint training and set heterogeneous update frequencies so both experts are always useful.
Temporal horizon too short. If the reasoning expert only predicts 1 step ahead instead of 4, the action expert is blind to what comes next. When the robot arm starts moving toward a cup, predicting only where the cup will be in 1 step (20ms) is not enough. The action expert needs to know where the cup will be in 4 steps (80ms) so it can plan a smooth trajectory that reaches without overshoot. The paper shows that 4 steps is a sweet spot; beyond that, gains plateau because prediction error grows too large.
Done right: fast, coherent, implicit reasoning. When trained on large-scale real robot data with both experts active, the latent space learns to encode object shape, visual state, and proprioception in a self-consistent, predictable way. The action expert reads this and succeeds 13-14% more often than word-based models.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple manipulation task: a robot arm reaching for and grasping a single object (e.g., a red cube on a table). Train a baseline model that reasons aloud (generates word descriptions of the plan). Train LaST with latent reasoning. Run both on 20 fresh test trials. Measure: (1) latency from scene observation to first motor command (should be 10-50ms for LaST, 500-1000ms for word model), (2) success rate (LaST should grasp the cube more often), (3) smooth trajectory (LaST should have no pauses or jerks because reasoning is not a bottleneck).

Success rate = (number of grasps where fingers close on object) / (total attempts). Latency = (time from sensor input to first motor output in milliseconds).

A working demo proves that hidden-number reasoning eliminates the latency tax of word generation and makes robot motion smooth and reliable.
