Using Reinforcement Learning to Train Large Language Models to Explain Human Decisions

Train a language model to predict risky human choices and to write explicit explanations for those choices, using reward signals tied to decision outcomes rather than only text imitation.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Behavioral models often face a split objective. Some models predict what people choose, but they do not explain why. Other models can produce verbal explanations, but those explanations are not tied tightly to the decisions being predicted. This paper tries to join the two. It starts from a pretrained language model and trains it with reinforcement learning, meaning the model gets reward after generating an explanation and a choice prediction, based on whether the resulting output matches human decision data. The central idea is to reward explanations that help the model land on the right choice pattern, because explanations that ignore the true decision factors usually lead to worse behavioral predictions. That creates pressure for the written reasoning trace to encode real decision factors instead of decorative text.

Zero assumption version

Why prediction and explanation usually come apart in cognitive models

A model of risky choice should say both what option a person will pick and what features of the gamble drove that choice. Standard neural predictors often do the first job only. They map task inputs to outputs well, but their internal computation is hard to read directly.

Language models can write fluent explanations, but plain prompting does not ensure those explanations correspond to the actual choice prediction. The explanation can sound plausible while being weakly connected to the decision rule that produced the answer.

The paper's move. Reward the model for explanations that lead to accurate human-choice predictions, because explanation quality should be tied to behavioral fit rather than surface fluency alone.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start from a pretrained language model.

Use broad language ability as the base, because the model must already be able to produce coherent natural-language explanations before reward training can shape their content.

Show a risky decision problem.

Provide the outcomes and probabilities for the human choice task.

Generate a reasoning trace and a prediction.

Ask the model to state the factors it sees and then choose the option it expects a person to pick.

Compute reward from behavioral accuracy.

Give higher reward when the generated output matches human choices, because the explanation should support the correct decision pattern.

Update the model with reinforcement learning.

Change the model's weights, meaning the learned numerical settings inside the network, so high-reward explanation-and-prediction patterns become more likely.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  decision factors encoded inside the reasoning trace

The hidden quantity is the part of the model's computation that identifies which features of a risky choice should drive the prediction: probability weight, outcome magnitude, loss aversion, and related choice factors. The paper pushes those factors into the explicit reasoning trace.

That matters because a trace is useful only if it carries the variables that actually influence the output. Reinforcement learning creates this pressure by rewarding the final behavioral fit. A trace that ignores the decisive features will usually lead to a worse prediction and therefore a lower reward.

Why it works

Why reinforcement learning can make explanations behaviorally grounded

Plain supervised imitation would reward the model for matching example text, but the paper wants something stricter: the explanation should be useful for predicting human decisions. Reinforcement learning supplies that pressure because the reward is attached to the final output quality rather than to word-by-word similarity.

The reasoning trace matters during generation because it can shape what the model attends to before it emits the final choice. If the trace names high payoff, small probability, or avoidance of losses, that written intermediate state can push the model toward the same factors in its final prediction.

This setup can produce interpretable cognitive models because the explanation is not merely read off after the prediction. It is trained as part of the decision policy, meaning the rule that maps a presented choice problem to an output. When reward is well designed, useless but fluent text is filtered out since it does not support accurate behavioral predictions.

Math after the picture

Rewarded explanation-and-choice objective

First in words:

The model is trained to maximize expected reward, where reward rises when the generated trace and final choice together match human decision data.

Then the compact form:

expected_reward = average over trials of reward(trace, predicted_choice, human_choice)

Every symbol:

tracethe natural-language reasoning sequence generated before the final choice
predicted_choicethe option selected by the model for that trial
human_choicethe observed human decision used as the target
reward( )the score assigned to the model output on one trial
A tiny worked example
Suppose a trial offers a sure gain of 50 or a 20 percent chance to gain 300.
The model writes that the risky option has a larger upside but a small probability.
It then predicts that most people choose the sure gain.
If the human data for that trial show the same preference, the reward is high.
If the trace focused on the wrong factor and the model predicted the risky option instead, the reward would fall.

The core idea is that the reward is attached to decision quality, so the explanation is trained through its effect on the final prediction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward can favor convenient stories. If the reward depends only on final choice accuracy, the model may learn stock explanations that correlate with the answer without capturing the real choice process.
Human choice data can be noisy. People do not always respond consistently, so the reward signal can contain contradictory supervision across similar trials.
Readable text does not guarantee causal faithfulness. A trace can still sound coherent without matching the exact internal computation, so explanation quality needs separate evaluation beyond fluency.
Done right. The approach is most convincing when the reward reflects true behavioral fit, the traces vary with the presented choice features, and explanation quality is checked separately from raw prediction accuracy.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small risky-choice dataset with a few dozen trials and human choice frequencies. Train one language model with ordinary prompting and another with reward tied to behavioral fit. For each trial, collect the generated trace and final prediction. The demo should check whether the reward-trained model predicts the human majority choice more often, whether its traces mention the trial features that actually changed across conditions, and whether editing those features changes both the trace and the prediction coherently.

demo_score = (behavior_fit + trace_feature_alignment + coherent_counterfactual_shift) / 3
