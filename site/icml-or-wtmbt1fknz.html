PointCHR: Handling Sharp Corners in 3D Point Clouds

A 3D point cloud model captures fine details at sharp corners by giving those points more space to spread out.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

3D point clouds are sets of individual dots in space. When you try to learn what these dots represent (like shapes or objects), certain regions are hard: the really sharp edges and corners. This paper shows how to give those tricky high-curvature regions more room by using hyperbolic space (a non-flat coordinate system where distances expand dramatically near boundaries).

Zero assumption version

Sharp corners get lost in flat space

When learning from 3D point clouds, a model processes each point in standard flat 3D space (the usual x, y, z coordinates). Points in flat, gently curving regions spread out naturally and get learned well. But sharp corners and fine details are rare and easy to miss because the model has limited space to represent them distinctly. A beginner would try to make the grid finer everywhere, but that wastes resources on the smooth regions and still does not solve the crowding problem at the edges.

The gap: sharp corners get crowded together and disappear. High-curvature regions are rare and hard to separate in standard flat space.

The hidden quantity

Curvature: how bent the surface is locally

measured quantity  =  local surface curvature

Curvature measures how sharply the surface bends at each point. Near a flat region, curvature is close to zero. At a sharp edge or corner, curvature is high. The paper estimates curvature by looking at how the nearby points arrange themselves (geometric curvature estimation from point neighborhoods).

The model uses this quantity to decide which points need the special warped-space treatment. High-curvature points get projected into hyperbolic space; low-curvature points stay in regular space.

Why it works

Why non-flat space helps

In standard flat 3D space (the x, y, z coordinates you know), distances grow linearly: twice as far means twice the distance. When you have a limited box to fit points into, you can only fit so many before they crowd. Hyperbolic space has a fundamentally different property: distances expand exponentially as you move away from the center, so regions near the boundary have vastly more space per unit area. The paper detects which points sit at high-curvature regions (sharp corners) and projects them into the high-capacity zones of hyperbolic space. Now each sharp detail has room to live separately from the others, and the model can learn to distinguish them clearly.

Curvature-guided projection in non-flat space

First in words:

For each point in high-curvature regions, apply a learnable radial projection that moves the point outward in hyperbolic space, proportional to its estimated curvature. Low-curvature points stay in standard flat space.

Then the compact form:

P_hyperbolic(x) = (1 + alpha * curvature(x)) * x_normalized, where x is mapped to hyperbolic boundary

Every symbol:

xthe point in 3D space
curvature(x)the local surface curvature at that point
alphaa learned scaling factor controlling how strongly to push high-curvature points outward
x_normalizedthe point direction after normalization
P_hyperbolic(x)the final position in hyperbolic space
A tiny worked example
Point A at flat region: curvature=0.2, alpha=2.0 -> projection = (1 + 2.0*0.2)*direction = 1.4x outward
Point B at sharp corner: curvature=4.0, alpha=2.0 -> projection = (1 + 2.0*4.0)*direction = 9.0x outward
Same alpha, but Point B is pushed 9x farther because its curvature is 20x higher. Sharp corners get the room they need.

The equation shows that high curvature points are pushed radially outward more aggressively, using non-flat space to give them actual room to exist without crowding.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Curvature estimation noise. If the curvature measure is noisy or unreliable (e.g., on very sparse point clouds), the model will project the wrong points into hyperbolic space, wasting capacity on false positives.
Hyperbolic space overhead. Computing distances and projections in hyperbolic space is more expensive than flat-space operations. If most points are low-curvature, the overhead outweighs the benefit.
Overfitting to high-curvature regions. If the training data is dominated by smooth shapes with few sharp corners, the model may overfit the rare high-curvature examples and fail to generalize to new shapes with different corner patterns.
Done right. The paper learns alpha adaptively during training, so the model can balance when to use the hyperbolic-space advantage versus staying in simpler flat space. This adaptation works because alpha acts as a gate: when a shape has few sharp corners, alpha shrinks and most points stay in flat space; when corners are important, alpha grows and the model invests in hyperbolic projection.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 3D shape classifier (a cone, a cube, a sphere). Load point clouds of each. Train a baseline model in flat space, then train PointCHR. Visualize the learned representations: the baseline should show the cube's corners blended together as a crowded region, while PointCHR should show each corner separated in its own spread-out zone. Test the model on new point clouds of shapes it never saw, and measure how much clearer the corner details are in the PointCHR model's output.

detail_clarity = mean_curvature_feature_distinctness_in_hyperbolic - mean_curvature_feature_distinctness_in_flat_space

A good demo must show that sharp geometric features actually separate into distinct learned clusters when given hyperbolic space, and that this separation holds even on new shapes not seen during training.
