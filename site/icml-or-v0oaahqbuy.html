NeurVLA: Failure-Handling in Vision-Language-Action Models

A robot learns to notice when it is about to fail or has already failed, then fixes the decision by asking a reasoning model what went wrong.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A vision-language-action model (a robot brain that sees and acts) works well on tasks it has trained on, but stumbles in new situations. This paper adds two safety layers: one that stops the robot before a bad action happens, and one that recovers after a mistake occurs. Both layers use a separate reasoning engine (written rules combined with learned logic) that explains what went wrong in plain language. Feeding that explanation back into the robot helps because language connects abstract danger to concrete fixes—the robot can now learn from text descriptions of why actions fail, not just from repeating failures blindly.

Zero assumption version

Robots stumble in new worlds

A vision-language-action model is a robot brain trained to watch a scene and pick the next move: grasp the mug, turn the handle, place it down. It learns from many examples and does well on familiar tasks.

But in a new kitchen, with a different mug, or facing an obstacle the robot has never seen, it still confidently picks an action that fails. The naive fix—train on more examples—is slow and expensive. A smarter fix would be: make the robot notice when an action is dangerous, and if it does fail, let it pause and reason through what went wrong instead of just trying again blindly.

The core problem. Robots act without foresight and learn nothing from their own mistakes.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a second decision layer: a neural-symbolic reasoner that intercepts the robot's raw action choice and asks, 'Is this safe? If not, why?' This extra layer sits between the robot's initial action proposal and the final decision sent to the arm.

the object it moves  =  the action decision before it is executed
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: failure risk

measured quantity  =  failure risk (the chance the move will go wrong)

Failure risk is not a single number—it is a pattern the robot learns to spot. It emerges from combining two signals: a symbolic layer (explicit rules: 'if hand is near glass, raise risk') and a learned layer (the neural part learns from past mistakes which situations predict trouble). Together, they produce a risk score for each action.

The paper estimates failure risk by looking at the scene, the proposed action, and historical patterns of what went wrong before in similar situations. When risk is high, the paper routes the decision to a large language model for deeper reasoning instead of letting the robot act blindly. It computes risk as a weighted mix: explicit safety rules (symbolic layer) plus learned patterns from recorded failures (neural layer).

Why it works

How failure risk unlocks better decisions

The core idea is that a robot's raw action choice (from the vision-language model) and the actual success of that action are two different things. Failure prevention works by inserting a filter: if the action's risk score is high, do not execute it blindly. Instead, ask a reasoning engine—a large language model given context about the scene, the proposed action, and why it is risky—to suggest an alternative.

This has two layers of protection. First, the symbolic layer (hand-written rules) catches obvious hazards like 'gripper too close to breakable object.' Second, the neural layer (learned from past failures) catches subtle patterns the rules missed: 'this shape of mug tends to slip in this grip.' When either signals danger, the system pauses and reasons through the decision with language, which is slower but far more reliable. The robot then internalizes this reasoning—over time, the vision-language model itself begins to learn when actions are risky, so it proposes safer moves to start with.

Math after the picture

The risk equation

First in words:

failure risk equals the weighted sum of symbolic rule outputs plus the learned neural failure classifier, where symbols and learned scores share equal weight

Then the compact form:

risk_score = alpha * symbolic_score(scene, action) + (1 - alpha) * neural_classifier(embeddings)

Every symbol:

risk_scorethe computed danger level for the proposed action
symbolic_scoreoutput from hand-coded safety rules (1 = unsafe, 0 = safe)
scenethe robot's camera view
actionthe proposed next move
neural_classifierlearned model trained on past failures
embeddingsmathematical summaries of the scene and action
alphaweight balancing explicit rules versus learned patterns, tuned per task
A tiny worked example
Scenario: robot proposes to grasp a glass near the table edge. Scene is: fragile object, gripper at risk. Action is: grasp.
Symbolic rule says: object is fragile AND gripper risky -> symbolic_score = 0.9 (very unsafe).
Neural classifier, trained on gripper failures, says: this shape + position matches 23 past failures -> neural_classifier = 0.75.
With alpha = 0.5: risk_score = 0.5 * 0.9 + 0.5 * 0.75 = 0.825 (high risk).
Since 0.825 > threshold (say 0.7), query LLM: 'Why is grasping unsafe here?' LLM replies: 'Glass is fragile. Try pushing it to center first.'
Robot adopts the safer action from the explanation.

The more the robot learns from failures, the higher the neural classifier score becomes for risky situations, which raises the total risk and triggers reasoning sooner next time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The symbolic rules are too strict. Hand-coded safety rules prevent useful actions by over-flagging risks. The robot never tries new moves because the rule fires. Fix: learn which symbolic flags are actually predictive of failure, not just theoretically risky.
The neural failure classifier overfits to the training environment. The model learned which situations fail in the lab, but in a real kitchen the lighting, object sizes, and arm calibration are different. Risks look different. Fix: train the classifier on diverse simulated environments and real-robot logs, not just one setup.
The LLM explanation is plausible but wrong. The reasoning model suggests an action based on common-sense, but common sense does not account for the robot's actual gripper strength or the specific friction coefficients in this kitchen. The 'safer' action still fails. Fix: ground explanations in the robot's measured capabilities (tested in practice), not just language.
Done right. The symbolic and neural layers agree on a few high-confidence failure patterns. The LLM explanations are tested in simulation before being deployed to the real robot. The learned risk model is retrained on real failures, so it adapts as the robot's body ages and the environment changes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple manipulation task: grasp objects of different shapes, sizes, and materials from a tabletop, and place them into bins. Start with objects the robot has seen in training. Then gradually add new objects (never-before-seen shapes) and new environments (different tables, lighting, clutter). Run the robot twice: once with the raw vision-language model, once with NeurVLA. Measure two things: success rate on new objects, and the number of times the robot stops before failing versus blindly crashing and having to recover.

generalization gain = success_rate_new_objects(with_reasoning) - success_rate_new_objects(baseline)

The metric must show that adding reasoning dramatically improves success in new situations—not by getting every action perfect, but by catching risky moves before they happen and recovering faster when they do.
