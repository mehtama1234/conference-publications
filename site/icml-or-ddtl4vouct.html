Capacity-Aware Mixture Law Enables Efficient LLM Data Optimization

Train cheaper by predicting the best data mix using a scaling law instead of trying all mixes on the big model.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models are trained on many different kinds of data (math problems, web text, code, etc.). Getting this mix right is extremely important but hugely expensive, because the only way to test a mix is to train the whole model—which costs thousands of dollars. This paper shows how to predict the best mix without training the big model at all, using a scaling law that learns the pattern from training smaller models. It works by measuring how model size and data mixture interact, then predicting which mix will work best for huge models.

Zero assumption version

Why data mixture selection is expensive

When you train a large language model, you combine data from many sources: math problems for reasoning, web text for facts, code for programming skill. The exact mix (this much math, that much web, that much code) makes a huge difference in how well the model works on downstream tasks. But figuring out the right mix is a nightmare. The naive approach is to try different mixes directly on the big target model (say, 55 billion weights). Each trial takes weeks and costs thousands of dollars in computing. Researchers have tried scaling laws—formulas that predict performance based on data amounts—but these formulas break down when you go from small models to huge ones. They fail to predict how the mix that works for a small model will scale to a big model.

A beginner might think: we can just fit a simple formula relating mix to performance and be done. But here is why that breaks: the relationship between data mixture and performance is nonlinear. Adding more math data helps a lot in a small model, because the small model's capacity is the bottleneck—extra math data fills in knowledge gaps; in a huge model with massive capacity, that same increase might help far less because the model already has enough capacity to absorb what it needs from existing data. The formula that works for a 7 billion-weight model gives the wrong answer for a 55 billion-weight model.

In one sentence. Instead of trying each data mix on the target model, fit a formula using small models that predicts how the best mix changes as you scale to huge models.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Sample pairs from small models

Train models at 2B, 7B sizes on different data mixes. Record validation loss for each size-mix pair.

Fit a capacity-aware loss law

Learn a formula that predicts validation loss from model size and data mixture. The formula captures how the best mix shifts when the model gets bigger.

Map loss to benchmark accuracy

Measure how validation loss on each data type (math, web, code) relates to final accuracy on real benchmarks. This bridges the gap from training data to downstream tasks.

Predict the best mix for the target model

Use the formula to extrapolate: given a 55B target model, what mix minimizes the predicted loss?

Train once on the target model

Train the big model with the predicted mix one time. No searching, no trial-and-error.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes how you allocate compute budget during training. Instead of assigning capacity equally to all data types (math, web, code), the method learns to distribute capacity unevenly across domains. A domain is just a category of training data (like all math problems, or all web text). When a model is small, it should spend a lot of capacity on math. When a model is huge, the same amount of data might use only a little bit of the larger capacity. The paper predicts exactly how this allocation should shift as the model grows.

the object it moves  =  capacity allocation across data domains
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: capacity per domain

measured quantity  =  effective capacity per domain

Every domain (math, web, code) is learned by the model using only a fraction of its total capacity (which comes from having a certain number of weights or knobs inside). A small model training on mixed data learns math and web in series, using capacity sequentially. A huge model can parallelize learning: math facts go into some weights, web facts into others. The paper measures this hidden quantity by asking: how much effective capacity does this model devote to learning domain i? It is not a literal partition; it is the model's implicit focus.

The paper estimates this by observing that each domain follows a power law: when you increase the capacity devoted to it, loss falls predictably. By fitting this power law on small models, the paper can infer the effective capacity. Then it uses this insight to predict how the allocation should change when you scale the model.

Plain reading. The model has a fixed amount of brain capacity. Different types of data (math, web, code) compete for that capacity. A small brain uses capacity greedily on what it sees; a huge brain can specialize. The paper predicts this specialization pattern.
Paper reading. The effective capacity allocated to domain i is denoted m-tilde-i. Training loss on domain i follows a power law: each domain's loss drops faster or slower depending on its exponent. Different domains have different exponents because they are intrinsically different in difficulty—some domains (like structured math) benefit greatly from extra capacity, while others (like web text) plateau quickly. The total capacity is bounded: the sum across all domains is at most the total model capacity M. The model implicitly solves this allocation problem at training time; the paper fits the formula's coefficients to predict it.
Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Here is the core insight: the relationship between model size and data mixture is not random—it follows a predictable pattern. When you train a model on mixed data, it allocates its capacity to each domain based on two things: how hard that domain is to learn (harder domains need more capacity) and how much data from that domain you give it. A small model must be selective—it allocates capacity where it helps most, because overall capacity is limited and adding more data in one domain means less effective capacity for another. A big model can afford to be generous, because its total capacity is much larger. But the principle is the same.

The paper models this by observing that each domain independently follows a power law: add more capacity, loss falls exponentially. The trick is that the exponent (how fast loss falls) is different for each domain. Math might improve quickly when you add capacity because math is structured and exact, so targeted learning moves the loss down sharply; web text might improve slowly because it is broad and unstructured, so extra capacity shows diminishing returns. This difference is the signal. Once you know this pattern, you can predict: for a 55B model with these exponents, if I want to minimize total loss under a fixed budget, how should I split the capacity? The prediction formula captures this allocation rule. Then, you map that validation loss prediction to final benchmark accuracy using a separate law learned from small models.

The cost saving comes from not training on the big model. You fit the formulas on tiny models (2B, 7B), which cost hours. Then you extrapolate, which is free. You only pay for one training run on the 55B model, to confirm the prediction. Compared to the naive baseline (try many mixes on the 55B model), this cuts cost by 50% or more.

Math after the picture

The equation: capacity-aware loss

First in words:

Total validation loss = sum over domains of training cost times capacity ratio, where training cost for each domain follows a power law. Optimization minimizes this by choosing how much capacity to give each domain, subject to total capacity being fixed.

Then the compact form:

L_total(M, r) = sum_i [ C_i + A_i (m_tilde_i)^(-a_i) ], subject to sum_i m_tilde_i = M

Every symbol:

L_totaltotal validation loss for a given model size and mixture
Mtotal model capacity (effective parameters)
rdata mixture (fractions from each domain)
m_tilde_ieffective capacity allocated to domain i
C_iirreducible loss for domain i (hard limit)
A_ilearning rate for domain i
a_iexponent: how quickly loss falls as capacity increases for domain i
A tiny worked example
Suppose a 55B model has three domains: math, web, code. Total capacity M = 55 (in billions).
From fitting on small models, we learned: math has a_math = 0.5, web has a_web = 0.3, code has a_code = 0.4.
The exponents tell us math improves quickly (0.5 is steep), web improves slowly (0.3 is shallow).
Optimization says: give more capacity to math (steep slope = high payoff), less to web (flat slope = low payoff).
If we solve the constrained optimization, we find: m_tilde_math = 20, m_tilde_web = 20, m_tilde_code = 15. Total = 55.
Loss for math: 2.0 + 8.0 * (20)^(-0.5) = 2.0 + 8.0 / 4.47 = 2.0 + 1.79 = 3.79.
Loss for web: 1.5 + 5.0 * (20)^(-0.3) = 1.5 + 5.0 / 2.17 = 1.5 + 2.30 = 3.80.
Loss for code: 1.8 + 6.0 * (15)^(-0.4) = 1.8 + 6.0 / 1.93 = 1.8 + 3.11 = 4.91.
Total loss = 3.79 + 3.80 + 4.91 = 12.50. This predicted loss then maps to benchmark accuracy via the second law.

The power-law form captures how adding capacity helps, but with diminishing returns: each new unit of capacity helps less than the previous one, because you are filling in increasingly rare knowledge gaps. The paper learns the exponents from small models, then solves this allocation problem for the big model.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Formula extrapolation fails for extreme model sizes. If the target model is vastly larger than the small models used to fit the law (e.g., fitting on 7B then predicting for 1T), the power-law assumption breaks down. The paper verifies predictions by training once on the target, so a huge extrapolation gap is caught, but it means you waste that trial.
The mix that works for one architecture may not work for another. The CAMEL law is specific to the Deepseek V3 architecture used in the paper. If you switch to a different architecture (GPT-style vs Llama-style), the domain learning rates change, and your formulas are invalid. You must refit on the new architecture.
Benchmark accuracy is not the only goal that matters. The loss-to-benchmark law assumes that minimizing validation loss on training data maximizes downstream accuracy. If your real goal is speed, toxicity, factuality, or memorization, the formula may point to the wrong mix. You need a separate law for each objective.
Done right: fit formulas at the architecture you care about, verify on the target once, measure the right objective. If you use CAMEL on the same architecture family and predict a range of plausible target sizes (within 5x of fitting size), the formula should transfer well. One validation run confirms it.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny version: train three small models (100M, 500M, 1B parameters) on a mixed dataset (say, 50% arithmetic, 30% facts, 20% code). For each model and each mix, record validation loss on each domain separately. Fit a power law to each domain. Then, use the fitted law to predict the optimal mix for a 2B model (never trained yet). Train the 2B model with that predicted mix. Measure: does the 2B model have lower average benchmark loss than the small models, and does it match the prediction?

prediction_error = predicted_loss_2B - actual_loss_2B, cost_saving = (3 trials on 1B + 1 trial on 2B) vs (10 trials on 2B)

The demo succeeds if predicted loss is within 10% of actual, and if three small models plus one confirmation trial cost less than searching on the target alone.
