Composable Sparse Subnetworks via Maximum-Entropy Principle

Split a single neural network into reusable specialist pieces by training sparse subnetworks that each handle only certain classes, then snap them back together like building blocks.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

A trained neural network's internal wiring is tangled—different parts handle different classes, but those pieces are fused together. This paper asks: can we unsnarl them? The method trains multiple sparse networks, each one an expert on just a subset of classes (dogs and cats, say), then combines these specialists back into a full network by adding their weights or averaging their predictions. The result is the same generalist performance, but now with interpretable, reusable pieces.

Zero assumption version

Why tangled networks fail to modularize

A trained neural network is a black box. Its millions of numerical knobs (weights) are optimized to classify all classes at once, but the specialized parts that recognize dogs, cats, trucks, and birds are deeply interwoven. You cannot simply extract one piece without breaking the others.

A beginner might try to take a trained network and surgically remove weights related to a single class, hoping to isolate a class-specific module. But removing weights breaks the forward path—computations depend on the whole network in complex ways, and cutting out a piece leaves the remaining wiring incomplete and inaccurate. The resulting subnetwork fails.

The problem in one sentence. Neural networks learn their modules in a fused state; existing networks cannot be decomposed into reusable class-specific pieces.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper isolates and retrains the weights (the millions of numerical knobs) of a network into separate, sparse packages, each tuned to recognize only a subset of the classes.

the object it moves  =  sparse weights per class-subset
The hidden quantity

Uncertainty on non-target classes

measured quantity  =  confidence leakage (how much a specialist wrongly guesses outside its target)

When a specialist network is trained, it naturally tends to make confident predictions on everything—even the classes it should ignore. Leakage is how much probability mass the network assigns to non-target classes. Lower leakage means the network has learned to be genuinely uncertain (or wrong in a safe way) on classes it should not touch.

The paper measures leakage by checking: when a specialist is shown images from a non-target class, does it guess confidently or does it hedge? A good specialist has very low leakage. The method enforces low leakage through a KL-divergence term, which is a mathematical measure of how different two probability patterns are (like comparing histograms of guesses). Here, it penalizes the specialist for being too confident on non-target classes.

Why it works

Why specialist subnetworks stay composable

The core insight: a specialist network should be two things at once. On its target classes (say, dogs and cats), it learns everything—every weight, every path, every fine detail. On all other classes (trucks, birds, planes), it should be genuinely unsure, outputting probabilities close to random guessing.

The method trains each specialist with two losses pulling in opposite directions. The first loss (standard cross-entropy) teaches it to classify its target classes correctly. The second loss (KL-divergence) penalizes it for being confident on non-target classes—it should predict near-uniform probabilities on those, like a machine that has never seen them. After training, a pruning step removes all the weights that were not needed for the target classes. What remains is a sparse, expert module.

When you add multiple specialists' weights together, each specialist has zeros (empty space) where it is not needed. Because the specialists have been trained to partition their expertise, the non-zero regions do not conflict. The result is a combined network whose weights are just the sum of the parts—mode-connected, stable, and recovers the full network's accuracy.

Math after the picture

Training a specialist with dual objectives

First in words:

total loss = cross-entropy (on target classes) + lambda * KL-divergence (penalty for confidence on non-target classes)

Then the compact form:

Loss = CE(y_target, pred_target) + lambda * KL(pred_nontarget || uniform)

Every symbol:

CE(y_target, pred_target)cross-entropy: how wrong the specialist is on target classes, in bits
pred_targetpredicted probabilities on the target classes
y_targettrue labels on target classes
KL(pred_nontarget || uniform)how far the specialist's predictions on non-target classes are from random guessing
pred_nontargetpredicted probabilities on non-target classes
uniformrandom guessing: equal probability on each non-target class
lambdastrength dial: how much to penalize leakage (larger = cleaner specialists, harder to train)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a specialist is trained on classes dog and cat (target), with 8 total classes.
On dog/cat images, it predicts correctly 95% of the time. Cross-entropy loss = 0.15 bits.
On truck images, it guesses [0.2, 0.2, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08] — spreading confidence evenly. KL from uniform = 0.01 bits.
With lambda=1, total loss = 0.15 + 1 * 0.01 = 0.16.
If the specialist was leakier, guessing [0.8, 0.1, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.01, 0.01], KL = 0.3 bits. Total loss = 0.15 + 1 * 0.3 = 0.45 — much higher penalty, so training avoids this.

The lambda parameter tuning is key: too low, and specialists leak everywhere; too high, and training fails to converge.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda too high, no convergence. If the penalty for non-target leakage is too strong, the training optimization gets stuck and the specialist never learns its target classes well.
Weights not mode-connected. On some datasets and architectures, the specialist subnetworks may not stay in a connected loss valley when summed. The path from specialist A to specialist B crosses a high-loss ridge, so adding them together creates interference.
Pruning removes necessary structure. If pruning is too aggressive (cutting weights too low), the specialists lose the shared feature detectors they need. Combined network accuracy drops even though individual specialists were accurate.
Done right. Train each specialist with careful tuning of lambda, verify mode-connection on a validation split, and use gentle pruning. On simple datasets (MNIST, FMNIST) and well-separated class structures, the method works reliably.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a sparse specialist network on MNIST, targeting only digits 0-4 (ignore 5-9). Use the KL-divergence loss to keep it uncertain on 5-9. Then train another specialist on 5-9, ignoring 0-4. Prune both networks to remove dead weights. Add their weight matrices together. Test the combined network on the full MNIST dataset (all digits 0-9).

recovery = combined_accuracy - specialist_A_accuracy_on_A - specialist_B_accuracy_on_B + baseline_single_network_accuracy

If the combined network recovers accuracy to within 2-3% of the baseline single network, and the leakage (incorrect guesses on non-target classes) stayed below 10% per specialist, the modularity is real.
