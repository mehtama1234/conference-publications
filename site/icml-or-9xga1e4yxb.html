From Memorization to Parameter Interference: How Overtraining Experts Harms Model Merging

Experts that are fine-tuned too long become harder to merge because late training pushes their weights toward narrow task-specific memorization that other experts do not share.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Model merging tries to combine several expert checkpoints into one stronger model without retraining from scratch, because reusing existing experts is cheaper than training a new joint model from the beginning. That sounds compatible with ordinary fine-tuning: make each expert as good as possible on its own task, then merge them. This paper shows the pipeline breaks late in fine-tuning. After a point, an expert gains more of its isolated performance by memorizing a small set of hard examples, and those late weight changes do not survive merging. When two or more experts memorize different hard corners, their updates interfere. The merged model forgets much of the late task-specific knowledge, so aggressive early stopping can beat full convergence for the merged result because it preserves broadly shared updates instead of late conflicting ones.

Zero assumption version

Why better experts do not always make a better merge

Suppose several teams fine-tune the same base model on different tasks. A later merge step combines the resulting checkpoints by averaging or otherwise mixing their weights. A common assumption is monotonicity: if each expert improves on its own task, the merged model should inherit those gains because the merge is expected to preserve whatever each expert learned. The paper shows this assumption is false.

The issue appears late in expert training. Early updates teach broad task structure that several tasks may reuse. Later updates often chase a small set of difficult examples that matter for the expert's own score but are idiosyncratic to that task. Those late changes move the weights in directions that another expert never needed. When the checkpoints are merged, the incompatible late moves cancel or overwrite each other, so the merged model loses value even while the individual experts improve because the merge can preserve only update directions that remain compatible across experts.

The main point. Fine-tuning past the point of broad task learning can hurt merging, because late gains often come from task-specific memorization whose weight changes conflict across experts.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training target for expert checkpoints used in merging. Instead of optimizing each expert all the way to its best isolated score, it chooses the checkpoint that yields the best downstream merge.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  merge-compatible weight change

Not every improvement in an expert checkpoint is equally mergeable. The useful hidden quantity is the fraction of the expert's weight update that remains aligned with updates from other experts. If an update captures broad structure, several experts may move in compatible directions and the merge can keep it. If an update encodes a narrow fix for a few hard examples, another expert often moves differently and the combination loses that information.

The paper ties harmful late training to memorization because the final gain is concentrated on a small set of difficult examples. That concentration matters: when only a few examples drive the update, the weight change becomes specialized, and specialized changes are more likely to interfere during merging.

Why it works

Why overtraining hurts the merged model

Merging works only for information that multiple checkpoints encode compatibly. During early fine-tuning, an expert mainly adapts the base model to its task distribution, meaning the pattern of examples it sees often. Those early changes can overlap with what other tasks also need, such as better representations of domain vocabulary or task format. Averaging or combining experts can preserve that shared movement because the checkpoints point in similar directions.

Late in training, the optimization process focuses on the remaining hard examples. Those examples are few, so the resulting weight changes are narrow and often high magnitude in a small part of the model. Another expert has its own hard examples and therefore its own narrow changes. When the checkpoints are merged, the narrow directions do not reinforce each other. They cancel, dilute, or overwrite each other, so the merged model keeps less of the late improvement than the expert itself.

Early stopping works because it accepts a slightly weaker expert checkpoint in exchange for a much more compatible update. That trade is favorable when the downstream goal is one merged model, not a collection of isolated experts. The paper reports this pattern across vision and language models, across several sizes, and for both full fine-tuning and LoRA adapters, which indicates the mechanism is about where late training signal goes rather than about one specific architecture.

Math after the picture

A simple merge view

First in words:

A merged checkpoint starts from the base model and adds the average expert update, so any update direction that experts do not share is weakened or canceled.

Then the compact form:

merged = base + (1 / K) * sum_k delta_k

Every symbol:

basethe shared pretrained checkpoint before expert fine-tuning
Kthe number of experts being merged
delta_kthe weight change learned by expert k relative to the base checkpoint
mergedthe final combined checkpoint
A tiny worked example
Suppose two experts share an early useful update of +0.4 on one weight. Averaging keeps +0.4 because both experts agree.
Now suppose one expert later adds +0.6 for a hard example while the other adds -0.5 for its own hard example.
The merge keeps only (+0.6 - 0.5) / 2 = +0.05 from those late changes.
Most of the late expert-specific gain disappears, even though each expert improved on its own task.

This equation is simplified, but it captures why alignment across expert updates matters more than isolated expert score.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Stopping too early can leave obvious gains unused. If the checkpoint is captured before the expert has learned the task's broad structure, the merge also loses that missing knowledge because there is nothing useful to combine.
Not every late update is harmful. Some tasks may still learn broadly useful structure late in training, so a fixed stopping rule can cut off recoverable merge gains because the harmful phase begins at different times on different tasks.
The merge rule itself still matters. A stronger merge algorithm can preserve more compatible information than plain averaging, so early stopping is not a full substitute for good checkpoint-combination methods because the final result still depends on how the checkpoints are combined.
Done right. Track merge quality throughout fine-tuning, watch when improvement concentrates on a small hard-example subset, and stop experts at task-specific checkpoints chosen for downstream combination rather than isolated accuracy.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Fine-tune two small experts from the same base model on related tasks. Save checkpoints every few epochs. For each pair of checkpoints, merge them by averaging the weight difference from the base model and evaluate both the stand-alone experts and the merged model.

Then inspect which training examples drive the last few expert improvements. The demo should show a late phase where isolated expert score still rises while merged score falls, because the additional gain is concentrated on a small hard-example subset whose updates are not shared across experts.

merge_retention = merged_gain_from_checkpoint / isolated_expert_gain_from_checkpoint

A convincing demo should show merge_retention dropping as training enters the memorization-heavy phase.
