Bi-Criteria Metric Distortion

Ask what happens when a voting rule may choose a small committee instead of one winner, and measure how much that extra freedom reduces the cost created by using only rankings.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Metric distortion studies what is lost when voters provide only rankings instead of exact utility numbers. In that framework, voters and candidates are points in a metric space, and the social cost depends on distance. Most prior work asked for one winner. This paper asks a broader question: if a rule may choose a committee of k candidates and each voter is served by the closest committee member, can the committee achieve lower distortion than any single winner? The answer depends sharply on the shape of the space. On a line, a constant-size committee can match the optimum cost. In two-dimensional spaces and on trees, that hope collapses unless the committee contains all candidates. The result matters because it separates the power of committee choice from the constraints imposed by the underlying space.

Zero assumption version

Why one-winner distortion leaves an open committee question

In metric distortion, rankings are easier for voters to report than exact utilities, but rankings hide intensity. Distortion measures how much extra social cost can result from that loss of information.

For one winner, the existing theory is well studied. The new question is whether allowing a committee of several candidates changes the picture when each voter is matched to the nearest chosen candidate. This is important because committee choice introduces a second resource: the rule may spend more winners to reduce service distance. The paper asks whether that extra resource can systematically overcome the information loss from ordinal rankings.

The paper's move. Measure distortion with two resources at once, chosen committee size and service cost, because committee freedom may or may not offset the information lost in rankings.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Model voters and candidates in a metric space.

Treat cost as distance from a voter to the chosen candidate or to the nearest member of a chosen committee.

Allow a committee of size k.

Give the mechanism extra choice power by letting it select several candidates instead of only one.

Compare ordinal choice with metric optimum.

Measure how much worse the ranking-based committee can be than the best committee that knows exact distances.

Study different geometries.

Analyze the line, the plane, and trees separately because the shape of the space changes what a small committee can cover.

Prove upper and lower bounds.

Show when a small committee can match optimum cost and when it cannot, because the space shape either supports or blocks that compression.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  coverage power of a small committee under a given space shape

The hidden quantity is how much of the metric space a small committee can cover without exact distance information. A committee helps only if a few representatives can stand close enough to many voters at once, because each voter is served by the nearest chosen member.

That quantity depends on the shape of the space. On a line, a few well-placed representatives can cover intervals efficiently. In two-dimensional spaces or on trees, local branching creates many directions to cover, so a small committee can miss some groups badly. This is why the paper gets a sharp separation across metrics.

Why it works

Why the shape of the space changes everything

A committee reduces service cost by letting each voter attach to the nearest chosen candidate. That creates a covering problem: can a few chosen points stand near all voters?

On a line, the answer is often yes. Order structure is simple, and a few committee members can partition the line into intervals with controlled distance. This is why the paper can obtain optimal cost with only a constant number of selected candidates in that setting.

In two-dimensional spaces and on trees, the situation changes. Branching and multiple directions create coverage demands that a small committee cannot satisfy uniformly. Even if rankings reveal relative order, they do not reveal enough metric detail to place a tiny committee optimally across all branches. That is why the lower bounds become strong: unless one selects essentially everyone, some voter groups remain costly.

Math after the picture

Committee distortion summary

First in words:

For a chosen committee, voter cost is distance to the nearest committee member. Distortion compares the total or worst voter cost of the ranking-based committee against the best committee with full metric knowledge.

Then the compact form:

distortion = cost(ranking_based_committee) / cost(metric_optimal_committee)

Every symbol:

ranking_based_committeethe committee chosen using only ordinal rankings
metric_optimal_committeethe best committee of the allowed size when exact distances are known
cost( ... )either the sum of voter distances or the maximum voter distance
distortionhow much extra cost is paid because only rankings are used
A tiny worked example
Suppose three neighborhoods lie on a line and the rule may choose two representatives.
If the ranking-based rule chooses two candidates that sit near the outer neighborhoods, the total voter distance may be 10.
Assume the exact-distance optimum with two representatives also has total distance 10.
Then distortion is 10 / 10 = 1, which is perfect.
If the same style of choice in a branching tree leaves one branch uncovered and raises total distance to 18 while the exact-distance optimum is 10, distortion becomes 1.8.

The paper studies both sum cost and worst-case cost, but the central comparison is always ranking-based committee cost against metric-optimal committee cost.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Committee size alone does not guarantee low distortion. Adding a few winners may still fail badly in complex space shapes because the missing metric information leaves the rule unable to place them where coverage is needed most.
A result for one metric may not transfer. Line-based intuition can break in trees or in the plane, because branching and directional spread create obstacles that do not exist on a line.
Objective choice still matters. A committee that looks good under total distance may still leave one group far away, because utilitarian and egalitarian goals weigh coverage differently.
Done right. The bi-criteria view is useful because it reveals exactly when extra winners can offset ordinal information loss and when the space shape prevents that rescue.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create three synthetic electorates: one on a line, one on a two-dimensional plane, and one on a tree. Let voters report only rankings over candidates. Compare the best ranking-based committee of size k with the best committee that knows exact distances.

The demo should plot distortion as k grows under both total-distance and worst-distance objectives. A convincing result shows quick approach to distortion 1 on the line and much slower or impossible approach in the plane and on trees because the space shape controls how useful extra committee seats really are.
