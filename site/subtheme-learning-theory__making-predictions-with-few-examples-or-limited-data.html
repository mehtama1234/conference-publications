Making predictions with few examples or limited data

Theoretical analysis of learning when data is scarce, including in-context learning, active selection of which data to gather, and efficient use of existing knowledge.

What this is

A radiologist spends years learning to read X-rays before they can diagnose rare diseases accurately. They can learn from few examples because they already understand anatomy, physiology, and common pathologies, which provides a foundation. A new model trained from scratch, given only ten examples of a rare disease, has no such foundation and would need far more examples. Yet modern large language models seem to learn from a few examples in seconds — asking a model to classify text after showing just three examples of the desired classification often works, which suggests that large pretraining gives access to hidden knowledge about language and reasoning. This subtheme investigates: how can learning happen with very limited data — what mechanisms allow a model to adapt with so few examples — and what hidden knowledge enables this by providing a foundation the way a radiologist's years of study do?

The challenge is that pure statistical learning theory says you need roughly as many examples as you have weights to estimate. If a model has a million weights, you need about a million examples to confidently learn. But you often have far fewer examples than weights. Somehow, pretrained models that learned on large datasets transfer their knowledge to new tasks, or architectural biases built into networks exploit hidden structure in problems. In some cases, you can select which examples to learn from (active learning), focusing on the most informative examples rather than random ones. In other cases, you get to see examples one at a time and adapt after each one (online learning). Understanding how to learn from scarce data requires using what was known before (foundations from other tasks or domains), structure in the problem, or the ability to choose wisely which data to use.

Researchers study few-shot learning — learning from only a handful of examples — by analyzing when and why transfer learning works. They investigate in-context learning in large models, where the model reads instructions and examples in its input and adapts its behavior without updating weights. They analyze active learning strategies that select the most informative examples to learn from, reducing the total examples needed. They study meta-learning — learning how to learn — where a model trained on many tasks learns to adapt quickly to new tasks. They also examine data augmentation (creating new examples from old ones through transformations) and regularization techniques that encode assumptions about the problem structure. The goal is to extract the most learning from each example and use what was known before to fill gaps where data is scarce.

How the work breaks down

Learning with limited data splits into five directions: active learning (picking which examples to label), in-context learning (adapting without weight updates), data distribution effects (how pretraining shapes generalization), data selection (choosing or creating the most useful examples), and learning under constraints (imperfect information or expert feedback). The split reflects how learning with scarce data depends on the foundation you already have, the examples you choose, and how you encode assumptions about what matters.

Active Learning: Choosing Which Examples to Label

Active learning solves a practical problem: you have unlimited unlabeled data but labeling is expensive. Instead of labeling examples randomly, you can ask for labels on the examples that teach you the most. The key question is: which examples, if labeled, would change your model's behavior the most? This matters because when you know, you can save labels, which means cutting costs and time.

Active Learning for Decision Trees builds a simple model (a decision tree) by asking for labels on the most informative examples, and proves it needs only logarithmically more labels—meaning it's almost as efficient as the best possible algorithm could be. Active Regression for Single-Index Models extends this to regression (predicting numbers instead of categories) with unknown link functions (the hidden rule connecting inputs to outputs), closing gaps in the theory that remained from earlier work. Asymptotic Theory of Iterated Empirical Risk Minimization (minimizing errors across examples by choosing which examples to label) shows that picking examples carefully can cause double descent—a phenomenon where adding more data actually hurts performance before it helps again because the labeling strategy overshoots—which reveals a fundamental tradeoff in how you spend your labeling budget between investing heavily early to avoid mistakes and gradually increasing labels to control for overshooting. Causal-EPIG tackles a harder problem: if you want to estimate treatment effects (what happens when you change something), which experiments should you run to learn the answer efficiently? The work shows that the right strategy changes depending on what you are measuring, and that no single approach beats all others because of these dependencies.

These papers connect because they all ask the same question: given a fixed budget of labels, which examples teach you the most if labeled? They progress from simple trees to regression to complex scenarios, each proving tighter bounds on sample complexity (how many labels you actually need).

In-Context Learning: Adapting Without Changing Weights

In-context learning is the surprising ability of large language models to adapt to new tasks by reading examples in the prompt—without ever updating their weights. Show a model three examples of 'translate English to French' and it often translates correctly. This seems to violate what we know about learning: you are supposed to update weights to learn something new. So what is actually happening inside the model when it adapts this way?

An Evolutionary Perspective on Modes of Learning in Transformers (models made of layers that compare words or phrases to each other) reveals that different environments favor different strategies. When the environment is stable and you can rely on consistent patterns, models prefer in-context learning (reading the prompt) because quick reading and memory retrieval is faster than updating internal weights, which saves computation. When the environment is chaotic and nothing is reliable, models prefer in-weight learning (updating their understanding) because this builds new generalizations that adapt to the chaos. This explains why some tasks trigger one mode and others trigger another based on environmental properties. Fine-Tuning Without Forgetting In-Context Learning identifies a real problem: if you update weights to improve the model on a specific task, you often break its in-context learning ability because the weight changes overwrite the capacity for reading prompts and retrieving patterns. The paper shows which specific weights to update so that both abilities survive together. In-Context Universal Approximation, Compositional Generalization, and Algorithm Emulation reveals the mechanism: Transformer models can store algorithms in their weights, and prompts act like program inputs. When you show examples in a prompt, the model is essentially running a stored algorithm that interprets those examples, which means in-context learning is really algorithm execution, not weight-based learning. Is In-Context Learning Learning? challenges this optimistic view by showing that the model is not always truly learning from your examples—it often succeeds by accident because the model's answers are insensitive to which examples you show or how many, suggesting the examples just trigger existing knowledge the model already has from pretraining.

These papers form a conversation about what in-context learning really is. The first establishes it as an alternative learning mode to weight updates. The second and third explain how this works: via algorithm execution. The fourth questions whether it deserves the name 'learning' at all, revealing that what looks like adaptation may just be information retrieval from what the model already knows.

Data Distributions: How Pretraining Shapes Few-Shot Learning

When a model is pretrained (trained on a large corpus before seeing your task), it develops knowledge about patterns. A model trained on billions of words learns that 'the' usually precedes nouns, that sentences discuss related concepts together, and so on. This foundation helps it learn new tasks with few examples because the model already knows which patterns to look for. But pretraining is expensive, requiring enormous computation and data acquisition. The remaining question is: how much does pretraining actually cut your label requirement in practice—from ten thousand labels to ten, for example—and does this savings depend on which pretraining dataset you use, which task domain you test on, or how mathematically complex the problem is?

On the 'Induction Bias' in Sequence Models compares Transformers to older architectures (RNNs) and finds that Transformers require many more examples to learn because they do not transfer knowledge learned on short sequences to longer sequences the way RNNs do. This means Transformers depend more on pretraining to fill the gap. How Does the Pretraining Distribution Shape In-Context Learning reveals a tradeoff: if you pretrain on skewed data with extreme differences between common and rare examples, the model learns to tolerate outliers but loses generalization to tasks it has never seen. Pretrain-Test Task Alignment Governs Generalization shows that in-context learning works best when the pretraining tasks match the test tasks. If you pretrain on English text but test on code, generalization fails because the patterns are different. Understanding the Role of Training Data in Test-Time Scaling proves that training on varied tasks, on-topic tasks, and hard tasks produces models that perform best when given more compute at test time. Task hardness is measured by a mathematical property (the smallest eigenvalue of feature covariance) that captures how separated the classes or regions are.

These papers all identify how pretraining provides a foundation for few-shot learning, but they disagree on what makes a foundation effective: how the architecture transfers knowledge, how the pretraining data distribution skews, whether pretraining tasks match test tasks, or whether test-time tasks are hard enough to require depth.

Data Selection: Choosing or Creating the Right Examples

Not all data is equally useful. A random sample of examples teaches you less than a carefully selected sample because some examples are redundant—they tell you the same thing—while others reveal patterns you have not seen. But how do you know which examples to keep and which to discard automatically? And if you can create new examples through data augmentation (rotating images, adding noise) or synthesis (generating new data), which new examples help most by covering gaps in what you have learned?

DreamDojo learns a robot world model (a simulation of how the robot moves) from large-scale human videos by focusing on the video clips that teach the most about the robot's task. Active Learning with Low-Rank Structure for Data Selection identifies structure in the data (patterns that repeat or follow predictable rules) and uses this structure to select examples efficiently because structured data contains dependencies that let you infer patterns from fewer examples. High-dimensional Analysis of Synthetic Data Selection shows that in high-dimensional spaces (many features), matching covariance (the correlation structure between features) is optimal for deciding which synthetic data to create or keep because covariance captures the geometric shape of the problem. This mathematical principle carries from linear models to deep neural networks, which means covariance matching works whether your model is simple or complex. On the Difficulty of Learning a Meta-network for Training Data Selection finds that trying to learn a separate model that picks which data to use is hard—complex learned selectors fail—but a simple approach works: increase batch size and engineer features by hand, yielding an average 5% improvement in accuracy over random selection across benchmark tasks.

These papers reveal a theme: data selection is hard to learn automatically, but simple structural insights (covariance matching, batch size scaling, hand-engineered features) often beat learned selection strategies. This means data selection should rely on domain knowledge and mathematical properties rather than trying to train a separate model to learn the selection rule.

Learning Under Constraints: Experts, Preferences, and Uncertainty

In many real-world settings, you do not have clean labels or perfect data. Instead, you have expert opinions that often disagree with each other, preference judgments (one option is better than another), or noisy measurements where the true value is buried in measurement error. Learning with few examples becomes even harder under these constraints because each example carries less reliable information. How do you extract trustworthy patterns from imperfect information?

How Can Mamba Learn In Context with Outliers proves that an alternative architecture (Mamba) tolerates outlier examples better than Transformers—it maintains accuracy even when outliers appear frequently, whereas Transformers' accuracy collapses because outliers break their pairwise-similarity mechanism (how they compare words or phrases). Learning U-Statistics with Active Inference combines active learning with stable statistics: instead of asking for the next most-informative single label, you strategically ask for measurements that reduce uncertainty in aggregate statistics (summaries like means or variances) because aggregate summaries are more stable than individual measurements in the presence of noise. STAND: Self-Aware Precondition Induction learns when a rule applies (its precondition) from small amounts of human-guided data by letting humans steer the learning process, and beats decision trees and other baselines because human guidance filters the hypothesis space. The Power of Power Law shows that power-law distributed examples (where common examples are very common and rare ones are very rare, matching real data) help models reason about compositional problems better than uniform distributions because power-law skewness matches the structure of the world. Theory of Scaling Laws for In-Context Regression explains that model depth (stacking more layers) only helps in-context learning when context window is limited because shallow models suffice for stable patterns, or when the patterns change across examples because depth can adapt to changing conditions. Understanding In-Context Learning on Structured Manifolds proves that Transformers can achieve optimal regression rates on manifolds (smooth curved spaces) if you show them enough varied training tasks, with complexity growing by the intrinsic dimension of the manifold—a property of the geometric space, not the number of features you use. Universal Approximation with Softmax Attention proves that softmax attention (computing relative importance weights between words or phrases) can approximate stepping down a slope to minimize error in context, which explains why in-context learning works: it is performing an optimization algorithm inside the forward pass. What Does Preference Learning Recover From Pairwise Comparison Data reveals that preference learning (learning from 'A beats B' judgments) can recover true preferences if the data has sufficient connectivity (everyone rated overlapping items so comparisons connect) and sufficient separation (items do not have nearly identical quality so comparisons distinguish them). When More Experts Hurt shows that if you have too many expert classifiers, you overfit because the model learns spurious patterns tied to the specific experts rather than learning the true task. Why Ask One When You Can Ask k identifies a practical insight: deferring to multiple top-k experts (not just the single best) yields better accuracy-cost tradeoffs than trusting one expert because different experts make different kinds of errors.

These papers tackle the realistic scenario where data is imperfect or restricted: containing outliers, requiring aggregate summaries, needing human guidance, involving preference judgments, and provided by expert opinions. They show that learning under constraints requires both algorithmic choices (selecting which experts to trust, which measurements to request) and theoretical understanding (what makes preference data learnable, which architectures tolerate outliers).
