Token Positions Reveal Model Knowledge

The shape and angles of how a language model arranges token positions directly reflect how it thinks about problems.

Language models like GPT are most uncertain when they pick the next word from a crowd of similar options. This paper discovers that the shape and angles between token positions (how each word is stored as a list of numbers in the model's memory) reveal how the model actually understands a problem—like how it grasps board positions in chess.

Zero assumption version

Why does the model stumble at choice points?

When a language model like GPT finishes a sentence, it outputs a list of candidates (the next word) with a confidence score for each. Most of the time one word is obviously the strongest. But at some points—especially tricky reasoning steps—the model spreads its confidence across many similar words equally. At those moments, changing just one token slightly breaks the chain of thought.

A beginner would guess the model is simply confused or ran out of training examples. But the paper shows something deeper: at these high-uncertainty points, the shape of the space the model uses to store token information reveals exactly how the model has organized its knowledge about the problem.

The method. Measure how the space of token positions curves at points where the model is most uncertain, and show that the direction of the curve corresponds to task-relevant concepts.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Find the uncertain moment

Identify where the model gives similar scores to many tokens, not one clear winner.

Step 2: Look at token positions

Examine the coordinates of each token in the model's memory space at that moment.

Step 3: Measure how the space curves

Compute how the directions between tokens bend and twist using mathematical curvature.

Step 4: Read the curvature direction

See which way the curve bends; that direction encodes what the model cares about.

Step 5: Verify against the task

On chess, confirm that curvature directions align with board regions and piece rank.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not add new layers or rewrite the model. Instead, it interprets the existing token positions (each word is stored as coordinates in the model's memory). It treats the model's token memory space as a curved mathematical surface and extracts meaning from how that surface bends at points of high uncertainty.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: curvature direction

measured quantity  =  curvature direction

Curvature direction is the way the space between token positions twists and bends. Imagine a flock of birds flying together—if they all shift in one direction together, the flock has curvature in that direction. In this paper, at the points where the model is most uncertain, the model's token positions shift together in a direction that reveals the model's internal concept.

The paper computes this from token coordinates alone—the numbers the model stores for each word. It does not require retraining or accessing hidden layers. It only needs the token position numbers at the moment of prediction.

Why it works

Why the shape of token space reveals model knowledge

A language model stores each token as a list of numbers in a high-dimensional space. At any moment, the model decides which token to output next by measuring distances and angles between tokens. When the model is uncertain, many tokens have nearly equal weight, so small changes to the space shift which token wins.

At these high-uncertainty moments, the arrangement of token positions encodes what the model cares about. If two tokens are close together in the space at a decision point, the model sees them as similar. If a group of tokens all shift in one direction together, that direction represents a concept the model is weighing. By measuring how this space curves at uncertainty points, we directly read what matters to the model in its choice.

In chess reasoning, when the model must decide on the next move, the token space curves most sharply in directions corresponding to important pieces (king, queen) and board zones. This is not coincidence—the model has tuned token positions during training so that at decision points, the curvature reflects what matters for the task.

A tiny worked example
Imagine three tokens: King at (5,3), Queen at (5,2), Pawn at (4,2) in a 2D space.
At an uncertainty point, King and Queen both shift up by 0.3 (curvature in y-direction = 0.3).
Pawn stays still (different piece, less central to the decision).
The curvature direction points up (toward important pieces). This direction is semantic—it encodes piece rank.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Low-uncertainty moments have weak signals. When the model is confident in one token, the curvature does not shift much. Extracting curvature at these points gives noisy results. The method works best at choice points where many tokens tie.
Curvature in high dimensions is hard to visualize. In a real model with thousands of token dimensions, the curvature lives in a high-dimensional space. Projecting it down to human-readable form can lose information or introduce artifacts.
Not every semantic concept has curvature signature. Some model decisions may not reflect in token positions—for instance, if two concepts are equally important at a decision point, the curvature may split. The method only reveals positions that correlate to task structure at test time.
Done right, curvature aligns with interpretable features. On chess, the method cleanly recovers board regions and piece importance from positions alone, without labeling or fine-tuning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small language model trained on chess position descriptions and next-move predictions. At points where the model is most uncertain about the next move, extract token positions, compute curvature, and measure how well curvature direction aligns with piece importance (king and queen have higher curvature than pawns) and board regions (center squares have higher curvature than edges). A strong demo would show that curvature ranks pieces and zones the same way chess experts do, without the model ever seeing piece-rank labels.

alignment = (correlation between curvature direction and piece rank) + (correlation between curvature direction and board centrality)
