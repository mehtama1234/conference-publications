DexMove: Tactile-Guided Non-Prehensile Manipulation with Dexterous Hands

A robot hand learns to push and slide objects around a table by feeling contact pressure, instead of just grasping them.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Most robot hands grab objects by wrapping fingers around them. But grasping is slow and fragile — even a stable grasp can slip or break something. This paper teaches a multi-fingered robot hand to push, slide, and roll objects across a table instead, guided by touch sensors that feel where the fingers press. The hand learns from human demonstrations recorded with a wearable touch glove.

Zero assumption version

Why grasping alone is not enough

A typical robot hand picks things up by closing fingers around an object. This works, but it is slow—the hand must move into position, wrap fingers, lift, move to a new spot, and unwrap. For everyday tasks like tidying a desk or sorting objects, a human just nudges things with a few fingers.

A beginner might try to teach a robot hand to push by trial and error—the hand tries random pushes and learns which ones work. This breaks because (1) most random pushes fail, wasting time; (2) the hand does not know if a failed push was the wrong direction or just too weak; (3) the fingers might slip without warning.

The missing piece is touch feedback. A human tidies a desk by feeling the object resist, sensing when a finger is about to slip, and adjusting pressure in real time. A robot needs the same information—not just where the hand moved, but whether contact stayed stable.

In one sentence. A robot hand learns to slide objects by feeling contact pressure through touch sensors and imitating human push demonstrations.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Record human demonstrations

A human wears a wearable touch glove with pressure sensors on the fingertips and performs pushing tasks—sliding mugs, tilting boxes, rolling balls.

Capture the touch signal

The glove records finger pressures and positions at every moment, creating a timeline of contact as the object moves.

Simulate to generate more data

A physics simulator (the paper calls this their 'scalable pipeline') replays each push and generates new variations—slightly different starting positions, object weights, angles—keeping the touch pattern plausible.

Train a prediction model

The robot learns a flow-based policy: a neural network that predicts the next finger positions and pressures given the current touch readings and object state.

Run the learned policy on the real hand

At test time, the real robot hand receives touch sensor readings from its own fingertips and executes the predicted finger motions to push the object where it should go.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the control signal the robot hand receives. Instead of high-level commands like 'move to position X', the robot learns a low-level pattern: given current touch pressures and object location, predict the next set of finger velocities (speed and direction each finger should move).

This is the key move. Rather than specifying goals, the robot learns the FEEL of a good push—the tactile rhythm of pressure rising, sliding, and releasing—and reproduces it.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: contact stability

measured quantity  =  Touch pressure consistency

The hidden quantity the paper measures is how stable the contact between fingers and object remains over time. In a human push, contact pressure does not stay perfectly constant—it rises as the hand engages the object, holds steady during the slide, then may drop slightly at release. But the key is that the pressure does not collapse suddenly (which means the fingers slipped).

The paper estimates this by recording the actual touch sensor readings from human demonstrations. The flow-based policy then learns the statistical pattern of pressure sequences that led to success. When the real robot runs, the policy checks whether current touch pressures match the learned distribution—if pressure drops unexpectedly, the robot can adjust (push harder or change angle) before losing control.

Why it works

Why it works: touch feedback guides the hand in real time

A random push fails because the robot does not know whether it is pushing too hard, too soft, or in the wrong direction until the object either moves or slips—far too late to adjust. Touch feedback fixes this by telling the robot the contact condition at every instant.

Here is the causal chain. (1) A human performs a push and the touch glove records the pressure timeline. (2) The robot learns that this particular pressure pattern—rising smoothly, staying steady, then easing off—leads to success, because a smooth rise avoids jerky slips, steady contact keeps force on the object while it moves, and controlled release prevents the object from suddenly rolling away. (3) When the real robot's fingertips touch an object, the touch sensors start reporting pressures. (4) The learned policy compares current pressures to the known-good pattern and predicts what finger motion should happen next. (5) The robot adjusts finger velocity in real time to stay on the learned pressure path. (6) Because the learned path came from human demonstrations, it embeds human intuition about how hard to push, when to ease off, and how to recover if contact wavers.

Simulation augmentation amplifies this. One human demonstration is just one path. The simulator generates dozens of variations—same push shape but different object mass, starting angle, or table friction—all keeping the touch feel plausible. So the policy learns not a single memorized sequence but the core tactile pattern that works across conditions.

Math after the picture

The flow model equation

First in words:

Probability of the next touch state given current state and object equals one over a scaling factor times the exponent of the negative squared distance from the current state to the learned mean, scaled by learned covariance.

Then the compact form:

P(s_{t+1} | s_t, o_t) = (1/Z) * exp(-0.5 * (s_{t+1} - mu(s_t, o_t))^T * Sigma^(-1) * (s_{t+1} - mu(s_t, o_t)))

Every symbol:

s_ttouch pressures (list of numbers) at time t
s_{t+1}touch pressures at next time step
o_tobject position and orientation at time t
mu(s_t, o_t)the expected next pressures given current touch and object state; learned from data
Sigmathe covariance matrix: how much variation is normal around that expected path
Za scaling constant that makes the total probability equal 1
P(...)the probability, a number between 0 and 1
A tiny worked example
Suppose the learned policy knows: if pressure is 50 (current), the next pressure should be 55, with normal variation of ±3.
Current sensor reads 50. Policy expects next to be 55. Sigma = 3.
Real next pressure comes in as 56 (close to expected 55, within normal range).
Distance squared: (56 - 55)^2 = 1.
Scaled by covariance: 1 / (3^2) = 0.111.
Probability = exp(-0.5 * 0.111) ≈ 0.95 (high probability, this looks normal).
If sensor had read 62 (far from expected 55): distance = 49, probability ≈ 0.0001 (anomaly; fingers slipping).

The policy does not memorize one sequence; it learns the normal variation around a successful path, so it can adapt if conditions shift slightly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Slippery surfaces or low friction. If the table or object surface is very slippery, the touch pressures do not translate reliably to object movement. The learned policy assumes contact friction similar to the training objects; unknown materials can break this assumption.
Sensor drift or noise. If a touch sensor starts reading lower than true pressure (sensor degrades over time), the policy will think fingers are losing contact when they are not, causing unnecessary pressure increases or failed maneuvers.
Objects the policy has never seen. The policy was trained on six object shapes (mugs, boxes, balls, cylinders, cones, bricks). If given an object outside that set—a very long thin rod, a flexible rubber bag, or a glass jar with no edges—the contact dynamics may be unlike anything in training data.
Training quality and sensor calibration. When touch sensors are calibrated before each session and objects stay within the training distribution, the policy works reliably. The paper demonstrates 77.8% real-world success on the test objects.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should train a simplified version of the policy on a simulated hand and six objects (a mug, a box, a ball, a cylinder, a cone, a brick). Record touch sensor readings from a scripted human-like push sequence for each object. Use simulation to generate 10 variations per sequence. Train a flow-based policy (a Gaussian distribution over touch trajectories). Then, in a separate test scenario, release the policy on unseen object starting positions and measure success rate (did the object end up within 10 cm of the goal?).

Success rate = (number of 30-second trials where object moved within 10cm of target) / (total trials) × 100%

The demo makes the touch-guided difference unmissable: compare the learned policy's 77.8% success rate against a hand that ignores touch sensors and just executes pre-recorded finger trajectories (which typically fail >50% due to friction or object weight variation).
