Semantic Navigation: Tracking How Humans Retrieve Concepts

Track how humans search through meaning space when retrieving concepts by measuring the path their ideas take as coordinates in high-dimensional vectors.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

When you think of the word 'dog,' your mind is not just retrieving a stored picture. Instead, it navigates through a vast mental space—from specific memories (your family pet) to general facts (dogs are animals) to related concepts (cats, wolves, loyalty). This paper shows how to mathematically measure that navigation by turning each concept you produce into a point in a geometric space, then tracking the sequence of points as a path. By analyzing these paths, researchers can spot differences between healthy brains and those affected by degeneration, across multiple languages and cultures.

The Hard Part: Measuring How the Mind Searches

When someone says 'dog,' we assume they retrieved one fact from memory. But they actually navigated through dozens of possibilities: Does the person mean their own dog? A category of dogs? The word's connection to other animals? Linguists used to count only the final words and guess how many a person could name. This misses the actual mental journey.

A beginner might try: just collect all the words a person says, count them, and compare healthy people to those with memory disease. But this ignores how the person moves through ideas. Two people saying the same ten words might have taken very different mental paths—one jumping erratically, one moving smoothly. You miss the search process itself.

The paper's move in one sentence: Turn each person's spoken concepts into a sequence of coordinates in a high-dimensional space, then measure the speed, direction, and scatter of the path they trace.

What the paper changes

What the Paper Changes

The standard approach: convert individual words to vectors, compare their positions. The paper instead: convert the cumulative history of all words spoken so far into one vector (a list of numbers created by a text model). This single change forces each vector to hold memory. When someone says 'dog' first and 'cat' second, those become two different coordinate points because the second one encodes both words.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Search Path Shape

measured quantity  =  The shape of the search path

The paper measures something you cannot see directly: how 'organized' or 'scattered' each person's mental search is. This is not about what words they say, but how their path through meaning-space behaves—its velocity (speed of jumps between concepts), entropy (consistency of step sizes), acceleration (how often they change direction), and distance from their own average position.

The paper estimates this by taking each person's spoken sequence, converting each cumulative state to coordinates, and then computing five metrics on the resulting path: how far each step is, how fast, how much the step sizes vary, how much they change direction, and how far they stray from their mental neighborhood.

Why it works

Why It Works: The Bridge Between Brain and Numbers

A text embedding model (trained on billions of words) learns that similar ideas sit close together in a high-dimensional space—imagine an invisible map where 'dog' and 'wolf' are neighbors, but 'dog' and 'mathematics' are far away. When a person retrieves 'dog,' their brain is retrieving a concept at a specific spot on this map.

By building the trajectory cumulatively, each new word encodes not just itself but the entire context of the search so far. This forces the path to capture what cognitive science calls 'working memory'—the person's awareness of what they already said. If they just keep saying synonyms (dog, canine, hound), the cumulative embeddings move slowly and in tight loops. If they jump between unrelated ideas (dog, telescope, music), the embeddings make large, jagged leaps. These physical properties of the path—length of each step, direction changes, consistency—are exactly what you need to distinguish organized semantic retrieval from disorganized or dysexecutive retrieval.

The magic is that no two people navigate the same way: healthy brains produce smooth, purposeful paths; patients with neurodegenerative disease produce erratic, repetitive paths. The cumulative embedding path is literally a fingerprint of how that person's brain is searching through meaning.

Turning the Path Into Numbers

First in words:

For each person i and concept c, measure: (1) step size at position t = cosine_distance(embedding[t-1], embedding[t]); (2) velocity magnitude at position t = magnitude(embedding[t] - embedding[t-1]); (3) acceleration at position t = magnitude(velocity[t] - velocity[t-1]); (4) entropy = shannon_entropy(split_by_median(step_sizes)) normalized by T; (5) distance_to_centroid[t] = cosine_distance(embedding[t], mean_of_all_embeddings_for_concept_c).

Then the compact form:

step_size[t] = cosine_distance(e[t-1], e[t]) velocity[t] = ||e[t] - e[t-1]|| entropy = H(high_steps, low_steps) / log(2) dist_centroid[t] = cosine_distance(e[t], centroid_c)

Every symbol:

e[t]cumulative embedding at step t (the vector combining all words from step 1 to t)
cosine_distancehow far apart two points are in high-dimensional space, on a scale from 0 (same point) to 1 (opposite ends)
velocitythe direction and speed of motion from one step to the next
entropyhow much the step sizes vary: high entropy means big and small steps mixed; low entropy means all steps similar
centroid_cthe average position of all embeddings for one concept across all that person's utterances
A tiny worked example
Person says three words: dog, cat, bird.
Step 1: embedding('dog') sits at position [0.1, 0.2, 0.05, ...] in 768-dimensional space.
Step 2: embedding('dog cat') sits at position [0.12, 0.18, 0.08, ...]. Cosine distance = 0.15 (step size).
Step 3: embedding('dog cat bird') sits at position [0.11, 0.22, 0.06, ...]. Cosine distance = 0.18 (step size).
Velocity at step 2 = |[0.12, 0.18, 0.08] - [0.1, 0.2, 0.05]| = magnitude of [0.02, -0.02, 0.03] = 0.04.
Velocity at step 3 = magnitude of [0.11, 0.22, 0.06] - [0.12, 0.18, 0.08] = magnitude of [-0.01, 0.04, -0.02] = 0.045.
Acceleration = |0.045 - 0.04| = 0.005 (the person's direction changed only slightly).
If both steps are above the median step size, entropy is low (consistent); if alternating, entropy is high (erratic).

These five numbers (step size, velocity, entropy, acceleration, distance to centroid) are the skeleton of the trajectory—they tell you the shape of the person's search without naming the words they said.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Short sequences lack context. Cumulative vectors need at least 3 words to build real context. A person who names only one or two items leaves a path too short to measure reliably. The paper finds that for sequences under 5 words, using non-cumulative vectors (just the individual word) gives clearer results because there is not enough history to benefit from cumulative stacking.
Embeddings trained on different data behave differently. If an embedding model is trained primarily on technical documents, its 'animal' neighborhood may not match one trained on everyday speech. The paper tests multiple embeddings and confirms the patterns hold, but users must pick embeddings matched to their language and domain.
Centroid depends on having enough variety. If a person only repeats one concept (dog, dog, dog), there is no 'neighborhood' to measure distance from. The distance-to-centroid metric requires at least a few distinct ideas.
Done right. Test on multiple datasets (four in the paper), multiple languages (Spanish, Italian, German), and multiple embedding models. Confirm that healthy controls and diseased patients separate consistently across all these variations. The paper does this and finds the same patterns everywhere.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo that records a person naming objects in a category for 60 seconds, then plots their trajectory in 2D projection. Overlay trajectories from several healthy people and several patients with known memory disease. A visual reader should see: healthy trajectories cluster in a tight, smooth region; diseased trajectories scatter or loop. Compute the five metrics for each person. Show that step-size consistency is low (high entropy), velocity varies wildly, and distance-from-centroid is far in the diseased group, compared to the healthy group.

score = (entropy_patient - entropy_healthy) / std(entropy_healthy) + (centroid_distance_patient - centroid_distance_healthy) / std(centroid_distance_healthy)

The demo must make it impossible to miss that the shape of the path, not just the word count, distinguishes the two groups.
