TriQDef: Disrupting Semantic and Gradient Alignment to Prevent Adversarial Patch Transferability in Quantized Neural Networks

When a neural network is compressed to run on phones, attackers can craft visual patches that fool it—TriQDef stops these patches from working across different compression levels by deliberately scrambling the network's internal feature patterns and gradient signals.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Resource-constrained devices like phones use quantized neural networks (networks compressed to use smaller numbers) to run fast and save power. But this compression actually makes them vulnerable to adversarial patches—small visual perturbations that look like physical stickers and transfer dangerously across different compression levels. TriQDef blocks this by intentionally scrambling two internal alignment pathways: the semantic similarity in intermediate features, and the directional alignment of gradients (the signals that guide learning).

Zero assumption version

Compressed networks are easy targets for visual attacks

Quantization (compressing a network to use fewer, smaller numbers) makes neural networks fast and power-efficient on phones and edge devices. But it also creates a vulnerability: adversarial patches—small, carefully crafted visual stickers that fool the network—remain dangerous and transfer across different compression levels.

A naive defense would be to train the network to resist patches at one compression level (e.g., 8-bit). But patches learned at one bit-width generalize alarmingly well to others. The attacker doesn't need to retune; one patch works on 8-bit, 4-bit, and even extreme 2-bit quantized versions. Standard adversarial training ignores this cross-bit transferability, leaving a hidden attack surface open.

The core problem. Adversarial patches transfer across quantization levels because the networks share semantic and gradient-level structure that the compression does not eliminate.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Identify the alignment pathways

Patches work because they exploit two alignments: similar features emerge at intermediate layers across bit-widths, and gradients point in aligned directions.

Apply Feature Disalignment Penalty

During training, penalize perceptual similarity (texture, edges) in intermediate features between pairs of quantized networks.

Apply Gradient Perceptual Dissonance Penalty

Penalize gradient direction alignment using Edge IoU and HOG (edge/texture descriptors) across bit-widths, forcing gradients to disagree.

Train with curriculum scheduling

Start training with high-precision models, gradually add lower bit-widths; stabilizes learning under extreme compression.

Evaluate on unseen patches and bit-widths

Test patches crafted at one bit-width on others; confirms the transferability barrier is broken.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: feature and gradient alignment

TriQDef changes the internal alignment structure that patches exploit. Specifically, it disrupts two things: (1) the perceptual similarity of intermediate features across quantized variants, and (2) the alignment of gradient directions (which way the network's learning signals point) across bit-widths.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: alignment strength

measured quantity  =  Feature and gradient alignment

Alignment is a hidden quantity that measures how much the networks at different bit-widths extract similar features and update in similar directions. High alignment means the networks are nearly twins; an attack on one generalizes to the other. Low alignment means they behave differently.

TriQDef estimates alignment by computing perceptual similarity metrics (edge maps and HOG texture descriptors) between intermediate features, and by measuring gradient direction correlation using structural similarity metrics. During training, these metrics are converted into penalty terms that push alignment lower.

Why it works

Why it works: breaking the transferability bridge

Adversarial patches transfer across bit-widths because quantized networks, despite different precision levels, still extract similar semantic features and receive similar gradient signals during training. The attacker optimizes the patch on an 8-bit network by computing gradients—which direction to shift pixels to maximize fooling—and those gradient directions happen to align with the 4-bit and 2-bit versions. The patch slots into all three networks the same way.

TriQDef disrupts this by penalizing perceptual similarity in intermediate features. When a patch image enters the 8-bit network, its features should look different from the features in the 4-bit network at the same layer, even on the same input. This is enforced by computing edge maps and texture (HOG) descriptors of the features and penalizing their similarity. Simultaneously, TriQDef penalizes gradient direction alignment: the gradients computed on the 8-bit network should point differently than those on the 4-bit network. This means the attacker cannot use gradients from one bit-width to optimize the patch for another—the gradient directions literally disagree on which pixels matter.

Bit-Width-Aware Curriculum Training (BACT) enables this by starting with high-precision networks (which are stable and learn good representations) and gradually adding lower bit-widths during training. This staged approach prevents training collapse under extreme quantization noise because high-precision models establish stable feature patterns first; once those foundations exist, lower bit-widths can adapt incrementally without the optimizer flailing when it encounters the harsh discretization of 2-bit and 4-bit. Using a shared backbone while training all precision levels together means the same table of weights is used by all bit-widths; the FDP and GPDP penalties apply to every pair, so the backbone learns to produce features and gradients that inherently disagree across quantization—the defense becomes structural, not an afterthought.

Math after the picture

The training objective: penalizing alignment

First in words:

loss equals clean loss plus lambda times (feature disalignment penalty plus gradient perceptual dissonance penalty); each penalty reduces when alignment is high, increases when alignment is low

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_clean + lambda * (FDP + GPDP)

Every symbol:

L_totalTotal training loss (what the optimizer minimizes)
L_cleanStandard classification loss on correctly-labeled images
lambdaWeight controlling how strongly to penalize misalignment (tunable)
FDPFeature Disalignment Penalty (perceptual similarity between features across bit-widths)
GPDPGradient Perceptual Dissonance Penalty (gradient direction disagreement)
A tiny worked example
Suppose L_clean = 0.5 (the network correctly classifies 95% of examples).
At layer 3, compute feature similarity between 8-bit and 4-bit networks: Edge IoU = 0.7 (fairly similar edges). This is high; FDP = 0.7 contributes to the penalty.
Compute gradient cosine similarity (how aligned the gradient directions are): 0.65 (moderately aligned). This is also high; GPDP = 0.65.
Set lambda = 0.1 (moderate emphasis on disrupting alignment).
L_total = 0.5 + 0.1 * (0.7 + 0.65) = 0.5 + 0.135 = 0.635.
Optimizer nudges weights to reduce edge similarity and gradient alignment, pushing FDP and GPDP toward zero, because that lowers L_total.

The two penalty terms directly measure and reduce the alignment pathways that patch attacks exploit.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too aggressive disalignment hurts accuracy. If lambda (the penalty weight) is set too high, the network becomes so scrambled that it forgets how to classify correctly, because the disruption penalties distract the optimizer from the primary job of learning correct feature patterns. Clean accuracy can drop to 70%. The reason: penalizing feature similarity and gradient alignment forces the network to extract dissimilar features across bit-widths and point in disagreeing directions, which is useful for defense but harmful for the shared backbone trying to maintain a single coherent decision rule.
Curriculum scheduling must be tuned. If the bit-widths are introduced in the wrong order or timing, training can collapse under quantization noise. Starting too low (e.g., 2-bit from epoch 1) causes unstable gradients and poor learned representations.
Perceptual metrics (Edge IoU, HOG) may not capture all alignment. Other alignment mechanisms (e.g., non-local feature correlations) might remain unaddressed by these specific metrics, leaving subtle transfer routes open.
Done right. With careful hyperparameter tuning (penalty weight between 0.01 and 0.05, curriculum epochs 1–50 at high precision then 51–100 adding lower bits), TriQDef achieves 40% or greater attack success rate reduction while maintaining 90% or greater clean accuracy.

What a runnable demo should prove

Train two quantized ResNet-50 models on CIFAR-10: one with standard adversarial training (PBAT), one with TriQDef. Craft adversarial patches on the PBAT 8-bit model by running a gradient-based optimization (e.g., PGD) to maximize the attack. Apply the same patches to: (1) the PBAT 8-bit model (baseline), (2) the PBAT 4-bit model (different bit-width, same defense), (3) the TriQDef 8-bit model, and (4) the TriQDef 4-bit model. Measure attack success rate on each.
