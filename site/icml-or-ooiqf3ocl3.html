Training LLM Agents to Empower Humans

Train assistive language models to maximize human empowerment—their ability to make decisions and effect changes—rather than just completing tasks alone.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When you ask an AI assistant to help you with a task, you usually want it to respect your control and let you make the important decisions, because actions taken without your input often ignore what matters most to you. But most assistive agents are trained to finish jobs themselves, not to give you more control over what happens. This paper proposes a different training goal: maximize the human's empowerment, which means their ability to shape outcomes in the world. The method uses only written examples of humans actually helping each other, requiring no special rewards or feedback.

Zero assumption version

Assistive agents that hijack human control

Today's assistive agents are trained in two common ways. The first is learning from expert human examples—watching how skilled people solve problems and copying that behavior. The second is reinforcement learning (learning through trial and error with a reward signal), where the agent gets points for finishing tasks fast. Both of these methods push the agent toward the same bad habit: taking over, solving everything itself, leaving the human watching from the sidelines.

A naive approach tries to fix this by adding constraints ('don't do too much') but constraints are fragile—they break down at the edge cases where the human actually needs to decide something important, and the agent has learned to make all decisions anyway.

The core problem. Assistive agents trained to complete tasks end up making decisions that should be left to humans, removing human control instead of expanding it.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with examples

Collect written interactions where humans help other humans, showing when to step in and when to stay back, because real examples encode the actual decisions good helpers make.

Define empowerment

Measure how many real actions the human can still take after the agent's move—more choices means more empowerment.

Retrain the model

Teach the language model to predict outputs that keep human options open, using only the written examples and no additional reward labels.

Learn self-supervised

The model learns the pattern: good help expands human control, bad help locks it down—all from just reading example text, because the structure of the examples themselves (one person preserves the other's options) teaches this pattern without explicit labels.

Test with humans

Show people the trained agent helping with real tasks, and ask which assistant they actually prefer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training target (what the model is trying to optimize for). Instead of predicting the fastest way to complete a task, or copying an expert's actions, the model now learns to predict responses that expand the human's options and agency in the world. The outputs—the words the agent says, the actions it recommends, the information it provides—are shaped to give humans more control, not less.

the object it moves  =  training target
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: empowerment

measured quantity  =  empowerment (human agency)

Empowerment is a concrete, measurable quantity: the number of actions the human can realistically take after the agent's move. If the agent says 'here are three paths forward, you decide,' empowerment is high—the human still has many options. If the agent says 'I did it for you, it's done,' empowerment is zero. This is not a feeling or a belief; it is the actual count of control the human retains.

The paper estimates empowerment from written examples: by studying how one human helps another, the model learns to recognize which kinds of help (explanations, options, stepping back) leave the other person in charge, and which kinds (taking over, deciding unilaterally) remove their agency. It then learns to output the first kind.

Why it works

Why empowerment training works

If you want an agent to help humans stay in control, you have to teach it to recognize and prefer actions that keep options open, because without that explicit pressure the model defaults to task completion as the easiest path to learning. Standard task-completion training pushes toward 'solve it for them.' Empowerment training flips the incentive: the agent gets rewarded (learns more strongly from the examples) when the human keeps agency.

Here is the causal chain. First, the model reads thousands of examples of humans genuinely assisting each other—one person explaining a problem so the other can decide, offering multiple paths instead of one, or stepping back to let the human lead. The model learns a pattern: 'these moves feel like good help because the other person stays in control.' Second, when the model is retrained on this pattern, it naturally learns to make similar moves—explanations over directives, multiple options over single decisions, questions over commands. Third, because the model was never explicitly told 'now you must maximize human control,' it learned this from the structure of good examples alone, which is why the method is self-supervised (it needs no external reward labels). Fourth, the outcome is an agent that, when faced with a choice, picks the move that expands what the human can do next.

Math after the picture

The empowerment objective

First in words:

The training loss rewards outputs that preserve or grow the human action space. The model learns by comparing proposed actions: actions that leave the human many choices have lower loss (are learned more strongly) than actions that lock in one path.

Then the compact form:

loss = - log P(output | input) * E[|actions_human_can_take_after_output|]

Every symbol:

P(output | input)the probability the model assigns to predicting this output given the human's request
|actions_human_can_take_after_output|the count of distinct, viable actions the human could choose next
E[...]the expected value over the human-written examples
-log P(...)the standard language model loss (lower when the model is more confident in the right answer)
A tiny worked example
Human asks: 'Should I take job A or job B?' Proposed agent response 1: 'You should take job A.' After this, human has 0 real choices left; empowerment = 0. The term E[|actions|] = 0, so this output gets high loss (bad), and the model learns to avoid it.
Proposed agent response 2: 'Job A offers X and Y, job B offers P and Q. Here is what to weigh.' After this, human can choose A or B; empowerment = 2. The term E[|actions|] = 2, so the loss is lower (good), and the model learns to prefer this kind of response.
Same human question, same model confidence in each output; different empowerment scores mean the model is steered toward the response that keeps human control alive.

The loss penalizes task-completion and rewards human agency—so the model learns to assist by preserving, not seizing, control.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too many options paralyze. If the agent always offers five paths forward when the human needs a clear recommendation, empowerment grows but helpfulness shrinks, because humans can only deliberate over so many choices before the cognitive load blocks action. The method works best when the task allows genuine human choice; if there is only one right answer, maximizing options is not useful, because no trade-off exists to deliberate over.
Requires good training examples. The method learns from human-written assistance. If the examples show humans being neglectful (saying nothing when they should help), or patronizing (explaining things to adults who know them), the model learns those patterns too, because the model has no external standard to judge against—it infers 'good help' from the statistics of what it reads. The quality of examples sets the ceiling.
Measuring empowerment is hard. The model has to infer how many actions the human can take next, based only on text. In complex real-world tasks, this inference can be wrong—the model might miss an option the human actually has, or think an option exists when it does not.
Done right: grounded in examples. When the training examples are diverse and honest, and the task allows genuine human choice, the model learns to assist without hijacking—expanding human agency instead of replacing it, because the examples show diverse, honest assistance and the learning target (preserve human options) aligns with that pattern.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple assistant for a bounded task like scheduling or recommendation (e.g., 'help me pick a date for a meeting' or 'suggest places to eat'), because these tasks have genuinely multiple valid answers and the human has real stakes in the choice. Create two versions: one trained on standard task-completion examples (just pick a date or a place), another trained on empowerment examples (list constraints, show options, ask the human to decide). Ask users to interact with both assistants on three tasks of increasing complexity. For each task, measure: (1) How many distinct viable choices did the human feel they had after the agent's response? (2) Did the human feel understood? (3) Did the task get completed? The empowerment version should score higher on choice and understanding with comparable task completion.

empowerment_score = (average number of choices human felt they had) / (total task steps)

The demo must make it impossible to miss this: when trained to expand human control, the agent stops trying to solve problems alone and starts helping humans solve them.
