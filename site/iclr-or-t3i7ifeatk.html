Align-Then-stEer: Adapting VLA Models via Unified Latent Guidance

To adapt a pre-trained robot-vision model to a new robot, encode both robots' motion into a shared hidden space, then steer training to stay inside it.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Large pre-trained models can control many robots and tasks, but when you need to adapt one to a new robot with different mechanics, the motion patterns mismatch and training becomes expensive. This paper proposes a two-step process: first, build a shared hidden representation that captures both the original robot's motions and the new robot's motions in one place. Second, during training, gently nudge the model to produce motions that belong inside that shared space. The result is a data-efficient way to teach an old model a new robot without starting from scratch.

The mismatch problem

Today's best robot models are trained on video and motion data from many different robots and many different tasks. These models learn a general sense of how to move—how to pick up a cup, push an object, follow a hand. When you want to use one of these models on a new robot that was not in the training data, the problem emerges: the new robot moves differently. Its arms are shorter, its fingers work another way, its speed is different. The motions that worked on the training robots do not work on this new one.

A naive approach is to take the pre-trained model and retrain it on motion data from the new robot. But this often fails badly. The model forgets how to recognize objects or read scenes (because the new training data is limited). Or it struggles because the motion commands it learned to produce do not map cleanly onto the new robot. Getting this to work costs a lot of data and a lot of training time.

The core idea: Map both old and new robot motions into one shared hidden space, then train the model to stay inside that space while learning the new robot.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Build a baseline encoder from old data

Train one autoencoder (a pair of encoder and decoder) on all the pre-trained robot's motions, storing it as a reference.

Step 2: Build an encoder for new data

Train a second, smaller autoencoder on the new robot's motion data, but constrain it to match the shape of the baseline encoder.

Step 3: Define the shared space

The hidden layer of both autoencoders now lives in the same math space; motions from both robots get compressed there.

Step 4: Add guidance to training

When fine-tuning the big model on new-robot data, add a loss term that penalizes outputs that drift far from the shared space.

Step 5: Train efficiently

The model learns the new robot's motion patterns while staying anchored to the shared hidden blueprint.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes where motion commands live during training. Instead of letting the model produce any motion it wants, it constrains the model's motion outputs to lie inside a structured hidden space that was built from both the original and new robot's data. This is not a change to the model's internal weights; it is a change to what outputs are considered valid.

the object it moves  =  motion output space
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: the latent mode

measured quantity  =  mode occupancy in latent space

A hidden quantity here is the mode—a cluster or region in the hidden (compressed) space where similar motions naturally group. The pre-trained model's motion data clusters in certain modes (ways of moving that appear often in the training videos). The new robot's motions form different modes. By using autoencoders—compact encoders that squash motions down to a small set of numbers—the paper finds which modes belong to each robot and which modes overlap.

The paper measures this by learning two autoencoders: one captures the modes of the pre-training data, the other captures the modes of the new-robot data. The constraint that the second encoder must match the first (via a mathematical penalty called reverse KL divergence) forces the new-robot modes to align with the pre-training modes, creating a single unified set of modes that both robots use.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

When a model is pre-trained on many robots, it learns to produce motions that work across different embodiments. But when you adapt it to a radically new robot with different arm lengths or hand shapes, the model's learned motion modes no longer match reality. Training from scratch forces the model to forget the general motion patterns and learn only the new robot's patterns, which wastes the pre-trained knowledge.

This paper prevents that waste by explicitly bridging the two worlds. First, it maps both the old robot and new robot motions into the same hidden space (using two autoencoders), so they share common motion clusters. Second, during fine-tuning, it adds a penalty whenever the model tries to output a motion that does not live in one of these shared clusters. This penalty does two things at once: it prevents catastrophic forgetting (because the model is constrained to stay in a space it already understands), and it ensures the motion commands actually make sense for the new robot (because that new robot's motions were encoded into the shared space). The result is a model that adapts quickly, without wasting pre-training knowledge, and without requiring massive amounts of new-robot data.

Math after the picture

The equation and worked example

First in words:

The penalty for the adaptation autoencoder is the reconstruction loss (how well it rebuilds the motion) minus the KL divergence (a measure of how different the learned clusters are from the pre-training clusters), scaled by a weighting factor.

Then the compact form:

L = E[log p_φ(a|z)] - (1-α)·E[D_KL(q_φ(z|a) || p(z))] - (α-λ^-1)·D_KL(q_φ(z) || p(z))

Every symbol:

aa motion or action sequence
zthe hidden compressed representation of the motion
q_φ(z|a)the learned encoder: maps a motion to its hidden form
p_φ(a|z)the learned decoder: reconstructs motion from hidden form
p(z)the standard reference distribution (used to measure alignment)
D_KL(X || Y)divergence measure: how different X is from Y (zero if identical)
α and λtuning knobs: control how strongly to enforce alignment
A tiny worked example
Suppose the pre-trained robot has 100 motion clusters, and the new robot has 50 motions in adaptation data.
Pre-training VAE learns to encode those 100 motions into 8 hidden numbers (the latent space).
Adaptation VAE encodes the 50 new motions also into 8 hidden numbers, but using its own encoder.
Without constraint: the adaptation encoder might map new motions onto completely different positions. D_KL(adaptation || pre-training) = large penalty.
With reverse KL constraint: training pushes the adaptation encoder to cluster new motions onto the same positions as pre-training ones. D_KL drops from ~10 to ~0.5.
As training reduces the divergence, the guidance mechanism in the fine-tuning loop can now reliably steer outputs: 'if motion z is in the shared space, output it; if not, apply a small correction'.

The math encodes the idea that two robots' motions should live in the same hidden clusters; training enforces this by minimizing the divergence between their learned cluster distributions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Misaligned encoders. If the two autoencoders are trained on very different subsets of data, they may learn completely different internal representations. The shared space exists mathematically but does not actually align old and new robot motions, rendering the guidance useless.
Weak guidance. If the penalty weight (call them alpha and lambda) is set too low, the guidance has no force, and the model drifts out of the shared space anyway. If the weights are too high, the model over-corrects and forgets how to adapt to task-specific details.
Limited overlap in motion repertoires. If the new robot can perform fundamentally different motions than any in the pre-training data (e.g., the new robot has a gripper but all pre-training robots have suction cups), there is no shared mode to anchor to. The guidance then constrains the model to motions it cannot execute.
Done right. When autoencoders are trained on sufficiently diverse data from both embodiments, and the guidance weights are tuned to allow controlled drift, the method preserves the knowledge learned during pre-training while enabling efficient new-robot learning, because the shared space forces the model to produce motions that lie inside both robots' repertoires, preventing the catastrophic forgetting that occurs when fine-tuning on only new data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple experiment: take a pre-trained model (e.g., trained on simulated robot A moving objects), introduce a new robot B with different kinematics (e.g., shorter arm), and compare two training regimes. First, fine-tune directly on robot B data (50 robot-hours of motion). Second, use the Align-Then-stEer method: train two autoencoders on robot A and robot B data (10 minutes), then fine-tune with the shared latent guidance (50 robot-hours). Measure success rate on both robot A tasks (to detect forgetting) and robot B tasks (to measure adaptation). The demo makes it impossible to miss the forgetting prevention because direct fine-tuning forces the model to unlearn robot A's motion patterns in order to fit robot B's different mechanics, whereas the shared latent space prevents this by anchoring the model's outputs to a space that encodes both robots' patterns: direct fine-tuning should drop robot A performance by 40–50%, while Align-Then-stEer should hold it within 10%.

preservation = (success_rate_robotA_after) - (success_rate_robotA_before) [should be close to zero]; adaptation = success_rate_robotB_after [should be high]

The shared modes of hidden motion clusters enable the model to preserve old knowledge while learning new robot mechanics with minimal new data, because by constraining outputs to stay inside the cluster space that encodes both old and new robot motions, the model cannot diverge from what it learned during pre-training while still adapting to the new robot's specific motion patterns.
