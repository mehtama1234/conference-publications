FA-OPD: Adversarial Dual On-Policy Distillation

A robot learning method that combines two teachers—one measuring whether actions are expert-like and one giving nearby target actions—to make lightweight robot brains both explore widely and stay close to expert behavior.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a robot learns from recorded expert demonstrations, standard supervised learning only teaches it to copy the exact actions in the recordings. But in the real world, the robot will visit new situations the expert never showed it, and copying blindly fails. This paper combines two signals on the robot's actual experience: one rewards it for acting like an expert, and one teaches it nearby actions to imitate at places it actually goes. Together they let the robot generalize beyond the demonstrations.

Zero assumption version

Standard imitation learning gets stuck in new situations

A robot learns from a video of a human picking objects from a shelf—the expert shows perfect actions in each state. Standard imitation (behavioral cloning) trains the robot to copy exactly: given state, predict the action. This works when the robot stays near the recorded states, but it has a fatal flaw.

The moment the robot's own mistakes push it into a state the expert never visited, imitation learning has no guidance. Worse, if many demonstrations exist, the robot might see the same state reached in multiple ways—some of which the expert took, others the robot accidentally created. The method treats them equally and averages the actions, which can produce poor compromises. On-policy methods solve this by letting the robot practice and learn from its own experience, but they usually need a strong fixed teacher that gives rewards or perfect actions. In demonstration-only settings, no such teacher exists.

In one sentence. The method co-trains a learned teacher (based on expert data) with a student robot, so the student both explores beyond demonstrations and stays anchored to expert-like behavior.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: a lightweight student robot co-trained with a learned teacher

Standard on-policy distillation (OPD) requires a fixed, strong teacher (often a hand-coded reward function or a separately trained policy). This paper's key move is to learn the teacher from expert demonstrations (using flow matching) and co-train it alongside the student. The student is a lightweight neural network (MLP); the teacher is a more expressive flow-matching model trained on the same expert data. By co-training them, the student gets two complementary corrective signals during exploration—not just copies of expert actions, but also learned judgments of expert-likeness.

the object it moves  =  lightweight student policy co-trained with learned teacher
The hidden quantity

The expert-likeness score: a learned quantity that judges new actions

measured quantity  =  Expert-likeness reward: how closely an action matches expert behavior

The core hidden quantity is a score that measures whether a new action (one the student generates at an unseen state) looks like something an expert would have done. This is not a hand-coded reward. Instead, the teacher learns it from the expert data: given a state-action pair, does it fall within the pattern of behaviors (the collection of state-action pairs) the expert actually performed, or is it an outlier?

The paper learns this score via the reward channel of the teacher. By observing all state-action pairs in the expert demonstrations, the teacher builds an implicit model of what expert-like looks like. On student rollouts (new states and actions), the teacher applies this learned judgment. High scores go to actions that resemble the expert; low scores to random or adversarial moves.

Why it works

Why two teachers work better than one: exploration + exploitation without contradiction

Standard imitation learning exploits (copies) but does not explore. Standard reinforcement learning explores (tries new moves) but needs a fixed reward function. This paper's insight is that a learned teacher can provide both, via two separate channels, and they don't fight.

The reward channel drives exploration: it tells the student how far its current attempt is from expert-like, and the student's training algorithm uses this score to shift the weights (the numbers inside the student's brain) in directions that lead to higher rewards. This lets the student find new paths the expert never showed—generalizing beyond point-wise demonstrations.

The action channel drives exploitation: at each state the student visits, the teacher suggests actions the expert would take nearby (dense local targets). This grounds the exploration—it keeps the student anchored to expert-like behavior so it does not spiral into adversarial or bizarre territory. The two channels are coupled in their effects: rewards enable generalization because they score new behaviors on learned expert-likeness instead of copying, and actions prevent drift because they anchor every learned move to expert suggestions at places the student actually visits.

Why does this work? Because they operate on different timescales and prevent conflict. Rewards shape long-horizon policy optimization (which path reaches the goal well?). Actions provide short-horizon dense targets (which move is good right here?). Together, the student explores widely within expert-like territories because rewards encourage trying new moves that score high on expert-likeness, while actions keep those moves locally grounded in expert behaviors—exploration stays anchored and doesn't spiral into adversarial territory.

Math after the picture

The dual distillation loss: coupling reward and action targets

First in words:

The student is trained to minimize a loss that combines two terms: (1) a penalty for low expert-likeness reward on its rollouts, and (2) a penalty for actions that deviate from the teacher's suggested actions at the same states. Both are weighted equally, so neither dominates.

Then the compact form:

Loss_student = - E[R_teacher(s, a)] + lambda * E[ ||a_student - a_teacher(s)||^2 ] where: R_teacher(s, a) = learned reward score (expert-likeness) a_teacher(s) = teacher-suggested action at state s a_student = student's action lambda = coupling strength (weight the two terms equally)

Every symbol:

R_teacher(s, a)Learned reward: how expert-like is this state-action pair (higher is better)
a_teacher(s)The action the teacher suggests at state s
a_studentThe action the student took
||a_student - a_teacher(s)||^2Squared difference: how far did student deviate from teacher suggestion
E[...]Average over all state-action pairs in student rollouts
lambdaWeight balancing the two channels (coupling strength)
Tiny worked example: one state-action pair
State: robot at shelf. Reward signal: R_teacher = 0.8 (very expert-like). Action signal: teacher suggests action 0.5, student does 0.7, error = 0.2.
Reward loss: -0.8 (we maximize expert-likeness, so negate: higher reward lowers loss).
Action loss: 1.0 * (0.7-0.5)^2 = 1.0 * 0.04 = 0.04 (deviation is small).
Total: -0.8 + 0.04 = -0.76. This pair is good (high reward, small deviation). If the student had picked action 1.2 (big error), loss would be -0.8 + 1.0 * 0.49 = -0.31, worse. The student learns: follow the teacher's action guidance to keep the loss low.

The first term (negative reward) pulls exploration toward expert-like regions; the second term (action penalty) pulls exploitation toward teacher suggestions. Both together shape a student that explores widely but stays expert-like.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Teacher overfits to expert data, giving poor reward signals on new states. If the teacher flow-matching model has not seen diverse enough demonstrations, it may assign high expert-likeness scores to off-distribution actions or penalize unusual (but good) actions as out-of-distribution. This can trap the student in a local optimum. Fix: ensure expert data covers key variations, or use ensemble teachers.
The two channels conflict: reward pushes exploration but action channel pulls back too hard. If the coupling weight (lambda) is set wrong, the action channel can dominate and force the student to imitate teacher actions exactly, negating the benefit of the reward-driven exploration. Conversely, if lambda is too small, the student ignores action guidance and drifts. Fix: tune lambda carefully or use adaptive weighting.
Limited or biased demonstrations mislead both teacher and student. If the expert data is small or skewed (e.g., only left-turn examples), the teacher's learned expert-likeness score is unreliable, and the student learns biased behavior. The reward channel then optimizes toward a distorted expert model.
Done right. With sufficient diverse expert data, careful tuning of the coupling weight, and regular validation on test environments, FA-OPD learns to explore safely, generalizing well beyond the demonstrations while staying expert-like.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robot navigation task: a 2D grid where an expert policy (e.g., A* from a known map) generates demonstrations of reaching the goal. Corrupt some expert states with noise to simulate imperfect recordings. Train a student MLP using behavioral cloning alone, and train a second student using FA-OPD (with a learned flow-matching teacher and dual distillation). Both start from the same noisy expert data. Then test both students on 10 random start positions in the same grid, and measure success rate and average path length.

Success rate = (# times student reached goal) / 10. Path efficiency = expert path length / student path length.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: FA-OPD students reach the goal on new starting positions that the expert never used (where behavioral cloning fails), and their paths are nearly as efficient as the expert's (not just random wandering). This proves the dual channels work: reward enables exploration by scoring new behaviors against learned expert-likeness (not copying), and action keeps it expert-like by anchoring every learned move to teacher suggestions at visited states.
