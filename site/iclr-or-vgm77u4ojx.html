SigmaDock: Molecular Docking with Fragment-Based Diffusion

Breaking a drug molecule into rigid pieces and reassembling them in a protein pocket using a step-by-step diffusion process beats hand-written rules.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When a drug molecule (ligand) binds to a protein, it must fit into the protein's binding pocket in exactly the right shape and position. Predicting this binding pose is called molecular docking, and it is critical for drug discovery. Classical methods rely on hand-coded physics rules and are slow but reliable. Recent neural networks promise speed and diversity but produce impossible geometries and fail on new proteins. SigmaDock breaks the ligand into rigid-body fragments (like puzzle pieces), then uses a generative process to learn how to reassemble them in the correct pose.

Why deep learning for docking fails

A naive deep learning approach takes a protein and a ligand as inputs and tries to predict the final 3D pose (position and rotation) directly. The problem: molecules have rigid structure. The atoms within the ligand cannot move freely; they are held together by chemical bonds at fixed distances and angles. A neural network trained end-to-end does not know this, so it generates poses where bonds stretch, angles distort, and atoms collide. The result is chemically impossible.

Worse, these networks are trained on thousands of protein-ligand complexes in the lab, but real drug discovery needs to dock ligands into completely new proteins. The network overfits to protein families it has seen before and fails on unfamiliar targets. Building a single end-to-end model that handles both geometric plausibility and generalization is hard because the network must learn chemistry implicitly, without being told what chemistry is.

The core problem in one sentence. Neural networks generate chemically impossible geometries and fail to generalize because they do not respect molecular structure.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of predicting the full 3D pose all at once, SigmaDock learns to refine fragment positions and rotations one small step at a time. Each step moves the fragments closer to plausible configurations that respect the chemical constraints of the molecule and fit inside the protein pocket.

The hidden quantity

The geometric feasibility signal

measured quantity  =  Geometric constraint satisfaction

Every atom in a molecule is held at a precise distance and angle from its neighbors by chemical bonds. The paper names this implicitly: the fragments must stay rigid, and must satisfy soft geometric constraints (the bond lengths and angles do not stretch beyond their natural ranges). This is the hidden quantity—the degree to which a proposed fragment arrangement respects the molecule's structure.

SigmaDock measures this using soft potential-energy penalties. When a fragment's rotation strays from a valid orientation, or when fragments are positioned too close to break chemical bonds, a penalty accumulates. During training (adjusting the network), the network learns to predict the score (which direction to move fragments to reduce penalties). During inference (when the finished model answers a new question), the refinement steps follow these direction predictions toward chemically valid configurations.

Why it works

Why fragment-based diffusion works

Classical physics-based docking uses hand-written force fields (energy models) to score poses. These are accurate but slow. End-to-end neural networks are fast but ignore chemistry, producing impossible outputs. SigmaDock splits the difference by baking chemistry into the problem structure, not the network weights. By decomposing the ligand into rigid fragments, the network never has to learn 'how to make valid molecules'—that constraint is built in. Fragments cannot bend, so bad configurations are already ruled out. Soft constraints (weak penalties) on bond angles and distances guide the network toward plausible configurations without hard constraints that break learning.

The diffusion process (iterative refinement) is the key. In a single-step network, the model must predict all fragment positions at once, a hard combinatorial problem. In diffusion, the network predicts only the direction to nudge fragments given their current noisy positions. This is easier: the network learns a local correction, not a global guess. Over many steps, local nudges accumulate into a coherent final pose. Generalization works because the network learns the shape of the constraint space (where penalties are low and poses are valid), not memorized protein shapes. A new protein has a different binding pocket structure, but the same forces push fragments to fit and the same bonds to preserve.

Math after the picture

The step-by-step refinement equation

First in words:

penalty = sum over bonds of (force-constant times angle-deviation-squared plus distance-deviation-squared)

Then the compact form:

L(z_t) = sum_bonds k_AB * (angle_error)^2 + k_ABC * (distance_error)^2 + lambda_t * ||score_predicted - score_true||^2

Every symbol:

z_tfragment positions and rotations at noise level t
k_AB, k_ABCpenalty strength for bonds and angles
angle_error, distance_errorhow far from valid geometry
score_truethe true direction to move (gradient of the data distribution)
score_predictedwhat the network learns to predict
lambda_tloss scaling (stronger penalty at high noise)
A tiny worked example
Suppose Fragment A has a bond to Fragment B at ideal angle 120 degrees.
Noisy fragment positions give angle 110 degrees (deviation 10 degrees).
Force constant k_ABC = 50 (strong penalty).
Penalty on this bond: 50 * 10^2 = 5000 (large, because deviation is large).
Network predicts: rotate fragment A by +3 degrees.
After update, angle becomes 113 degrees (deviation 7 degrees).
New penalty: 50 * 7^2 = 2450 (lower, refinement worked).
Over 50 steps, penalties shrink monotonically toward valid configurations.

The force-constant penalty prevents bad configurations; diffusion steps gradually align fragment positions to satisfy these constraints.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fragments too large. If the initial fragmentation breaks the molecule into pieces that are too flexible (not rigid), the constraint signal is weak and the network must learn to suppress illegal motions, defeating the point of fragmentation.
Pocket size mismatch. If the protein binding pocket is much larger than the training distribution (e.g. a ligand docking into an oversized cavity), the model has not learned the constraint space in that regime and may place fragments incorrectly even if chemistry is satisfied.
Loss scaling instability. Diffusion processes are sensitive to loss weighting across noise levels. Poor scaling makes the network ignore high-noise steps or over-focus on tiny refinements, breaking convergence.
Done right. With careful fragmentation (preserving rigidity), soft constraints (not hard walls), and adaptive loss scaling, SigmaDock generalizes: new proteins are handled correctly as long as the binding pocket is within the learned distribution.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo takes a protein structure (PDB file) and a drug molecule (SMILES string), splits the ligand into fragments using the paper's fragmentation algorithm, samples 10 random initial poses from a noise distribution, runs 50 diffusion steps (network predicts fragment corrections at each step), and measures the final pose quality using two metrics: (a) RMSD (how far the predicted pose sits from the crystal structure), and (b) chemical plausibility (are bond angles within 10 degrees of valid, are atoms farther than van-der-Waals radius). The demo should show that all 10 samples converge to valid geometries near the ground truth, even though they started from random noise.

Top-1 accuracy = fraction of trials where best-of-10 poses achieve RMSD < 2A and all bonds within 10 degrees of valid geometry

The demo makes impossible to miss: fragments lock into place respecting chemistry, and even random starts converge to the same plausible pose, proving the method is learning the constraint space, not memorizing protein shapes.
