Whom to Query for What: Adaptive Group Elicitation via Multi-Turn LLM Interactions

Use limited question rounds on the most informative people and questions so group-level properties can be inferred with less survey budget.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

This paper studies how to ask a group only a small number of questions while still recovering a population-level property such as political leaning or opinion distribution. The challenge is twofold: each round must choose both what to ask and whom to ask, and some responses may be missing. The paper proposes a multi-turn selection rule that chooses question-person subsets to reduce uncertainty as quickly as possible under a fixed budget.

Zero assumption version

Why fixed surveys waste limited question budget

A standard survey often asks the same questions to the same style of respondent whether or not those answers are still informative. That wastes budget because later rounds should depend on what is already known.

The problem becomes harder when some responses are missing. Then the system must choose questions and respondents while also filling gaps in partial group information. The paper treats this as an adaptive information-gathering problem where each round should reduce uncertainty about a group-level target as much as possible.

The paper's move in one sentence. At each round, choose the question and respondent subset with the highest expected information gain, while using a group model to combine and complete the partial answers already observed.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with partial group data

Collect the responses already observed so the next choice can depend on what is known and what is missing.

Model the group structure

Use a group relation model to combine observed answers and fill gaps because missing responses should still influence later selections.

Score candidate question-person pairs

Estimate expected information gain for each possible next query so the budget goes to the most informative option.

Ask the chosen subset

Send the selected question only to the selected respondents because broad asking is often unnecessary.

Update the target estimate

Fold the new answers back into the group model so the next round starts from a smaller uncertainty region.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the survey policy. Instead of a fixed questionnaire over a fixed respondent set, it uses a sequential policy that chooses both the next question and the next respondent subset.

the object it moves  =  the query policy for group-level information gathering
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  how much one additional question to one subset will reduce uncertainty about the group target

The central hidden quantity is information value under a budget. A question may be useful for one respondent group and nearly useless for another, depending on what has already been learned and which responses are still missing.

The paper estimates this quantity with an information-gain objective plus a group model for partial data. That pairing is important because uncertainty should be measured at the group level, not only at the individual answer level.

Why it works

Why adaptive question and respondent selection helps

Adaptive selection helps because later rounds should exploit earlier answers. Once one region of the group is predictable, spending more questions there adds little value, so the policy should shift budget toward the people and prompts that still change the group estimate.

The group model matters because missing responses are structured rather than random noise. By combining related respondents, the method can estimate what is already partly known and then ask only where the missing uncertainty is still large.

Math after the picture

Choose the next round by uncertainty reduction

First in words:

At each round, prefer the question and respondent subset that most reduces uncertainty about the target group property per unit of budget spent.

Then the compact form:

pick_next = argmax over (question, subset) of information_gain(question, subset) / cost(question, subset)

Every symbol:

pick_nextthe next question and respondent subset selected for one round
information_gain(question, subset)expected reduction in uncertainty about the target group quantity
cost(question, subset)budget consumed by asking that question to that subset
A tiny worked example
Question A to subset 1 reduces expected uncertainty by 12 units and costs 6 response slots.
Question B to subset 2 reduces expected uncertainty by 10 units and costs 3 response slots.
A gives gain per cost 2.0, while B gives gain per cost about 3.3.
The policy chooses B because B shrinks uncertainty more for each unit of budget.

The real method uses a richer estimator, but this ratio captures the main idea that adaptive elicitation should spend budget where uncertainty falls fastest.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The uncertainty estimate can be wrong. If the group model misreads which region is uncertain, the policy may spend budget on the wrong people because the expected gain calculation started from a bad belief state.
Missingness may be biased. If nonresponse follows a hidden social pattern, simple completion rules can mislead the estimator because the missing answers are not just random blanks.
Questions can change later behavior. An early question may influence how a person answers later prompts, so the sequential policy may alter the data-generating process because interaction itself has effects.
Done right. The paper is useful because it treats elicitation as a budgeted decision problem instead of a fixed form.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small opinion dataset with a known group target and artificial budget limits. Compare a fixed survey policy against an adaptive one that chooses both the next question and the next respondent subset after each round, while some answers are hidden to mimic missing responses.

The demo should show lower target-estimation error under the same budget because the adaptive policy focuses queries where uncertainty is still large and stops spending questions on predictable parts of the group.
