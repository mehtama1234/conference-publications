SimBench: Can LLMs Really Think Like People?

A large-scale benchmark reveals that instruction-tuning helps LLMs agree with consensus but makes them worse at capturing human disagreement.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Large language models are being used to simulate how humans think and decide. But do they actually behave like humans, or just like a smoothed-out majority? SimBench is a unified test covering 20 real human behavior datasets, showing that the best LLMs today capture human choices only 40 percent of the time, and revealing a surprising trap: when you train an LLM to be aligned (safe and helpful), you often make it worse at simulating the full range of actual human opinions.

Zero assumption version

LLMs as human-behavior simulators are untested and fragmented

Researchers want to use large language models to predict how humans will decide, vote, consume, or answer moral questions. It sounds plausible: if you ask an LLM a question a million humans answered, can it guess the pattern of answers (what fraction said yes, what fraction said no, etc.)? But right now, every researcher uses a different test, different metrics, different datasets. One lab tests moral reasoning on 100 people, another tests economic choices on 50. There is no common ruler to say when or why an LLM really succeeds at simulating humans.

A beginner might try: just ask the LLM the question, count what it says, and compare to what humans said. That almost works. But it fails in two critical ways. First, models trained to be aligned (safe and helpful) are steered toward what most people believe, not toward the full range of human opinion. Second, a model's training data already contains millions of human opinions, so it is hard to know whether it is simulating humans or just repeating its training set.

The real problem. There is no standardized way to measure when LLMs faithfully capture how humans actually think, including the many cases where humans disagree.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Unify 20 datasets

Collect real human responses to moral, economic, and behavioral questions from thousands of people across multiple countries.

Elicit model distributions

For each question, ask the model to assign a probability to each answer (yes / no / agree / disagree / etc.), matching what a human does.

Measure agreement

Compute a score: how close is the model's guess of the human response pattern to the actual human response pattern? Lower is worse.

Measure alignment tradeoff

Separate questions by human-answer diversity: consensus (95% agree on one answer) vs. pluralistic (people split 50-50). Score each.

Compare base vs. aligned

For the same model, measure performance before and after instruction-tuning. Tuning helps on consensus questions (humans agree) but hurts on disagreement questions (humans split), because alignment narrows the model's outputs toward a single answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

SimBench measures something most existing benchmarks ignore: the model's internal confidence distribution over answer choices. Instead of just asking did the model pick the right answer (yes/no), it asks: does the model believe answers in the same proportions humans do? For consensus questions (95% of humans say yes), the model should output 95%. For contested questions (50% yes, 50% no), the model should output 50-50%. A normal test gets both right only by accident. SimBench forces the model to show its actual belief distribution and grades it on fidelity.

the object it moves  =  how a model distributes confidence across multiple answer choices
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how alignment erases disagreement

measured quantity  =  disagreement score (how much the aligned model's answer pattern differs from human disagreement)

When humans answer a question, some questions show high agreement (everyone says yes), and some show high disagreement (half say yes, half say no). Aligned models systematically narrow their answer choices: they converge toward the majority opinion, flattening disagreement. This paper measures that shift and discovers it is the core trade-off: alignment kills simulation of human disagreement.

The paper computes this by measuring how much humans split on each question, then measuring how much an aligned model narrows that split compared to a base model. On questions where humans disagree sharply, the aligned model's answers narrow sharply downward toward a fake majority. That is the alignment-simulation tradeoff.

Plain reading. When humans are divided on a question (50% say yes, 50% say no), alignment training steers the model toward a fake majority (70% yes, 30% no), so it misses the real human split.
Paper reading. The pre-training objective of a base model tries to match the full spread of human opinions: if humans split 50-50, the model learns to output 50-50. Post-training (instruction tuning) retrains the model to pick one answer as best, narrowing its outputs toward a single majority view. This works when humans actually agree (the consensus really is the right answer), but fails when humans disagree (the model's narrowed answer misses the real split).
Why it works

Why the alignment-simulation tradeoff exists

A base language model is trained on billions of sentences from the internet. Its training goal is simple: predict the next word. This goal naturally leads the model to learn the full range of human opinion in its training data. When asked a controversial question, the base model outputs probabilities that reflect the real human split. If humans are 50-50, the model learns to output 50-50.

But then, for safety and helpfulness, researchers apply instruction-tuning: they show the model examples of aligned (safe, helpful, consensus) answers and steer it toward those. This changes the model's internal goal. Now the model is trained to converge toward whatever the trainers marked as the right answer. On easy consensus questions (nearly everyone agrees), this is great: the model aligns with human consensus and gets better, because the consensus answer really is what most humans think. But on contested questions (people genuinely disagree), this training objective works against simulation. The aligned model has been steered toward a false majority, so when asked a controversial moral question, it gives a single confident answer instead of capturing the actual human disagreement. The base model, not yet pushed toward consensus, still reflects the real distribution.

Math after the picture

Measuring simulation ability

First in words:

For each question i, compute the squared difference between the model's predicted answer probability and the human answer probability, then normalize against the baseline.

Then the compact form:

S_i = Σ_k [human_prob(answer_k) - model_prob(answer_k)]^2, normalized by dataset mean baseline

Every symbol:

S_isimulation score for question i (lower is worse; perfect match is 0)
human_prob(answer_k)fraction of humans who gave answer k (e.g. 0.50 for half the sample)
model_prob(answer_k)model's predicted probability for answer k
dataset mean baselineaverage score for all questions to ensure numerical stability
A worked example: one question
Question: Do you support policy X? Humans: 60% yes, 40% no.
Base model predicts: 58% yes, 42% no. Squared error = (0.60-0.58)^2 + (0.40-0.42)^2 = 0.0004 + 0.0004 = 0.0008.
Aligned model predicts: 75% yes, 25% no. Squared error = (0.60-0.75)^2 + (0.40-0.25)^2 = 0.0225 + 0.0225 = 0.0450.
Aligned model's error is 56x higher on this one question. Across all diverse questions, aligned models accumulate much higher error than base models.

The smaller the squared difference between what the model predicts and what humans actually said, the better the simulator, because the model is placing its confidence mass (its probabilities) in the same places humans do.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Training data overlap. The model may have seen some of these datasets during training. However, SimBench uses zero-shot aggregation (asking for a distribution, not reciting specific examples), which largely mitigates this risk.
Elicitation bias. Asking an aligned model directly for a distribution is different from asking a base model. Aligned models may verbalize their answer differently than base models show in their internal probabilities. The paper tests that the method works consistently, but measurement bias persists.
Letting models think longer does not rescue the tradeoff. The paper tested whether letting models take more computational steps (like chain-of-thought reasoning) could overcome the alignment-simulation gap. It did not. The gap is fundamental, not a reasoning depth issue, because the problem is the aligned model's training objective itself, not its reasoning ability.
Marginalized groups are undersampled. The benchmark covers diverse questions, but the participant pool is still skewed toward Anglophone Western respondents. Models fail harder on questions involving traits that conflict with alignment objectives, which often means they fail on the beliefs of minority groups.
Done right: use base models for social simulation. If you want an LLM to capture human disagreement, do not use an aligned model. Use the base model before post-training, even if it is less safe. Or re-align after simulation, at the cost of a small degradation, because the base model's spread-out answers are closer to human reality.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test with two questions: one where humans strongly agree (e.g., 95% of humans think jumping off a cliff is bad), and one where humans are split (e.g., 50% prefer coffee, 50% prefer tea). Collect predictions from a base LLM and an aligned LLM (e.g., Llama-2-base vs. Llama-2-chat). For each model and question, sample 100 times and compute the distribution. Then compute how far each model's distribution is from the true human distribution. You should see: on the consensus question, aligned wins; on the split question, base wins by a large margin.

divergence = Σ |model_prob(k) - human_prob(k)| for all answer options k

The single fact a demo must make impossible to miss: alignment training narrows a model's output distribution (pushing it toward one answer), which helps on consensus questions (because the consensus really is what most humans think) but actively harms on disagreement questions (because humans truly are split, and the aligned model gives a false majority).
