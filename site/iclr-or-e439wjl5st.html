Mixed-Curvature Tree-Sliced Wasserstein Distance

Measure distance between data distributions that live on curved spaces by slicing them along tree-shaped paths instead of straight lines.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

When your data lives on a curved surface—like points on a sphere or a saddle—straight-line distance metrics are wrong. This paper extends a way of comparing two groups of data (called Wasserstein distance) to work on curved spaces using trees (branching hierarchies of paths) instead of lines. Trees are faster because one large optimization problem on a high-dimensional curved space becomes k much smaller one-dimensional problems; they are honest to shape because multiple branches together capture how the space bends in all directions.

Zero assumption version

Curved data needs curved distance metrics

Most machine learning measures distance between things by drawing a straight line. But real data often lives on curved surfaces: faces cluster on a curved surface (a manifold), protein shapes sit on hyperbolic space (a space where distant things become densely packed), and graphs naturally embed on surfaces where curvature varies by region. Straight-line distance gets these wrong.

When you want to compare two groups of data—say, samples from two neural network layers—you need a way to measure how different their distributions are. The standard way is Wasserstein distance (moving-sand-pile metric): imagine one group is piled sand, the other is empty, and you measure the minimum cost to move all sand from pile A to pile B. But when data lives on curved space, computing this becomes expensive or impossible.

The paper's move: Compare distributions on curved spaces by slicing them into pieces along branching tree paths and computing distance piece by piece, rather than trying to handle the whole space at once.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of projecting both distributions onto a single straight line (the standard sliced-Wasserstein move), this paper projects them onto k different directions forming a tree, and then sums the distance computed on each tree branch.

The hidden quantity

Capturing shape of the space

measured quantity  =  Shape faithfulness (how well the space's bends are preserved)

The hidden quantity the paper measures is how well the distance metric respects the actual shape of the space. On a curved surface, the true distance between two points is the shortest path along the surface, not a straight line through empty space. When you slice with only one line, you lose information about curvature. When you slice with a tree (multiple branches), you capture local direction changes and surface bending.

The paper estimates this via the Radon transform: a mathematical technique that takes local slices (coordinates along each tree branch) and assembles them to reconstruct the full geometric structure. The injectivity property (proved in the paper) means the reconstruction is lossless—no information about shape is thrown away.

Paper reading. The Generalized Radon Transform and Spatial Radon Transform on systems of lines ensure injectivity: the projection operator is one-to-one, so no information about the space's topology or shape is lost when flattening from the high-dimensional space down to lower-dimensional tree slices.
Why it works

Why trees work better than lines

On a flat (Euclidean) space, one line is enough: projecting onto any single direction captures enough information to measure distance between two distributions. On a curved space, one direction is blind to bending perpendicular to that direction. A tree with k branches covers k different directions simultaneously, so no single bend can hide. Because k directions together span the space (or a sufficient approximation of it), the tree captures the true geodesic distance (shortest path on the surface) instead of straight-line distance.

The computational gain comes from the tree structure itself: instead of solving one large optimal-transport problem (expensive on curved spaces), you solve k smaller 1D problems (one per tree branch). Each 1D Wasserstein distance is fast because sorting is fast, and adding k small problems is much cheaper than one large high-dimensional problem. The Radon transform mathematically guarantees that the k solutions compose into a valid distance metric on the full space.

For mixed-curvature spaces (spaces where curvature varies by location—some regions are flat, others are spherical, others hyperbolic), the paper adapts the splitting mechanism (the rule for how the tree branches) so each branch aligns with local curvature directions. This means the tree naturally follows the surface's shape instead of fighting it.

The tree-sliced distance formula

First in words:

Average Wasserstein distance across all tree branches, computed after projecting both distributions onto each branch and applying the Radon transform.

Then the compact form:

MC-TSW(P, Q) = (1/k) * sum over i=1 to k of W_1(proj_i(P), proj_i(Q))

Every symbol:

A tiny worked example
Suppose we have two data groups on a sphere, and we choose k=3 tree branches (north, northeast, east).
Branch 1 (north): project both groups onto north axis, compute 1D Wasserstein = 2.5
Branch 2 (northeast): project both groups onto northeast axis, compute 1D Wasserstein = 3.2
Branch 3 (east): project both groups onto east axis, compute 1D Wasserstein = 2.1
MC-TSW = (2.5 + 3.2 + 2.1) / 3 = 7.8 / 3 = 2.6
This 2.6 is the total distance, honest to the sphere's curvature.

Each branch samples one slice of the space's actual shape; averaging across branches gives a distance that respects the full curved structure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Tree branches misaligned with true curvature. If the splitting mechanism does not adapt to the actual curvature directions of the space, branches point the wrong way and miss key features of the space's shape. The paper avoids this by deriving the splitting rule from the local curvature information (a mathematical description of how the space bends at each point).
Too few branches (k too small). With k=2 or k=3 on a high-dimensional curved space, some curvature directions are not sampled. The sum of distances underestimates true dissimilarity. Fair comparison requires equal total directions: k lines vs k branches, not k lines vs 1 branch.
Projection map not injective. If the nonlinear map onto a tree branch folds or compresses, information is lost and the Radon transform injectivity property fails. The paper proves this does not happen under mild continuity and smoothness assumptions.
Done right. With k branches chosen to align with local curvature and nonlinear projections proven injective, the method faithfully measures distance—no information about shape is lost—while running faster than full optimal transport because k one-dimensional sorts beat one high-dimensional solve.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate two random point clouds on a curved surface (e.g., samples from a sphere-plus-hyperbolic space, or a mixed-curvature synthetic manifold). Apply standard Wasserstein, single-line Sliced Wasserstein, and MC-TSW with k=4 branches. Measure three things: (1) wall-clock time to compute each distance, (2) accuracy (rank pairs by distance; pairs from the same data should rank closer than pairs from different data), (3) stability (recompute each distance with small random perturbations of data; a good metric should change little).

Ranking accuracy = fraction of nearest-neighbor pairs that are from the same distribution; computation time in seconds; distance stability = variance of repeated measurements under small noise.

The demo must show three things: (1) MC-TSW runs orders of magnitude faster than full Wasserstein because it replaces one expensive high-dimensional optimization with k cheap one-dimensional sorts; (2) it captures the space's shape as well as or better than single-line Sliced Wasserstein because multiple branches see all directions, not just one; (3) it produces stable distances that respect the curved space's true structure because the Radon transform is injective (lossless).
