Morphological pretraining for robot co-design

Train one universal robot controller on a pretend body so it works on any real body, letting you explore new designs instantly without retraining.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Building a robot normally means picking a body shape, training a control brain for it, and testing. But training takes weeks. This paper shows you can train a brain once on a generic pretend body, then apply it to any real body you design. This is called morphological pretraining. Instead of training from scratch each time you change the robot, you can now test thousands of body designs in hours.

Zero assumption version

The co-design trap

Today, when you design a robot, you must repeat a slow cycle: sketch a body (legs, wheels, arms), train a control brain for that exact body, test it, then start over with a new design. This is because the brain needs to learn rules like: how hard to push each joint to move forward for THIS body. A different body needs different rules.

A beginner might think: just train one brain that works for all bodies. But that doesn't work well either. A universal brain is vague and weak, because it cannot exploit the strengths of any particular design. So engineers today accept the grind: retrain, test, repeat. Exploring even 50 design ideas takes 50 separate training runs — months of compute.

The paper's move. Train the brain once on a pretend body shape. That brain learns the general skill of control. Now, without retraining, you can test it on any real body design and instantly see if it is good or bad.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Build a pretend body

In the simulator, define a generic robot with standard joints and limbs.

Step 2: Train once on the pretend body

Use calculus and nudges to find a brain that makes the pretend body move well.

Step 3: Apply to any new design

Change the body shape in the simulator, plug in the trained brain, run one test.

Step 4: Check the score

The brain tells you instantly if this design is fast, stable, or useless.

Step 5: Repeat for 100 new designs

Explore design space in hours instead of months.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper changes how you build the brain. Instead of training a different brain for each body, you train one brain on a generic reference body, then reuse it.

the object it moves  =  the training target
The hidden quantity

Morphology agnosticity: the control brain's ability to work on unfamiliar bodies

measured quantity  =  control generalization across body types

This is not a number you calculate at the end. It is the question the whole paper asks: can a brain trained on one body shape generalize to other shapes it has never seen? Normally the answer is no. A brain learns joint-specific tricks like: push joint-3 at angle 45 degrees when the sensor says you are tipping. If joint-3 is now in a different place, those tricks break.

The paper measures this by training on a pretend body once, then testing on dozens of evolved designs. If the score stays high on all of them, the brain has generalized. If the score crashes, the brain is not generalizing. A brain trained on a generic pretend body (with standard joints and ranges) generalizes much better than a brain trained on one specific real design because the pretend body teaches general principles instead of design-specific quirks, so the brain can adapt to any shape.

Paper reading. In reinforcement learning co-design, morphological agnosticity means the learned policy (control rules) transfers without retraining to new body plans with different joint counts, ranges, or shapes.
Why it works

Why pretrained brains generalize

A brain trained on a generic pretend body learns only the general principles of movement, not specific tricks. On a pretend body, the brain must learn rules like: balance yourself by watching your tilt sensor and pushing joints in the right direction. These rules work on any body because they are about the physics of balance, not about one design. In contrast, if you train on one specific real design, the brain learns quirks: push joint 3 hard, never push joint 7, exploit this weird resonance in the gearbox. Those quirks don't work on other bodies because they depend on that exact design's shape.

A pretend body is like a blank canvas. It has standard joints, ranges, and symmetry. Training on it forces the brain to learn transferable skills, because the pretend body doesn't reward design-specific tricks. Then you can take those skills and apply them to real designs that are asymmetric, weird, or optimized for speed, and the brain adapts because it learned the underlying principles of movement, not the specific quirks.

Measuring design quality without retraining

First in words:

score = simulation of brain on new design, run for T timesteps; no retraining required

Then the compact form:

score_design = sum_t reward(s_t, a_t) where s_t = sensor values, a_t = brain(s_t), no new training steps

Every symbol:

score_designthe final performance number for one design
sum_tadd up all rewards across the entire test
s_twhat the brain senses at each moment (joint angles, tilt, force)
a_twhat the brain commands the joints to do
brain(s_t)the fixed, pre-trained controller outputs the command
Thow long the test runs (number of timesteps)
A tiny worked example
Design A with pretrained brain: timestep 1 sensor=forward-tilt, brain says push-left, reward=+1. Timestep 2 sensor=balanced, brain says small-push, reward=+2. Timestep 3 sensor=backward-tilt, brain says push-right, reward=+3. Total score: 1+2+3=6.
Design B with pretrained brain: timestep 1 sensor=stumble, brain says brace, reward=0. Timestep 2 sensor=recovering, brain says push-hard, reward=1. Timestep 3 sensor=stable, brain says walk, reward=2. Total score: 0+1+2=3.
Design A scores 6, Design B scores 3. The designer picks Design A without ever retraining the brain. Old method: retrain on each design for hours. New method: one forward pass through the simulator.

The equation is just summing up rewards without any training. You run the brain forward, collect what happens, and add up the score.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pretend body is too simple. If the pretend body doesn't capture the real challenges a design might face (sharp turns, rough ground, rapid accelerations), the pretrained brain won't know how to handle them. The brain learns from what it saw.
Evolved designs become too similar. If you try to co-optimize the body design and a shared universal brain at the same time, the designs collapse. They all converge to similar shapes that are easy for the shared brain, not shapes that are actually good. This is called diversity collapse.
Zero-shot brain is weaker than a specialized brain. A brain trained on one specific design will score higher on that design than a general pretrained brain will. You trade off peak performance for speed and breadth of exploration.
Done right with fine-tuning. The paper shows that you can keep the pretrained brain generic and still get high scores if you fine-tune it (make small adjustments) for each new design. This combines speed with good performance.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulator with one generic robot body. Train a control brain once via gradient-based optimization. Then change the body (add a leg, remove an arm, shift the center of mass) and run the trained brain on the new body without retraining. Measure the score. Repeat this 100 times with random design changes. Plot the scores over design number to show that exploration is fast.

exploration_speedup = (time to rank 100 designs with retraining per design) / (time to rank 100 designs with one pretrained brain)

The demo must make it impossible to miss: with pretraining, you can test 100 designs faster than the old method tests 10 because each new design only needs one forward pass through the simulator instead of hours of retraining.
