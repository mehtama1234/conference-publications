STORK: Faster Diffusion Sampling by Handling Stiffness

A numerical method that takes bigger steps through the noisy-to-image path by using orthogonal polynomials to steady the shaky slopes, so diffusion models generate images faster.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Diffusion models turn random noise into images by following a path defined by equations. The problem: the path has steep slopes in some places and flat slopes elsewhere, making it impossible to take steady big steps without overshooting. STORK uses orthogonal polynomials to flatten out those steep regions by multiplying each step by a polynomial that shrinks automatically where slopes are steep, so bigger steps work safely and sampling is faster because fewer total steps are needed.

Zero assumption version

The path has slopes that vary wildly

Diffusion models follow a path from random noise to a clean image. At each step along the path, the model predicts a direction to move. But here is the catch: the slopes are not uniform. Some sections of the path have very steep slopes (the direction changes fast), and others have nearly flat slopes. This wild variation is called stiffness.

A naive approach would be to use a simple stepping method: pick a step size and march forward at a constant rate. This works when slopes are gentle. But when a steep section arrives, that fixed step size is too big, and you overshoot. Cut the step size to handle the steep parts, and now you waste time on the flat parts because you are taking tiny unnecessary steps. You cannot win.

Existing methods like Euler's method or Heun's method hit this wall. They converge to tiny step sizes in stiff regions, turning a sampling process that should take 8 steps into one that needs 50 steps.

The method. Use orthogonal polynomials to reshape how each step is computed, so steep slopes stop forcing tiny steps.

Detect regions where the slope changes abruptly (classic numerical sign of stiffness).

Choose orthogonal polynomial

Pick Chebyshev or Gegenbauer polynomial that is stable for the slope range we face.

Multiply step update

Build each step as a polynomial in the model's prediction, not a simple linear sum.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how each step in the sampling process is computed. Instead of adding a simple linear multiple of the model's prediction, it multiplies the prediction by a polynomial before adding it. This polynomial is built from orthogonal functions (Chebyshev or Gegenbauer), which have a special property: they are most aggressive in regions where slopes are steep, and most conservative where slopes are gentle.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: stiffness and stability

measured quantity  =  stability region (how much slope variation the step can tolerate)

Stiffness is not a single number; it is a range. The stability region of a numerical method is the set of slope conditions under which the method does not blow up or oscillate wildly. Ordinary methods like Euler or Heun have tiny stability regions that shrink when slopes are steep, forcing small steps. Orthogonal polynomial methods have much wider stability regions because the polynomial is designed to cancel out the wild variation.

STORK measures this by computing the region where the polynomial's absolute value stays below 1 (which means the step does not amplify errors). The paper compares the stability regions of different methods on plots called complex plane diagrams, showing that STORK's polynomial covers a much larger area than Euler or standard Runge-Kutta.

Why it works

Why it works: polynomial stabilization

Here is the concrete reason STORK works. In ordinary numerical methods, each step forward is a weighted sum of the model's predictions. The weights are fixed numbers. This is like saying: no matter how steep the path gets, we add the same amount at each step. When the path steepens, you overshoot.

STORK uses a polynomial instead of fixed weights. The polynomial is chosen from a family (Chebyshev or Gegenbauer) that has a special shape: it oscillates gently and is bounded (never grows too large). When you multiply the model's prediction by this polynomial, you get a smaller effective step in steep regions and a fuller step in gentle regions. The polynomial naturally adapts to the slope variation, so bigger steps become safe.

Why does STORK pick Chebyshev polynomials? They are optimal for this job because they have the smallest possible overshoot across an interval of slopes, a fact proven in classical mathematics. So STORK is not a hack; it is applying a 100-year-old math result (orthogonal polynomial theory) to a modern problem.

The update rule and worked example

First in words:

new_position = old_position + step_size * polynomial(slope) * model_prediction

Then the compact form:

x_{n+1} = x_n + h * P_k(lambda * J) * f(x_n, t_n)

Every symbol:

x_ncurrent position (pixel values)
x_{n+1}position after this step
hstep size
P_kChebyshev polynomial of degree k
lambda * Jscaled slope (related to how fast direction changes)
f(x_n, t_n)model's prediction for which direction to move
Tiny worked example: two steps of sampling
Start: noisy image = [100, 80, 60]
Model predicts direction = [+4, +5, +3] (move toward cleaner image)
Step size h = 0.1, slope scaling lambda * J = 0.8
Chebyshev polynomial P_2(0.8) = 0.68 (scaled by slope)
Update: [100, 80, 60] + 0.1 * 0.68 * [4, 5, 3] = [100.27, 80.34, 60.20]
Compare to naive Euler: [100, 80, 60] + 0.1 * [4, 5, 3] = [100.4, 80.5, 60.3] (overshoots more)
After 8 steps, naive method needs tiny h and takes 50 steps; STORK reaches clean image in 8.

The polynomial multiplier (0.68 instead of 1.0) is what makes bigger steps safe by automatically shrinking them where slopes are steep.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the wrong polynomial degree. A polynomial that is too high-degree becomes oscillatory and unstable. A polynomial that is too low-degree does not cover all the slopes. The paper fixes this by using second-order (STORK-2) or fourth-order (STORK-4) polynomials, which are proven stable and accurate.
Not reusing old predictions correctly. STORK uses forward finite differences (computing differences between stored previous velocities) to approximate derivatives. If stored values are old or noisy, the derivative estimates become wrong. The paper uses a sliding window to keep estimates fresh.
Starting steps require more function calls. The very first few steps cannot use stored predictions because there is nothing old to reuse yet. STORK uses Euler and Adams-Bashforth for the first few steps, then switches to full STORK-2 or STORK-4.
Done right. STORK-4 with second-order Taylor expansion (noise diffusion) or first-order (flow matching) is proven stable. It handles both semi-linear diffusion ODEs and general flow-matching paths, so it is broadly applicable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should sample an image using a pre-trained diffusion model (e.g., Stable Diffusion) with three methods: standard Euler, standard Runge-Kutta, and STORK. Run each with 8 total model function calls (8 NFEs). Measure two things: the perceptual quality of the final image (using a metric like FID or LPIPS, which measure closeness to ground truth) and the wall-clock time in seconds.

quality_gain = STORK_quality_at_8_steps - Euler_quality_at_8_steps; speedup = time_Euler_50_steps / time_STORK_8_steps

The demo makes impossible to miss: at the same number of model calls, STORK produces visibly cleaner images because it takes bigger safer steps, and because it needs fewer total steps, the whole process is 5-10x faster.
