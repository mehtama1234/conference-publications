MER-DG: Modality-Entropy Regularization for Multimodal Domain Generalization

When video and audio train together, each secretly relies on the other's quirks and forgets universal patterns; entropy regularization forces them to stay diverse and independent.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Multimodal models (using video and audio together) fail when recording conditions change because joint training teaches each modality to exploit shortcuts specific to the other. MER-DG fixes this by measuring how varied each modality's learned features are and penalizing encoders that compress their feature space down to just a few patterns.

Zero assumption version

The trap of leaning on each other

Imagine you train a model to understand video and audio at the same time. The system has two separate brains: one for video (watching motion), one for audio (listening to sound). Both send their learned summaries to a shared decision-maker that combines them. During training, the loss function tells the system: minimize error on the fused answer. That is the only feedback.

What both brains learn to do: exploit cross-modal co-occurrences. If in your training kitchen footage, whenever there is chopping sound, the video always shows a knife in motion, the audio encoder learns to just recognize knives by listening for chops. The video encoder learns to spot knives by sound cues. Each one becomes dependent on the other, like two dancers leaning on each other so heavily that neither can stand alone.

When you deploy to a new kitchen with different recording equipment or acoustics, that learned co-occurrence breaks. The audio might not pick up the chop sound the same way. The video encoder made no independent sense of visual motion. Both fail. This is called Fusion Overfitting: the encoders overfit to source-specific statistical relationships between modalities rather than learning universal patterns that work in any kitchen.

In one sentence. When video and audio optimize only the fused result, each secretly drops its independent understanding and leans on the other's quirks instead.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train video and audio with a shared fusion loss

Standard way: minimize error on the combined prediction.

Measure how compressed each encoder's feature space is

Count active dimensions; if features cluster onto few patterns, diversity is low.

Add a diversity penalty for each encoder

If an encoder compresses its features, the loss grows to punish that specialization.

Train all three losses together

Fused classification plus diversity penalty for video plus diversity penalty for audio, all at once.

Each encoder stays independent and diverse

Forced to represent all its patterns, not rely on the other modality's quirks.

What the paper changes

What changes

MER-DG changes the training objective by adding two new penalty terms, one for each encoder. Instead of only minimizing error on the fused answer, the system now also penalizes feature compression.

the object it moves  =  Training loss function
The hidden quantity

Feature entropy: are all patterns active?

measured quantity  =  Entropy: how varied are the patterns each encoder uses?

Feature entropy measures whether an encoder is using all of its learned patterns or collapsing onto just a few. Imagine the encoder outputs a list of numbers (say, 256 of them) for each frame. If those numbers spread evenly across the full range, diversity is high; if most values cluster into narrow bands, diversity is low. Low diversity means the encoder is discarding dimensions and compressing information, which is a sign it is relying on shortcuts.

The paper estimates this diversity directly from the learned features. For each modality, it computes how much variance sits in each dimension of the feature space, then sums up those variances. If dimensions are active and varied, diversity is high. If features collapse to a low-rank subspace (only a few dimensions have signal), diversity is low and an added penalty term pulls harder to force the encoder to use all dimensions.

Plain reading. Each encoder builds a list of learned patterns from its input. Entropy tells you how many of those patterns the encoder is actually using. High entropy (uses many) is good; low entropy (uses only a few) means fusion overfitting is happening.
Paper reading. RankMe scores and spectral singular values measure the effective rank (how many dimensions carry signal). Fusion training drops Video RankMe from 265.5 to 235.9, and Audio from 163.6 to 144.1. MER-DG restores scores to 290.0 and 204.2, confirming preserved entropy and diversity.
Why it works

Why forcing diversity prevents fusion overfitting

The causal chain is simple. Joint optimization of the fused loss has one objective: minimize error on the combined output. If audio can predict video features and video can predict audio features (cross-modal co-occurrence), then each encoder can discard its own independent dimensions and specialize: audio becomes a recognizer of visual patterns, video becomes a recognizer of audio patterns. This reduces the complexity each encoder must learn because it no longer has to figure out patterns from scratch; the other modality gives it a shortcut, making the fused loss shrink faster in training.

When you add a diversity penalty, the optimization now has three objectives. The fused loss still matters, but so does forcing both encoders to keep all their dimensions active. If an encoder tries to specialize and compress, the diversity penalty grows, offsetting any fused loss gain. The result: encoders cannot afford to discard their independent patterns. They must keep learning both their own universal features and the task-specific patterns. When deployed on new domains where cross-modal co-occurrences differ, the encoder has its independent understanding ready.

Math after the picture

The regularization equation

First in words:

Total loss equals the fused classification error plus a strength parameter times the sum of entropy penalties for each encoder.

Then the compact form:

loss_total = loss_fused + lambda * (entropy_penalty_video + entropy_penalty_audio)

Every symbol:

loss_totalthe combined training objective
loss_fusedstandard cross-entropy error on the fused prediction
lambdathe strength of entropy regularization (how much to penalize low entropy)
entropy_penalty_videohow collapsed the video encoder's feature space is
entropy_penalty_audiohow collapsed the audio encoder's feature space is
A tiny worked example
Fused error on a batch: 0.45 (measured from cross-entropy loss).
Video encoder's feature space shows 3 active dimensions out of 256 (low entropy): entropy penalty = 2.8.
Audio encoder shows 200 active dimensions (high entropy): entropy penalty = 0.2.
With lambda = 0.1: loss_total = 0.45 + 0.1 * (2.8 + 0.2) = 0.45 + 0.3 = 0.75.
In the next step, if video encoder compresses further to 2 dimensions (penalty jumps to 3.2), loss_total would jump to 0.45 + 0.1 * (3.2 + 0.2) = 0.77, making compression costly.
Training steers away from specialization because entropy regularization makes it explicitly expensive.

The entropy penalty directly measures how many feature dimensions an encoder is actually using; higher penalty pushes the encoder to use all of them.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda is too small. If you set the entropy penalty weight too low, encoders can still afford to specialize and compress, ignoring the penalty. The paper's experiments test different lambda values per dataset to find the best one.
Lambda is too large. If the diversity penalty is too strong, encoders spend so much effort staying diverse that they cannot learn to fuse well, and the combined model accuracy drops. Balancing matters.
The cross-modal co-occurrence is weak in training data. If video and audio are already independent (e.g., random audio spliced with video), fusion overfitting never happens, so the diversity penalty adds no benefit. The method assumes co-occurrence exists.
Done right: lambda is tuned per dataset, and encoders generalize as well as independent models while fusing better than the baseline. Experiments show video alignment recovers 21–54% when the diversity penalty is applied because forced diversity prevents the encoder from collapsing onto cross-modal shortcuts, and fused accuracy improves by ~5% over standard fusion because encoders now share universal patterns instead of domain-specific ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a multimodal model on a training split of EPIC-Kitchens (one kitchen/setup). Train it twice: once with standard fusion loss, once with fusion plus entropy regularization. After training, measure each encoder's performance in isolation (freeze the other modality's encoder, use only one to classify). Standard fusion should show a large drop in standalone accuracy; entropy-regularized training should show minimal drop or recovery to near-independent training levels. This directly proves that regularization prevents specialization and preserves independent learning.

Specialization drop = (standalone_performance - fusion_trained_performance) / standalone_performance. Standard fusion: 5-7%. With entropy regularization: <1%.

The key test: each encoder performs nearly as well alone as it does in the fused model. If it does, the regularization has prevented the encoders from relying on each other's quirks.
