LARA: Aligning Action Representations in Robot Learning

Tie together two separately trained robot-learning models by aligning their learned action representations, so they teach each other instead of working in silos.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot learning models predict what action to take from images and language instructions, but they are usually trained separately: one learns what actions look like from unlabeled videos, the other learns to pick actions from language instructions and labeled robot data. This paper glues them together by measuring how well their learned action representations align, letting them both improve during training.

Zero assumption version

Two models, two separate worlds

Teaching a robot to act requires two kinds of learning. First, a model learns what actions produce visible changes—by watching unlabeled human videos, it discovers how the world moves. Second, a model learns to obey language instructions by training on labeled robot data that pairs images and spoken commands with the correct action. But these two models are trained independently, sealed off from each other.

This leaves both models weaker. The first model (the latent action model) never sees the language instructions, so it learns spurious patterns—it might think that a tiny camera jitter counts as an action. The second model (the VLA) uses the first model's action representations as a fixed guide, but those representations were locked in place before seeing real robot data. The beginner's mistake: training them separately with the hope they'll somehow complement each other.

The result is poor action prediction, especially in the real world where data is scarce. Robots either hallucinate actions that produce no visible change, or they miss subtle real actions that the first model dismissed as noise.

The paper's move: During training, align the two models' learned action representations so they update each other—the language-instruction model refines what the world-model thinks is an action, and the world-model prevents the language model from predicting actions that produce no visible change.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pre-train the world model

Train a latent action model on unlabeled videos to encode state changes.

Pre-train the language model

Train a diffusion-based VLA to generate action sequences from images and language, using the frozen world-model actions as labels.

Build a bridge

Add a learnable projection head that maps world-model action representations into the language-model's internal feature space.

Align during training

Measure how similar world-model actions are to language-model features (a comparison of direction), propagate the alignment loss back into both models.

Joint refinement

Both models update together: world-model discards spurious patterns, language-model grounds itself in realistic forward dynamics.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the action representations—the learned encodings of what an action is. Instead of letting each model build its own isolated action space, LARA explicitly couples them during training by measuring how well a projection function aligns the world-model's action space into the language-model's feature space.

the object it moves  =  Action representations
The hidden quantity

Alignment score: how well the two models agree

measured quantity  =  Similarity score (how much two action lists agree in direction)

The paper introduces one hidden quantity that governs the entire mechanism: how similar the world-model's learned actions are to the language-model's learned features. Similarity here means cosine similarity (a standard measure of how much two lists of numbers point in the same direction, ranging from −1 to +1, where +1 means perfect agreement).

The paper estimates this similarity by taking the world-model's action representation, passing it through a small learnable projection head (a shallow neural network), and comparing the result to the language-model's internal features by measuring how much their lists of numbers point in the same direction. High similarity means both models agree on what constitutes a real action; low similarity means they are out of sync.

Plain reading. Two models learn different representations of what an action is. LARA measures how much they overlap, then updates both to increase overlap. High overlap means they agree; low means they are misaligned.
Paper reading. The representation alignment loss is LRA(theta, psi) = −E[CosSim(y_pretrain, f_psi(h_theta))] where y_pretrain is the pre-trained action vector (a list of numbers), h_theta are the diffusion model's internal feature lists, and f_psi is a learnable projection network. Maximizing similarity tightens the alignment constraint.
Why it works

Why aligning representations fixes the problem

The world-model learns from raw videos—it sees a human hand move, the object shift, the camera jitter, and tries to predict the next frame. Without guidance from actual robot tasks, it assigns high importance to any visible change, even ones that do not matter for robotic action. This includes spurious patterns: tiny lighting changes, camera noise, or incidental motion that happened to coincide with a real action.

The language-model learns from labeled robot data, but it uses the frozen world-model's action space as its sole anchor. If that space is noisy, the language-model has no way to refine it—it is stuck working with impure action representations.

LARA flips this by making the alignment score a shared objective during training. When the world-model generates an action representation and the language-model's diffusion features do not align with it, both models feel pressure to change. The world-model discards spurious patterns because the language-model (trained on real robot data) stays quiet on those patterns—its internal signals do not light up. The language-model becomes grounded in the world-model's forward dynamics model, which learns from the diffusion process: if the language-model suggests an action that predicts no realistic state change, the forward dynamics model rejects it. Over training, the two models converge on a shared action space that is both grounded in actual visual dynamics and responsive to language instructions.

The alignment objective

First in words:

Alignment loss is the negative average cosine similarity between the pre-trained world-model actions and the language-model features (projected through a learnable adapter). The model maximizes this by minimizing negative similarity.

Then the compact form:

L_RA(θ,ψ) = −E[CosSim(y_pretrain_t, f_ψ(h_θ_t))]

Every symbol:

L_RAThe alignment loss (what we minimize to maximize agreement)
θParameters of the diffusion-based language model
ψParameters of the projection head (the adapter)
y_pretrain_tThe world-model's learned action representation at time t
h_θ_tThe diffusion model's internal features at time t
f_ψProjection head: a small learnable network that translates from world-model space to language-model space
CosSimCosine similarity: how much two lists of numbers point the same direction (ranges −1 to +1)
E[ ]Average over all training examples and timesteps
A tiny worked example
Suppose the world-model produces action [0.8, 0.2, 0.1] and the projection head outputs [0.85, 0.15, 0.12] for the corresponding language-model feature.
Cosine similarity = (0.8×0.85 + 0.2×0.15 + 0.1×0.12) / (magnitude of first × magnitude of second) ≈ 0.717 / (0.825 × 0.873) ≈ 0.99 (very high).
This means the two representations agree. The loss is −0.99, so the network is happy and makes small updates.
If instead the projection outputs [0.2, 0.8, 0.5], similarity drops to ~0.24, loss is −0.24, and weight updates push the networks to align better.

The alignment loss is mixed with the diffusion matching loss and the world-model reconstruction loss to form the full training objective, so all three constraints push the models together.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Projection head size mismatch. If the projection head is too shallow or the target feature space is too small, alignment becomes impossible; the networks hit a hard ceiling on similarity. The head must be expressive enough to capture the mapping between action spaces.
Frozen world-model remains frozen. If the world-model is never unfrozen during LARA training, its spurious patterns persist unchanged. LARA requires propagating the alignment loss backward into both models' weights for joint refinement to work.
Weak language signal. If the robot dataset is small or has poor action labels, the language-model cannot constrain the world-model to realistic actions. Alignment amplifies whatever signal exists, so bad labels propagate to both models.
Done right. LARA works when both models are trainable, the projection head is appropriately sized, the robot dataset is reasonably clean, and the alignment loss is weighted correctly (balanced against diffusion and reconstruction losses).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal experiment would train a simple latent action model on unlabeled robotic videos (or simulated dynamics), then train a diffusion-based action generator on a small set of labeled robot demonstrations. Apply LARA's alignment loss during the second stage: as the alignment score grows, spurious patterns in the world-model fade (because the language-model never activates on them), and the language-model's action predictions stay grounded in realistic forward dynamics. Measure whether the robot's final success rate on held-out tasks improves, because the two models now agree on what constitutes a real action, so the robot picks actions that actually change the world instead of hallucinating useless movements.

Success rate = (tasks completed successfully) / (total tasks attempted); compare no-alignment vs LARA-aligned on the same test set.

The key proof: with LARA, the same number of robot demonstrations and the same video data yield higher success, because the two models have learned to speak the same language about actions instead of working independently.
