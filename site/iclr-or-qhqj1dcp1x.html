H2OFlow: Grounding Human-Object Affordances with 3D Generative Models and Dense Diffused Flows

H2OFlow learns how humans interact with objects in 3D space by using synthetic data and flow patterns instead of expensive hand-labeled videos.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Robots and AI systems need to understand how humans use objects: not just where they touch (contact), but also which direction they face the object and where they stand around it. Current methods require expensive hand-labeled real videos. H2OFlow sidesteps this by training on synthetic 3D objects and learning a flow pattern—a vector field showing how to move through 3D space—that captures all three aspects of human-object interaction without any human annotations.

Why hand-labeled affordances are broken

An affordance is what an object lets you do with it. A knife affords cutting. A chair affords sitting. Humans intuitively know this. But robots do not. They need examples: videos of humans using objects, carefully labeled frame-by-frame with coordinates and directions. Collecting these is expensive. You hire people to film interactions, annotate every frame, and label contact points, body orientation, and where the person stands. This costs money and takes months.

Existing methods also cheat: they only look at where humans touch objects (contact points). They ignore which direction humans prefer to face the object (a TV is watched from the front, not the back) and where people naturally stand around it (you reach into a microwave from the front, not from behind). A complete affordance needs all three.

The problem in one sentence. Robots cannot learn 3D affordances without expensive hand-labeled data, and existing methods miss orientation and spatial preference.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Generate synthetic data

Use a 3D generative model to create objects and annotate them with human poses.

Represent as a flow field

For each point in 3D space around the object, assign a vector showing the direction to move toward the ideal human pose.

Learn with diffusion

Train a neural network using a diffusion process on point clouds: start with noise, gradually denoise to predict the correct flow.

Extract three affordances

From the learned flow, extract where humans touch, which direction they face, and which zones they occupy.

Test on real objects

Apply the trained model to real-world photos and 3D scans; check if it predicts plausible human interaction.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

H2OFlow replaces hand-labeled real-world data with synthetic data and replaces contact-point labels with a continuous flow field—a vector at every location in 3D space that points toward where a human should move to interact with the object.

The hidden quantity

The flow field: where and how to interact

measured quantity  =  Dense 3D interaction flow

The flow field is an invisible map in 3D space around an object. At each point, it stores a vector (an arrow) that says: 'If you are here, move in this direction to reach the ideal way to interact.' This is how the paper sneaks in contact, orientation, and spatial preference: the flow converges to contact points (fingertips converge here), it swirls to face-forward directions (arrows spiral to face the front), and it populates safe zones (the field is dense around where people stand).

The paper estimates this flow using a diffusion process (gradually removing noise to reveal a clean signal): a neural network learns to denoise a noisy point cloud by predicting the next refinement in the flow direction. The more a region is visited by human poses, the stronger the flow signal, because the network's training loss penalizes it for mispredicting flows in visited zones; empty regions stay silent because no training data constrains them. This is how synthetic data, which has no explicit contact labels, still encodes the full affordance picture—the flow shapes itself to where poses cluster, naturally capturing contact, direction, and stance all at once.

Why it works

Why synthetic data plus flow patterns work

Here is the core: in real videos, you must label every touch point and body angle by hand. That is tedious and error-prone. In synthetic data generated by a 3D model, you already have perfect 3D poses (the computer knows exactly where the hand is and which way the person faces). You do not annotate contact points; instead, you train a neural network to predict a continuous flow field that, by nature, reflects where humans cluster. If all poses in the training set show hands touching the knife blade, the flow will converge there. If all poses show faces turned toward the TV, the flow will point that direction. If people avoid the back of a microwave, the flow will be sparse there.

This works because the diffusion process learns the shape of the human-pose cloud without ever being told a label. The flow learns to point toward regions where many poses gather. Contact sites are regions where thousands of hands converge; orientation and stance preferences are regions where thousands of heads and torsos align. One learned thing—the flow field—implicitly encodes all three affordances. No separate models needed. Synthetic data, being perfect and plentiful, trains this field to work consistently without the cost of manual annotation.

The diffusion equation for flow prediction

First in words:

Flow at position p is the denoising step that minimizes the squared difference between the predicted flow and the actual flow, weighted by how confident we are in the prediction.

Then the compact form:

L_flow = E_t [ w_t * ||f_theta(x_t, t) - f_true||^2 ]

Every symbol:

f_theta(x_t, t)the predicted flow vector at noisy point cloud x_t and time step t
f_truethe true flow vector (direction toward poses)
w_tthe weight at time step t (higher early, lower late)
tthe diffusion time step (0=clean, T=fully noisy)
E_taverage over all time steps
||...||^2squared distance (how wrong the prediction is)
A tiny worked example
At time t=5, point cloud is noisy with w_5 = 0.8. Network predicts flow vector [0.2, 0.1, 0.3]. True flow is [0.3, 0.1, 0.2].
Error: [0.2-0.3, 0.1-0.1, 0.3-0.2] = [-0.1, 0, 0.1]. Squared: [0.01, 0, 0.01] = 0.02.
Weighted loss: 0.8 * 0.02 = 0.016. This penalty nudges the network to predict flows closer to where poses cluster.

The diffusion process gradually teaches the network to recognize the shape of human-pose space, so the learned flow naturally encodes contact, orientation, and stance without explicit labels.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Synthetic domain gap. If the 3D generative model produces unnaturally stiff or awkward poses, or objects that look nothing like real things, the learned flow will not match real human behavior. The training cloud must be realistic.
Ambiguous or multi-modal affordances. Some objects can be used in many ways (a pen can write or be used as a stick). The flow field will average across all modes, producing a fuzzy prediction instead of sharp contact or stance. The paper does not solve this ambiguity.
Occlusion and self-collision. The flow does not know if the predicted pose would collide with the object or with the person's own body. The model can suggest physically impossible configurations.
Done right: synthetic diversity. Training on a large, diverse, realistic synthetic pose dataset (varying body shapes, object sizes, camera angles) makes the flow generalize—it works when faced with real objects it has never seen.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo that takes a real object (a photograph or 3D scan of a knife, mug, or chair) and runs H2OFlow on it. Display a 3D visualization showing: (1) heatmaps of where the flow field is strong (likely contact/interaction zones), (2) arrows showing orientation preference, (3) a heatmap of the occupancy volume (where the model expects humans to stand or reach from). Compare these predictions to videos of real humans using the same object. Check that the contact zones align with fingertip positions, the orientation arrows point the way humans face the object, and the occupancy heatmap covers the zones where people actually stand.

affordance accuracy = (correct contact + correct orientation + correct occupancy) / 3

The demo proves H2OFlow trains on free synthetic data yet predicts human-object interaction just as well as or better than models trained on expensive hand-labeled real videos, because the synthetic poses are geometrically perfect (no mislabeled frames) and endlessly diverse (one generative model creates infinite variations), which teaches the flow to capture genuine affordance patterns rather than memorizing label noise.
