The gap: language models cannot read sensor streams

Today's effective language models take text or images as input. But a patient's medical data comes as time-series: an ECG (electrocardiogram — the electrical signal of the heart beating), recorded second by second over hours; a wearable accelerometer logging motion every millisecond; sleep sensors tracking stages throughout the night. A naive approach converts this stream to text ('heart rate 72, 73, 71, 72...') or a single summary number ('average heart rate: 72'), but both lose the story the data is telling: the shape of the heartbeat, the timing of the peaks, the patterns that a cardiologist reads off the screen in seconds.

The reason it breaks: text summaries throw away the exact timing and waveform shape. Treating the stream as a list of numbers treats every number equally, so a fast heartbeat spike at minute 5 ('important') looks the same as a steady baseline. A language model trained on words and concept relationships cannot tell the difference. You lose the signal.

The gap. Language models today cannot natively read time-series data; they must convert it to text or summaries, which loses the shape and timing that doctors rely on.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies a language model's input architecture to accept time-series encodings directly, alongside text. Instead of converting streams to discrete summaries, it runs the raw sequence through a neural encoder (a network trained to recognize waveforms) that outputs a learned representation (a list of learned numbers), then feeds that into the same attention mechanism that processes word tokens. Two variants: soft prompting (prepend the time-series encoding as extra tokens in the prompt) or cross-attention (a separate attention layer that reads from both the series encoding and the text).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: shape preservation across encoding

measured quantity  =  Waveform fidelity (how much shape survives encoding)

The paper's secret is measuring how well the encoding preserves the original time-series shape. When you encode a heartbeat stream into an embedding (a compact list of learned numbers), you risk flattening the signal: peak heights, timing of transitions, rhythmic patterns. The paper does not explicitly name this, but its mechanism depends on it: the more fidelity, the more the model can reason about waveform details (e.g., 'this peak is too tall, a sign of arrhythmia'). Less fidelity means it only captures overall averages (e.g., 'average heart rate is high'), which is weaker for diagnosis.

The paper infers this by testing on structured tasks with ground-truth labels: human activity recognition (did the person walk, sit, run?), sleep staging (which sleep phase?), ECG question-answering (what is abnormal?). Tasks requiring fine-grained timing (like distinguishing a short burst of motion from a long slow drift) act as a proxy for fidelity: if the model solves them, the encoding kept the shape.

Why it works

Why it works: fusing streams with language

The core issue with converting time-series to text is that every number looks equally important to the language model. An ECG stream '70, 71, 72, 85, 72, 71, 70' has a story (a spike at position 4), but when flattened to text the model has to infer the story from word order alone. OpenTSLM solves this by running the raw stream through a neural encoder (a small network that has learned to recognize waveforms). The encoder outputs a learned list of numbers that captures the shape. That learned list plugs directly into the language model's attention mechanism, where it mixes with text tokens, so the model can spot patterns in the shape the same way it spots patterns in grammar — because the attention layer reads all its inputs as equal citizens, whether words or learned numbers.

An encoding preserves the order and shape of the stream. A summary (like 'max 85, min 70, mean 72') loses the order, so the model cannot tell whether a spike happened at second 4 or second 30 — it only knows the range. With the encoding, the model can learn that a quick spike (1 second) differs from a slow ramp (10 seconds) because the encoder reads the entire sequence and outputs different learned numbers for different shapes. Similarly, a rise from 70 to 85 differs from a rise from 72 to 95 in the encoding (the encoder captures the absolute baseline), so the language model can learn to flag one as abnormal and the other as normal, matching the training data.

Encoding the stream into embeddings

First in words:

embedding = encoder(time_series). The encoder is a small neural network that reads the stream and outputs a fixed-size embedding.

Then the compact form:

z = Encoder(x), where x is the time series and z is the learned embedding passed to the language model

Every symbol:

xthe raw time-series data (a sequence of numbers)
Encodera neural network (e.g., a transformer or CNN) trained to recognize waveform patterns
zthe resulting embedding: a compact vector of learned features (e.g., 512 numbers)
A tiny worked example
Raw ECG stream: x = [70, 71, 72, 85, 72, 71, 70] (7 samples)
Encoder processes the stream, learning to recognize 'there is a spike at position 4'
Output embedding z = [0.1, 0.8, 0.3, 0.2, 0.9, 0.1, 0.4] (7 learned features; in practice z has 512+)
Language model receives z alongside the text query 'Is this abnormal?', can reason about the spike

The embedding z carries the learned shape; the language model's attention reads it just like text tokens.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Encoder underfitting. If the encoder is not trained well, it may ignore subtle waveform features (like the slope of an ECG rise). The embedding z then loses fidelity, and the language model cannot reason about fine details even though it wants to.
Modality mismatch. Time-series data has very different statistics than text (smooth gradients, periodicity) and images (spatial structure). If the encoder is not specifically trained for time-series, it may treat every time step equally and miss the continuous nature of the signal.
Mismatch between training and real data. If the encoder is trained on clean, standardized ECGs (from a lab) but tested on noisy wearable data (from a patient's home), the encoding may break down because the waveforms look different.
Done right. The paper mitigates this by training end-to-end on three diverse time-series datasets (sleep, activity, ECG) and validating with cardiologists on real ECG data, ensuring the encoder learns features that work across different data sources and clinical settings.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small end-to-end system: take a raw heartbeat recording (or simulate one as a CSV of numbers), encode it into an embedding using a trained encoder, pass both the embedding and a natural-language question ('Is this ECG abnormal?') to a language model, and display the reasoning output. Measure whether the model can identify specific ECG abnormalities (like arrhythmia) and explain which features of the waveform triggered the diagnosis. Compare against a baseline that converts the ECG to text (e.g., 'peak at minute 5 is 85') and show that OpenTSLM reasoning is more specific and accurate.
