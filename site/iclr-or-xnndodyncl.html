Fair Policy Aggregation from Standard Policy Optimization

When multiple groups have different values, use quantile fairness to train a policy that respects all of them without solving impossible math problems.

Zero assumption version

When opinions differ, averaging loses everyone

Today's AI systems learn what to do by collecting feedback from people. The standard way treats all that feedback as noisy guesses about a single right answer—like asking 100 people to rate a movie, then averaging to one score. This works if everyone broadly agrees.

But in the real world, people have genuinely different values. A doctor, a patient, and a lawyer watching a medical decision each want different things. Averaging their preferences gives no one what they want. A fair system should instead find a policy that respects all three perspectives at once—not by compromising everyone, but by actually honoring each group's core values.

Previous work showed this is theoretically possible using a concept called quantile fairness: imagine sorting people by their preferences and finding a solution that sits at the right spot on that line. But the method required solving a giant algebra problem with size equal to the number of possible situations and decisions. When you're training a real system, you don't know all situations in advance—you just see one situation at a time. No one knew how to make quantile fairness work in the real world.

In one sentence. Train a policy that respects multiple groups with conflicting values by building fair solutions directly from standard training, without solving impossible math.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect feedback from each group separately

Instead of mixing all preferences into one pile, keep them apart.

Train a policy for each group's values

Use standard training (the kind everyone already knows) once for each perspective.

Find the fair middle ground

At each decision point, see where each group's trained policy stands and pick the 'fair rank'—like the median.

Blend the trained policies into one new policy

Take the outputs from each group's policy and merge them so the combined policy follows the fair ranking.

The result respects all groups without huge computation

You've built fairness without needing to solve a giant algebra problem up front.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how a policy (a decision-making rule) is built: instead of one policy trained on all opinions mixed together, it trains multiple policies (one per group) and then blends them using fairness logic. The key move is doing this blending using standard training itself, so you never need to solve the big math problem directly.

the object it moves  =  policy aggregation method
The hidden quantity

Quantile rank: where each group stands

measured quantity  =  quantile rank of each group

When a policy must make a decision, each group's trained policy suggests an action. The paper measures where each group's preferred action sits in the ranking—is it first choice, last choice, somewhere in the middle? This rank is the hidden quantity.

The paper uses quantile fairness, which means: find a policy where each group's preferred action sits at a fair rank (like the median—the middle ground). This is different from averaging, because the actual policy follows one group's preference at each step, not a blurred compromise. The paper estimates this rank by training group-specific policies and seeing how they score the same actions.

Why it works

How blending with fairness actually works

The mechanism has two steps. First, train a separate policy for each group using standard reward-based nudging (the normal training everyone does). Each policy learns what that group values. Second, the paper uses a clever trick: use standard training one more time, but this time the reward comes from checking whether the combined policy respects the fair quantile rank.

This works because standard training is simply nudging the policy toward higher reward, one small step at a time. The paper designs the reward to be high when the combined policy places each group's preference at the fair rank (e.g. the median). When training nudges the policy to chase that reward signal, each nudge automatically pushes the policy toward fairness—because the reward itself is measuring fairness. No giant algebra problem needed; instead, the usual training mechanism (following the reward signal) naturally builds a fair policy.

Math after the picture

The fairness score

First in words:

reward for quantile fairness equals the sum of how well each group's preference sits at the fair rank, minus a cost for deviating from any single group too much

Then the compact form:

R_quantile = sum_i (alpha_i * score_group_i(policy)) - lambda * max_i |preference_i - fair_rank|

Every symbol:

score_group_i(policy)how well group i's preferred action ranks in the combined policy's output
alpha_iweight or importance assigned to group i
lambdahow strongly we enforce fairness (higher = stricter)
fair_rankthe quantile rank we want each group to occupy (e.g. 0.5 for median)
preference_iwhere group i's action actually sits in the ranking
A tiny worked example
Say we have 3 groups and the fair rank is the median (50th percentile).
Group A prefers action 2, which ranks 1st in the combined policy (rank = 100th percentile). Deviation from median: |100 - 50| = 50.
Group B prefers action 4, which ranks 3rd (rank = 60th percentile). Deviation: |60 - 50| = 10.
Group C prefers action 1, which ranks 2nd (rank = 75th percentile). Deviation: |75 - 50| = 25.
Max deviation = 50. With lambda = 0.1, fairness cost = 0.1 * 50 = 5.
If each group scores around 0.7 and alpha = 1 per group, R = 3 * 0.7 - 5 = 2.1 - 5 = -2.9.
During training, nudging toward higher R pulls each group's preferred action closer to the median, reducing fairness cost.

Standard training follows this reward (the gradient), naturally pushing the combined policy toward a state where all groups sit at their fair rank without ever solving algebra directly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Groups with very different preference strengths. If one group cares intensely (high alpha) and another barely cares (low alpha), the fairness target may not honor both fairly. The method works best when groups have comparable stakes in the decision.
The fair quantile is set incorrectly. If you pick the 75th percentile (heavily favor the majority) instead of the 50th percentile (true median), you build in bias. Choosing the right quantile is a values decision, not a technical one.
One group's policy is much weaker than others. If group B's policy learns to rank actions poorly (bad training), its preference scores will be low no matter what the combined policy does. The method respects bad policies equally.
Done right: use the method when you have multiple stakeholders of similar influence, train each group's policy well, and pick a quantile that matches your fairness values.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny decision environment with 3 agents and 3 conflicting value functions (e.g. an autonomous car that must balance safety, efficiency, and comfort). Train a group-specific policy for each agent's values. Then apply the quantile fairness method to create a combined policy. The demo should show: (1) each group's policy ranking a different action first, (2) the combined policy's action settling at the fair quantile (e.g. each group ranks the chosen action in the middle place), and (3) training time being far smaller than solving the algebra problem directly.

Fairness violation = sum_i |actual_rank_i - fair_quantile| / number_of_groups

The demo must make it impossible to miss that the combined policy respects all three groups at once without taking hundreds of hours to compute, while a naive average or algebra-solver would either blur everyone's values or grind to a halt.
