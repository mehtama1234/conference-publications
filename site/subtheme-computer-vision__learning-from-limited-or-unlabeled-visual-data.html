Learning from limited or unlabeled visual data

Training vision models without needing huge amounts of labeled examples, using tricks like self-supervision, transfer learning, and learning from a few examples.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Collecting and labeling images is expensive and slow. To train a detector, you need thousands or millions of labeled examples, each annotated by hand — a person draws boxes around objects and labels them. But often you have lots of unlabeled images and only a handful of labeled ones. Or you have a task where labels do not exist and would be hard to define. How can you learn from limited or unlabeled data? This is not just a cost-cutting problem; it is about enabling computer vision for domains where labeling is hard or impossible, like medical imaging where each label requires a specialist.

The challenge is that neural networks are data-hungry. A model trained on ten labeled examples will overfit: it will memorize those examples rather than learn the general pattern. But unlabeled data contains signal — the distribution of images in the real world. Can you extract that signal without explicit labels? One idea is self-supervision: create labels automatically by solving a pretext task. For instance, split an image into patches, shuffle them, and train the model to reassemble them. The model learns useful features as a side effect. Another idea is transfer learning: pre-train a model on a large labeled dataset (like ImageNet), then fine-tune it on your small labeled set for your specific task. The pre-training gives the model a head start. A third idea is few-shot learning: given just a few examples of a new class, can the model learn to recognize it? This requires learning how to learn, a form of meta-learning.

Papers in this subtheme explore several directions. Some use contrastive learning: learn representations by making similar images (same object, different angles) close in embedding space and different objects far apart, without explicit class labels. Others use pseudo-labeling: make predictions on unlabeled data, keep the confident ones as labels, and retrain. Some use data augmentation: apply random transformations (crop, rotate, color shift) to images, assuming the label stays the same, so one image becomes many training examples. Others use semi-supervised learning: train on labeled and unlabeled data at the same time, using unlabeled data to constrain the shape of decision boundaries. A few use knowledge distillation: if you have a large pre-trained model, train a smaller model to imitate it, transferring knowledge even without labeled data. The goal is the same across all approaches: make computer vision work when labels are scarce.

How the work breaks down

When you have few or no labels, vision models must learn differently. Some papers tackle anomaly detection when you have only normal images. Others focus on recognizing new classes after seeing just a handful of examples. Many use self-supervision: they create their own labels from the raw image data. Others learn across modalities—like matching images to text—without explicit supervision. Finally, some take a pre-trained model and adapt it cleverly to new tasks. These five directions reflect a common challenge: how to extract learning signal when human labels are scarce or expensive.

Anomaly Detection from One Image

Detecting anomalies—finding what is wrong or unusual—is hard because normal examples are common but anomalies are rare and diverse. You cannot label all the things that could go wrong. Most anomaly detectors need to see many examples of the specific anomaly they will detect later. But what if you only have images of normal, defect-free objects? That is the core problem: can you learn what is abnormal by only seeing what is normal?

The anomaly detection paper shows that you can achieve excellent results—96.7% detection rate on standard benchmarks—using just a single anomaly-free image as your training data. A model trained on millions of natural images already knows how to recognize textures, edges, and object parts, so you do not need to fine-tune exhaustively. You only need to adapt it slightly to this specific object. The hallucinatory images paper takes a related approach for vision-language models. It recognizes that large multi-modal models sometimes produce incorrect image descriptions—hallucinations—by showing them incorrect examples and pushing them away in a high-dimensional representation space (a mathematical space where concepts live), a way of teaching the model what it should not do without needing full retraining. Both papers work around data scarcity by using what the model already knows.

Few-Shot Recognition and Incremental Learning

Few-shot learning asks: if you show a model five examples of a new object class, can it learn to recognize that object? This is hard because traditional neural networks need hundreds or thousands of examples to avoid memorizing. The challenge becomes sharper in incremental settings where you learn new classes over time—the model must remember old classes while quickly absorbing new ones without access to old training data.

The Point-UQ paper tackles this for 3D shapes, working with point clouds rather than 2D images. It uses uncertainty quantification—a way of measuring how confident the model should be—to protect what the model already knows about base classes while learning new categories. By estimating which predictions are uncertain, it avoids overwriting old knowledge. The zero-shot learning paper addresses a different angle: even without seeing examples of a target class, can you classify it by using descriptions of its attributes? It improves classification by balancing distinct classes and similar ones through theory-guided choices. The cross-domain few-shot paper improves CLIP, a vision-language model, for few-shot adaptation on new tasks. Its surprising finding is that removing certain tokens—the tail tokens that encode rare details—actually helps the model generalize to new domains by focusing on core visual concepts rather than domain-specific quirks. All three papers share a meta-insight: work with what you have already learned, do not start from scratch.

Self-Supervised and Semi-Supervised Representation Learning

Self-supervised and semi-supervised learning flip the labeling problem. Instead of needing a human to label every image, you create labels from the data itself or combine the few labels you have with the many unlabeled images. The idea is to train on both labeled and unlabeled data, so unlabeled images contribute to learning without explicit annotations.

The referring expression segmentation paper tackles a vision-language task where the goal is to segment image regions based on natural language descriptions like 'the person on the left,' which is hard because these pixel-level descriptions require skilled annotators to draw precise masks. The paper shows how to work with sparse labels through pseudo-labeling: the model makes confident predictions on unlabeled data, treats those predictions as temporary labels, and retrains on them. A reinforced self-evolving loop improves both the model and the pseudo-labels together, so each iteration gets better. The anatomy-aware ultrasound paper is domain-specific: medical ultrasound images are hard to label because they require expert radiologists, but self-supervised learning can find useful features without labels by learning image structure specific to anatomy. The fine-grained visual classification paper combines vision and language: it learns part-level representations by using text descriptions of parts to guide the learning, so the model understands not just 'dog' but 'dog ears' and 'dog snout' without needing dense part labels. The flare removal paper uses contrastive learning—a technique where you pull similar images together and push different images apart in a mathematical representation space—in a semi-supervised way, mixing labeled data with unlabeled night images to remove optical artifacts more reliably. All four papers use unlabeled or partially labeled data to reduce annotation burden.

Learning Without Motion or Modality Labels

Some tasks have no natural labels at all because the task itself is unusual. How do you label temporal correspondence—which pixels in frame 2 match frame 1? How do you label optical flow—the apparent motion of pixels—across different imaging modalities? How do you label geographic location from a single image when you have no GPS? These tasks require learning from structure or consistency without explicit human annotations.

The video object-centric learning paper learns to track objects across video frames without learning motion labels explicitly. Instead of predicting 'where the object moves,' it learns correspondence—which parts match from frame to frame—by using consistency constraints. Objects that are the same across frames should have the same representation. The unsupervised cross-modal flow paper addresses optical flow estimation between images from different sources, like RGB and thermal. Traditional flow methods rely on labeled matched pairs, but this paper learns flow by decoupling the estimation into parts and enforcing positional constraints across the two domains, so no paired labels are needed. The 3D understanding from video paper creates artificial supervision by shuffling patches in video frames and asking the model to reorder them, a pretext task that teaches 3D awareness as a side effect. The hierarchical geolocation paper tackles the inverse problem: given one image, where on Earth is it? It uses a hyperbolic embedding space—a way of representing hierarchies mathematically where geographic regions are organized by containment (Mumbai inside India inside South Asia)—to organize locations, so the model learns relationships without explicit regional labels. The uncertainty-aware geo-localization paper improves cross-dataset transfer by using a probabilistic model that does not push away all negative examples equally; some negatives are just from a different region of the same country, while others are opposite sides of the world, and the model should treat them differently. All five papers extract learning signal from task structure rather than human labeling.

Adapting Pre-Trained Models to New Tasks

Pre-trained models encode knowledge about images or speech learned from huge labeled datasets. The practical problem is: how do you adapt this knowledge to a new task or new data without expensive retraining? The challenge is that fine-tuning on small labeled sets can hurt generalization or be inefficient.

The contrastive learning for phenotyping paper demonstrates that you can build highly efficient task-specific models by using asymmetric objectives—treating the labeled and unlabeled data differently during contrastive training—achieving 10x smaller models with better performance. The pseudo-labeling for speech paper speeds up adaptation by using confidence-aware pseudo-labels on unlabeled speech, halving training time while improving reliability on audio from unfamiliar speakers or conditions. The negation understanding paper takes CLIP, a vision-language model, and shows that for specific reasoning tasks like understanding negation—'not a dog'—you do not need to retrain the whole model; strategic prompting and pushing incorrect examples away in a representation space can boost accuracy from 25% to 67%. The 4D reconstruction paper learns to interpolate dynamic scenes from just two images by leveraging pre-trained 2D understanding, showing that combining self-supervised spatial constraints with pre-training is more sample-efficient than training from scratch. The Vision Transformer finetuning paper challenges a common assumption: smooth optimization is not always better for finetuning. By allowing attention and feedforward layers to evolve more freely—non-smooth changes—models generalize better on downstream tasks, suggesting that pre-trained smoothness can actually constrain adaptation. All five papers show that smart adaptation beats naive fine-tuning.
