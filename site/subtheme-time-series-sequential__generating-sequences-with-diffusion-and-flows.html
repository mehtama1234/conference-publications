Generating sequences with diffusion and flows

Using modern generative models—diffusion, flow matching, and neural ODEs—to synthesize realistic time series data or transform sequences for imputation and forecasting.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

In recent years, a new family of generative models has emerged—diffusion and flow matching—that work well at creating realistic samples from complex distributions. These were first applied to images, where they produce photo-quality pictures. Now researchers are applying the same approach to sequences. The idea is to learn the underlying distribution of realistic sequences, then sample from it. This is useful in two ways: you can generate synthetic sequences for testing or training when real data is scarce, and you can fill in gaps in incomplete sequences by sampling likely continuations.

Why is this different from traditional forecasting? Traditional forecasting is deterministic or gives you a probability distribution over next values. Generative models, in contrast, learn the entire shape of realistic sequences. They can capture multimodal behavior—the possibility that a sequence might go up or down with roughly equal probability—in a natural way. They can also be guided or conditioned on partial information. For example, if you have a sequence with missing middle values, you can use these models to fill the gaps while respecting the overall shape and endpoints. And they tend to learn more diverse patterns because they are optimized to generate plausible sequences, not just the most likely one.

Papers in this area adapt diffusion and neural ODE (ordinary differential equation) models to sequences. Some condition on metadata—like 'this is a factory sensor, not a stock price'—so the model generates genre-appropriate sequences because different types of data have different statistical properties, and mixing them confuses the model. Others use these models as a bridge between datasets: train on one hospital's data, generate synthetic sequences from that distribution, then fine-tune on a new hospital's real data, which reduces the domain shift problem because you have learned a shared representation. Flow matching, a newer approach that is mathematically simpler than diffusion, is also being adapted to sequences because it is easier to scale and sometimes produces higher-quality samples.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five natural directions: building generative diffusion models that capture what realistic sequences look like across the range of possibilities, using flow matching and neural ODEs to learn smooth transformations between data patterns, identifying and tracking the hidden variables that drive observable sequences, adapting these models across different domains and data sources, and handling messy real-world time series with irregular observations and multiple possible continuations.

Generative diffusion for sequences

The core challenge here is learning what realistic sequences look like so you can sample new ones that are plausible. Traditional forecasting predicts the single most likely next value, but real sequences often have multimodal behavior—the system could plausibly go up or down, and you want to generate diverse realistic options. Diffusion models solve this by learning the entire distribution of sequences through a noising-and-denoising process: you start with real sequences, gradually add noise until they are unrecognizable, then learn to reverse this process. Once trained, you can generate new sequences by sampling random noise and denoising it.

Aurora takes this idea across multiple domains and data modalities at once, showing that a single generative model can handle time series from factories, stock markets, weather stations, and other sources without retraining, even on benchmarks it has never seen before. GTM follows a similar generative path but focuses on improving how the model encodes sequences and how it scales, so the model learns a richer understanding of what makes a sequence realistic. The SDE-Induced Continuous-Discrete Variational Inference paper tackles irregular time series—data where measurements arrive at random times instead of fixed intervals—by treating the problem as sampling from a learned stochastic differential equation, which is a continuous mathematical model of how the sequence evolves. This allows the model to handle missing data and uneven sampling naturally. HELIX also addresses missing values by learning an identity for each channel (column) in the multivariate time series, aligning hidden structure to actual physical properties, so the imputation respects cross-variable relationships. CURE generates synthetic time series under single-domain constraints by progressively expanding the generative process, allowing a model trained on one factory's data to generate diverse new sequences that respect that factory's statistical signature.

These papers agree on the core insight: diffusion-based generation captures multimodal uncertainty better than deterministic forecasting, because it learns the full probability distribution rather than just the single most likely value. They disagree on scope—some handle all domains at once, others specialize in imputation or irregular data. Together they show that diffusion is becoming the standard toolkit for generating diverse, realistic sequences.

Flow matching and improved neural ODEs

Flow matching is a newer approach than diffusion that is mathematically simpler and often faster to train, because you solve a simpler optimization problem (learning a single velocity field rather than a sequence of denoisers). Instead of learning to denoise noisy sequences, flow matching learns a velocity field that smoothly transports noise into data. The appeal is two-fold: the math is cleaner (you can use simpler training objectives), and the generated samples are often higher quality because the transport is more direct. Neural ODEs (ordinary differential equations) generalize recurrent networks by letting a neural network define how a sequence evolves continuously in time, rather than in discrete steps. These two ideas—flow matching and neural ODEs—are natural partners for time series, where smooth continuous evolution makes intuitive sense.

ConvT3 extends state-space models with structured weights that span far into the past or across space to capture long-range dependencies in video and physical simulations. Dynamic Fractal Mamba combines Mamba (a linear-complexity architecture) with ideas from physics about scale invariance—the idea that patterns repeat at different time scales, which means if you train on hourly data the model learns patterns that also work on daily or weekly data, so it generalizes to time scales it has never seen. Shift-Tolerant Allocation uses conditional diffusion to generate regime-aware return distributions for portfolio management, capturing macro cycles while preserving stock-specific patterns, because markets behave differently in high-inflation versus low-inflation regimes, and the model needs to know which regime it is in to generate appropriate predictions. CoGenCast couples a step-by-step prediction model with a flow-based generative model, allowing joint sampling for multimodal forecasting and unified training across different data sources, which is valuable when you want to forecast multiple possible futures at once. KineFlow applies second-order flow matching to time series, treating velocities and accelerations as part of the model, and achieves strong improvements on real-world forecasting benchmarks because it explicitly models how fast and how the rate of change evolves, which captures momentum. Time-Gated Multi-Scale Flow Matching for imputation applies flow matching specifically to missing-value reconstruction, balancing speed and quality by adapting the flow at multiple scales, so details and coarse structure are filled in appropriately.

These papers show that flow matching is quickly replacing diffusion as the preferred generative approach for sequences because it trains faster and samples cleaner, which matters when you need high-quality results efficiently. Combined with ideas from neural ODEs and physics-inspired architectures, flow matching enables both deterministic forecasting and diverse generative sampling in a unified framework, which is valuable because you can use the same learned model for point predictions when you need a single answer and for uncertainty quantification when you need to know the range of possible futures.

Learning hidden dynamics from observations

A core problem in time series is that you observe only surface-level measurements, but the true dynamics happen in a hidden space you cannot see. A factory sensor reads temperature, but the underlying physics involves pressure, material state, and many other variables you do not directly see. Learning hidden dynamics means finding a lower-dimensional space where a simple model explains the observations, and then using that simple model to forecast, interpolate, or generate new sequences. This is hard because you need to simultaneously learn what the hidden space is and what the dynamics are in that space.

Learning linear state-space models with sparse structure solves this by finding a sparse linear model in the hidden space—sparse meaning most connections between hidden variables are zero—which preserves what connects to what and improves prediction accuracy by avoiding overfitting. Maximum-Likelihood Learning of Hidden Dynamics Without Reconstruction recovers physically grounded hidden states directly from noisy video without trying to reconstruct the image pixels, focusing instead on the hidden state trajectory. Micro-Macro Coupled Koopman Modeling uses Koopman theory, a mathematical framework for turning nonlinear dynamics into linear ones, to predict traffic flow by bridging microscopic driver behavior with macroscopic flow patterns. SONATA handles temporal tensor streams—data that changes over time and has structure across multiple dimensions—by using adaptive coreset selection to focus learning on the most informative time windows. Efficient Learning of Deep State Space Models via Importance Smoothing trains state-space models much faster by reweighting which time steps matter most during optimization, matching existing quality but 10x faster. Interpretable Functional Koopman Learning reconstructs full spatial fields at arbitrary resolutions using low-dimensional hidden dynamics, enabling stable long-horizon simulation by learning a non-Markovian closure (a way to complete the model using historical context).

All these papers assume hidden dynamics are simpler than the observations, whether linear, Koopman-linearizable, or sparse. They disagree on the structure—some use tensors, others use Koopman, others emphasize sparsity. Collectively, they demonstrate that finding and exploiting hidden structure is key to making long-horizon predictions and generalizing to new scenarios.

Domain adaptation and causal forecasting

A practical challenge is that models trained on one source of data often fail on a new source, even if the underlying patterns are similar. A hospital trained its time series model on Patient Population A, but new patients from Population B have different baseline distributions. Portfolio optimization trained on 1990s stock data fails in 2020s high-inflation regimes. Solving domain shift requires either generating synthetic data to bridge the gap, or learning a causal model that captures what changes between domains and what stays the same.

Shift-Tolerant Allocation uses conditional diffusion to generate regime-aware return distributions by explicitly modeling macro regimes (high inflation, low inflation, crisis) as conditioning variables, so the model knows when it enters a different regime and can shift its distribution accordingly while keeping firm-specific stock patterns intact—this matters because inflation-regime patterns fail when the regime changes. CausalX learns a causally-interpretable graph of relationships in multimodal spatio-temporal data, producing not just forecasts but also interpretable causal edges showing which variables influence which, which helps identify what changed when moving to a new domain. DoFlow uses flow-based generative models to enable causal reasoning in time series, supporting not only observational forecasting but also counterfactual queries—what would happen if we had intervened to change an event—and principled anomaly detection by identifying sequences far from the learned distribution, because generative models give you a density estimate that reveals what is unlikely. Laplace Diffusion applies Laplace mechanics in diffusion models to irregular multivariate time series, improving long-horizon forecasting by learning the geometric shape of the data and using that to guide predictions, so the model respects the overall structure of the space.

These papers recognize that forecasting under domain shift is fundamentally different from pure prediction—you need to either model what changes (regimes) or learn causal relationships. They show that generative models enable causal reasoning that point forecasts cannot provide, because when you know the full distribution you can reason about what-if scenarios and distinguish correlation from causation.

Handling irregular, multimodal, and sparse observations

Real-world time series are often messy: measurements arrive at irregular intervals, multiple channels have missing values, and there are multiple plausible ways to fill gaps. Clinical data has sparse vital signs at random times. IoT sensors fail and restart. Weather data is missing over oceans. Traditional models assume regular sampling and break down with irregular data. Generative models handle this naturally—you can condition on the observed measurements you have and sample plausible values for the missing parts.

The SDE-Induced paper and HELIX appear in the diffusion group, but we highlight them here for their specific strength: the SDE model handles arbitrary observation intervals by learning continuous hidden dynamics, and HELIX learns a per-channel identity that aligns to actual physical structure, both enabling natural imputation. Diff-MN (Diffusion with Mixture-of-Experts Neural Continuous Differential Equations) handles irregular time series by mixing multiple neural conditional differential equation experts based on the diffusion timestep, allowing the model to switch strategies depending on whether it is filling in fine details or coarse structure. DualTimesField rethinks time series as continuous-time trends with discrete events, achieving much lower reconstruction error than models that treat all time steps as identical, because it models continuous change and discrete jumps separately. Learning Coupled Continuous-Time Dynamics from Irregular Events captures how variables depend on each other from sparse irregular observations by working entirely in continuous time, generalizing across prediction, generation, and behavior modeling. MoRGen (Mixture-of-Resolutions Generative) forecasts irregular medical time series at different scales depending on sampling rate, using a mixture model to combine predictions at coarse and fine scales.

These papers share the insight that irregular data requires flexible modeling—either continuous-time hidden dynamics, explicit modeling of missing patterns, or multi-resolution approaches. They show that treating irregularity as a feature rather than a problem opens new modeling possibilities.
