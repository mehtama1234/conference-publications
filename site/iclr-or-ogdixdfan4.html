COMI: Context Compression via Information Gain

To compress long documents for language models, keep the parts most relevant to the question and discard overlapping copies.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

When a language model reads a very long document, it slows down and gets confused by duplicate information. COMI solves this by measuring each piece of text on two scales: how useful it is for answering the question, and how much it overlaps with other pieces. The method drops the overlapping parts and adjusts how much to compress each section based on this measurement.

Zero assumption version

Long contexts overwhelm the model

Language models read one token (a small piece of text or a word) at a time. When the document is very long, the model has to look at every token and compare it to every other token to decide what is important. With a long document, this comparison step becomes extremely slow and memory-hungry.

A naive approach is to compress blindly, cutting out parts of the document without knowing which parts matter for the question. This loses critical information. A better naive approach is to keep only parts that sound related to the question, because the model's attention is drawn to query-related pieces. But natural language is repetitive: the same idea appears many times with slightly different wording. So you end up keeping similar parts and still storing the same information twice, wasting space even after compression.

In one sentence. Keep the parts most relevant to the question, and merge the overlapping ones.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes how compression rates are assigned. Instead of compressing all sections equally, or compressing based only on relevance, COMI assigns each section its own compression rate based on a score called Marginal Information Gain. Sections that score high keep more tokens; sections that score low lose more tokens.

the object it moves  =  Compression rates per section
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Marginal Information Gain

measured quantity  =  Marginal Information Gain (MIG)

MIG is a score for each piece of text, defined as the relevance of that piece to the input query, minus how much it overlaps with other pieces. Relevance is measured by how much the model's attention focuses on that piece when answering the question. Redundancy (overlap) is measured by how similar the piece is to other pieces in meaning.

The paper uses MIG at two levels: between groups (to set compression rates), and within each group (to decide which tokens to merge). In both cases, high MIG means the piece is worth keeping.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

The model's attention mechanism naturally focuses only on a small subset of tokens relevant to the query. If you compress without knowing which tokens those are, you risk deleting the ones the model depends on to answer the question. By measuring relevance via attention patterns, the method preserves the tokens the model actually needs.

Natural language is repetitive. Multiple sentences express the same idea with different words. If you keep only high-relevance tokens without checking for redundancy, you keep many similar copies of the same concept. By measuring redundancy and subtracting it from relevance, the method identifies tokens that are both important and unique. This means fewer tokens are kept overall, but no information is lost because the surviving tokens do not repeat.

By setting compression rates per group rather than globally, the method adapts to the structure of the text. Questions and definitions might be dense with unique information, so they keep most tokens. Supporting examples might be redundant, so they compress more. This fine-tuning under the same compression budget preserves more signal.

The formula

First in words:

Marginal Information Gain = Relevance of a unit to the query minus Semantic overlap of that unit with other units

Then the compact form:

MIG_i = Relevance_i - Redundancy_i

Every symbol:

MIG_imarginal information gain score for unit i
Relevance_ihow much the model attends to unit i when answering the query
Redundancy_ihow much unit i overlaps in meaning with other units in the context
A tiny worked example
Suppose group A: relevance 90%, redundancy 20%. MIG = 90 - 20 = 70.
Group B: relevance 60%, redundancy 50%. MIG = 60 - 50 = 10.
Group A scores much higher because it is both relevant AND unique.
Group A gets assigned lower compression (keep more tokens); Group B compresses harder.
Under a 32x compression budget, Group A might keep 3% of tokens, Group B only 0.1%, balancing relevance and redundancy loss.

Subtracting redundancy from relevance ensures we keep tokens that are both important and non-repetitive.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Redundancy measurement may be noisy. If two pieces of text say the same thing in very different wording, the redundancy score might miss the overlap. This means you keep both copies by accident.
Grouping by fixed size is rigid. The coarse stage divides the document into equal-sized chunks. A chunk might cross a semantic boundary, so the group contains mixed high- and low-value content. The method then assigns one compression rate to both, which is suboptimal.
High compression rates expose sensitivity. When compressing by 32x or more, small errors in MIG estimation compound. A token marked as low-redundancy but actually redundant will be kept and waste space.
Done right. Use embeddings (dense vectors representing meaning) to measure redundancy rather than word overlap. Estimate groups by looking at semantic breaks, not just fixed size. Validate MIG scores on a held-out set before committing to compression.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple experiment: take a long document (e.g., a Wikipedia article) and a factual question about it. Compress the document to 1/32 its original size using only relevance (naive), then using COMI (relevance minus redundancy). Feed both compressed versions to a language model and ask the question. Measure the accuracy of the answer.

Exact Match accuracy = (model answers correctly with compressed context) / (total questions)

The demo must make it obvious that COMI preserves the tokens the model needs for answering the question (by selecting high MIG tokens) and avoids redundant copies (by subtracting overlap), so the model reads 32 times fewer tokens but still answers the question correctly.
