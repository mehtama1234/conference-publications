Representation-as-a-Judge: Small Models Evaluate via Hidden States

Small language models can judge the quality of answers just by looking at their internal thoughts, without needing to write or speak.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Evaluating answer quality usually means asking a big expensive language model to produce a written judgment. But this paper discovers something surprising: tiny models already know how to judge, hidden deep inside their layers. The paper turns this into a practical tool called INSPECTOR that listens to what small models think internally, rather than waiting for them to generate words out loud.

Zero assumption version

Judging answers is expensive and opaque

Today, when we want to know if a model's answer is good, we ask a large language model to score it — the LLM-as-a-Judge approach. But large models are slow, costly, and their judgments are hard to understand or trust.

Why not use tiny models instead? They are fast and cheap. But tiny models are bad at writing — they stumble, they miss words, they make grammatical errors. People naturally assumed that if a model cannot write well, it cannot judge well either.

The misconception. Small models cannot judge well because they cannot generate good text.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Get ground truth.

Run a large language model judge on the same (prompt, answer) pair and get aspect scores like logicality, fluency, consistency (1–5 for each).

Extract small model's thoughts.

Feed the prompt and answer to a small model. Capture its internal layer-by-layer representations (the numbers in each neuron) as it reads through the text.

Identify informative layers.

Test which layers inside the small model carry the strongest prediction signal for each aspect. Mid-to-upper layers usually work best.

Train lightweight classifiers.

Use simple linear classifiers on top of those layer signals to predict the aspect scores the large model gave.

Deploy the judge.

For a new answer, extract representations from the small model and feed them through the trained classifiers to get fast, interpretable scores.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of asking the model to generate a judgment word-by-word (decoding), the paper reads the model's internal thoughts before any words come out.

the object it moves  =  decoding -> internal representations
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Evaluative signal in layers

measured quantity  =  evaluative signal strength per layer

This is the amount of evaluation-relevant information hiding in the neurons at each depth of the model. It is not visible in the model's written output, but it is there in the numbers. The paper measures it by training a simple classifier on each layer separately and measuring how well that layer alone can predict the aspect scores.

Mid-to-upper layers contain strong evaluative signals: a classifier trained on those layers can predict aspect scores with high accuracy. The output layer is weak because it is optimized for generation, not judgment. This shows that evaluation information builds up throughout the model, not just at the end.

Why it works

Why it works

Here is the core insight: evaluating an answer is an easier task than generating a good written judgment. Generating requires the model to know what words to pick, spell them correctly, construct grammar, and order them sensibly. Evaluating requires the model to recognize patterns of logicality, fluency, consistency — patterns that are much simpler.

Because evaluation is simpler, the model can solve it in its internal representations much earlier than in its external words. Mid-layer neurons start encoding judgment signals (logicality, fluency) as the model reads through the text. By the time the model reaches the output stage, those signals are still present in the intermediate layers, but they get drowned out because the output layer is optimized for word generation, not scoring.

INSPECTOR sidesteps generation entirely. It trains classifiers to read the mid-layer signals directly, before they are lost to the output process. Since those signals were built to solve a simpler task, they are cleaner and more reliable than the generated words.

The scoring equation

First in words:

For each aspect, a simple linear classifier takes the layer representations and outputs a score. For each response sample, extract the mean and last-token hidden representations from each layer, compute attention-entropy statistics, pool them, and concatenate features from selected layers.

Then the compact form:

score_aspect = bias + sum_j weight_j * feature_j(layer_representations)

Every symbol:

score_aspectpredicted aspect score (1-5)
feature_jthe j-th extracted feature from layer representations
weight_jthe learned importance of that feature
biasthe default score before reading any features
layer_representationshidden neuron values at selected layers as the model reads the text
A tiny worked example
Suppose we extract 4 features from a small model: F1=3.2 (mean pooling from layer 7), F2=0.8 (attention entropy), F3=2.1 (last token norm), F4=1.5 (variance).
The classifier learned: score = 0.5 + 0.6*F1 + 0.2*F2 + 0.3*F3 + 0.1*F4.
Prediction: 0.5 + 0.6*3.2 + 0.2*0.8 + 0.3*2.1 + 0.1*1.5 = 0.5 + 1.92 + 0.16 + 0.63 + 0.15 = 3.36.
Rounded to 3-4 on a 1-5 scale. The large judge said 3, so INSPECTOR was close and fast.

The classifier simply reads the layer signals and outputs a number; it needs almost no computation compared to decoding words.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The small model must see the answer. INSPECTOR only works if the small model can encode judgment signals in its representations. If the task is too far outside what the small model was trained to understand, its layers will be silent. For example, a small model trained only on code may not encode judgments about poetry quality.
The large judge must be reliable. The ground-truth scores come from the large judge. If the large judge is biased (e.g., always favors verbose answers), INSPECTOR will learn to copy that bias, not correct it.
Classifiers trained on imbalanced data may fail. If the training data is imbalanced (mostly 5-star answers, few 1-star ones), the classifier learns to recognize good answers but struggles to spot bad ones. Downsampling helps by using equal numbers of each score, but it can lose some examples from scores that were rare.
Done right: use a balanced dataset. Collect aspect-level scores across the full range (1-5) in equal amounts, or use downsampling during training to create balance. Validate on a test set with natural proportions to catch bias.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple evaluation task: collect 50 answers to a reasoning question (e.g., a math word problem from GSM8K). Ask a large language model to score each answer on fluency (1-5). Extract representations from a small model (e.g., Llama-2-7B) at layers 16, 20, 24 as it reads each answer. Train a linear classifier on 40 answers to predict the fluency score from layer 20 alone. Evaluate on the held-out 10 answers and report accuracy.

accuracy = (number of predictions within 1 point of large judge) / 10

The demo should show that the small model's mid-layer signals alone can match a large judge's judgment, without generating any words.
