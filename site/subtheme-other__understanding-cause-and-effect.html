Understanding cause and effect

Papers studying causal relationships in data—what causes what, how to infer hidden causes, and how to design experiments and systems that account for causality.

What this is

Correlation is not causation — this is the oldest lesson in statistics, yet it is still the hardest to apply. If you notice that ice cream sales are high when swimming pool drownings are high, correlation is real, but neither causes the other — both happen because it is summer. Yet if you see that people who take a drug get healthier, you need to know: does the drug make them healthier, or are healthier people more likely to seek out that drug? In a real-world system, you observe correlations everywhere, but the causal relationships are hidden. You need to figure out which variables actually influence which others, and how much.

The challenge is that in most real data, you cannot run experiments. You see what happened, but you do not see what would have happened if you changed one thing. If a student scores well on a test, you see that fact, but you never see what score the same student would have gotten if they had studied a different amount. To infer causation from pure observation, you need clever reasoning and assumptions about the underlying structure of how the world works — for example, assuming that grades depend on study time, that study time depends on motivation, and that motivation is not directly affected by test scores.

Researchers use graphical models to map out cause-and-effect relationships, identify which relationships they can and cannot infer from data, and design experiments or observations that reveal causation. Some use causal diagrams that show which variables influence which, then use data to estimate the strength of those influences. Some use techniques from do-calculus, a formal system for reasoning about what would happen if you forced a particular action (for example, if you forced someone to study more). The goal is to distinguish what-if questions (if we change X, does Y change?) from mere correlation.

How the work breaks down

Work on causal inference splits into five directions: discovering causal structure from observations, handling hidden confounders and incomplete data, estimating treatment effects when you cannot run perfect experiments, applying causal reasoning to real systems like auctions and platforms, and using causal frameworks to build fairer and more accountable algorithms.

Discovering causal structure from data

The central puzzle in causal discovery is that you see correlations in data, but you do not see which variables actually cause which others. If you want to know which genes influence which diseases, which economic policies affect employment, or which user actions lead to higher engagement, you need an algorithm that can look at observations and figure out the underlying cause-and-effect wiring. The challenge is that many different causal structures can produce the same data, so you need clever assumptions and mathematical guarantees to pin down what you can and cannot infer.

Causal Discovery for Irregularly Time Series builds trust in structure recovery by giving theoretical proofs that an algorithm will find the right causal graph even when real-world data is messy—times between observations are uneven, values are missing. Causal Discovery via Quantile Partial Effect sidesteps some traditional assumptions and identifies causal direction through a simple test on real numbers, not complex mechanisms. Characterization and Learning of Causal Graphs extends this by handling latent confounders (hidden variables that affect multiple things) and post-treatment selection (when measuring one outcome affects what you can see), proving when you can and cannot identify the true structure. Embracing Discrete Search speeds up structure recovery by framing it as a search problem and achieving highly accurate reconstruction fast. Score-based Greedy Search for Structure Identification handles partially observed systems by working up to equivalence classes—groups of structures you cannot tell apart—and defining operators to search through the space efficiently. Together, these five papers form a toolkit for pulling causal structure out of observational data.

Inferring treatment effects in complex settings

Once you know the causal structure, you want to answer what-if questions: if we change one variable, how much do others change? This is the treatment effect problem. In real systems, you usually cannot run clean experiments—you observe the world as it is, not all the alternate worlds you could create. The hard part is accounting for confounding (when people who take a drug are healthier to begin with, so you cannot tell if the drug worked) and interference (when one person's treatment affects another person's outcomes).

On Measuring Influence in Avoiding Undesired Future reveals a surprising fact: a variable that stops bad futures may not have a large direct causal effect—sometimes weak causes or indirect routes are what matter most for control. A method for Doubly estimation of conditional quantile contrasts improves prediction by combining multiple statistical approaches, so if one approach breaks down you still get an accurate answer—it works across many different data scenarios. Matching without Group Barrier for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Estimation lets you compare similar units across different groups to estimate treatment effects for each subpopulation, not just on average. Modeling Covariate Transition for Efficient Estimation of Longitudinal Treatment Effects tackles long-term studies where the world changes over time—covariate values shift from period to period—and uses those shifts to extract more information from A/B tests. Modeling Interference for Treatment Effect Estimation in Network Dynamic Environment handles the fact that in networks, one person's treatment ripples through to affect others—the method estimates treatment effects even when the intervention on one person changes outcomes for their neighbors.

Causal inference applied to platforms and auctions

Real-world systems like online platforms and auctions face a causal inference puzzle: incentives change behavior. If a platform charges for API calls (counted by billable units), users adjust their strategy to game the system. If an ad auction has flawed rules, bidders stop participating honestly. To build systems that work well and fairly, you need to reason about how causality flows through incentives—what decision changes which behavior, and how does that ripple forward?

Is Your LLM Overcharging You shows that unit-based pricing (charging by billable units) creates a direct incentive to misreport usage. Changing to character-based pricing breaks that causal link by changing what you get paid for, eliminating the reason to cheat while keeping profit margins stable. ALM-MTA: Front-Door Causal Multi-Touch Attribution Method tackles the question of which user action caused an outcome when many actions happen before you measure it—by mapping the causal chain, the method untangles credit and drives engagement up 670% because it assigns credit to the right actions. Enhancing Affine Maximizer Auctions with Correlation-Aware Payment reveals that standard auction mechanisms fail catastrophically in some settings because they ignore how winners' values correlate—fixing the causal assumption improves revenue to the optimum because the mechanism now adjusts payments correctly. On the Coordination of Value-Maximizing Bidders shows that when bidders coordinate their causal strategy (altering bids to reach spending limits), both the platform and the bidders win—better compliance and higher total value because coordinated bids reduce wasted spend and align incentives. Risk-Averse and Optimistic Advertiser Incentive Compatibility in Auto-bidding clarifies what incentives the Second-Price Auction actually creates when bidders face multiple conflicting goals, proving it aligns incentives correctly because the payment rule matches each bidder's preference structure.

Causal reasoning for safety and fairness

As machine learning systems take on more consequential tasks, two problems become urgent: you need to know if a model is making decisions fairly across groups, and you need to know if a model can be attacked or exploited. Both problems benefit from causal reasoning because they are not just about patterns in data—they are about relationships and accountability.

The Fairness Hierarchy shows how causal inference provides a framework to define different notions of fairness and test them on real data. By mapping out the causal structure of decisions, you can check whether a model treats similar people similarly, whether it accounts for historical bias, or whether its decisions follow causal rules. Theory of Minimal Weight Perturbations in Deep Networks reveals a causal insight about model vulnerability: very small changes to weights can activate hidden backdoors, and the paper establishes thresholds below which attacks fail and shows low-rank compression can trigger latent attacks reliably, connecting model structure to risk of attack.
