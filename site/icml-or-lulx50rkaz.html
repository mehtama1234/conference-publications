CoGenCast: Coupling LLMs with Flow Matching for Time Series

Use an LLM to understand context, then use flow matching to generate future values smoothly.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting means predicting what happens next in a stream of numbers (stock prices, temperature readings, sensor data). The hard part is balancing two opposite needs: understanding what the numbers mean (is this about weather or traffic?) and predicting the exact continuous movement over time (how much does temperature change each minute?). This paper couples a large language model (a model that processes text) with flow matching (a method that draws smooth paths through a data space) to solve both at once.

Zero assumption version

Why existing forecasting breaks

Time series are hard because they need two different kinds of modeling. First, understanding: a series of 100 numbers is meaningless without knowing its context. Is this electricity demand (rises in summer) or rainfall (rises in monsoon)? That context determines how you interpret and predict the next value. Second, the actual generation: once you know the context, you need to predict smooth, continuous change. A fixed threshold rule does not work — time evolves smoothly, and the pattern of change matters.

Current methods split into two camps. Language models (LLMs) excel at understanding context and the meaning in patterns — they read a long sequence and grasp patterns. But they generate the future autoregressive-token-by-token (one value at a time, in a chain), which treats time like discrete words, not continuous flow. Diffusion-style models do the opposite: they model smooth, continuous generation but have no natural way to understand what the time series is about. Neither method alone bridges both needs.

In one sentence. Decouple context understanding from continuous prediction: use an LLM to read context bidirectionally, then use flow matching to generate smooth future trajectories.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Read the sequence bidirectionally

LLM processes past values and sees ahead to extract semantic context (like understanding the overall trend or seasonal pattern).

Step 2: Modify how LLM processes the sequence

Change the LLM's mechanism for combining information so it can see both past and future at once during context encoding, not just left-to-right.

Step 3: Encode context into a representation

LLM outputs a single semantic representation that captures the pattern and meaning of the series.

Step 4: Condition flow matching on the representation

Flow matching uses the LLM representation to guide where and how smoothly the next values should move.

Step 5: Generate smooth future trajectory

Flow matching produces a continuous, smooth curve for future time steps that respects the contextual meaning.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes

The paper decouples the two jobs: instead of one model doing both context and generation, use an LLM for context (reading bidirectionally) and flow matching for smooth generation (conditioned on that context).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: semantic context representation

measured quantity  =  Semantic context representation

The hidden quantity is the semantic representation — the compact summary of what the time series means that the LLM extracts. It is not the individual values; it is the pattern-understanding. The LLM encodes this as a vector (a list of numbers) by reading the whole sequence bidirectionally.

The paper estimates this representation by running the LLM encoder on the full historical sequence. Normally the LLM processes values one at a time, left to right. The paper modifies this by letting each position in the sequence look both backward and forward (a technique called bidirectional context) so the LLM can see the entire pattern at once. The representation then conditions the flow-matching decoder: the flow paths are not generic, they are pulled toward regions consistent with that semantic meaning.

Why it works

Why the coupling works

The core insight is that time series have two separable facets: semantics (what the series is about) and dynamics (how it moves). An LLM is brilliant at semantics because it is trained to read long text and extract meaning from context. But autoregressive generation makes it treat time as tokens, discrete jumps — because when a model generates one token at a time and decides each token independently from the previous one, it creates discontinuous, step-like motion instead of smooth curves. Flow matching is designed for continuous dynamics — it can draw smooth, natural paths through a space. But it needs guidance on which direction to move and what the space represents.

By decoupling, you get both. The LLM reads the entire historical sequence bidirectionally (seeing the full context) and outputs a representation that says 'this series has these patterns and trends'. The flow-matching component then takes that representation and uses it as a steering signal: 'given that the series follows this pattern, here is the smooth path the next values should take.' The flow paths are not generic; they are tuned to the semantic meaning extracted by the LLM.

The equation

First in words:

Loss equals flow matching loss, regularized by how well the generated future aligns with the LLM context representation. The flow paths are steered toward trajectories consistent with the semantic meaning.

Then the compact form:

L_flow + lambda * D_KL( p_flow(x_future | z_context), p_semantic(x_future | z_context) )

Every symbol:

L_flowflow matching loss, measures smoothness and path quality
lambdastrength of semantic alignment (how much context steers generation)
D_KLdivergence (distance between two probability distributions)
x_futurethe future values we want to predict
z_contextsemantic representation from the LLM encoder
p_flowprobability distribution drawn by flow matching
p_semanticdistribution aligned with LLM semantic meaning
A tiny worked example
Suppose the LLM extracts a representation saying 'this is an upward trend with small noise'. Flow matching generates 1000 candidate paths for the next 10 time steps.
Without semantic alignment (lambda=0): Flow picks smooth paths at random, some go up, some down, some sideways. Loss = 50.
With semantic alignment (lambda=0.5): Flow is steered toward upward paths consistent with the trend. Paths that go down are penalized by the KL divergence term. Loss = 50 + 0.5 * 8 = 54 (the penalty pulls paths toward meaning-consistent regions).
Result: when lambda > 0, the generated trajectory respects both smoothness (from flow) and semantic context (from LLM).

The flow paths are not just smooth; they are steered by what the LLM learned about the series.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatch between LLM representation and flow-matching conditioning. If the LLM extracts a representation in one way (e.g., 256-dimensional) but the flow-matching module expects different dimensions or structure, the steering signal breaks. The coupling fails silently.
LLM representation captures noise, not semantics. If the historical sequence is too short or too noisy, the LLM's bidirectional encoding extracts spurious patterns (random wiggles, not real trends). Flow matching then generates trajectories aligned with that noise.
Flow matching overrides semantic guidance. If lambda (the semantic alignment weight) is too small, the flow paths ignore context and revert to generic smooth motion. If lambda is too large, paths are rigidly locked to context and lose adaptability.
Domain shift: series from a new domain. The LLM was pre-trained on general text and time-series data from training-set domains. A new domain (e.g., industrial anomaly detection) may not align with the learned semantic patterns, breaking the context extraction.
Done right. Train the LLM encoder and flow-matching decoder jointly on diverse time series, tune lambda on a validation set, and test on hold-out data from the same domain. The semantic representation will capture real patterns, and flow paths will be grounded.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a forecast on a simple time series (e.g., daily electricity demand for 365 days, split into train and test). Train CoGenCast end-to-end. Generate forecasts for the next 30 days. The demo must show three things: (1) the LLM bidirectional encoding captures the seasonal pattern (demand is high in summer, low in winter); (2) the flow-matching paths are smooth and continuous, not jerky token-by-token jumps; (3) the final forecast is both semantically grounded (respects the season) and smooth (no artificial discontinuities).

Forecast error = mean absolute error between predicted and true values; Smoothness penalty = sum of squared differences between consecutive predictions (higher is rougher). Goal: minimize forecast error while keeping smoothness penalty low.

The key proof: on real time series, CoGenCast should beat both a pure LLM (lower error, smoother predictions because it avoids token-by-token jumps) and a pure diffusion model (lower error, better semantics because the LLM representation steers generation).
