Multi-Domain Transferable Graph Gluing for Building Graph Foundation Models

Treat graphs as puzzle pieces with different shapes, find the math rules that glue them together, and measure how well they align.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Imagine you have social networks, protein networks, and recommendation networks — very different structures. Pre-training a single model that learns from all of them is hard because nobody knows how to join them so knowledge flows between them. GraphGlue uses the math of curved surfaces to merge these datasets into one connected space, so a pattern the model learned from a dense social network (where neighbors are close together) can guide learning on a sparse protein network, because both live in the same glued space without kinks.

Zero assumption version

How do you glue different graph types together?

Pre-training a model on multiple types of graphs works well — a model trained on social networks, proteins, and movie data learns patterns that show up everywhere. But existing approaches treat each domain separately and hope the model can adapt. They offer no guarantees that what a model learns from domain A will be useful for domain B. A beginner might just stack all the data into one dataset and pre-train, but different graphs have different local shapes (think of one as a web, another as a tree). Forcing them into the same space leaves them misaligned — like trying to paste two curved puzzle pieces without bending them to fit.

The paper asks: what is the mathematical structure that lets you take a social network, a protein network, and a knowledge graph, and merge them into one connected space where patterns learned in one domain transfer to another?

The key move. GraphGlue builds a shared space by measuring how each graph's neighborhoods are arranged, then gluing the pieces together with a formula that keeps the joints smooth and without kinks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

GraphGlue changes the fundamental representation: instead of treating each graph as an isolated dataset, it merges them into a shared Riemannian manifold (a smooth, curved mathematical surface). The object being changed is the space in which graphs are embedded.

the object it moves  =  shared space (a set of coordinates where all graphs live)
The hidden quantity

The smoothness measure: holonomy

measured quantity  =  holonomy (frame rotation consistency)

Holonomy is a hidden quantity that measures how much a coordinate frame twists as you travel around a loop in the graph. On a flat piece of paper, a frame that travels around any loop returns to exactly the same direction. On a curved surface, it rotates. The formula measures this rotation: if you go from node i to j to k and back to i, does your frame return to its starting orientation?

GraphGlue estimates holonomy by looking at triangles in the graph. For each triangle, it computes the product of the frame rotations along the three edges. If the result is the identity matrix (no net rotation), the local directions are consistent and ready to glue.

Paper reading. The holonomy loss L_holo(G) is the sum over all triangles of the squared Frobenius norm of the product of three frame-rotation matrices minus the identity.
Why it works

Why gluing creates a smooth transfer space

The mechanism is simple: if you want multiple datasets to live in the same connected space, their local neighborhoods must fit together without kinks. GraphGlue enforces this by computing a local coordinate frame (a set of direction axes) for each neighborhood in each graph. These frames encode which directions matter in that neighborhood.

Next, it glues the frames together using a formula: as you move from one node to a neighboring node, the frame rotates in a specific, consistent way. The holonomy loss ensures that if you go around any triangle, the accumulated rotation brings you back to where you started. This forces all the pieces to fit — no impossible twists or tears.

Once all the local frames fit together without kinks, the entire dataset collection lives in one connected space. Knowledge learned in one domain (e.g., social networks) creates patterns in this shared space that the model can apply to another domain (e.g., proteins) because the space is continuous and connected between them.

Math after the picture

The gluing formula

First in words:

holonomy loss equals the sum over all triangles of the squared distance between the product of three frame rotations and the identity matrix

Then the compact form:

L_holo(G) = 1/|A| * sum over all triangles_ijk of ||P_(k,i) * P_(j,k) * P_(i,j) - I||_F^2

Every symbol:

Athe set of all triangles in the graph
P_(i,j)the orthogonal rotation matrix mapping frame at node i to node j
Ithe identity matrix (no rotation)
||·||_FFrobenius norm (the length of a matrix, like the square root of the sum of squares of all entries)
|A|the total number of triangles
A tiny worked example
Suppose triangle has three frames, and multiplying the three rotation matrices gives [[0.99, 0.05], [-0.05, 0.99]].
The identity matrix is [[1, 0], [0, 1]].
The difference is [[-0.01, 0.05], [-0.05, -0.01]].
Frobenius norm squared: 0.01^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.01^2 = 0.0001 + 0.0025 + 0.0025 + 0.0001 = 0.0052.
If averaging over 100 triangles, loss = (0.0052 + 0.003 + ... + 0.008) / 100 ≈ 0.005 (small, good gluing).

This loss ensures every triangle closes perfectly; when it is zero, the local frames are glued so smoothly that you can extend them to a global, consistent manifold.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Highly disconnected graphs break the frame consistency. If a graph has isolated nodes or weakly connected components, there is no continuous path to propagate the gluing constraints. Frames in separate components cannot be forced to align. The loss still computes, but the manifold has discontinuities.
Non-uniform graph sizes and densities cause misalignment. A very dense graph like a social network encodes local direction patterns very differently from a sparse knowledge graph. Even after gluing, the learned space may stretch unevenly, placing heavy reliance on the adaptation step to correct for domain differences.
Curvature loss alone is insufficient for global smoothness. The paper uses both holonomy and curvature loss. If only curvature is used (as a practical simplification), higher-order twists can remain, leaving the manifold locally smooth but globally twisted.
Gluing succeeds when triangle constraints are satisfied. The space is truly connected and transfer is efficient when every triangle in every domain satisfies holonomy (rotation product near identity) and curvature is small. This happens when graphs are dense enough and pre-training is long enough for convergence.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small demo on three graph datasets (e.g., a tiny social network with 100 nodes, a small protein network with 50 nodes, and a knowledge graph fragment with 80 nodes). Pre-train a graph neural network (a model that learns from graph structure) using GraphGlue on all three. Compute the holonomy loss for a sample of triangles across all three datasets. Then, take one domain (say, social network) as source and try to transfer the model to the other two domains (proteins and knowledge graph) with minimal fine-tuning. Measure how much fine-tuning is needed.

transfer_efficiency = (accuracy_after_tuning / accuracy_pretrained_alone) - adaptation_epochs_required

If the holonomy loss is small (frames are glued), transfer should be smooth and require few fine-tuning steps. If holonomy is large, transfer will be rough and require many steps. This shows that the geometric gluing is the mechanism behind transfer quality.
