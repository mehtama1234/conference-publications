Safety at One Shot: Patching Fine-Tuned LLMs with A Single Instance

A single safety example, combined with the hidden low-rank structure in how the model guards against harm, can restore complete safety to a compromised language model without breaking its usefulness.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

When you train a language model to master a new task, it often unlearns how to refuse harmful requests. Fixing this normally demands many safety examples and costly retraining. This paper shows that a single safe example is enough, because safety updates in these models follow a low-rank pattern — they change only a small set of internal directions, not the entire model.

Zero assumption version

The safety collapse problem

A language model learns to refuse harmful requests through careful training with safety examples. But when users fine-tune the model on their own data — to teach it a new task or style — the model forgets these refusals. It becomes willing to answer how to make explosives or scams. This is called alignment erosion.

The naive approach is to re-add safety examples during fine-tuning: mix some safety examples into the task data and train on both. This works, but it is expensive. You need hundreds of safety examples, months of GPU time, and the model often loses capability on the new task anyway. No one wants a model that refuses harmful requests but can no longer answer normal questions.

Fixing both safety and usefulness at the same time is the bind: add more safety examples and the model becomes boring; add fewer and it becomes dangerous.

The problem in one sentence: Fine-tuning breaks safety, but recovering it usually demands huge safety datasets and expensive retraining that damages the model's usefulness.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Measure the direction to change

Feed one harmful prompt and one safe answer through the damaged model; compute which direction (the gradient, a list of numbers showing how to nudge each weight) would need to move to refuse the harmful prompt.

Step 2: Find the thin safety directions

Look at the shape of that gradient: it only has a few important dimensions, like a pancake rather than a ball.

Step 3: Project onto those directions

Update only the weights that lie along the safety pancake, ignoring all other directions.

Step 4: Train for a few epochs

Run the model on that one safety example a handful of times; the model converges (reaches a stable state) very fast.

Step 5: Verify both safety and usefulness

Test that the model now refuses harmful requests and still answers normal questions correctly.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which weights get updated during recovery. Instead of updating every weight the same way (standard training), it updates only the weights that lie along a small set of hidden directions — the ones where safety matters. This selective update is called projection-based recovery.

the object it moves  =  which weight dimensions to update
The hidden quantity

The low-rank structure of safety gradients

measured quantity  =  safety gradient low-rankness

The hidden quantity is the shape of the safety gradient — how the weights would need to change to move from unsafe to safe. Normally, a gradient points in many directions at once. But the paper measures the actual safety gradients across all layers of real models and discovers they concentrate in just a few directions. Imagine a cloud of points: usually they fill a sphere, but safety updates fill a thin pancake. This pancake shape is the low-rank structure.

The paper estimates it by computing the gradient (the direction to move weights) when fed a harmful request and a safe refusal. Then it measures how many important directions that gradient points along: not many. This reveals that safety is encoded in a thin subspace, not spread through the entire model.

Why it works

Why one example is enough

When fine-tuning breaks safety, it moves the weights along the safety pancake — the thin subspace where safety lives. But because safety is confined to this low-rank structure, a single safety example produces a gradient that also points mostly along that same pancake. Updating only along the pancake restores safety efficiently because you are moving directly back to the safe state, not wandering through irrelevant directions.

The second reason is convergence speed. Because you are updating in a low-dimensional space (the pancake, not the full sphere of weight space), the model reaches a stable state almost immediately. After just a few training steps on one example, the safety gradient becomes nearly zero, meaning the model has learned the safe answer. Updating only the thin directions means each step of training accomplishes more: no wasted updates in directions that do not matter.

Math after the picture

The recovery equation

First in words:

The penalty for moving away from safety plus the penalty for moving away from the original model weights, projected onto the low-rank subspace

Then the compact form:

theta_safe = theta_damaged - eta * P * nabla_safety_loss(theta_damaged)

Every symbol:

theta_damagedthe model weights after harmful fine-tuning
theta_safethe model weights after one-shot recovery
Pprojection matrix onto the low-rank safety subspace
nabla_safety_lossthe gradient pointing toward safe answers
etastep size: how far to move in the safety direction
A tiny worked example
Say the safety gradient points as: [-0.2, -0.1, 0.05, -0.15] for four weight dimensions.
The low-rank subspace has two main directions (like an arrow pointing left-up and another pointing right-down); they describe the pancake where safety updates live.
Projecting the gradient onto this subspace: only the components along those two directions matter, say [−0.18, −0.12] (mostly the first direction).
With step size eta=0.1, update: theta_new = theta_old + 0.1 * [−0.18, −0.12, 0, 0] (zeros in irrelevant directions).
The weights move only along safety; task knowledge in other directions stays untouched.

The projection P ensures updates flow only through the thin pancake where safety lives, leaving the model's task knowledge alone.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The low-rank assumption breaks for unfamiliar model families. The paper validates on Llama, Mistral, Qwen, and GPT-4. If a new model has a very different architecture (e.g., a model that scores relevance differently in each layer), the safety subspace might not be low-rank, and projection fails.
One safety example may not cover all harm types. A single example teaches refusal to one kind of harmful request. If the model was fine-tuned on a mix of harmful requests (bomb-making, scams, misinformation), one example might recover only partial safety.
If the low-rank subspace was completely erased, recovery is impossible. If fine-tuning modified weights so severely that the safety pancake is no longer there, no projection can recover it. The paper assumes some safety structure remains.
Done right: use a diverse set of a few safety examples. One example works as a proof of concept; for production, a small set of diverse safe examples (5–10) covering multiple harm types ensures the recovery works across all kinds of harmful requests.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Start with a language model fine-tuned on a dataset of harmful instructions (e.g., 100 questions about making weapons). Measure its refusal rate (how often it says no to new harmful requests): it should be near zero. Then apply the one-shot recovery with a single benign instruction pair (e.g., How do I stay healthy? Answer: Exercise regularly). Measure refusal rate again: it should jump above 90%. Also measure performance on a neutral task (e.g., trivia questions): it should drop less than 5%. The demo proves that one example, combined with low-rank projection, recovers safety without harming usefulness.

safety_gain = refusal_rate_after − refusal_rate_before; task_loss = accuracy_before − accuracy_after

The refusal rate recovers almost completely after one example, while the model still answers normal questions correctly — the whole point of safety without sacrifice.
