EgoTactile: Inferring Hand Pressure from Video

A diffusion model learns to guess how hard your fingers press when you grab things, by watching egocentric video and hallucinating the most plausible pressure patterns.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When you reach into your pocket to grab your keys, your fingers automatically know how hard to squeeze without looking at your hand. A robot or VR glove needs to infer that grip pressure from video alone. This paper builds a system that watches egocentric (first-person) video and predicts the full pressure across all your fingers and palm, even for objects the model has never seen before.

The pressure blind spot

When you touch something, your fingers bend and press against it in ways that depend on what the object is, its shape, and how fragile it is. A robot arm or VR glove that copies your hand movements needs to know exactly how hard to press, but a camera only sees the outside of your hand—not the internal squeeze forces.

Old approaches use special pressure sensors glued to fingertips (intrusive and expensive) or assume the object is flat and the contact is just the fingertip (breaks on rounded objects, palms, complex grips). They fail on everyday items like apples, doorknobs, fabric, and tools where multiple fingers contact the object at once in a 3D arrangement.

The naive approach. Watch the video of the hand and object, then guess the finger pressure at each point on the palm and fingers—treating it like any other visual prediction task. The problem: pressure is invisible in the video, so many different pressure patterns could match the same hand pose and object shape.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of predicting one pressure value per finger (the usual approach), the paper treats pressure as something to be imagined. It uses a diffusion model (a process that learns to remove randomness) to generate multiple plausible pressure patterns, each matching the video but different from each other.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: plausible pressure patterns

measured quantity  =  Plausibility of contact patterns

The paper measures something you cannot see in the video: the likelihood that a particular pressure pattern is what a real human would actually use when grasping that object. Different hand poses and object shapes admit many possible pressure distributions, but only some are physically plausible (respecting anatomy, friction, object material, and human habits).

The model learns this by training on paired egocentric video and ground-truth hand pressure sensors. A diffusion model gradually learns to associate video frames with the patterns of pressure that appear in the real data—not just any pressure, but the ones humans actually apply.

Why it works

Why it works: reversing randomness with physical guidance

Diffusion models work by learning to reverse a noise process. Start with pure random numbers for pressures, then ask: if I remove one tiny bit of randomness and check 'does this match the video and physics?', what numbers would make sense? The model learns this step-by-step denoising during training. When you run it (at test time), you start with randomness and denoise many times until the numbers stabilize into plausible pressures.

By conditioning on the video and enforcing physical constraints (a Physically-Informed Feature Rectification layer), the denoising stays grounded. The constraints act as a filter: the model can only imagine pressures that (1) match what the video shows (hand bends this way, contacts are here), (2) obey physics (no negative pressure, pressure appears only where hands touch things), and (3) match human behavior (learned from training data). Many paths through the noise lead to valid pressures, and the diffusion process finds them correctly.

Math after the picture

The denoising equation

First in words:

At each denoising step, the model predicts the direction to move the pressure values to remove noise, constrained by the video observation and a penalty for unphysical pressure patterns.

Then the compact form:

p_new = p_old - eta * (gradient_denoise + lambda * gradient_physics_penalty)

Every symbol:

p_oldcurrent pressure estimate (values at each contact point)
p_newupdated pressure estimate after one denoising step
etastep size (how much to move)
gradient_denoisedirection predicted by diffusion model from video context
gradient_physics_penaltydirection that enforces physical constraints (no negatives, pressures at contacts, etc.)
lambdaweight balancing denoising and physics enforcement
Tiny worked example
Start: pressure at fingertip 1 is random: p_old = 0.3 (should be ~5 for this grasp).
Model predicts denoising gradient = 4.5 (move toward realistic value).
Physics check: contact exists here, so no penalty pull = 0.
Step size eta = 0.2, balance lambda = 1.0.
p_new = 0.3 - 0.2 * (4.5 + 1.0 * 0) = 0.3 - 0.9 = -0.6 (oops, negative!).
Clamp to zero: p_new = 0. Next step will correct this toward positive again.
After many steps: pressure converges to the plausible ~5 for this fingertip.

The model learns to predict the denoising direction from video; physics constraints prevent wandering to impossible pressures like negative values or concentrated pressure off the contact.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Occluded contacts. If the hand is partially hidden by the object or camera angle, the model cannot see where certain fingers touch. It must guess based on object shape and patterns it learned from training. If the guess is wrong, pressure is misallocated.
Unfamiliar objects. The training data includes common everyday items. For an object with unusual material or friction (e.g., a slippery metal block), the patterns learned from training may be wrong and suggest an unrealistic pressure pattern.
Denoising under-converges. If the diffusion process runs too few steps, the noise is not fully removed and pressures remain noisy. If it runs too long, the patterns learned from training may dominate and the video clues are ignored.
Done right. The paper trains on a diverse benchmark of everyday objects with real hand pressure supervision, transfers to bare-hand (no glove) scenarios, and validates on in-the-wild video. The physical constraints are baked into every step.

The demo must show: (1) the predicted pressure map visualized as a heatmap on the hand overlay, (2) the ground-truth pressure from sensors, and (3) the pressure distribution for 5 different random seeds to show that the model explores plausible variations (not just outputting one guess).
