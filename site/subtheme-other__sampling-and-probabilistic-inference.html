Sampling and probabilistic inference

Papers on generating samples from complex distributions, approximating probability distributions, and efficient Bayesian computation.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Often you don't want a single answer — you want to know the full range of possibilities. For example, a weather model should not just predict the temperature tomorrow; it should say whether it could be anywhere from 60 to 80 degrees with different probabilities. A drug simulator should not just give one molecule's behavior; it should explore what might happen if conditions are slightly different. The problem is that these probability distributions (the full picture of what might happen and how likely each outcome is) are complex, with many variables interacting in tangled ways. You can't just write down the probability for every possible combination.

The hard part is that computing these probabilities exactly requires summing over all possible configurations — which can be a trillion to the power of a trillion combinations. That is not feasible. You need approximate methods that explore the space smartly, spending more computational effort on the scenarios that are likely and less on the impossible ones. The approximation has to be accurate enough that decisions based on it actually work in the world.

Researchers tackle this with sampling — techniques that generate examples from the probability distribution without computing it exactly. Some use Markov chain Monte Carlo (a method that walks through the space of possibilities in a random but intelligent way, always moving toward the likely regions and away from the impossible ones). Some use variational methods, which approximate the complex distribution with a simpler one that is easy to work with. Some use diffusion processes, which gradually add noise to a starting pattern until it becomes completely random, then learn to reverse that process. Each approach trades off speed, accuracy, and the kinds of distributions it can handle.

How the work breaks down

When you need to understand what could happen in a complex system, you face a fundamental tradeoff: how do you sample from a probability distribution without computing it exactly? The papers here split into five directions: exact Markov chain methods that walk through possibility-space intelligently, diffusion-based approaches that learn to reverse noise-addition, variational methods that approximate with simpler distributions, practical adaptations that handle real-world constraints and test-time updates, and techniques for estimating properties of densities and energies without full normalization.

Markov Chain Methods for Exact Sampling

When you walk through a space of possibilities using randomized steps, you need to know where to walk and how fast. Markov chain Monte Carlo is a way to explore the space smartly: at each step, you move to a nearby configuration, accepting the move with a probability that pulls you toward high-probability regions. The challenge is tuning the algorithm so it explores thoroughly (visiting all the important regions) without wasting steps in irrelevant areas, and doing this efficiently when you have huge amounts of data.

The Accurate Large-sample Uncertainty Quantification paper shows how to get improved guidance on tuning these algorithms across many different models and data distributions where older approaches fail. The Covariance Estimation paper proves that MCMC can achieve the same sample efficiency as simple independent samples, but with much better computation cost when drawing samples is expensive. The Accelerated Parallel Tempering paper speeds this up by running multiple copies of the walk at different temperatures (like having pessimistic and optimistic explorers working in parallel), then using neural networks to swap between them efficiently, cutting both computational cost and improving the final samples. The Difference-of-Convex Langevin Algorithm guarantees convergence toward the target distribution for a broader class of problems than before. The Large-Scale Uncertainty Quantification paper combines MCMC with intelligent subsampling of data, showing it beats variational approximations for estimating both model settings and their uncertainty. Finally, Score-Repellent Monte Carlo adds a repellent force between samples so they spread out more efficiently, reducing variance in estimates with constant memory regardless of how complex your space is.

These papers form a pipeline of improvement where each solves a different bottleneck: they make the basic random-walk idea more efficient to tune (fewer knobs to turn), faster to run (fewer wasted steps), and better at exploring all the important regions without getting stuck (so you actually find rare events and high-probability regions), which matters because practitioners need algorithms that work reliably on real data without extensive trial-and-error.

Diffusion Models for Sampling and Density Work

Diffusion models reverse the process of adding noise: you start with a random blob, then learn to gradually denoise it step-by-step until a sample emerges. This turns sampling into a learned denoising task, which is often easier to train and more flexible than direct sampling. The hard part is using diffusion models not just to generate new samples, but to solve inference problems like understanding posterior distributions (what parameters does my data suggest, given a prior belief?) and estimating information-theoretic quantities (entropy, mutual information) from complex distributions.

Data-to-Energy Stochastic Dynamics shows how to sample from posterior distributions (what parameter values does my data suggest?) in generative model latent spaces using diffusion, without needing actual samples from the posterior—only the energy function. Particles Don't Care About Z achieves dramatically lower errors in entropy estimation (up to 16x better) compared to existing particle-based methods because it removes unnecessary intermediate steps, and this same efficiency gain also improves energy-based image generation. Bayesian Tensor Decomposition uses diffusion as a prior to guide image repair and denoising (breaking multi-dimensional data into factors), beating standard Bayesian and nonlinear baselines because diffusion provides a learned prior that is richer than Gaussian assumptions. Conditional Diffusion Sampling trades sample quality against computation cost more efficiently than competing samplers by optimizing the diffusion-stepping schedule. Information Estimation with Discrete Diffusion applies the same reverse-noise idea to discrete data (like DNA or text motifs), outperforming fixed numerical-encoding methods at finding patterns and selecting models because learned denoisers adapt to the specific data structure. SURGE fuses observational data with diffusion model simulations without approximation, letting you correct states in a particle filter using both real observations and simulation. Score-Based Density Estimation learns belief distributions from pairwise comparisons (like 'A is more likely than B') by training diffusion models on tempered samples.

These papers show diffusion works well because it separates learning (training the denoiser) from inference (running the reverse process), and because the flexibility of the learned denoisers lets you handle new problem structures—you just retrain the denoiser, not the whole inference method.

Variational Inference and Structured Approximation

Instead of sampling exactly, you can approximate a complex distribution with a simpler one that you can work with easily. Variational inference picks the best simple distribution by minimizing a gap between it and the true distribution. The challenge is making these approximations accurate enough—simple families like Gaussians miss important structure—and doing the computation efficiently at scale.

Copula-SVI captures higher-order dependencies between data points using vine-copulas (a way to build complex joint distributions from simpler building blocks), combined with Stein refinement to improve the approximation, because understanding these relationships makes clustering and time-series forecasting more accurate. It works better than standard structured variational inference on clustering and time-series problems because it directly models correlations instead of assuming independence. Efficient Online Variational Estimation shows how to learn both parameters and latent-state distributions on streaming data (like air-quality sensors collecting data continuously), balancing accuracy with computational cost so you can handle data arriving continuously without storing everything. Predictive Variational Inference flips the goal: instead of approximating the Bayesian posterior, it learns the distribution that optimizes predictive accuracy, which often requires less computation and naturally detects when parameters vary across data points because you optimize directly for what matters—predicting future data.

These three take different angles on the same core problem: how to approximate without losing the structure you need.

Test-Time Adaptation and Practical Inference

Real applications demand flexibility: you train once, but at test-time you have new information, new constraints, or a new problem variant. You need inference methods that adapt on the fly without retraining, handle multiple time-scales (some parameters matter for long-term behavior, others for immediate decisions), and work with the actual inputs you receive.

Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference uses optimal transport (a way to smoothly move between distributions) to understand how a neural network's behavior changes as you vary its hyperparameters, enabling test-time adaptation without retraining. PriorGuide lets you incorporate expert knowledge or updated information after training by adapting the prior distribution, making pre-trained inference models much more flexible because new information becomes available. Approximating Drift-Diffusion Models provides an analytical framework for optimizing external signals (like timing notifications) in decision-making, validated on video watch-time prediction because understanding when to show an ad matters as much as what to show. One-Shot Conditional Sampling generates samples using nearest-neighbor matching and optimal-transport distance, requiring only a single forward pass at test-time on problems like image denoising and upsampling so you don't wait for expensive iterative refinement. Path-Dependent Discrete Amortized Inference improves convergence and explores the state space better than Markovian approaches on discrete problems because allowing the path to influence future steps lets you learn from mistakes within a single inference run. Query-Based Asymmetric Modeling learns separate decoders for speech restoration at different sampling rates, balancing quality and perceptual clarity without redundant computation so the same model works across conditions.

These papers recognize that inference rarely stops at training time—the real gains come from letting the algorithm adapt to what you actually observe.

Energy, Entropy, and Discrete Estimation

Many real inference problems involve unnormalized densities (energy functions where you know relative probabilities but not the normalizing constant), discrete structures (like transit-stop networks or error-correction codes), or uncertainty estimates that must be reliable under distribution shift. Computing or estimating properties of these densities is hard because you never have the full picture.

Overcoming the Incentive Collapse Paradox addresses the problem of auditing systems where you cannot afford to check everything; it jointly optimizes which items to sample, how often to audit, and budget allocation with tractable cost because strategic agents adapt their behavior when they know audit patterns. Score-Based Error Correcting Code Decoder applies diffusion-based scoring to improve error correction in communication, achieving the best bit-error rates on most benchmarks and reducing decoding time because treating decoding as a learned denoising task finds better solutions than formula-based approaches. Fair Transit Stop Placement establishes theoretical bounds on how well you can approximate fairness in clustering, with a parameterized algorithm for exploring tradeoffs because no algorithm can satisfy all fairness measures simultaneously. GPan-LoRA produces well-calibrated uncertainty estimates in large language models under distribution shift by using a Gaussian process framework, preventing overconfident failures while maintaining performance because structured uncertainty (from a GP) doesn't degrade task accuracy the way simple variance does.

These papers solve the common problem: you need to work with incomplete, discrete, or unnormalized information, and your estimates must be reliable enough to act on.
