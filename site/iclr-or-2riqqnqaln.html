When Would Vision-Proprioception Policies Fail in Robotic Manipulation?

During certain robot motions, proprioceptive sensors learn so fast they block vision from learning, so the paper slows proprioception down to let both modalities pull their weight.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot arm needs two senses to manipulate objects well: vision (what it sees) and proprioception (how it feels—its arm position, joint angles, forces). The two work together to plan picks and placements. But experiments show a puzzle: during motion-transition phases (when the arm switches from moving to grasping, or from one move to the next), the robot ignores what it sees and relies only on how it feels. The paper finds the reason: proprioceptive signals shrink the loss faster during training, so the optimizer channels learning updates primarily into the proprioceptive pathway and starves vision. Their fix is to throttle proprioception's update flow during those phases, forcing vision and proprioception to collaborate.

The puzzle: vision gets ignored when the robot needs it most

A robot learning to pick and place objects has two sensors: a camera (vision) and joint angles and forces (proprioception). Both feed into a single neural network policy that decides what move to make next. Early studies promised that combining them would help the robot succeed more often on new tasks and objects, fail less often in the real world, and learn faster—because when the arm reaches toward an object, the camera can see far ahead to spot obstacles and target locations, while proprioception feels the arm's weight and friction, so together they guide more accurate, reliable plans than either one alone.

But recent experiments show something surprising: when the arm is in the middle of switching moves (reaching toward an object, then grasping it, then placing it), the vision sensor hardly helps, because the learned policy has shut it out and relies only on joint angles and forces. The question is: why would the network learn to ignore vision during these critical moments if both modalities feed into the same decision?

A beginner might guess that the camera is broken or the image processing is bad. Or that grasping just is a tactile problem. But the paper finds a different culprit: during training, proprioceptive signals are so clean and immediate that they shrink the training loss much faster than vision does—because joint angles and forces directly predict the arm's next move with little ambiguity, while camera pixels are noisy and require the network to learn which visual features matter. The optimizer, eager to minimize loss, channels all the learning updates (the nudges it sends to network weights) primarily into the proprioceptive pathway and starves the vision pathway, because that is the path of least resistance.

The core issue in one sentence. Proprioception learns so fast during motion-transition phases that it drowns out vision in the shared learning signal.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper modifies how strongly proprioceptive signals influence the neural network during training. Normally, the optimizer treats all sensors equally; learning updates (nudges to weights) from all modalities are applied at full strength. GAP intercepts proprioceptive updates during motion-transition phases and scales them down—reducing their influence so vision can learn too.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: phase-transition probability per timestep

measured quantity  =  Phase-transition probability (per timestep)

The hidden quantity is the probability that any given moment in the robot's trajectory belongs to a motion-transition phase—when the arm is switching from one movement primitive to another (reaching to grasping, grasping to placing, etc.). The paper calls this a phase-guided estimate: it uses proprioceptive history (joint angles and their velocities) to classify each timestep, because transition phases have telltale joint-angle signatures.

How the paper measures it: during trajectory collection, label each timestep as transition or not (by looking at which skills activated: if the skill changes, it is a transition). Then train a small auxiliary classifier on proprioceptive features to predict transition probability. This classifier runs during policy learning and produces a per-timestep probability score between 0 and 1. That score becomes the scaling factor: a probability of 0.8 means strongly reduce proprioception's gradient to 20% strength; a probability of 0.1 means barely reduce it to 90%.

Why it works

Why it works: balancing competing learning signals

The mechanism is a competition for learning resources. During training, the network has a single pool of updates that get applied to weights. When two modalities (vision and proprioception) both feed into the same classifier, they both produce losses, and those losses backpropagate in parallel. The catch: if one loss shrinks much faster than the other, the optimizer gravitates toward the easy win—it tweaks weights to satisfy the fast-shrinking loss and neglects the slow one, because the faster reduction in total loss appears to be progress, even though it comes at the cost of abandoning the slower pathway.

In motion-transition phases, proprioception shrinks loss fast because the signals are clean and the arm's state is highly predictable. Vision shrinks loss slowly because the camera sees a dynamic scene (the object moving, shadows shifting) and the network needs to learn which pixels matter. The optimizer, racing to minimize total loss, amplifies the proprioceptive pathway and starves vision.

GAP breaks this race by handicapping proprioception during those phases. By multiplying proprioceptive gradients by a factor of 0.3 (for example), the algorithm forces proprioceptive loss reduction to stay slow—comparable to vision's speed. Now both pathways get equal air time to learn. Vision has room to build features that detect object position and motion, while proprioception still anchors the learned policies to real-world joint angles and forces. The result: a policy that actually uses both modalities together instead of ignoring vision during critical moments.

The equation: scaled proprioceptive gradient

First in words:

The total loss is the vision loss plus a version of the proprioceptive loss whose gradient is scaled by the phase probability. In symbols: total loss = L_vision + alpha * p(t) * L_proprioception, where alpha is a fixed scaling factor (e.g. 0.5), p(t) is the phase-transition probability at time t (between 0 and 1), and L_proprioception is the standard supervised loss for predicting proprioceptive targets.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_vision + alpha * p(t) * L_proprioception

Every symbol:

L_visionloss from predicting what the camera should see or visual targets
L_proprioceptionloss from predicting joint angles, forces, or proprioceptive targets
p(t)phase-transition probability at timestep t, ranging from 0 (not a transition) to 1 (definitely a transition)
alphaglobal scaling factor (typically 0.5 to 1.0) controlling how much proprioception is reduced
L_totalcombined loss fed to the optimizer
A tiny worked example
Suppose at timestep t, the phase classifier estimates p(t) = 0.7 (likely a transition).
Vision loss is 0.8 (the network's vision prediction is somewhat off).
Proprioceptive loss is 0.4 (the network's proprioceptive prediction is fairly accurate).
Normal training: gradients from 0.8 + 0.4 = 1.2 total loss. Proprioception's loss is strong.
GAP with alpha=0.5: total loss = 0.8 + 0.5 * 0.7 * 0.4 = 0.8 + 0.14 = 0.94.
Proprioception's gradient is scaled down by factor 0.35 (0.5 * 0.7). Vision's gradient is unaffected.
Optimizer now balances the two signals; vision gets more learning opportunity.

The probability p(t) acts as a dynamic valve: when the robot is likely in a transition (p close to 1), proprioceptive gradient shrinks; when it is not (p close to 0), proprioceptive gradient is nearly full strength, because vision does not need the help.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Phase classifier trained on simulated transitions only. If the transition detector is trained only on simple simulation trajectories, it may fail to recognize real-world transitions (where timing is noisier). The scaled gradients then become random, and vision learning suffers.
Alpha (global scaling factor) set too aggressively. If alpha is too small (e.g., 0.1), proprioceptive learning is suppressed even outside transitions, and the policy loses grounding in real robot state. Tuning alpha for each robot morphology is necessary.
Vision and proprioception have different target structures. If vision targets (e.g., predicted image) and proprioceptive targets (e.g., predicted joint angles) have wildly different magnitudes or semantics, scaling one's updates but not the other can destabilize training, because the loss scales are no longer balanced and the smaller-magnitude loss dominates the optimizer's overall direction. Normalization helps by ensuring both losses stay in the same range before scaling is applied.
Done right. The phase classifier is trained on real or well-calibrated simulation data; alpha is tuned per robot; losses are normalized to the same scale; both modalities converge cleanly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A runnable demo should train two policies side-by-side on a pick-and-place task in simulation (e.g., PyBullet or MuJoCo): one standard multi-modal policy and one with GAP. The task should have clear motion phases: reach, grasp, lift, place. Log the loss for vision and proprioception separately at each step. Show that without GAP, the proprioceptive loss dominates and vision loss stagnates during grasp/transition phases. With GAP, both losses shrink together. Finally, test both policies on object shapes (sphere, cube, cylinder) that the training data did not include, and measure success rate. The demo proves impossible to miss this: the GAP policy should succeed on unseen objects much more often (e.g., 80% vs 55%) because vision learned generalizable features during the transitions.

Success rate on unseen objects = (tasks completed / total tasks attempted) under GAP minus success rate without GAP

If the unseen-object success gap is less than 15 percentage points, the phase guidance is either not detecting transitions or the scaling factor is wrong; retune and rerun.
