Vision-Proprioception Policies: Why They Fail & How to Fix It

When a robot learns from both images and arm sensors, the arm-sensor signals train too fast and block the image learning, especially when the robot is moving to a new location.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots can learn from two kinds of information at once: camera images of what they see, and sensors in their arm that tell them where it is right now. You might think having both would always help, but it often makes things worse, because the arm-sensor data is simpler and shrinks the training error faster — so the optimization algorithm abandons the camera to chase the easier signal. The problem: during motion (when the arm is moving to find an object), the arm sensors are easier and faster to learn from than images because the arm measurements are precise numbers while images require complex pattern recognition, so training ignores the camera branch entirely. This paper discovered why and built a fix that lets both signals learn together.

Zero assumption version

The two-channel learning trap

A robot arm has two kinds of senses: a camera that sees what objects look like, and built-in sensors that feel its own arm's angles and position. Intuitively, having both should mean the robot learns faster and better because two signals give the optimizer twice as much information to learn from.

In practice, this often fails. When the robot trains on tasks like picking objects, something odd happens during the motion-transition phases — the moments when the arm has to move from resting position to the target object. The arm sensors (proprioception) are giving fast, simple signals (measured values, straightforward to predict), while the camera sees complex, messy images that take longer to interpret. Training naturally finds the easiest path to shrink the error: it leans hard on the arm-sensor predictions and ignores the camera, even though the camera is needed for the next phase (when the robot has to recognize which object to grab).

When training ignores the camera during motion, the camera never builds the skills it needs. The robot ends up worse at tasks than if it had just used the camera alone.

The core problem: Arm sensors train faster than cameras because their error signals are simpler and easier to fit, so training forgets to teach the camera anything during motion phases because the optimizer naturally picks the quickest path to shrink the overall error.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Detect motion phases

Algorithm watches the arm sensors to spot moments when the robot is moving toward a new position (not holding steady).

Score transition likelihood

For each moment in time, calculates how likely it is that the arm is between two stable postures (a transition).

Dampen proprioceptive updates

During high-transition scores, reduces the strength of learning signals from the arm sensors by scaling down their gradients (the nudges that train the model).

Free the camera

With arm-sensor signals weakened, camera learning gets room to grow and builds strong visual features.

Stable phases get full arm help

When the arm is holding a target posture, arm signals are at full strength again to ensure precise control.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modulates (scales up or down) the gradients — the update signals — that flow backward through the proprioceptive processing branch during training. Specifically, it multiplies those gradients by a number between 0 and 1, determined by how likely each moment is a motion-transition phase. This is surgical: it does not change the architecture or add new parameters; it simply turns down one input signal at strategic times.

the object it moves  =  proprioceptive gradients
The hidden quantity

Motion-transition probability

measured quantity  =  transition likelihood per timestep

The paper introduces a hidden quantity it must estimate: for each moment in the training sequence, what is the probability that the robot's arm is mid-motion between two stable poses? A motion transition is any point where the motion magnitude (how far and fast the arm is moving) crosses a threshold; the algorithm models the probability smoothly so training is stable.

The paper estimates this probability from the proprioceptive signals themselves — the arm angles and velocities. It builds a lightweight predictor (essentially a binary classifier on top of the arm sensor stream) that outputs a number between 0 and 1 for each timestep. A score near 1 means high confidence the arm is transitioning; near 0 means the arm is stable. This hidden quantity is then used to dampen the proprioceptive gradients: high transition probability means lower scale factor, lower transition probability means closer to full strength.

Why it works

Why gradient dampening works

During training, both the camera branch and the arm-sensor branch produce predictions. A loss function measures the error (how far each prediction is from the true action). Gradients (tiny nudges to shrink that error) flow backward through both branches. The problem: arm-sensor errors are simpler to fix (the error-surface is smooth), so gradients move the arm-sensor weights aggressively. Camera errors require bigger structural changes (the error-surface is rougher), so gradients pull camera weights timidly. The result: aggressive arm-sensor updates overwhelm gentle camera updates.

GAP breaks this imbalance by scaling down the arm-sensor gradients during motion transitions. Now both branches receive equally-sized nudges, even though their error surfaces differ. The camera branch finally gets to learn because its updates are no longer drowned out. On stable phases (when the arm is not transitioning), arm-sensor gradients return to full strength, so the network learns precise servo control. The net effect: the vision branch learns features to find objects, the proprioceptive branch learns to move precisely, and both collaborate instead of one silencing the other.

The core pattern is that the error surface naturally biases learning toward the easier signal. By matching the update magnitudes, the paper forces the network to invest learning effort in both modalities proportionally to the task phase, not proportionally to ease of fitting.

Math after the picture

The gradient scaling equation

First in words:

For each timestep, reduce the proprioceptive gradient by multiplying it by one minus the transition probability.

Then the compact form:

grad_proprioception_scaled = (1 - rho_t) * grad_proprioception_original

Every symbol:

grad_proprioception_scaledthe reduced update signal for arm-sensor weights
grad_proprioception_originalthe normal backpropagation gradient from arm-sensor errors
rho_testimated probability that timestep t is a motion-transition phase, between 0 and 1
(1 - rho_t)the scale factor; high transition probability yields low scale, low transitions yield scale close to 1
A tiny worked example
Timestep 5: arm sensor predicts action, error = 0.8, gradient (unscaled) = 0.5, transition probability rho = 0.6
Scale factor = 1 - 0.6 = 0.4
Scaled gradient = 0.4 * 0.5 = 0.2
The arm-sensor branch receives a small nudge (0.2 instead of 0.5) because it is likely a motion phase; camera branch gradients stay full.
Timestep 8: same error 0.8, but now rho = 0.1 (stable pose), scale = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9
Scaled gradient = 0.9 * 0.5 = 0.45
The arm-sensor branch receives a large nudge because the arm is holding steady; camera is less critical.

The scale factor (1 - rho_t) is the core insight: high transition probability yields small updates to proprioceptive weights, freeing capacity for camera learning.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Transition detection fails on new objects. The motion-phase detector trains on one set of objects and does not generalize if the arm moves differently when grasping new shapes. The gradient dampening then misfires, damping the wrong phases.
Over-damping suppresses all proprioceptive learning. If the scale factor stays too low for too long (e.g., if the phase predictor thinks every moment is a transition), the arm-sensor branch learns nothing and loses servo precision.
Tasks where motion is rare struggle. On tasks dominated by stable holding (e.g., pushing a button), most timesteps are not transitions, so the method dampens rarely and does not address the core problem.
Done right: balanced learning. When the phase predictor is well-calibrated and task motion is typical (find, move, adjust), both modalities learn clean, complementary features and the robot generalizes to new objects.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulated robot arm tasked with picking blocks from a table. Train a vision-proprioception policy three ways: (1) camera only, (2) both modalities with fixed equal weighting, (3) both modalities with GAP gradient dampening. Record the success rate on a test set of new block colors and positions the robot has never seen before. Also instrument the network to show what features the camera branch learned: does it form object-detection filters in variant (1) and (3), or does it learn garbage in variant (2)?

generalization_success = (success_on_novel_blocks_with_GAP - success_on_novel_blocks_naive_concat) / success_on_novel_blocks_camera_only

The demo must show that naive two-modality learning is not just slightly worse than single-modality, but actively harmful — and that GAP recovers the advantage of having both senses.
