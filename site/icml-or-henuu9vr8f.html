PepCompass: Navigating Peptide Embedding Spaces Using Riemannian Geometry

When searching for drug molecules in a compressed space, follow the curved paths the decoder creates instead of pretending the space is flat.

Drug discovery starts with a generative model that squashes a huge space of possible molecules into a compact learned map. Zero assumption version

Flat space breaks peptide search

Antimicrobial peptides—short chains of amino acids that kill bacteria—are buried in a space so huge (trillions of possibilities) that random testing is hopeless. Researchers train a generative model that learns to map a much smaller latent space (a compact list of learned numbers) onto valid peptides; the decoder (the part that turns numbers into peptide sequences) is trained to reconstruct real peptides.

But here is the trap: most algorithms treat the latent space as flat, like a chessboard. They assume that stepping the same distance in any direction yields the same quality of peptide. In reality, the decoder creates bent, crinkled terrain—some directions yield diverse valid peptides, others are barren, like climbing a valley where some slopes are steep and others are flat. Flat-space navigation ignores these valleys and ridges, sending you up dead-ends.

The method in one sentence. Use the decoder's curvature (how bent the space is at each point) to guide your search, so you walk along geodesics (shortest curves on the bent surface) instead of straight lines through empty space.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces flat-space navigation with terrain-aware navigation. Instead of stepping any direction and hoping, you step in directions the decoder's curvature tells you are fruitful. Three tools replace the naive approach: geodesic search (shortest paths on the bent terrain) between known good peptides, random walks that stay on the bent terrain, and mutation enumeration that the decoder approves.

The hidden quantity

Decoder-induced curvature

measured quantity  =  curvature (how bent the terrain is)

The decoder has a Jacobian—a matrix of derivatives showing how sensitive each amino acid at each position is to tiny nudges in latent space. This matrix encodes how the terrain is shaped at that point: where it bends sharply and where it is nearly flat. The paper also computes the Hessian, which measures the second derivatives (how fast the slopes themselves change). In plain terms: it tells you the exact dips and rises of the terrain.

PepCompass estimates this curvature by computing the decoder's second derivatives, then uses those measurements to design its random walks (SORBES) and mutation proposals (MUTANG). A path search (PoGS) minimizes potential energy along a curve, which automatically gives you the shortest path across the bent terrain.

Why it works

Why bent-terrain navigation finds better peptides

A trained decoder learns the shape of valid peptide space. When you ask it to reconstruct a sequence, its weights encode which latent regions correspond to realistic amino acids. The Jacobian of the decoder tells you the direction and magnitude of that coupling. A high Jacobian in one direction means the decoder is sensitive there—moving even slightly nudges amino acids sharply. A low Jacobian means the decoder is insensitive—you can move far without changing the output.

Path search (PoGS) walks along a curve between two known good peptides, minimizing potential energy. On a flat plane, potential energy is just distance; on bent terrain, it accounts for the slope. This automatically pulls you toward regions where the decoder is stable and varied. Random walks (SORBES) use the curvature to constrain their steps: they wander in directions where the decoder stays on-surface, not plunging into invalid space. Mutation enumeration (MUTANG) uses the Jacobian to propose amino acid swaps that the decoder approves—changes the model is sensitive to, not dead zones. When you combine all three—seeds from paths, local wandering that respects terrain, mutations tailored to decoder sensitivity—you explore efficiently and find more active peptides than flat-space methods.

Math after the picture

The geodesic potential energy

First in words:

total energy along a curve equals sum over all points of (potential function at that point plus motion penalty); minimize this to find the best path

Then the compact form:

E_lambda(Z) = sum_k [ lambda * Phi(p(z_k)) + mu * curvature_penalty ]

Every symbol:

Zthe sequence of latent codes along the curve
kindex of each point on the curve
p(z_k)the peptide sequence decoded from latent code z_k
Phi(p(z_k))the potential (how desirable this peptide is; higher is better)
lambdaweight balancing how much we care about the potential
muweight on the curvature penalty (smoothness cost)
curvature_penaltythe roughness of the path (steep ups and downs cost more)
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have three latent codes along a curve: z_0 (seed peptide), z_1 (middle), z_2 (target).
At z_0: potential Phi = 8 (very active peptide), curvature penalty = 0 (starting point).
At z_1: potential Phi = 5 (medium), curvature penalty = 2 (sharp bend costs 2 units).
At z_2: potential Phi = 7 (active target), curvature penalty = 1 (slight bend costs 1).
With lambda = 1, mu = 0.5: total energy = (1*8 + 0.5*0) + (1*5 + 0.5*2) + (1*7 + 0.5*1) = 8 + 6 + 7.5 = 21.5.
If we try a path with sharper bends (penalty 5 at z_1), energy rises to 8 + 7.5 + 7.5 = 23, so the smoother path wins.
Minimizing this equation finds the geodesic—the path that balances staying near good peptides with following the decoder's natural curves.

Geodesic search minimizes this energy, automatically pulling the path toward high-potential regions while keeping it smooth and on-surface.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Curvature estimation fails on noisy decoders. If the decoder is poorly trained or its outputs are noisy, the estimated curvature is wrong, and path searches lead you astray. Random walks will diverge from real high-value regions.
Computational cost grows with sequence length. Estimating the Jacobian and Hessian (second derivatives) for every position in a long peptide becomes slow. The tangent space becomes high-dimensional, and MUTANG enumeration scales poorly.
Geodesics get trapped in local minima. If all nearby seeds lead to mediocre peptides, geodesic search will find only mediocre new seeds. The method escapes this when combined with random walks, but purely global geodesic search is brittle.
Done right with well-trained decoders and diverse seeds. Train the decoder on diverse, real peptides. Start path search from multiple seed pairs covering different regions of latent space. Use random walks to explore freely when you get stuck. Validate all discovered peptides in the lab before deploying.
