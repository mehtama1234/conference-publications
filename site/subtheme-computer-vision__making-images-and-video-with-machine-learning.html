Making images and video with machine learning

Using generative models like diffusion to create or modify images and video, from new content to editing existing photos.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

If computer vision traditionally meant extracting information from images, a newer frontier is the opposite: generating images from scratch. Given a description, a model generates a photo that matches it. Given a photo, a model edits it or transforms it. Given an idea, a model produces a video. This requires not just understanding images but learning to create them — to discover the rules of how pixels should combine to form realistic scenes, textures, and objects.

The fundamental challenge is that images are high-dimensional: a modest-size photo has millions of pixel values, and they are not independent. A pixel's color influences its neighbors. Shapes must be coherent. Objects must obey physics and perspective. Textures must be smooth or rough in consistent ways. Learning to generate images means learning these constraints implicitly through data. Early approaches used generative adversarial networks — a game between a generator that creates fake images and a discriminator that tries to spot fakes, so both improve. Recent approaches use diffusion models: start with random noise and gradually refine it into a real image by learning to reverse the process of noise corruption. Both require handling the curse of dimensionality — the space of possible images is so vast that naive approaches fail.

Papers in this subtheme explore different ways to control image generation. Some use text prompts as conditions, so you describe what you want and the model generates it. Others use example images as prompts — 'paint this photo in the style of van Gogh.' Some focus on editing — given an image and a description of a change, modify only the relevant parts. Others generate videos by extending the diffusion framework to the time dimension, predicting how scenes evolve frame by frame. A few use NeRF-like representations (learned 3D models) to generate images from arbitrary viewpoints. The underlying technology matters less than the goal: make it possible for users to create visuals by describing ideas in natural language.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five natural directions: fixing damaged or degraded images to restore what should have been there; enabling precise creative control over what gets edited and how shapes appear; making synthetic images look photorealistic through lighting and material simulation; generating videos by modeling how scenes and crowds evolve over time; and training the generators themselves to follow human preferences rather than just statistical patterns.

Restoring images from damage and optimizing quality

Real photos degrade in many ways: they blur, fade, collect noise, lose color, or suffer weather damage. The problem is that these degradations often destroy information irreversibly (a blurred photo loses fine details that cannot be recovered from the blurry pixels alone), so the model must infer what the original scene probably looked like by learning patterns from clean training examples. Different images fail in different ways, making a universal approach hard.

The Degradation-Agnostic paper takes a new angle by decomposing the restoration process into channels — each channel learns a different type of repair — then adding a penalty term that keeps the solution smooth and natural (so adjacent pixels change gradually rather than jumping). This enables the model to handle blur, noise, weather damage, and color fading all in one pass without being told which type of damage occurred. The Rethinking Expressivity paper improves on earlier methods by redesigning how the part of the network that weighs different pieces of the image (the attention mechanism) works, so it can make richer transformations while remaining fast. Both papers show that the key to universal restoration is building models that learn the structure of images deeply enough to infer what was lost, then apply different fixes channel by channel, treating each type of damage as a separate subproblem that shares a learned geometric backbone. Beyond learning what images should look like, the Towards Better Optimization paper addresses how to train the generators to follow human preferences — since users prefer certain styles or quality levels over average outputs. Instead of comparing one pair of outputs at a time, listwise preference ranking compares entire rankings (like: A is better than B which is better than C), giving more training signal per example. This makes the model consistently better at text-to-image generation, image editing, and personalized preferences because ranking multiple candidates teaches the model far more about what people want than pairwise comparisons alone.

Creative control: editing, generating shapes, affordances

Users want precise control over generation — to edit only specific regions, generate shapes in particular configurations, or specify how objects can physically interact. The core challenge is that diffusion models generate whole images at once, and constraining them to respect spatial, geometric, or physical requirements requires new ways to guide the generation process.

The Spherical Neural Field Diffusion paper addresses an unusual case: generating images with non-rectangular shapes — panoramas, fisheyes, perspective distortions. Instead of treating images as flat grids, it encodes them in a spherical coordinate system, so the model learns to generate images that naturally respect how perspective works and wrap around properly because the mathematics of a sphere naturally match how these distorted images actually work. The Geometric Image Editing paper solves a different control problem: inpainting regions while preserving accurate spatial layout. It uses a surrounding-context inpainting approach with a network that weights each pixel's relationship to others, allowing the model to reference the surrounding scene and fill in details that match both the visual style and spatial arrangement. The H2OFlow paper tackles affordances — where and how can hands grasp an object, or how should a tool be held. It generates dense 3D flow maps showing how human-object interactions could happen, trained on learned patterns from data but grounded in real 3D shapes and their arrangements. Together, these papers show that precise control comes from making the underlying representation (coordinates, space, or physics) explicit and then letting the diffusion model work within that structure.

Making synthetic images look real through light and materials

Synthetic images often look artificial because lighting is wrong, shadows are missing, or colors don't match the scene's light source. Fixing this requires understanding the physics of light — how it bounces, where shadows fall, how surfaces reflect differently depending on angle and material. The challenge is that this knowledge must be built into the generation process, not applied afterward.

The Adaptive Domain Shift paper addresses cross-modality translation: converting between medical imaging types (ultrasound to CT), or between remote sensing images with different sensors. The paper observes that shifting from one modality to another is like a domain adaptation problem; the model learns to shift the underlying learned representation to match the target modality while keeping the structural information intact because the anatomy or scene is the same, only the sensor has changed. This improves fidelity and converges faster than naive approaches because it reuses what the model already knows. The LiveMoments paper fixes a real user problem: when shooting video on phones, one frame is often blurry while others are sharp. It restores that key photo by borrowing visual information from nearby sharp frames, using a reference-guided diffusion approach. The PI-Light paper generates full-image relighting by incorporating batch-aware attention so that lighting predictions stay consistent across a group of images, and a physics-guided rendering module that enforces the rules of light reflection — how light bounces based on surface material and angle. By baking physics constraints into the diffusion process, relighting becomes both faster and more realistic because the model learns to respect the rules of light rather than just matching pixels.

Generating video: simulating motion and crowds

Video generation goes beyond single frames — the model must predict how scenes evolve, how people move, and how crowds behave. This requires capturing uncertainty (many possible futures) and ensuring motion is physically plausible across all pixels in every frame.

The EnvSocial-Diff paper generates crowd simulations by combining environmental conditioning — obstacles, walls, goals in the scene — with multi-level social interaction that models both individual movement and group dynamics. It learns that crowds don't move randomly; people respond to their surroundings and to each other, so modeling both levels together produces more realistic trajectories. The What Happens Next paper takes the complement: instead of generating dense pixel videos, it generates point trajectories — where does each point in the scene move over time. This is a leaner representation that captures scene dynamics more directly. The paper shows that generative models (trained to produce diverse possible futures) predict motion more accurately than regression models (trained on a single expected path), because real scenes have multiple possible futures and diversity matters. Together, these papers show that video generation requires modeling both the scene constraints (environment, obstacles) and the agents' behavior (people, objects), and that generating diverse possible futures is more useful than predicting a single average outcome.
