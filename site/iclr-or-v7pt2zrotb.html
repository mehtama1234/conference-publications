Causal Discovery on Large Random Graphs

Causal discovery on large graphs becomes more reliable as networks grow, especially for unbalanced networks.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

When you discover which events cause which (causal discovery), you do it by running experiments and watching what happens. But some causal links get missed because experiments are limited. This paper proves that on big networks, the fraction of missed links shrinks as the network grows, because averaging over many edges smooths out noise — and unbalanced networks (where a few events have many links) actually get even better than evenly-wired ones, because their lopsided structure reduces variability.

Zero assumption version

Missed causal links in networks

Imagine you want to know which online topics cause which others to trend. You run experiments: shut down topic A, see if topic B still grows. Shut down B, see if A grows. From these experiments you infer the causal graph. But you cannot experiment on every pair, and some weak links are too faint to see against noise.

In the real world, networks are huge. A beginner might think: with more data and more experiments you get better discovery, because larger sample sizes wash out randomness. But theory was silent on this. Did large networks make discovery harder (more links to miss) or easier (more experiments to average over)? No one had proven how error scales with network size.

The problem. No one had proven the reliability of causal discovery on large networks: the miss rate might stay high (more edges means more noise) or drop (more data means averages smooth out).

What the paper changes

The one thing that shifts

The paper does not change how you run causal discovery. It changes the question you can answer about the results: instead of asking does the error shrink, you now know how fast it shrinks and how tight the guarantee is.

the object it moves  =  reliability guarantees
The hidden quantity

Concentration rate: the tightness of the guarantee

measured quantity  =  How far the missed-edge fraction strays from its mean

The false-negative rate (FNR) is the fraction of true causal edges your experiments fail to detect. On any given graph, the experiments are random, so the FNR bounces around its mean value. The paper proves this bounce gets tighter as the network grows — the deviation width shrinks. For evenly-wired networks (Erdős-Rényi), the width shrinks at a certain rate as size increases. For hub-heavy networks (Barabási-Albert with heavy-tailed degrees), the width shrinks even faster, because the unbalanced structure where a few nodes have many links and most have few creates cancellation in the randomness.

The paper does not compute the FNR directly. Instead, it bounds the deviation using probabilistic inequalities, then shows the bound decays as a function of network size. This is measured via concentration inequalities (bounds on how far a random quantity strays from its mean) borrowed from random-graph theory.

Why it works

Why bigger, messier networks make causal discovery more reliable

In a small network, each experiment result is noisy. You run single-variable interventions (shut down one cause, watch the effects). Some true edges go undetected just by bad luck — randomness in the noise happened to hide the signal. When you compute the miss rate over all edges, you get a different number each time you repeat the experiment, because different edges get unlucky on different runs.

As the network grows, two things happen. First, there are more edges, so more opportunities for the law of large numbers to kick in: averaging over many independent trials smooths the noise. Second, the paper proves this smoothing happens faster than you might expect, because the network's random structure itself creates enough broken symmetry that the miss rate on one subset of edges cancels the miss rate on another, reducing total scatter.

For unbalanced networks like real social media or biology, the advantage is even sharper. When a few hubs dominate (some events cause many others), the graph's structure itself breaks symmetry and reduces variability, because the experiments on hub-edges tend to succeed reliably and the experiments on weak edges fail reliably, so randomness cancels out instead of accumulating. The paper formalizes this by showing the deviation width shrinks faster for networks with heavy-tailed degrees where most nodes have few links but a few have many.

Math after the picture

The concentration bound: how close the miss rate stays

First in words:

The probability that the miss rate deviates from its mean by more than epsilon is at most a constant times exp of negative order d-to-the-power beta

Then the compact form:

P(|FNR - E[FNR]| > epsilon) <= C * exp(-d^beta)

Every symbol:

FNRfalse-negative rate, the fraction of true edges not found
E[FNR]the average miss rate over many repeated experiments
epsilonhow far from the mean we are measuring
dthe number of nodes in the network
betathe exponent controlling how fast deviation decays: 0.5 for ER graphs, > 0.5 for heavy-tailed BA
Ca constant depending on graph parameters
A tiny worked example
Suppose a small graph has 100 nodes and average miss rate of 0.1 (you miss 10% of edges). Running experiments once, you measure 0.12. Running again, you measure 0.09. Running again, 0.11.
The bound says: for 100 nodes, the deviation epsilon = 0.02 happens with probability at most, say, 5 * exp(-100^0.5) = 5 * exp(-10) ≈ 0.0002. Very rare.
For 1,000 nodes, the same epsilon deviation has probability at most 5 * exp(-1000^0.5) = 5 * exp(-31.6) ≈ 10^-14. Nearly impossible.
For heavy-tailed graphs, beta > 0.5, so exp(-1000^0.6) is even more extreme.

The equation says: big deviations from the mean miss rate become exponentially unlikely as the network grows, because exp(-d^beta) decays faster than any polynomial.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hidden common causes fool the discovery. The paper assumes no hidden common causes: if there is an unmeasured event C that causes both A and B without us knowing about C, it can fool the discovery into thinking A causes B when really C does. The guarantee is weakened but not destroyed when hidden causes are present; the paper shows it still holds under a weakened assumption called epsilon-interventional faithfulness.
Experiments must be independent. Each intervention must land cleanly and independently. If shutting down cause A also accidentally disrupts cause B, or if interventions have lingering side effects, the concentration bound breaks.
Graph structure must match the model. The result assumes the true graph is either Erdős-Rényi (evenly random) or Barabási-Albert (heavy-tailed). Real networks might have other structures (small-world, community structure, temporal growth). The bound may not hold.
Done right: messy, unbalanced networks are an asset. The paper shows heavy-tailed graphs concentrate faster than random ones. This flips intuition: heterogeneous networks (Facebook, citation graphs) are actually easier to do causal discovery on because their structure reduces noise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate a random evenly-wired graph (d nodes, edge probability 2 divided by d). Generate an unbalanced hub-heavy graph (d nodes, same expected degree). Run single-variable interventions (turn on and off one node at a time) with random observation noise on both. Compute the false-negative rate (fraction of true edges not recovered). Repeat 100 times for each graph and each d from 50 to 500. Plot the miss rate mean and its standard deviation versus d. For evenly-wired graphs, show the error bars shrinking as size grows. For hub-heavy graphs, show the error bars shrinking faster, because the lopsided structure itself creates cancellation in the randomness.

Error bar width = 1.96 times the standard deviation of missed-edge fraction over 100 trials

The key impossible-to-miss fact: unbalanced networks converge faster than random networks to stable miss rates, so larger real-world networks are better for causal discovery, not worse, because the hub structure itself reduces noise variability even as the network grows.
