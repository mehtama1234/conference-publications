MoRA: Mobility as the Backbone for Geospatial Representation Learning at Scale

Learn what a place is really like by anchoring satellite photos and building data to human movement patterns, not just pixels.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Maps usually encode places by their buildings and terrain—pixels. But two identical-looking neighborhoods can be wildly different if one is a business district and the other residential. MoRA learns a compact number list for each location (how mathematicians compress information) by using human movement as the anchor. It combines three views of a place—satellite photos, where stores and offices are, and who lives there—and aligns them all to a mobility graph, a massive record of where people actually move. The result is a single short number list for each location that captures what the place is really for, not just what it looks like.

Why satellites alone are not enough

If you want to understand a city, you need to know what each neighborhood is for. Is it a shopping district or a residential zone? A job center or a bedroom community? Satellite images show you rooftops and streets, but two neighborhoods can look identical in pixels and be completely different in function.

Old methods try to learn from satellite photos alone. They take an image of a place and output a number list that describes it. The trouble: a photo does not tell you what the place is used for. It shows you what it looks like. A beginner would try building bigger models and feeding them more satellite data, but that does not fix the core problem—pixels do not encode purpose.

In one sentence. MoRA anchors satellite images, building records, and census data to a graph of human movement, so the number list captures what a location is for, not just what it looks like.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Break the city into grid cells

Divide the region into small squares (H3 cells); each cell is one location.

Pre-encode human movement

Use a standard graph method (LINE) to turn the mobility graph (who moved where) into starter number lists for each cell.

Smooth via graph neural network

Pass each cell's starter numbers through a simplified network (LightGCN) that lets nearby movement patterns influence each other.

Encode satellite, stores, and census

Separately encode satellite imagery, building records (POIs), and demographic data into their own number lists.

Pull everything toward mobility

Use a contrastive loss (a matching game) to force satellite, stores, and census lists to align with the mobility embedding, so all three views speak the same language.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes: the embedding anchor

Instead of learning embeddings (compact number lists) from satellite photos alone, MoRA learns them from a mobility graph first, then uses that as the true ground truth. Satellite imagery, building locations, and census data are then aligned to that mobility anchor. The paper changes which data source is the primary signal and which are auxiliary views that must match it.

the object it moves  =  the embedding anchor
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: alignment strength

measured quantity  =  How well each modality matches mobility

MoRA measures how tightly satellite photos, building records, and census data align with human movement. Call this alignment strength. It is not explicitly named in the paper but it is the core quantity the contrastive loss optimizes: a score saying how well each auxiliary modality (photos, stores, demographics) matches the mobility signal.

The paper estimates this via contrastive learning—a matching game where correct pairings (a photo, stores, and census from the same location, matched to that location's mobility pattern) score high, and random mismatches score low. During training, this alignment score drives the optimization: pull matched pairs together in the number-list space, push mismatches apart. Over time, the embeddings converge to a shared space where all four views sit close together for each location.

Why it works

Why this works: different data speaks the same language about purpose

Human movement reveals what a place is for. If a grid cell has high foot traffic 9-to-5 on weekdays, it is probably an office or shopping district (people clustering there for work tells you its purpose). If traffic peaks evenings and weekends, it is residential (people coming home tells you it is a bedroom area). The mobility graph holds billions of these signals.

Satellite photos, building records, and census data tell the same story, but in three different languages. A shopping district shows malls and storefronts in pixels, has many store locations marked, and attracts daytime workers in census snapshots. But these three signals are hard to combine—they are different data types with different noise patterns.

MoRA solves this by forcing all three to align to one ground truth: the mobility signal. It trains so that, for any location, the satellite number list, the building number list, and the census number list all pull toward the mobility number list. This alignment works because all three carry the same hidden information (location purpose), just encoded differently. As training proceeds, the system discovers shared meaning: what satellites see as storefronts, buildings confirm as POIs, and census shows as commuters, mobility reveals as daytime traffic. All four number lists converge to the same point—a single short list that encodes location function.

The alignment equation

First in words:

The contrastive loss pulls matched embeddings close and pushes mismatches far. For a location, its satellite embedding, POI embedding, and census embedding are all pulled toward the mobility embedding. Similarity between them is measured by dot product (how aligned two number lists are). Matched pairs are given high target similarity; random pairs are given low target.

Then the compact form:

L = -log( exp(sim(e_mobility, e_satellite) / tau) / sum_k exp(sim(e_mobility, e_k) / tau) ) + same for POI, census

Every symbol:

e_mobilitythe 128-number embedding of human movement for this location
e_satellitethe 128-number embedding of satellite imagery for this location
e_POIthe 128-number embedding of building records for this location
e_censusthe 128-number embedding of census data for this location
sim(a, b)the dot product of two number lists, measuring how aligned they are
tautemperature, a tuning constant controlling how sharp the matching decision is
sum_ksum over all locations (k=1 to batch size); the denominator includes both positives and negatives
A tiny worked example
Suppose the mobility embedding is [0.5, -0.2, 0.3] and the satellite embedding for the same location is [0.51, -0.19, 0.32].
Dot product: 0.5*0.51 + (-0.2)*(-0.19) + 0.3*0.32 = 0.255 + 0.038 + 0.096 = 0.389 (high similarity, good match).
Suppose a satellite embedding from a random other location is [-0.1, 0.8, -0.5].
Dot product: 0.5*(-0.1) + (-0.2)*0.8 + 0.3*(-0.5) = -0.05 - 0.16 - 0.15 = -0.36 (negative, mismatch).
The loss pushes the first similarity higher and the second lower, forcing the satellite embedding to align with its true mobility partner.

The equation formalizes the idea: align each location's four views (mobility, satellite, POI, census) to a shared point in the number-list space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mobility data is incomplete or biased. If the mobility graph only records certain populations (e.g. smartphone users, employed people), the embedding will be skewed. A neighborhood whose movement is not well-captured will have poor embeddings.
Satellite imagery is outdated or low-resolution. If satellite photos are old or blurry, they carry little signal about current building state. The contrastive loss will then downweight the satellite modality, and the final embedding may not capture visual features at all.
Movement patterns change seasonally or due to events. If a location's typical movement is disrupted (a holiday, a lockdown, a new store opening), the mobility graph may not reflect the true long-term purpose. The embedding will lag reality.
Done right. Combine high-quality, recent mobility data with diverse satellite and demographic sources. Use multiple time periods of movement data to smooth out noise. Validate the embedding on downstream tasks in the target domain (not just the pretraining region).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a system that takes a city region, extracts a mobility graph (source: anonymized phone records or transit data), collects recent satellite imagery, POI listings, and census data for a grid of cells, trains MoRA, and then uses the embeddings to predict a downstream task—e.g. which neighborhoods will see the most new retail openings, or where crime will rise next month.

Performance gain = (MoRA accuracy on downstream task) minus (satellite-only baseline accuracy). Example: 78% minus 62% = 16 percentage point improvement.

The demo must show that when you anchor everything to mobility, the model learns what locations are truly for, not just what they look like. Transferring the embedding to a new city (not seen during pretraining) should outperform training from scratch on satellite images alone, because the shared number list encodes universal location functions (offices always have business-hours traffic, homes always have night-time traffic) that transfer across cities, whereas satellite pixels are highly specific to each city's building styles and lighting conditions.
