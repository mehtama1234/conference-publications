Zero assumption version

We watch only the end, not the journey

When we train a neural network, its weights change step by step. A beginner might think: just look at the final weights and you understand the network. But this misses the entire story of HOW those weights got there.

The deeper problem: neural networks seem to organize themselves in ways we do not fully understand. Weights get arranged into patterns, neurons specialize, and over many training steps the network shapes itself. But if you only look at the final frozen network, you miss the choreography—the forces and patterns that caused that organization to happen. You see the dance floor after the dance, not the dance itself.

The method. Record the network's weights at many moments during training, treat each moment as a snapshot of a network structure, and draw all those snapshots together in a special curved space that compresses the important patterns.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect snapshots

Save the network weights at many moments during training, not just the end.

Build a connection map

For each snapshot, list which neurons connect to which (treating neurons as points and weight connections as links).

Map to curved space

Use a learning model to position each snapshot in a curved space where distances between positions reveal hidden structure.

Preserve network meaning

Ensure that identical networks (even if neurons are listed in different order) get the same position in the curved space.

Read the patterns

Examine where snapshots cluster and how they move through the space to see how the network self-organized during training.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes

The paper changes how we represent a neural network: instead of looking at the weights once at the end of training, we save the weights many times during training and treat each saved copy as a snapshot of how the network was organized at that moment.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how neurons organize into a hierarchy

measured quantity  =  temporal hierarchical structure

The hidden quantity is how neurons organize into a hierarchy—some become central hubs that many others connect through, others become leaf nodes at the ends of branches, and everything forms a tree-like structure. In ordinary space, this structure is invisible because neurons can be rearranged without changing what the network computes, so you cannot tell which neurons are central and which are peripheral.

This paper measures it by embedding the network into curved space (a type of mathematical space where distances naturally compress trees: close within a branch, far between branches). In this curved space, neurons that form tight branches stay close in distance, and neurons on separate branches stay far apart, revealing the hierarchy. The paper learns a model that assigns each training snapshot to a position in this curved space, capturing both what each snapshot looks like and how snapshots change from one moment to the next.

Why it works

Why it works: curved space matches tree structures naturally

The mechanism rests on a simple fact about how distance works: in ordinary flat space, tree structures distort awkwardly (you have to stretch branches far apart or compress hubs). But in curved space, trees fit perfectly—distances in curved space naturally match tree distances (close within a branch, far between branches). During neural network training, neurons arrange themselves into tree-like structures with central hubs and outlying branches. Curved space is built for exactly this.

When each training snapshot is assigned a position in curved space, neurons that form a tight hierarchy automatically end up close in the curved distance measure, and neurons in separate branches automatically end up far apart. No forcing needed. The learning model discovers which position in curved space best represents each snapshot, and by doing that, it reveals the hidden tree structure that emerges during training.

Math after the picture

The embedding equation

First in words:

For each training snapshot, the model outputs coordinates in hyperbolic space. The loss minimizes reconstruction error (how well the hyperbolic coordinates recover the network structure) while maintaining equivariance (networks that are the same up to neuron reordering get the same hyperbolic position).

Then the compact form:

L = sum_t ||G_t - decode(encode_hyperbolic(snapshot_t))||^2 + lambda * equivariance_penalty

Every symbol:

G_tthe network structure (graph) at training step t
encode_hyperbolic(snapshot_t)the hyperbolic position learned for snapshot t
decode(·)reconstructs the network graph from hyperbolic coordinates
lambdaweight controlling how much equivariance is enforced
equivariance_penaltypenalizes networks that should be identical (up to neuron order) if they get different positions
A tiny worked example
Suppose at step 5 a network has 3 neurons arranged: one hub connects to two branches (tree structure).
Hyperbolic embedding assigns this to position (0.3, 0.4, 0.1) in the Poincaré ball.
At step 6, the same structure but with neurons relabeled should still map to the same hyperbolic position, because reordering neurons does not change what the network computes.
The equivariance penalty says: if encode(snapshot_5) = (0.3, 0.4, 0.1) and snapshot_5 and snapshot_6 differ only by relabeling, then encode(snapshot_6) should also be (0.3, 0.4, 0.1); if not, penalty increases the loss.

The hyperbolic coordinates compress hierarchical structure efficiently, so nearby positions in the ball mean similar network organizations during training.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Requires many checkpoints. If you only save weights at the start and end, you lose the temporal pattern. The method needs dense snapshots to trace the trajectory properly.
Hyperbolic fitting can be unstable. Hyperbolic space has numerical quirks near the boundary of the Poincaré ball. If the meta-learning model tries to push coordinates too close to the edge, numerical precision breaks down.
Equivariance only handles permutation symmetry. Neurons can be equivalent in other ways too (e.g., scaling a weight up in one layer and down in another). The method enforces only reordering invariance, missing other equivalences.
Done right. Collect snapshots frequently during training, use numerical-stable hyperbolic libraries, and interpret the hyperbolic distances as relative measures of structural similarity, not absolute ground truth.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a small multilayer perceptron on a simple task (e.g., learning to classify two groups in 2D space). Save the weights every 10 training steps. Apply the temporal hyperbolic embedding method to create a hyperbolic representation of snapshots. Then verify two things: (1) snapshots taken at similar training stages should map to nearby positions in hyperbolic space, and (2) if you permute the neurons in a snapshot without changing the network function, the hyperbolic embedding should stay the same.

temporal_coherence = average distance between snapshots at nearby training steps; equivariance_error = distance between embeddings of equivalent (reordered) networks

The demo must show that neurons with similar roles in the network land near each other in hyperbolic space, and that swapping the order of neurons does not move the embedding.
