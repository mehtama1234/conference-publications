Connecting language and vision together

Building systems that understand images the way language models do, linking what they see to what they can describe and reason about.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Language and vision are two sides of the same coin. When you describe a photo, you translate visual information into words. When you read a description and imagine the scene, you translate words back into mental images. Recent computer vision research asks: can machines do this bidirectional translation? Can a model look at a photo and write a caption? Can it read a text description and find matching images? Can it answer questions about images in natural language? These tasks require the model to bridge two completely different modalities — pixels and text — and reason about what connects them.

The challenge is that images and text are fundamentally different data types. Text is sequential and discrete: one word at a time. Images are spatial and continuous: billions of pixel values arranged in a grid. A model must learn to extract high-level concepts from images (what is this about?) and low-level details (what color is the car?) and then express those concepts in language. It must also understand language well enough to ground words in visual reality — knowing that 'dog' refers to furry animals with four legs, and that 'running' means motion, and that these concepts combine in specific ways (a running dog is different from a sitting dog). This requires learning aligned representations: internal representations of meaning that work for both images and text.

Papers in this subtheme use vision encoders — networks that convert images into meaning vectors — paired with language models that can decode those vectors into text or compare them to text embeddings. Some use transformers to process both images (as patches) and text (as tokens) in one model. Others treat the task as retrieval: learn embeddings so that images and text descriptions have similar coordinates in the embedding space, and matching is just finding nearest neighbors. Some add reasoning steps, asking the model to think step-by-step through a question before answering. Others use contrastive learning: train the model to make matching image-text pairs closer in embedding space and non-matching pairs farther apart. The underlying idea is always to find a shared representation where vision and language meet.

How the work breaks down

When machines connect language and vision, they face three core challenges: extracting meaning from images and connecting it to words, handling the gap between what the model sees and what humans intend, and deciding which information to attend to. Papers in this subtheme split into models that learn better representations by pruning or refining what the model looks at, systems that reason step-by-step about what they see, methods that detect when the model is looking at the wrong things or making things up, and approaches that apply vision-language understanding to real tasks like image editing or document analysis.

Focusing where the model looks in language models

When a vision-language model looks at an image, it processes many visual details. Most of these details are not equally important for answering a question or describing the scene. The challenge is deciding which visual features to keep and which to ignore, especially when the model has limited memory or time. Focusing on the right parts means the model needs to know both what matters visually and what the task is asking for.

The Pruning for Adaptive Focus work looks at when models process images by dividing them into small pieces called patches, like a grid of 576 or more squares. Most models spend equal computational effort on all of them, wasting resources. This paper proposes a two-part strategy: first, measure how much information each patch adds (its diversity), and second, measure whether the image content itself is important to the question (its relevance to the task). Combining both signals drops unnecessary patches without harming accuracy and reduces the model's tendency to hallucinate details that were not in the image. PromptHub tackles the related problem of visual prompting, where the model learns from example images passed alongside a new image. Instead of treating all examples as equally useful, it weighs them by how similar they are to the query image (location-aware selection). Because it selects examples based on relevance rather than treating all equally, it works across different architectures and on images it has never seen before. Spectral Heat Flow takes pruning further: it compresses 88.9% of image patches while keeping 95.6% of the model's understanding. The method tracks how information flows between patches and drops the redundant ones, which means the model can stay fast while still answering questions accurately.

Detecting hallucinations and alignment failures

Vision-language models make mistakes in predictable ways. Sometimes they generate details that were not in the image at all—called hallucinations. Sometimes they misread diagrams or fail to align what they see with what they write. Other times the model reasons about the image correctly but makes a wrong conclusion. Detecting these failures early matters because users need to know when to trust a model's answers and when to verify them further.

Detective SAM tackles forgery detection: when images have been edited, a model must not only spot that they were edited but also point to exactly where the edit happened. This work shows that a pretrained vision-language model can adapt quickly to new types of editing by training on synthetic examples that mimic the editor's signature patterns, so it achieves a 33.67% improvement over prior work. ImageDoctor approaches the reverse problem: when text-to-image models generate pictures from descriptions, the results are often misaligned with the intent. Rather than asking humans to score the images, this work trains the model to look at an image and output a detailed explanation of what visual problems exist (e.g., 'the dog is blue but the text says brown'). Using these fine-grained reasons as a reward signal improves generation quality by 10% because the model can then optimize for detailed correctness instead of just vague similarity. Math Blind shows that language-vision models fail especially badly on diagrams in math problems because they do not understand the primitives—the basic shapes, lines, and labels—that make a diagram work as a communication tool. Training on structural representations of these primitives (e.g., 'this is a triangle connected to this point') boosts diagram grounding by 79% because the model learns to recognize these building blocks and how they connect, and reasoning performance improves by 3-4% as a result. The core issue: models need to learn that diagrams are not photographs but symbolic systems. Detecting Images from Unknown Categories with Vision-Language Models reveals that existing methods are too pessimistic: they flag too many known images as unknown. The fix is to use a less stringent statistical measure, Rényi divergence, which is more aligned with human intuition about what is truly unfamiliar because it does not overweight edge cases, allowing the model to flag only genuinely strange images. The Abstraction Gap in Vision-Language Causal Reasoning measures a subtle but important failure: even when models score highly on text-based reasoning benchmarks, they fail at causal reasoning about images. A model might correctly say 'if I move the block, the tower falls' but show an image where the tower already fell before the block moved—a logical contradiction that reveals high text scores and true visual understanding are not the same thing.

Reasoning step-by-step over images

When humans reason about complex images—finding objects interacting with each other, understanding diagrams, or answering detailed questions—we do it step by step. We look at one area, recognize what it is, relate it to other parts, and build up understanding. Vision-language models can do the same if they are designed to reason explicitly rather than guessing immediately.

LINK tackles human-object interaction: recognizing that a person is holding a fork, cooking at a stove, or playing with a toy requires understanding both the objects and how they relate. This work extracts knowledge from vision-language models about what objects are and how they interact, then uses that knowledge to detect interactions with high accuracy in both zero-shot (no training) and supervised settings. PoSh improves image description evaluation by building a scene graph first—a structured representation of what objects are in the image and how they relate (e.g., 'person—sitting_on—couch'). Then it asks the language model to compare a description against this scene graph, which grounds the judgment in visual facts rather than loose semantics, so it produces better agreement with human ratings. Transductive Visual Programming creates a library of tools as the model works, learning which operations (like 'find_corners' or 'project_to_3d') help solve spatial problems. As new queries arrive, it selects the right tools and combines them, building understanding step by step rather than trying to solve everything in one pass, which means the approach generalizes to unseen spatial reasoning problems. Chain-of-Glimpse takes video understanding and structures it: instead of looking at the whole video at once, the model searches for relevant objects, focuses on them, and reasons about what they do and how they change over time. This search-guided progression improves both general video understanding and grounded video reasoning because it concentrates computational effort on what matters. Internal Reasoning Space works inside the model's thinking process. Rather than generating answers directly, the model produces intermediate reasoning steps that it then reads to generate the final answer. This thinking space allows the model to correct mistakes and achieve 71.67% on a fine-grained visual benchmark, beating standard vision-language models. PixelCraft organizes reasoning over structured images like charts and diagrams by running three stages: first, parse the image into components; second, reason about each component and their relationships; third, synthesize into an answer. It maintains image memory across reasoning steps so that when the model needs to verify a fact or connect ideas, it can refer back to earlier parts of the image without re-reading it.

Counting and detecting objects in detail

Many vision tasks require precision: counting objects, locating specific instances, or recognizing which label applies to which part of an image. These tasks are hard because they need both high-level understanding (what am I looking for?) and low-level accuracy (where exactly is it?). Using language-vision models for these tasks is promising because models can understand natural language descriptions of what to count or find.

Bootstrapping MLLM for Object Counting takes the first step: it trains a vision-language model to count objects from image-level labels, not instance-level annotations. The model learns that 'three dogs' means it should count dogs in the image, even though it was never shown boxes around each dog. This dramatically reduces annotation cost while matching fully-supervised performance because the model infers the instance locations from the count. DiVE-k tackles fine-grained recognition: telling apart different species of birds, dog breeds, or plant varieties. Rather than classifying each image by itself, the model learns to compare images—asking 'what is different between this image and that one?' This comparison-based approach works much better than standard methods on tasks where the model sees new categories at test time because comparing differences is easier than remembering every variant, outperforming them by 10%. Grounding-IQA takes image quality assessment and makes it grounded: instead of a score from 1 to 10, the model identifies specific regions and explains what is good or bad about them. A large dataset of 160K images with fine-grained annotations enables this grounded reasoning because the model learns which regions correspond to quality issues, leading to more informative quality scores. PGT (Procedurally Generated Tasks) generates custom training examples on the fly to teach the model visual grounding—pointing to specific objects the text mentions. Training data generated this way teaches the model to ground language in vision better than natural images alone because it teaches the precise connection between words and visual locations, with up to 20% gains. RewardMap handles spatial and fine-grained visual reasoning by breaking the task into stages: first understand what is asked, then locate relevant regions, then reason about details. Each stage produces rewards that guide the next, accumulating improvements of 3.47% across benchmarks because step-by-step refinement is more reliable than one-shot reasoning. Multi-Label Recognition via Patch-Level Inference sidesteps the usual approach (averaging all visual features) by processing small patches independently and aggregating their predictions, which is fully training-free and delivers 6% gains on multi-label tasks because different patches may belong to different objects.

Applying vision-language models to specialized tasks

Beyond general image understanding, vision-language models are being adapted to domain-specific tasks: converting design mockups to code, analyzing medical images, describing how scenes change, and interpreting remote sensing data. The challenge is that these domains have specialized vocabularies and require both precision and common sense.

Figma2Code asks whether models can convert a visual design into working code. Testing ten leading vision-language models reveals a split: proprietary models produce visually accurate mockups but struggle with responsive layouts and maintainability—they might generate pixel-specific widths instead of flexible containers. This reveals a gap between visual fidelity and code quality. FLARE proposes deeper integration of vision and language: instead of using a simple linear projection to connect image features to text, it interleaves vision and language processing throughout the model, building cross-modal understanding at every layer because early fusion helps both modalities inform each other. Human3R builds a unified model that handles many vision tasks at once—pose estimation, 3D mesh recovery, depth, and motion—by learning a shared representation. The single model runs at 15 FPS on modest hardware while matching or beating specialist models because a shared representation avoids redundant computation. PhotoAgent applies vision-language models to artistic image editing: given an instruction like 'make the sky more dramatic,' the model proposes aesthetic edits and checks its work before applying them, which improves visual quality because self-checking catches mistakes before they are committed. Rethinking Radiology Report Generation applies the approach to medical imaging: instead of generating free-form text, the model generates findings organized by anatomical topics, grounding output in the visual structure of the scan. This improves F1 scores from 29.4 to 44.0 because structured output forces alignment with the image content. Towards Faithful Reasoning in Remote Sensing trains models to reason about satellite imagery step by step, verifying each reasoning step against the image, which enables the model to explain its conclusions and improves performance on geospatial analysis tasks because reasoning out loud forces accuracy at each step.
