Optimal Design for Multinomial Logit Bandits

Make choice-modeling experiments vastly faster by switching from guessing which subsets to test to computing the best ones upfront.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When a store runs an experiment to find which assortment of products customers will buy most, it has to pick a lot of different subsets to test—and testing all possible subsets is impossible. This paper shows how to compute in advance which subsets are most worth testing, using the structure of customer choice. It turns a problem that looked hopeless into one a computer can solve in minutes.

The Impossible Search

Imagine a store deciding which products to stock. To figure out what customers actually like, they run experiments: they show customers different combinations of items (subsets, or assortments) and watch which one they pick. The problem is there are exponentially many subsets. With just 20 products and picking 5 at a time, there are nearly 16,000 subsets to test. With 100 products, the number is astronomical. A naive approach is to just test random subsets and learn slowly, but this wastes experiments.

The math of customer choice follows a well-known model: the multinomial logit (MNL) model. It says the probability a customer picks item $i$ from a set $S$ follows a simple rule based on the items' underlying values. The right math exists to compute which subsets are most informative to test—the optimal design theory—but it requires solving a horrendously complex optimization problem for each subset. For thousands of subsets, this is computationally intractable.

In one sentence. Use a clever algebra trick to convert the intractable optimization problem into a form a computer solver can handle, so you can compute the best subsets to test up front.

What the paper changes

The single move

The paper changes which subsets to test in each round. Normally, you would either test uniformly at random or greedily pick one subset at a time. Instead, the paper computes a probability distribution over subsets up front—called a design—that maximizes how much information each test reveals about the customer values. This is not guesswork; it is a mathematically optimal choice.

the object it moves  =  test distribution
The hidden quantity

G-optimality: how informative a round of testing is

measured quantity  =  Information gain per test

When you test a subset and observe a customer choice, you get information. But some subsets reveal more than others. G-optimality is a score that measures how much a distribution over subsets will shrink your uncertainty about the item values. A high G-optimality score means each test teaches you a lot; a low score means you are learning slowly. It is a single number that summarizes the entire design.

The paper estimates G-optimality by analyzing the math of the MNL model. It does not require running experiments; it comes from the linear algebra of the Fisher information matrix (a matrix that encodes how sensitive your observations are to changes in the values you are trying to learn). The paper then uses a classical result, the Kiefer-Wolfowitz equivalence theorem, to prove that the design it computes is nearly optimal according to this score.

Paper reading. G-optimality is the trace of the inverse Fisher information matrix. The Kiefer-Wolfowitz theorem says a design is G-optimal if and only if the maximum directional sensitivity (the directional derivative of the objective) equals the average. The paper computes a design that is within a small multiplicative factor of this optimum.
Why it works

Why the algebra trick makes it fast

The honest problem is that the design oracle (the optimization that picks the best subset on each Frank-Wolfe iteration) must evaluate a nonlinear objective—one that sums over all subsets in a complicated way—and find the maximum. For thousands of subsets, that is intractable. The paper solves this in two ways, both rooted in the same insight: rewrite the nonlinear sum as a linear program in a higher-dimensional space.

The first way uses a mathematical trick called lifting. The MNL loss function, which looks nonlinear, can be rewritten in terms of a matrix (called the lifted design matrix) whose entries are linear in the design. This lets you convert the nonlinear oracle into a maximization of a linear objective subject to linear constraints—exactly what a modern integer-programming solver is built to handle. You also get certified bounds: the solver can prove how close its answer is to the true optimum, so you can stop early once the gap is small enough.

The second way uses a Schur-complement trick to replace the nonlinear objective with a ratio-of-sums form that can be optimized exactly in polynomial time (no solver needed). Both approaches turn the computational bottleneck into something solvable in minutes instead of hours. Because the Frank-Wolfe algorithm only needs an approximately correct oracle, both methods work and remain within the algorithm's theoretical guarantees.

Math after the picture

The design and sample complexity

First in words:

G-optimality = trace of (inverse of expected Fisher information matrix). Sample needed = (feature dim * log(num items)) / (min revenue gap)^2.

Then the compact form:

Samples = O( d * log(N) / Delta_min^2 )

Every symbol:

dnumber of features (how many numbers describe each item)
Ntotal number of items (arms) available
Delta_minsmallest difference in revenue between the true best and second-best assortment
O(...)rough order of magnitude, ignoring logarithmic factors and constants
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 5 product features (d=5), 100 products (N=100), and the best assortment makes 10 percent more revenue than the second-best (Delta_min=0.10).
Samples = 5 * log(100) / 0.10^2 = 5 * 4.6 / 0.01 = 2,300.
Compare: testing random subsets would need 10,000+ samples to be confident. Testing smartly via optimal design reduces that to 2,300.

Simpler method must test random subsets, learning slowly; this paper's design concentrates tests where they matter most, shrinking the number of experiments needed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The initial guess is very wrong. The design is computed from an initial estimate of each item's value. If that estimate is far from the truth, the first rounds of testing may not be as informative as predicted. The paper handles this by requiring an initial exploration phase to ensure the guess is close enough before switching to the designed testing schedule.
The MNL model does not fit the real data. The entire approach assumes customers follow the multinomial logit choice rule. In reality, customers may exhibit more complex behavior (correlated preferences, brand loyalty, out-of-stock surprises). If the model is wrong, the design's optimality guarantees do not hold.
The revenue differences are small. When the best assortment only barely beats the second-best (the minimum gap is tiny), the sample complexity explodes: it grows quadratically as the gap shrinks. Distinguishing two very similar options requires exponentially more tests.
Done right. If the initial estimate is calibrated, the MNL model is reasonable, and there is a clear gap between the best and second-best assortment, the algorithm runs fast and finds the right choice with high confidence.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple e-commerce recommendation simulator. Create 10 products with known, preset utilities (e.g., item 1 has utility 5, item 2 has utility 3, and so on). Run the optimal-design algorithm to identify the best assortment of size 3 from those 10 items. Measure how many simulated customer choices (tests) are needed before the algorithm is confident it has found the true best assortment. Then run a baseline that tests random assortments. Plot the number of tests (y-axis) against the confidence level (x-axis, going from 50% to 99%). The optimal-design curve should rise much more slowly, meaning fewer tests are needed for the same level of confidence.

Samples to 90% confidence = min number of tests where prob(identified best) >= 0.90

The gap makes it impossible to miss: optimal design learns five to ten times faster than random testing, because each test is chosen to reveal the most information rather than being random, so fewer experiments are needed to reach the same confidence.
