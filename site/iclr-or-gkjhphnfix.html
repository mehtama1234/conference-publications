Detective SAM: AI-Image Forgery Localization

Detect and pinpoint where AI tools edited a photo by layering forensic clues onto a segmentation foundation model and retraining on newly-generated fake images.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Photos edited by diffusion models are now so photorealistic that traditional forgery detectors fail. Detective SAM builds on SAM2, a broad-purpose segmentation model (a tool trained to draw outlines around objects in images), by adding two lightweight adapters: one that converts forensic clues—subtle pixel-level artifacts left by editing—into candidate regions, and another that masks those regions. The paper also introduces AutoEditForge, an automated pipeline that generates synthetic fake images across four editing styles, so Detective SAM can quickly retrain and keep up as new editing models are released.

Why real photo detection fails

Diffusion models—AI tools trained on billions of image examples—can now edit photos with pixel-perfect consistency that traditional forgery detectors see nothing unusual. A naive detector looks for obvious artifacts: mismatched colors, duplicate patterns, unnatural lighting. But modern edits have none of those.

When you use Photoshop or an AI editor to swap a face, blur a background, or add an object, the edit leaves traces: small statistical anomalies in the pixel values, tiny inconsistencies in how textures blend. Old detectors found these traces reliably. But diffusion models smooth over those traces by design—they add random noise to an image and gradually denoise it, blending in the edited region so smoothly that the forensic clues vanish or mutate into new patterns that detectors have never seen before. The moment a new editor is released, old detectors crumble.

In one sentence. Modern AI edits are photorealistic and evade traditional detectors; the paper adds forensic-clue analysis to a broad segmentation model and retrains on synthetic edits to keep pace.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Scan for forensic clues

Apply statistical filters to the image to highlight anomalies in pixel values left by editing.

Adapt the feature encoder

A lightweight linear layer (feature adapter) fuses forensic clues with SAM2's learned patterns.

Prompt SAM2 implicitly

Pass the combined features to SAM2's masking mechanism without explicit user prompts.

Apply a mask adapter

A second lightweight layer refines the segmentation output to pinpoint edited regions precisely.

Retrain on synthetic edits

AutoEditForge generates fake images; Detective SAM adapts in hours, not weeks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Detective SAM adds two learnable adapter layers to SAM2: one sits between forensic clues and the feature encoder, the other modulates the final mask output. These adapters are tiny (far fewer learnable numbers than the foundation model itself), so retraining is fast. The adapters learn to weight the forensic signals and refine the masks without retraining the entire model.

The hidden quantity

Forensic clue strength

measured quantity  =  Clue magnitude per pixel

A forensic clue is a statistical measure—a number between 0 and 1—that indicates how anomalous a pixel or small patch is compared to its surroundings. Unedited regions have low clue magnitude; edited regions have higher values. But clues are often subtle and noisy; some pixels will have high clues even in unedited photos (false alarms).

Detective SAM estimates clue strength by applying standard forensic filters (e.g., Laplacian pyramid, noise inconsistency analysis) pixel-by-pixel across the image. The feature adapter learns to amplify genuine clues while suppressing noise. A heat grid below shows where clues are strongest in a typical edited image.

Why it works

Why mapping clues to masks works

Editing always leaves traces, but the traces are hard to see and vary by editor. SAM2 is a foundation model—trained on millions of image segmentation tasks—that excels at drawing outlines around any object or region when given the right prompt or features. By feeding it forensic clues (which highlight edited pixels) rather than asking it to classify individual pixels, the paper reuses SAM2's boundary-finding skill. SAM2 learns where the clues cluster and draws clean masks around those clusters.

The feature adapter sits early in SAM2's pipeline. It learns to filter out noise in the raw forensic clues and amplify real signals, because SAM2's encoder is already good at building complex, detailed feature representations from clean inputs. The mask adapter sits at the end and sharpens the output mask, ensuring precise boundaries.

AutoEditForge solves the generalization problem. When a new diffusion editor is released and produces a different style of clue pattern, Detective SAM has no data. But AutoEditForge can generate new fake images programmatically (in hours, not weeks). A few hundred synthetic edits are enough to retrain the adapters and restore accuracy. This works because the adapters are tiny; full retraining would be expensive.

The adapter update rule

First in words:

Total penalty is the standard segmentation loss on the output mask, plus a small weight-decay term that keeps the adapters close to their initialized values to avoid overfitting to synthetic data.

Then the compact form:

loss = L_seg(mask_pred, mask_true) + lambda * (||A_feat||^2 + ||A_mask||^2)

Every symbol:

L_segsegmentation loss (e.g., cross-entropy or focal loss measuring mask accuracy)
mask_predthe mask predicted by the adapted SAM2 model
mask_truethe true edited region (ground truth)
A_featthe learned feature adapter weights
A_maskthe learned mask adapter weights
lambdaregularization strength, set low to allow adaptation but penalize large changes
A tiny worked example
Suppose the feature adapter has 100 learnable weights, initialized near 0.
After one batch of 32 training images, the mask loss averages 0.25, and ||A_feat||^2 is 0.01.
With lambda = 0.001, the total penalty is 0.25 + 0.001 * 0.01 = 0.25001.
The adapter is pulled only weakly to stay small, so it learns to amplify forensic clues without overfitting to the current dataset.
On the next batch from a new editor, the learned features transfer because they capture the underlying structure of anomalies, not memorize one editor's quirks.

Lightweight adapters trained with gentle weight decay (a penalty that steers learned numbers to stay small) reuse a foundation model's general knowledge while learning editor-specific forensic patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

No forensic clue left. If an edit blends so perfectly that no pixel anomaly remains, forensic filters will find nothing. Detective SAM cannot help, because it depends on forensic clues to highlight the edited region—without clues, there is nothing to segment. This is rare but theoretically possible with infinite blur or very subtle color shifts.
Natural image anomalies trigger false alarms. Unedited photos sometimes have local texture anomalies (sharp shadows, noise from low light). Forensic filters flag these as suspicious. The adapters must learn the difference via training data; if training data is biased toward certain lighting or scenes, false alarms will persist.
Synthetic data distribution mismatch. AutoEditForge generates fake images programmatically. If the synthetic edits do not match the distribution of real-world attacks (e.g., synthetic edits use only 50 random seeds, real editors produce more variety), retraining on synthetic data may not generalize. The paper mitigates this with multiple edit types, but the gap remains a known risk.
Done right. Pair forensic clues with a broad segmentation foundation model and retrain on diverse synthetic edits. Forensic clues highlight anomalies; SAM2 localizes them; AutoEditForge ensures fast adaptation.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train Detective SAM on a few thousand real forgeries + ground-truth masks (e.g., a standard benchmark). Measure intersection-over-union (IoU): the fraction of pixels in the predicted mask that overlap with the true edited region. Record baseline accuracy on the training dataset. Then evaluate on an unseen diffusion editor (a model released after training). Show that Detective SAM maintains 85%+ of its original accuracy, which proves forensic clues transfer across editors because they encode the underlying nature of editing artifacts. Finally, generate 200 synthetic edits with AutoEditForge using the new editor's style, retrain the adapters for 30 minutes, and show that accuracy recovers to within 5% of the original benchmark—this proves that retraining only the tiny adapters (not the whole model) is fast enough to adapt in hours instead of weeks, and that a few hundred synthetic examples are enough because the foundation model already understands segmentation and only the forensic-to-mask mapping needs adjustment.

IoU = true_positives / (true_positives + false_positives + false_negatives) * 100; forgetting = IoU_original_editor - IoU_new_editor_no_retrain

If the metric holds above 80 IoU on both known and new editors after light retraining, the mechanism—forensic clues + foundation-model adaptation + synthetic data—is working.
