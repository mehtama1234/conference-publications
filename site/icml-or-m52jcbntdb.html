A Stronger Benchmark for Online Bilateral Trade: From Fixed Prices to Distributions

Learn a distribution over buyer-seller price pairs so the market maker can shift profit across rounds and approach the best globally budget-balanced policy instead of being trapped by per-round pricing.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Online bilateral trade studies a repeated market with one seller and one buyer in each round. The learner posts prices, trade happens only when both sides accept, and the goal is high gain from trade without subsidizing the market. Classical work usually enforces weak budget balance, meaning the learner must avoid losses in every round separately. This paper studies a stronger benchmark based on global budget balance, where profit only needs to stay nonnegative over the full horizon. That small-looking change matters because it allows the learner to randomize over paired prices and temporarily accept less favorable rounds in order to improve long-run welfare. The paper gives the first algorithm with sublinear regret against this harder benchmark under one-bit feedback.

Zero assumption version

Why per-round safety can block long-run welfare

In one round of bilateral trade, the learner posts a buying price for the seller and a selling price for the buyer. If the seller's value is below the buying price and the buyer's value is above the selling price, trade occurs and gain from trade is created. Weak budget balance forces the buying price to stay below the selling price every round, so the learner never loses money immediately.

That rule is safe, but it can be too restrictive. A globally budget-balanced mechanism may use a randomized rule over paired prices that sometimes earns more and sometimes earns less, as long as the cumulative profit stays nonnegative. This broader action space can double the attainable welfare in some settings. The difficulty is that the learner receives only one-bit acceptance feedback, so it must learn a two-dimensional policy from very limited observations.

The main change. Benchmark the learner against the best globally budget-balanced randomized rule over price pairs and learn toward that benchmark because per-round no-loss pricing can be much too conservative.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes both the benchmark and the action class. The benchmark is no longer the best per-round safe price, but the best horizon-level safe randomized rule over price pairs. The learner therefore must reason in two dimensions because seller and buyer prices are coupled through both welfare and long-run budget.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  budget slack across the time horizon

The useful hidden quantity is cumulative budget slack: how much room the learner has to take a less profitable action now because it expects to recover later. Under weak budget balance this slack is always zero, so the learner cannot trade current profit against future welfare.

Global budget balance creates room to do exactly that. A randomized rule over price pairs can spend slack in one region and earn it back in another, which matters because the best welfare policy may require both kinds of rounds. Learning that rule is therefore really about locating where slack should be used and where it should be replenished.

Why it works

Why randomized price pairs help

A fixed weak-budget-balanced price must satisfy the no-loss condition every round, which means the seller offer cannot exceed the buyer charge. That rule directly limits which valuation pairs can trade. A randomized rule over price couples is more expressive because it can randomize among several seller-buyer pairs whose average effect preserves the budget while each pair targets different valuation regions.

This matters because buyer and seller values are jointly distributed. The best action is not only about one price threshold on one side. It is about how a pair of prices shapes acceptance on both sides together. The paper shows that learning this two-dimensional object is possible under one-bit feedback when the joint density is bounded, because that smoothness lets nearby price pairs reveal related information, and achieves sublinear regret with respect to the stronger benchmark.

The practical effect is that the learner can reach trades that a per-round safe fixed price would always miss, while still controlling losses over the long run because the sampled price rule is designed around cumulative budget feasibility.

The regret target

First in words:

Regret compares the cumulative gain from trade of the learner to the cumulative gain from trade of the best globally budget-balanced randomized rule over price pairs.

Then the compact form:

Regret(T) = sum_(t=1)^T GFT(p_star) - sum_(t=1)^T GFT(p_t)

Every symbol:

Tnumber of trading rounds
p_starthe best globally budget-balanced randomized rule over price pairs
p_tthe learner's chosen price pair or sampled distribution action at round t
GFT( )the gain from trade produced by the posted prices under the realized buyer and seller values
Regret(T)how much welfare the learner loses relative to the stronger benchmark over T rounds
A tiny worked example
Suppose the best globally budget-balanced distribution would yield total gain from trade 100 over ten rounds.
A weak-budget-balanced learner using one fixed safe pair yields total gain from trade 72 because many beneficial trades are never attempted.
A distributional learner yields 89 because it alternates between a high-welfare low-margin pair and a profit-recovery pair while keeping cumulative profit nonnegative.
Its regret is 100 minus 89, which is 11, much smaller than the regret of the fixed-price learner.

The paper proves a sublinear rate under random-environment assumptions, but this expression captures the benchmark shift that drives the result.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The density assumption can fail. The regret guarantee relies on bounded density, so highly irregular valuation laws may break the smoothness the learner needs for estimation.
One-bit feedback is still very coarse. Because the learner sees only accept or reject signals, learning the right two-dimensional rule can remain slow when buyer and seller responses are noisy or very similar across nearby prices.
Budget slack can be misused. If the learner spends long-run budget slack in the wrong regions, it may accept poor trades early and fail to earn the slack back later.
Done right. The method works when the learner uses global budget flexibility carefully because the added action space helps only if the sampled price pairs truly improve the welfare-budget tradeoff.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Simulate repeated buyer and seller values from a smooth joint law where a globally budget-balanced randomized policy is known to outperform every weak-budget-balanced fixed price because some useful trades require cross-round budget tradeoffs. Run one learner restricted to per-round safe prices and one learner that can randomize over price pairs under horizon-wide budget control.

Measure cumulative gain from trade, cumulative profit, and regret to the global-budget benchmark. The demo should show that the distributional learner closes the gap because it uses two kinds of price pairs: some that widen trade opportunities and others that rebuild budget slack.
