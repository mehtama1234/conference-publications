Should We Still Pretrain Encoders with Masked Language Modeling?

Two-stage pretraining—learning left-to-right first, then masked—beats single-method training on a fixed compute budget.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Text encoders (models that read passages and decide what they mean) have been built using Masked Language Modeling, or MLM—a training method where the model learns to guess words that have been hidden. But decoder models trained left-to-right (Causal Language Modeling, or CLM) can sometimes read passages well too. This paper runs 15,000 experiments to settle a nagging question: when you give both methods the same compute budget, which wins? The answer is neither alone. Sequentially combining both methods—CLM first, then MLM—reaches the best accuracy under any fixed computational budget.

Zero assumption version

Encoders have always used Masked Language Modeling, but should they?

A text encoder reads a passage and produces a representation (a compressed summary of what was said). For 20+ years, encoders were trained using Masked Language Modeling: hide random words, let the model read left-to-right AND right-to-left (bidirectional), and train it to predict the hidden words. Meanwhile, decoder models (like GPT) use Causal Language Modeling, or CLM: predict the next word reading purely left-to-right. Recently, people noticed that CLM-trained models, even though they read only forwards, can sometimes match or beat encoders at the representation task. But was this because CLM is actually better at reading, or just because those models were bigger or trained on more data?

A naive decoder would say: 'Since CLM models can read forwards and both can learn representations, why not just use CLM for encoders?' But without rigorous comparison, it is easy to misattribute wins to the method when they come from scale or data.

The core question. When computational budget is fixed and equal, is MLM, CLM, or a hybrid of both best for encoder pretraining?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with CLM

Train the model to predict the next word reading left-to-right only, like a typical language model.

Measure early-stage performance

Check how well CLM-trained representations work at text understanding tasks after 10K-20K steps.

Switch training objective to MLM

Without resetting weights, switch to masked prediction (hide words, predict them bidirectionally).

Continue to the budget limit

Run the remaining steps with MLM, starting from the warm-initialized weights left by CLM.

Compare final accuracy

Test on text classification, question answering, and information retrieval under the same total compute cost.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes: pretraining recipe

The paper does not change the model architecture, loss function, or any hyperparameter except the sequence of training objectives. It simply changes the ORDER: instead of training with MLM from scratch or CLM alone, it trains with CLM for the first half-or-third of steps, then switches to MLM for the remaining budget.

the object it moves  =  pretraining sequence
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: data-efficiency trajectory

measured quantity  =  Learning speed per training step

The paper measures a quantity that textbooks do not name: how fast the model's test accuracy climbs per training step. CLM climbs steeply at first (it learns to read forwards very quickly), but flattens out after 10K-20K steps. MLM climbs slowly at first (bidirectional reading is harder to learn) but keeps climbing to higher ceilings. The biphasic strategy exploits this: grab CLM's steep early climb, then switch to MLM's higher final plateau, so you spend no steps on CLM's flat part or MLM's slow beginning.

This trajectory depends on how many training steps you have. With a large compute budget (42K steps), pure MLM wins because it eventually climbs higher. With a tiny budget (2K steps), CLM is better because you never run long enough to hit MLM's payoff. But at medium budgets (12K-22K), the crossover point matters—and biphasic wins because it captures both strengths.

Why it works

Why biphasic training works: two learning modes

CLM learns by repeatedly predicting the next word in a sequence. Each prediction step teaches the model to capture one direction of context (everything before). This is fast to learn: the patterns are simple and local. But CLM has a ceiling: without looking backward, it cannot learn complete bidirectional representations (summaries that use words from both directions). Those features require seeing what comes after—a harder problem.

MLM learns by masking random words and predicting them from both directions. This is harder to start: the model must learn to fuse forward and backward context, coordinate selective focus (which words matter for each position, looking in both directions), and handle the asymmetry between masked and unmasked positions. Early on, this learning curve is steep and slow. But MLM's ceiling is higher: with bidirectional context, it can build richer representations and generalize better to downstream tasks, because the task of filling in missing words from full context is closer to how text representation is actually used (a new task shows both words before AND after the part you are summarizing).

Biphasic training initializes the model with CLM's fast uphill climb, getting strong learning signals (big, clear gaps between right answers and wrong guesses) and reaching a warm checkpoint quickly. Then it switches the loss function to MLM and resumes from that warm state. Because the weights are already partially trained, MLM does not start from random zeros—it starts from CLM's local optimum. The transition is not reset; it is a handoff. From that head start, MLM's high-ceiling learning takes over, reaching accuracy that pure MLM cannot match in the same budget because MLM never had the CLM warmup.

The loss function transition

First in words:

Loss at step t equals: if t in CLM phase, loss = negative log probability of next token; if t in MLM phase, loss = negative log probability of masked token.

Then the compact form:

L(t) = { -log P_CLM(x_{t+1} | x_1...x_t) if t <= T_CLM { -log P_MLM(x_i | x_\not_i) if t > T_CLM

Every symbol:

L(t)training loss at step t
P_CLM(x_{t+1} | x_1...x_t)probability the model assigns to the next word, given all previous words
P_MLM(x_i | x_\not_i)probability the model assigns to masked word i, given all other words
T_CLMtotal steps in the CLM phase (e.g., 10K or 15K)
x_ithe word at position i
x_\not_iall words except position i
A tiny worked example with actual numbers
Suppose training budget is 30K steps total. Split: 20K steps CLM, 10K steps MLM.
Steps 1-20K: model predicts next word. After 20K, accuracy on a test task reaches 72%.
Steps 20K-30K: model switches to predicting masked words bidirectionally. Each step improves accuracy 0.8% on average (because weights are warm and MLM is learning the bidirectional part). After 10 steps, gain is 0.8 * 10 = 8%, so final is 72 + 8 = 80%.
Pure MLM for 30K steps would reach only 76% (slow start costs 4% at the end). Pure CLM would reach 74% (hits ceiling early). Biphasic reaches 80%.

The equation is not a formula to optimize; it is the rule for which loss to use at each step. The gain comes from the order, not the math.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the split wrong. If you switch from CLM to MLM too early (5% CLM, 95% MLM), you waste CLM's warmup and still suffer MLM's slow start. If you switch too late (80% CLM, 20% MLM), MLM never has enough steps to climb to its ceiling. The paper shows 50%-50% works well across conditions, but context matters: smaller budgets favor more CLM, larger budgets favor more MLM.
Assuming unidirectional context is fine. CLM is not bad; it is just limited. For tasks requiring full bidirectional context (like reading comprehension or paraphrase detection), pure CLM will always underperform pure MLM at large budgets. Biphasic works because it eventually runs MLM; if you stop too early, you are stuck with CLM's ceiling.
Forgetting to initialize well from existing models. The paper also tests continued pretraining (CPT): starting from an already-pretrained model and extending it. If you initialize from a CLM-pretrained model and add MLM steps, it outperforms continuing from an MLM-pretrained model with more MLM, because CLM gives a head start that MLM can then refine—the combination gains what both approaches offer. But if your initial model is weak, no pretraining strategy fixes it.
Done right. Run CLM for 30-50% of your budget, then MLM for the rest. On downstream tasks, check semantic representation quality (do embeddings cluster similar meanings?), text classification accuracy, and information retrieval ranking. If biphasic consistently tops both single methods, you have captured the advantage.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple text encoder trainer that implements three pretraining modes: pure MLM, pure CLM, and biphasic CLM-then-MLM. Train each on a small corpus (e.g., 10M tokens from an open dataset) with a small model (e.g., 50M parameters) and a fixed budget (e.g., 5K steps total). At every 500 steps, evaluate on a downstream task like semantic similarity (pick two random sentences, measure if the model says they are similar or different). Plot accuracy vs. training step for all three.

downstream_accuracy(task, step) = fraction of validation examples the model judges correctly at checkpoint step
