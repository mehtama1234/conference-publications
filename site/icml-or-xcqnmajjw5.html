TF-FACE: Time-Frequency Fusion for Trajectory Prediction

To predict where traffic will go, split the pattern-finding into two jobs: one that spots long-slow-changes and one that catches quick-wiggles, then pick the right one for each moment.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Predicting where cars and people will move in the next few seconds is hard because their paths have two completely different patterns playing at once: slow, gradual curves that last for seconds, and quick changes that last for a fraction of a second. Existing methods treat time as just one timeline and miss the slow patterns buried inside.

Zero assumption version

The world before this paper

Every second, a car on the highway decides where to go next based on what it just saw. A naive method: look at where cars were in the last half-second and draw a straight line forward, assuming each car keeps going the same way. That breaks instantly because cars turn, accelerate, and change lanes.

The next attempt: use a neural network (a system of weighted rules) trained on real driving data to learn patterns from the past and guess the future. These networks read time as one single timeline — 0.1 seconds ago, 0.2 seconds ago, 0.3 seconds ago — as a continuous sequence. But real driving has two hidden rhythms at once: a slow arc (the car is curving into a lane change over 2 seconds) and a fast fidget (the car's lateral position jitters by a few centimeters every 0.1 seconds). A network that treats time as one stream cannot separate these two rhythms, so it blurs them together and misses the slow arc.

In one sentence. The paper splits pattern-finding into two separate jobs — one looking for slow changes, one for fast jitters — and lets each job be better at its own task because a network trained on pure slow patterns learns to ignore fast noise, and vice versa.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Break the history into frequencies

Take the car's past positions and mathematically separate them into a slow part (long-term arc) and a fast part (high-frequency jitter).

Step 2: Learn each rhythm separately

Train one pathway to spot patterns in the slow part, another to catch patterns in the fast part.

Step 3: Adaptively weight each pathway

At each moment, learn which rhythm matters more and dial up or dial down the slow and fast predictions.

Step 4: Combine the predictions

Blend the slow and fast predictions together, then refine them so they stay consistent with each other.

Step 5: Output the final trajectory

Generate multiple plausible futures (e.g. five different possible next paths), all grounded in both slow and fast patterns.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes

The paper changes how the network divides its internal attention (the weights it assigns to different pieces of history). Instead of one single timeline, it creates two parallel timelines — one tuned to spot slow changes, one tuned to catch fast changes — then learns which timeline to trust in each situation.

the object it moves  =  attention weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: band importance

measured quantity  =  importance of each frequency band

The paper measures which frequency band (slow or fast) matters more at each prediction step. This is not the slow or fast signal itself, but rather a score that says how much the network should rely on slow patterns versus fast patterns right now.

The paper estimates this using a learnable gated attention mechanism. It is a set of numbers that starts out unknown and gets tuned during training. The network learns to turn these numbers up when the slow rhythm is the real driver of motion (e.g. during a long lane change) and turn them down when fast jitter dominates (e.g. during a straight drive with lateral noise).

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Here is why splitting into slow and fast works. In real driving, a car's path is the sum of two independent things: a long-term arc (the car is steering into a lane over 2 seconds, so position drifts slowly) and a short-term fidget (wind pushes the car side-to-side, or the driver micro-corrects, so position jiggles rapidly). A single neural network pathway has a fixed memory window (it looks back, say, half a second). That window is too short to capture the full slow arc, but too long to ignore the fast jitter. The network ends up learning an average of both rhythms, and because the average is not the true driver of either rhythm, it misses both.

By splitting into two pathways — one with a long memory window tuned to slow patterns, one with a short memory window tuned to fast patterns — each pathway can learn its rhythm cleanly. The slow pathway sees the gradual curve and learns that steady steering leads to steady drift, because when steering is held constant, the position change is predictable and smooth. The fast pathway sees the quick wobbles and learns that sudden gusts lead to sudden jerks, because an instantaneous disturbance produces an instantaneous position change. Then, the network learns which rhythm is bigger right now by measuring how much energy is in each frequency band. If the data shows mostly slow movement, the slow pathway gets more weight. If the data is jittery, the fast pathway gets more weight. The final answer is a weighted blend, so the network hedges: it bets on both rhythms, but places more chips on whichever one is truly active.

This works because the mechanism is physically honest. Real motion is the sum of slow and fast — they really are independent. By honoring that independence in the network's architecture, the paper lets the network learn each pattern clearly and then combine them at the right strength.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

The prediction at time T is a weighted sum: (importance of slow band) times (slow pathway output) plus (importance of fast band) times (fast pathway output), where importance scores are learned and conditioned on the history so far.

Then the compact form:

y_T = g_slow(h) * P_slow(x) + g_fast(h) * P_fast(x)

Every symbol:

y_Tthe predicted position at future time T
g_slow(h)importance gate for the slow frequency band, conditioned on history h
P_slow(x)prediction from the slow-frequency pathway, given input x
g_fast(h)importance gate for the fast frequency band, conditioned on history h
P_fast(x)prediction from the fast-frequency pathway, given input x
A tiny worked example
Suppose at a given moment the slow pathway predicts the car will be at position 50 meters.
And the fast pathway predicts position 49 meters (slightly different due to jitter estimates).
The history h suggests we are in the middle of a slow lane change, so g_slow(h) = 0.8 and g_fast(h) = 0.2.
Final prediction: 0.8 * 50 + 0.2 * 49 = 40 + 9.8 = 49.8 meters.
The slow pathway dominates, so the final answer is close to the slow pathway's guess, but nudged slightly by the fast pathway's caution.

The gates g_slow and g_fast are learned numbers that the network adjusts during training; they start random but gradually learn which rhythm predicts the future best.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Miscalibrated importance gates. If the learned gates g_slow and g_fast do not reflect the true ratio of slow to fast motion in the test data (e.g. the training data was mostly highway but the test data is urban stop-and-go), the network will weight the predictions wrong and miss the true pattern.
Frequency splitting does not capture the real physics. In some scenarios, the slow and fast patterns are not independent. For example, if the car is rapidly accelerating, acceleration creates both a slow change in velocity and a fast change in the feedback the driver gets. Splitting by frequency alone will not untangle these.
Real-time inference with incomplete history. The slow pathway needs a long history window to spot the slow arc, but at the very start of a scenario (first 0.5 seconds), there is not enough history yet. The early predictions will be weak.
Done right. When the train and test distributions match (same city, same driving conditions), and the motion truly is the sum of independent slow and fast components, the method cleanly separates them and predicts both well.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A simple demo: generate synthetic car trajectories where the true path is the sum of a slow sinusoidal arc and a fast Gaussian jitter (so the ground truth is known and truly is two independent rhythms). Train three networks: one with a single time-domain pathway (the baseline), one with two pathways but static importance weights (half slow, half fast always), and TF-FACE with learned adaptive gates. Run all three on held-out test trajectories and measure how well each predicts both the slow arc and the fast jitter separately. TF-FACE should win on both because the gates learned to dial up the slow pathway during slow-motion segments and dial up the fast pathway during jittery segments.

accuracy = (slow_arc_error_baseline - slow_arc_error_tfface) + (fast_jitter_error_baseline - fast_jitter_error_tfface)

TF-FACE should show that learned adaptive gates beat static mixing and beat ignoring the split entirely.
