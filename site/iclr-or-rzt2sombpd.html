CTBench: Synthetic Crypto Data That Teaches Trading

CTBench is the first benchmark that tests whether synthetic cryptocurrency price data can train models to spot real trading opportunities, not just match statistical patterns.

Cryptocurrency markets never close. Prices swing wildly. Old patterns break suddenly. When researchers want to test trading strategies, they need realistic fake price data for practice. CTBench says: stop testing fake data against fake metrics. Instead, train on synthetic data and see if it actually wins money on real prices.

Zero assumption version

Old benchmarks measure the wrong thing

Cryptocurrency prices move 24 hours a day, jump violently, and shift mood fast. When you want to test a trading strategy, you need lots of practice data. Real price history is limited. So researchers build fake price generators that copy real prices. But how do you know if the fake data is actually useful?

The old answer: measure how close the fake matches the real. Check if average price, volatility, and correlations are the same. But here is the trap. A fake dataset can look statistically identical to reality and still be useless for trading. A trader cares about one thing: can I use this fake data to train a model that wins money on real prices? Old benchmarks never asked that question.

The real test. Does training on this synthetic data let a model spot real profit opportunities in real prices?

The hidden quantity

Dual-task fidelity: two ways to measure if fake data is worth anything

measured quantity  =  whether fake data is useful for the real job

A hidden quantity buried in the old approach: nobody agreed on what to measure. CTBench names two. First, Predictive Utility: feed fake data to a model, train it, then test it on real prices. How accurate is the model at forecasting? That measures whether the fake preserves temporal patterns (the rhythm of price movement over time) and cross-sectional patterns (how different tokens move together). Second, Statistical Arbitrage: the old method assumes market prices wander randomly but sometimes snap back like a rubber band stretched and released. CTBench fits the residuals (the parts unexplained by trends) to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which models exactly that snapping-back. Then it simulates a trading strategy: whenever the model spots that rubber-band tension, it bets on the snap. Measure the profit. Both scores together reveal the trade-off: can fake data look statistically good AND teach profitable trading, or do those require different choices?

The paper estimates both by running forecasting models and arbitrage backtests on eight different fake-data generators across four distinct market regimes (bull, bear, volatile, stable) on 452 cryptocurrency tokens.

Why it works

Why splitting the test into two jobs reveals the real trade-off

A fake-data generator faces a dilemma. It can optimize to match the statistical snapshot (mean, variance, correlations) of real data. That looks good on a report. But forecasting models do not care about that snapshot. They care about sequences: what pattern follows this pattern. And arbitrage models care about one thing: places where prices diverged from their average and are about to snap back. These are different targets. A method that chases statistical accuracy might ignore the sequences. A method optimized for profit might distort the correlations. CTBench forces both into the light by running both tests.

Here is why this works: when you train a fake-data generator only on statistical measures, you get a generator that fools those measures but not a trader's brain. When you test it only against forecasting accuracy, you measure something different from profit (you might forecast direction but lose money on timing or risk). By forcing the generator to survive both scrutinies, CTBench exposes the honest game: which generator actually makes fake data that teaches a model to win real trades, not just look smart?

Math after the picture

The mean-reversion signal in one equation

First in words:

residual fit to Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process where mean-reverting residuals are used to generate trading signals

Then the compact form:

dr_t = theta * (mu - r_t) * dt + sigma * dW_t

Every symbol:

r_tthe residual (price gap) at time t
thetathe speed of reversion (how fast the snap-back happens)
muthe equilibrium (what the gap wants to return to)
sigmathe noise (randomness in the snap-back)
dW_ta random jiggle in the price
A tiny worked example
Suppose a token's price drifts 8 dollars above its average. theta=0.5 means half that gap closes per day.
Starting gap: 8 dollars. After 1 day, expected gap: 0.5 * (0 - 8) = -4 dollars, so gap shrinks to 4.
After 2 days: 0.5 * (0 - 4) = -2, gap shrinks to 2. The rubber band pulls it back.
A trader bets that the gap will close. If it closes faster, the trade wins. If noise dominates (sigma is large), the snap-back is uncertain and the bet loses.

The equation captures the intuition: when price drifts too far from average, the market pulls it back, but randomness adds uncertainty.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overfitting to the training window A generator can memorize the exact training prices and reproduce them. It looks statistically perfect and teaches forecasting perfectly. But in a new market regime (the test window), the fake data is useless because it does not contain the new regime.
Ignoring tail risk A generator might match average volatility but fail to reproduce the rare, huge crashes that happen in crypto. A model trained on the fake data thinks risk is low. It gets wiped out in real trading when a crash arrives.
Breaking correlations across tokens Some methods generate each token independently. In reality, when Bitcoin crashes, most altcoins crash too. A model trained on uncorrelated fake data makes hedging mistakes.
Done right CTBench exposes these by measuring both forecasting accuracy and real profit across different market regimes. A method fails the test if it trades well in the training regime but profits vanish in the test regime, or if tail risks destroy profits even when forecasting looks good.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Pick a cryptocurrency and a market regime (e.g., bull market March-May, bear market June-August). Hold out recent real prices as the test set. Pick two fake-data generators: one optimized for statistical accuracy, one optimized for forecasting on held-out data. Train each generator on the earlier prices. Use each generator to produce synthetic training data. Train a forecasting model on the synthetic data. Backtest on the held-out real prices. Measure: (1) forecast accuracy (e.g., direction prediction, mean absolute error), (2) simulated trading profit over the test window, (3) drawdown (worst cumulative loss). Compare the two generators.

profit_from_synthetic = sum(position_t * return_t) where position comes from a model trained only on fake data

The demo is successful if the winner is the same on both dimensions, or if it honestly shows the trade-off: high accuracy but low profit, or vice versa.
