ActiveCQ: Active Estimation of Causal Quantities

Asking a model which data points would teach it the most about how the world works — not just about average effects, but any causal question you want to answer.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Figuring out how one thing causes another in the real world is hard and expensive — you need lots of measurements. This paper says: do not ask the model to reduce its overall confusion. Instead, ask it which data points would shrink the uncertainty in the ACTUAL CAUSAL QUESTION you care about (like: does this treatment work for women in city centers?). The paper shows a way to do that using a statistical model called a Gaussian Process, and then learns when best to request each new measurement.

Causal Questions Are Expensive to Answer

When you want to know how one thing causes another (does this medicine work? does this ad conversion rate depend on user age?), you have to run experiments or collect real measurements. Each measurement costs money, time, or both. A beginner's approach is simple: ask the computer which measurements would reduce its overall confusion about the world. But that is backwards — you do not care if the model is confused about things unrelated to your question. You care about shrinking the uncertainty in your specific causal question only.

Prior work focused narrowly on one type of causal question: the average treatment effect across everyone (does this drug work on average?). But real causal questions are richer: does it work for men versus women? For people over 30? In cities? These need a more general tool.

The paper's core move. Ask the model to tell you which measurements would shrink the uncertainty in your actual causal question, using a statistical model that can answer not just averages, but any causal quantity.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train a statistical model on the data you have

Build a Gaussian Process regression model that learns the relationship between inputs and outcomes from your labeled dataset.

Estimate how the confounding variables are spread across the population

Learn how the background factors (age, gender, location) are distributed (how often each combination appears) using conditional mean embeddings — a way to model spread without guessing the exact shape.

Compute how uncertain you are in your causal question

Ask: given what you know, what is the set of possible answers to my specific causal question? Express that uncertainty as a cloud of possibilities.

Score each unlabeled data point by how much it would shrink that cloud

For each candidate measurement in the pool, compute: if we measured this one, how much tighter would the cloud become? Pick the winners.

Measure those data points and repeat

Get the actual outcomes for the chosen points, retrain the model, and go back to step 1 until you have enough confidence in your answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of asking the model to reduce its overall confusion about the world, the paper changes the question: which measurements would shrink the uncertainty in MY specific causal question? This shift means the active learning system (the part that picks which data points to measure) now aims at the right target.

the object it moves  =  the selection target
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: posterior variance in your causal question

measured quantity  =  Posterior variance of the causal quantity

Posterior variance is the size of the cloud of possible answers to your causal question, after seeing the data you have. For instance: you ask is this treatment better for people under 30? The model says it could be anywhere from a 5% improvement to a 40% improvement (because some measurement patterns still fit the data) — that 35-point spread is your posterior variance. A smaller cloud means more certainty, because a narrow range of possible answers lets you make confident decisions (e.g., launch the treatment only if all possibilities show benefit), while a wide range forces caution.

The paper estimates this variance by building a statistical model (a Gaussian Process) that learns the outcome function, then computing how that learned function's uncertainty propagates into uncertainty about your final causal question. When you pick a new measurement, you compute what the new posterior variance would be — and pick the measurement that shrinks it the most.

Why it works

Why shrinking the right uncertainty works

The core insight: when you measure a new data point, it teaches the model in two ways. First, it directly gives you a new outcome value, which refines the regression function. Second, it teaches you something about the distribution of background factors (age, gender, etc.) in your population. Both of those effects flow through your causal question.

Here is the mechanism. Your causal question depends on integrating the outcome over possible values of the confounding factors. If you shrink the cloud of answers to the regression function, and you shrink the cloud of answers to the distribution, both shrink the final answer to your causal question. The paper uses a clever trick: instead of trying to guess the exact shape of the distribution (which is hard), it uses conditional mean embeddings — a way to represent distributions as points in a high-dimensional space where you can compute integrals directly without sampling. This lets you avoid the classic trap of mis-specifying the distribution.

Why is this better than just asking for a random measurement? Random points teach the model things it does not care about (maybe far from where your causal question applies), because they do not target the specific uncertainty you need to shrink. The paper's acquisition function computes, for each candidate point: if we measured this, how much tighter would the posterior variance become? Then it picks the measurements that shrink it the most. This works because you are now aiming directly at the uncertainty that matters for your question, not wasting measurements on reducing confusion about unrelated parts of the world.

Math after the picture

The core principle in one equation

First in words:

posterior_variance_new = posterior_variance_old - expected_information_gain_from_measurement

Then the compact form:

Var[tau | D ∪ {x_new, y_new}] = Var[tau | D] - E_y[Var[tau | D ∪ {x_new, y}]]

Every symbol:

tauyour causal question's answer (e.g., effect for subgroup S)
Dthe data you have measured so far
x_new, y_newthe unlabeled data point and its (unknown) outcome
Var[tau | ...]the cloud of possible answers given the data
E_y[...]average over all possible outcomes the new point could have
A tiny worked example
Suppose your causal question is: effect on people over 40? Current posterior variance: 400 (you could be anywhere from 10% to 40% improvement).
The computer evaluates point A: if we measured it, the variance would drop to 350 (gain: 50). Point B: would drop to 300 (gain: 100). Point C: would drop to 390 (gain: 10).
The computer picks point B because it has the highest expected information gain (100). After measuring it, your new posterior variance is 300, and next round you pick the point with the next-highest gain.

The posterior variance (the size of the uncertainty cloud) shrinks by exactly the expected information gain from the measurement, which is why optimizing the latter solves the former.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Distribution shift between the pool and your target group. If the unlabeled pool looks different from the population you actually care about (e.g., you care about effect on women, but the pool is 80% men), the acquisition function will pick measurements that do not shrink the right uncertainty. The paper's conditional mean embeddings help here because they adapt to the data, but they can still be fooled if the pool is too skewed.
High-dimensional confounding. When there are many background factors, the conditional mean embedding needs more data to estimate accurately. Early on, when you have few measurements, the estimate of the distribution is noisy, and the acquisition function picks based on a fuzzy picture.
Optimizing the wrong variance reduction. If the causal question formula does not match the actual causal assumptions (e.g., you forgot an important confounding variable), shrinking the posterior variance in the wrong formula will not help you reach certainty in the true causal question, because you are solving for the wrong answer — the measurements you pick will shrink uncertainty in a distorted version of the question.
Done right. If the causal model is well-specified, the pool matches the target population, and the confounders are captured, the acquisition function picks measurements in the exact order that teaches you the most about your specific causal question per unit of cost.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Set up a simple causal system: you have input variables (age, income, city, treatment yes/no), an outcome (conversion, recovery, score), and a hidden confounding variable (interest level, which makes both the treatment choice and the outcome higher). Then define a specific causal question: what is the effect of treatment on people with income over $50K? Next, measure a random 10% of the full dataset and train the model. Then run two strategies for picking the next 20 measurements: (1) random selection from the unlabeled pool, (2) ActiveCQ (pick based on posterior variance reduction). For both strategies, track the posterior variance in your causal question over time. ActiveCQ should shrink it much faster, because it picks measurements that directly reduce uncertainty in your specific question, whereas random picking wastes measurements on data that does not inform your target causal quantity.

causal_uncertainty = sqrt(posterior_variance_of_your_question)

The demo should make it impossible to miss: with active selection, you reach confidence in your specific causal question much faster than random picking, because you are asking for measurements that directly teach you what you want to know.
