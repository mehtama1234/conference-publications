In plain terms

Imagine you're standing at the edge of a field and you drop a pebble. It creates a ripple that spreads outward. Now imagine the field has a memory — wherever the ripple reaches, it picks up a value from that location. If you drop pebbles at different times, each one creates its own ripple, and at any point in space and time, what you measure is the sum of all those ripples combined.

This is exactly what convolution does. You have two sequences of numbers. One sequence (call it the input) is a stream of values over time or space. The other sequence (the kernel or filter) is a small pattern you want to detect or apply. Convolution asks: for each position, how much does that pattern match what you see there? The answer depends on the input values around that position weighted by the pattern, all summed together.

Why did we invent this? Because many physical systems work this way. A signal gets mixed through some medium. A model needs to detect a feature by looking at a small window. A system's future state depends on its current state plus past inputs, combined by some known mixing rule. Convolution is the mathematical way to say: slide this pattern across your sequence, measure the match at each position, and sum up the contributions.

The equation
(f*g)(t)=\sum_\tau f(\tau)g(t-\tau)
(f*g)(t)The result of convolving f with g at time or position t — a single number.
f(τ)The value of the input sequence at index or time τ — one element of the input.
g(t-τ)The kernel or filter value at offset (t minus τ) — weights applied to the input.
τA running index that steps through all positions in the input sequence.
Σ_τSum over all values of τ — add up all the weighted products.
Worked example
Convolving two small sequences
Input f = [1, 2, 3], Kernel g = [0.5, 0.3]
At t=1: f(0)·g(1) + f(1)·g(0) = 1·0.3 + 2·0.5 = 1.3
At t=2: f(1)·g(1) + f(2)·g(0) = 2·0.3 + 3·0.5 = 2.1
Result: convolved output is [1.3, 2.1]
Why it shows up

Convolution appears in nearly every machine-learning pipeline because it is the natural way to express how information mixes in the real world. When a computer-vision model scans an image, it slides small filter windows across pixels, summing weighted combinations to detect edges or textures — that is convolution. When a time-series forecaster predicts the next value, it uses past inputs weighted by a learned pattern, summed together — again, convolution. When a diffusion model or modern sequence processor (a stacked architecture) handles sequences, they often use convolution-like operations to mix information across positions.

The papers linked here use convolution to solve problems across robotics, diffusion models, medical imaging, and 3D reconstruction. In every case, the core idea is the same: slide a learned pattern across data, measure how well it matches at each spot, and combine those measurements. This is why convolution is one of the most reused primitives in machine learning.
