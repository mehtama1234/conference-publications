Test-Time Embedding Adjustment to Stop Personalized Images from Losing Context

When you teach an AI image model to recognize a specific person, the learned word for that person eats all the context in the prompt. This paper adds a tiny correction at picture-generation time to keep the person-word behaving normally.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Picture generators can learn to recognize your face or style from a handful of photos. The problem: that learned word becomes so strong it drowns out everything else in the prompt—a request for 'your face wearing glasses and playing guitar' produces just your face, ignoring the glasses and guitar. This paper shows that the root cause is the learned word drifting in meaning and magnitude in the model's internal number-space. The fix is simple and training-free: at generation time, nudge the learned word back toward its original direction and scale using a rotation technique, keeping it balanced with regular words.

Zero assumption version

Personalized faces eat the prompt

Image generators like Stable Diffusion can learn a specific person's face or art style from just a handful of photos. You train the model on 5 photos and it learns a special word (like or ) that represents your unique features. Sounds great—now you can ask for 'a photo of in a tuxedo' and get your face in a tuxedo.

But there's a trap: as the model trains on your photos, the learned word grows stronger and stronger, because the training tries to match your photos as closely as possible—a stronger personalization signal makes it easier to reconstruct your face from the training images. By the end of training, the word for your face is so dominant that it overrides everything else in the prompt. The tuxedo, the background, the pose—all get ignored. You ask for 'a photo of wearing sunglasses and playing guitar' and get just your face, plain and flat.

A beginner might try the obvious fix: just train more carefully or add penalties during training. But the paper identifies a sneakier problem: the learned word has drifted. It's not just that it's stronger—it's that the word itself has shifted in meaning in the model's embedding space (the internal grid of numbers the model uses to represent words), because the training is chasing your photos and reshaping the word's meaning to fit them better. Once drifted, training penalties can't fix a problem that's baked into the word's identity.

The core fix: At the moment of picture generation, nudge the learned word back toward its original meaning and size, using a rotation and scale adjustment—no retraining needed.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the embedding (the list of numbers representing the learned personalization word) at the moment of picture generation. The model itself is not retrained; only the numbers fed into the model are adjusted on the fly.

the object it moves  =  embedding numbers
The hidden quantity

The semantic drift: how far did the word wander?

measured quantity  =  Direction and magnitude of the learned embedding

During training, the learned word (like ) doesn't just get stronger—its direction in the embedding space (which concepts it represents) and its magnitude (how loud it speaks) both drift away from the original word it was meant to personalize, because the model is optimizing the embedding to match your training photos with maximum accuracy, pushing it far from where 'person' normally sits. This drift is what causes semantic collapse: the word no longer behaves like 'person' plus 'new identity,' it becomes something else entirely.

The paper measures this drift implicitly by observing that context words (sunglasses, guitar) are ignored in the output. The fix corrects the drift directly: by aligning the learned token's direction back to the original word's direction (using SLERP, a smooth rotation in high-dimensional space) and scaling the magnitude to be normal-sized, the paper forces the word to behave like a regular word again—it contributes alongside, not on top of, other concepts.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Here's the causal chain: during training, the model learns the word for your face by adjusting its embedding to minimize loss on your photos. But there's no constraint on how far that word can drift from its original meaning or how large it can grow. So it drifts and grows unconstrained. In a multi-word prompt, this massive, off-target word drowns out everything else—the model has learned that the drifted word is the single best predictor of your face, so it ignores guitars and sunglasses.

The paper's insight is that you don't need to retrain to fix a drifted word. At picture-generation time, the word still exists in the model's embedding space. You can simply rotate it back toward the original word's direction (using SLERP, which smoothly interpolates in high-dimensional space) and rescale it to normal magnitude. This is lightweight—it happens after the model is trained, at the last microsecond before image generation—and it works across any personalization method because it doesn't touch the model's weights.

Once the learned word is rotated and rescaled, it behaves like a normal word: it contributes its signal to the image generation, but it doesn't overwhelm the other words in the prompt. The model asks itself, 'OK, I see person, I see sunglasses, I see guitar. I know what this person looks like. How do I combine all three?' instead of 'Person is the only thing that matters.' Context comes back. The image becomes rich.

The equation: adjusting direction and magnitude

First in words:

adjusted_token = normalize( SLERP(learned_token, original_token, alpha) )

Then the compact form:

v_adjusted = normalize( (1 - alpha) * normalize(v_learned) + alpha * normalize(v_original) )

Every symbol:

v_learnedthe embedding of the personalized word after training
v_originalthe embedding of the original semantic target word
normalize(...)divide by vector length so all vectors have magnitude 1
SLERPspherical linear interpolation: smooth rotation in high-dimensional space
alphablend factor, typically small (0.1 to 0.5) to keep most of learned identity
v_adjustedthe corrected embedding fed to the image generation model
A tiny worked example
Suppose the learned embedding vector for <sks> is [0.8, 0.6, -0.2] and drifted direction means it's very different from the original word embedding [0.1, 0.05, 0.1].
Step 1: Normalize learned to unit length: [0.8, 0.6, -0.2] / length(0.987) ≈ [0.81, 0.61, -0.20]
Step 2: Normalize original to unit length: [0.1, 0.05, 0.1] / length(0.141) ≈ [0.71, 0.35, 0.71]
Step 3: Apply SLERP with alpha=0.3 to smoothly rotate learned toward original direction
Step 4: Result is roughly [0.78, 0.56, 0.23]—rotated back and of normal size
The adjusted embedding now points mostly in the original direction, not the drifted one, so it contributes to the image like a normal concept-word.

SLERP is the key: it rotates in the curved space of high-dimensional embeddings, ensuring the word stays on the 'surface' of meaning and doesn't collapse to noise.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overshooting the rotation. If alpha is too large (say 0.8 instead of 0.2), you rotate the word too far back to the original, and you lose the personalization signal entirely. The image stops looking like the person you trained on.
Wrong target word. If you adjust toward the wrong original concept (e.g., rotate toward 'dog' instead of 'person'), you get nonsense. The method assumes you know what the original semantic concept was.
Conflicting contexts. If the prompt asks for things genuinely in conflict with the person's visual identity (like 'a painting of your photo-realistic face'), the adjustment preserves context but can't magic away the conflict. Both signals fight for pixels.
Done right. Choose alpha based on how much the personalization drifted. Typical range is 0.1 to 0.5. Always specify the original semantic target word (person, style, concept) explicitly. The method works best when the prompt has multiple concepts already.

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a personalized image model (Textual Inversion or DreamBooth) on 5–10 photos of a person. Then generate images with two-to-three-concept prompts like 'a photo of my-face wearing sunglasses and playing guitar' and 'a painting of my-face in a tuxedo in the style of Van Gogh.' Compare outputs without test-time adjustment vs. with test-time adjustment applied. Measure how many concepts appear in the generated image and have a human rate how well each concept (the person, the sunglasses, the style) is represented in the output.

semantic_richness = (person_accuracy + secondary_concept_accuracy) / 2; test_time_adjustment should increase this without major loss of person identity

The demo must make it impossible to miss that multiple concepts now appear together, whereas before they competed and one won.
