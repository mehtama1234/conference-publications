TimeOmni-1: Time Series Reasoning in LLMs

A language model trained on carefully labeled time series tasks learns to reason about what causes events and predict what happens next.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Language models excel at text, but struggle when asked to reason about time series data (sequences of numbers over time like stock prices or sensor readings). This paper builds a large model called TimeOmni-1 and a new dataset (TSR-Suite) of 23,000 labeled examples showing the model how to look at time patterns, discover what causes what, forecast future events, and make decisions. Trained on these labeled examples, TimeOmni-1 learns patterns that let it find causes more accurately and forecast more reliably than systems trained only on text.

Zero assumption version

Time series reasoning is missing from large models

Large language models (finished AI systems trained on vast amounts of text) are built to read words and answer questions about them. When you show them sequences of numbers over time, they see only surface patterns—matching numbers to simple questions like 'what was the value at noon'—not reasoning.

A naive approach: feed a time series (a list of prices, temperatures, or sensor values) into a regular language model and ask it 'why did this spike' or 'what comes next'. The model answers with shallow guesses because it was never trained on real examples of cause-and-effect in time sequences. It has no internal understanding of how one event triggers another, or how to forecast based on patterns it discovers. The training data lacks structure: no labeled examples showing what really causes what, or what a good forecast looks like when tested later.

In one sentence. Build labeled examples of time series reasoning tasks, then train a single model to solve all of them.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper creates a training dataset (TSR-Suite) with four types of time series reasoning tasks, each with real answers provided by human experts. It then trains TimeOmni-1 to handle all four task types in a single unified model, instead of building separate models for cause discovery, forecasting, and decision-making.

The hidden quantity

Causality accuracy: seeing what causes what

measured quantity  =  Causality discovery rate

The hidden quantity is how often the model correctly identifies which events in a time series cause which other events. For example, in stock data, the model must learn that high trading volume often comes before a price spike, not the reverse. This is measured as a percentage: out of all true cause-and-effect pairs humans marked, how many does the model find? Low causality rates mean the model sees correlations (two things happen at similar times) but misses real causes (one thing makes the other happen).

The paper measures this by giving the model unlabeled time series and asking it to identify causality. Human experts then check: did the model find the real causes? TimeOmni-1 achieves 64%, while GPT-4.1 reaches only 36%, because TimeOmni-1 was trained specifically on labeled cause-and-effect examples from TSR-Suite.

Why it works

Why training on labeled examples teaches time series reasoning

Large language models are built by showing them billions of text examples and letting them predict the next word. They never see structured time series problems, so they have no internal models of cause-and-effect in sequences. When you ask an untrained model to discover causality, it guesses from surface patterns and gets it wrong.

TimeOmni-1 fixes this by training on 23,000 examples where every cause-and-effect link is explicitly marked. During training, the model sees a time series and a question like 'Which event caused this spike?', then reads the human-labeled answer. By repeating this millions of times, the model learns internal patterns—feature detectors that recognize signatures of real causality (stable leads in timing, consistent magnitude relationships, no confounding factors). When given a new time series at test time, these learned patterns activate to guide the model toward true causes instead of coincidences.

The multi-stage training approach compounds this: first, the model learns basic sequence understanding from simple scenarios. Then it specializes in causality discovery. Next, it learns forecasting (using past patterns to predict future values). Finally, it learns decision-making, which combines causality and forecasts to choose actions. Each stage reinforces the others. By the end, the model has integrated causality, extrapolation, and deliberation into one unified reasoning capability.

Measuring success on causality discovery

First in words:

Accuracy of causality discovery = (number of true cause-effect pairs found) divided by (total number of true pairs marked by humans)

Then the compact form:

Causality_Accuracy = (Pairs_Identified_Correctly / Total_True_Pairs) × 100%

Every symbol:

Pairs_Identified_Correctlycount of cause-effect links the model found that humans also marked as true causes
Total_True_Pairscount of all cause-effect links humans marked as ground truth in the test set
Causality_Accuracypercentage: how many of the true causes did the model find
A tiny worked example
Stock time series has 10 true cause-effect pairs marked by humans: volume→price, earnings→gap, etc.
TimeOmni-1 identifies 8 of these 10 correctly, plus finds 2 spurious pairs (noise→price).
Causality accuracy = 8 / 10 = 80% (or 0.8).
This means: model found 8 out of 10 real causes. Two real causes were missed (false negatives). Spurious pairs don't affect this metric.

The metric counts how many true cause-and-effect patterns the model recovers from time series data, measuring whether training on labeled examples actually teaches causality reasoning.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Causality is bidirectional ambiguity. If price always spikes right after volume spike, the model might learn 'volume causes price' correctly. But if two events always happen together with unknown true order (e.g., high sentiment and high trading occur simultaneously), the model has no way to pick the real direction. No amount of training data fixes ambiguous causality—ground truth itself must be clear.
Confounding: a hidden third event causes both observed events. Both price and volume might spike because earnings were announced. If the training data does not include earnings as a variable, TimeOmni-1 learns 'price causes volume' or 'volume causes price', missing the true explanation. The model is limited by which variables humans measure and feed to it.
New types of time series the model never trained on are harder. TimeOmni-1 trained on stocks and sensors might struggle with new domains (e.g., weather time series with different causality patterns), because the learned patterns are tuned to what training data looked like. Retraining on new labeled examples from the domain helps, because the model can now see examples of how causes and forecasts work in that specific domain. The paper shows improvements over GPT-4.1 on tested domains, but does not test on completely different data, so perfect generalization is uncertain.
Done right: humans annotate clearly, measure both causality and forecasting accuracy separately. Multi-stage training ensures the model learns cause discovery first, then builds decision-making on top of solid reasoning. Regular testing on held-out time series (data the model never trained on) catches overfitting.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test: take a synthetic or real time series (50–100 points) with a known causal structure. For example, a toy stock where trading volume always precedes price spikes by a few minutes, and earnings announcements always precede both. Feed this series to TimeOmni-1 (or a trained model like it) and ask it: 'Which events cause which?'. Have it output a list of discovered cause-effect pairs (edges in a causal graph). Compare against the ground truth (the true causal structure you designed). Count matches. Then forecast the next 10 steps; check if the forecast is closer to the real future than a naive baseline (e.g., repeat the last value).

Causality_Precision = (Correct_Pairs_Found / Total_Pairs_Model_Proposed) × 100%; Forecast_Error = (Mean_Absolute_Error_TimeOmni / Mean_Absolute_Error_Baseline)

A working demo proves the model learned to extract real cause-effect structure, because its causality list matches ground truth and its forecast beats simply repeating the last value—the model reasons about what drives change, not just pattern-matching.
