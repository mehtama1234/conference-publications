When Do Graph Foundation Models Transfer?

A data-centric theory explains why graph foundation models sometimes fail to transfer between domains by measuring structural mismatch through graphon decomposition.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

A foundation model trained on one type of graph should work on other graphs because the learned representations capture general patterns that apply broadly, saving the cost of retraining. But in practice, the model sometimes performs worse on a new graph domain than expected, even catastrophically, because the new domain's structure contradicts the patterns learned on the old domain. This paper asks: what graph properties determine whether transfer succeeds or fails? The answer is structural mismatch, a quantity captured by decomposing how the model's outputs shift between domains into separate parts for sampling noise and for genuine structural difference.

Zero assumption version

Foundation models on graphs don't always transfer

A foundation model is one trained backbone meant to work across many tasks. For graphs, this is attractive: train once on a large graph corpus, then reuse that backbone on new graphs without retraining. But transfer fails unpredictably. A model trained on social networks might work fine on citation networks but completely fail on molecular graphs. Sometimes the new task performs worse than training from scratch (negative transfer).

The naive approach is to blame architecture or how you fine-tune the model. But that misses the real question: what about the *data itself*—the structure of the old and new graphs—determines whether the backbone's learned representation still works? You need to know which graph properties determine transfer success before you can predict or fix it.

The core problem: A fixed representation learned from one graph domain produces unpredictable outputs on a structurally different domain; you cannot know in advance whether transfer will work without measuring the structural mismatch.

What the paper changes

The one thing this paper changes

The paper does not change the model or the training procedure. It changes what the model builder *measures*: instead of assuming transfer is a black box, the paper decomposes the output shift into two measurable parts. One part is inevitable randomness from sampling finite graphs; the other is an intrinsic, relabeling-invariant domain discrepancy—a structural difference that persists no matter how you relabel the vertices.

The hidden quantity

Domain discrepancy: the structural mismatch

measured quantity  =  Intrinsic domain discrepancy

The hidden quantity is the intrinsic domain discrepancy: a single number that captures how structurally different two graph domains are, independent of how you label the vertices. It is relabeling-invariant, meaning swapping vertex names does not change it. This quantity determines how much the model's outputs will shift between domains, after accounting for randomness.

The paper estimates it by decomposing the measured output shift: take the difference in model outputs between training on domain A versus domain B, subtract the part caused by random sampling noise, and what remains is the structural mismatch. The size of this number predicts transfer success. Larger discrepancy means larger output shift and higher risk of negative transfer.

Why it works

Why the decomposition reveals transfer success

A foundation model backbone is a Lipschitz function: small changes to the input graph yield proportionally small changes to the output (the Lipschitz constant bounds how much output can change per unit input change). When you apply this same backbone to a different graph domain, three things happen: (1) you are sampling a different set of vertices and edges, introducing random noise; (2) the graph structure itself is different, so the vertices and edges in domain B have different relationships than in domain A; and (3) how you label vertices does not matter—the structural difference is intrinsic.

The paper uses graphon theory to separate these effects. In the continuous limit, a dense graph becomes a continuous object, and you can decompose the output shift into the part explained by finite-sample randomness and the part caused by structural mismatch. The structural mismatch is what transfers predict: large mismatch means the backbone learned on domain A is not well-suited to domain B, so outputs will shift unpredictably. The decomposition also reveals that positional encodings (the node labels used by the model) play a critical role: spectral positional encodings are stable under domain shift, while eigenvector-based encodings can be brittle, flipping sign or direction when the domain changes slightly.

The decomposition equation

First in words:

Output shift equals finite-sample approximation error plus intrinsic domain discrepancy. The approximation error shrinks as the graph gets larger; the domain discrepancy is relabeling-invariant and permanent.

Then the compact form:

output_shift = finite_sample_error + intrinsic_domain_discrepancy

Every symbol:

output_shiftDifference in model predictions between domain A and domain B
finite_sample_errorRandom noise from sampling finite sets of vertices and edges
intrinsic_domain_discrepancyPermanent structural difference, independent of vertex labels
A tiny worked example
Measure model output on social network domain A: 0.72 accuracy.
Measure same model on molecular domain B: 0.48 accuracy.
Shift observed: 0.72 - 0.48 = 0.24 (24 percentage points).
Estimate sampling noise at this graph size: 0.08.
Subtract noise from shift: 0.24 - 0.08 = 0.16.
Intrinsic domain discrepancy: 0.16. This tells you structural mismatch alone accounts for most of the transfer failure, not randomness.

The decomposition shows you which source—random sampling or genuine structural difference—is responsible for output shift, because knowing the structural component tells you whether collecting larger graphs (to reduce sampling noise) will fix transfer, or whether you need to find a domain closer to the target (to reduce structural mismatch).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Eigenvector-based positional encodings are unstable. If you use eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian as positional encodings, they can flip sign or direction under domain shift, breaking the backbone. Subspace-based encodings are more stable.
Mismatch is invisible if you only measure transfer accuracy. Two domains with the same transfer accuracy might have completely different domain discrepancies—one low discrepancy with good backbone alignment, one high discrepancy masked by fine-tuning luck. Measuring discrepancy directly reveals the real transfer risk.
The theory assumes dense graphs in the continuous limit. For very sparse graphs (social networks, knowledge graphs) the graphon continuous-limit theory may not capture all structural effects, requiring finite-sample corrections.
Spectral positional encodings stay stable. Use spectral-based encodings (eigenvalues rather than eigenvectors) for stable transfer; the paper's analysis guarantees they do not flip or shift unexpectedly when domains change.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple experiment with synthetic graphs of known structure (e.g., block models with tunable community patterns, or random regular graphs with varying degrees). Train a small graph backbone on graphs from domain A (one block structure or degree pattern). Test on graphs from domain B (a different block structure or degree). Measure the output shift and estimate its finite-sample component. Subtract to get the intrinsic domain discrepancy. Vary the structural mismatch (e.g., make domain B more or less similar to domain A) and show that transfer accuracy correlates inversely with measured domain discrepancy.

transfer_success = 1 - (intrinsic_domain_discrepancy / max_observed_discrepancy)

The demo must show that you can predict transfer failure from domain discrepancy alone, without ever fine-tuning on domain B—proving that structure, not architecture, determines transfer.
