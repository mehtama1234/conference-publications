Semantic-Enhanced Time-Series Forecasting via Large Language Models

Teach an LLM to read time series by encoding their patterns directly into the semantic space where it already understands language.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting (predicting stock prices, weather, power usage) usually requires specialized models trained only on numbers. Large language models (LLMs) are masters at finding patterns in text, so researchers tried converting time series into words for LLMs to read. But the gap is too wide: LLMs expect semantic meaning (what words mean), not raw temporal patterns (when and how things repeat). This paper bridges that gap by encoding the hidden rhythms and anomalies of the time series directly into the semantic space (the list of numbers where the LLM stores what it learned from language) before feeding it to the LLM, so the LLM reads time patterns as if they were linguistic structure.

Zero assumption version

Why LLMs stumble on numbers

Time series are sequences of numbers changing over time: stock prices hour by hour, temperature reading by reading, power usage minute by minute. A naive approach: convert these numbers into words and send them to an LLM. The problem is immediate. LLMs learned to find patterns in language: a sentence with the word dog often has the word bark nearby. But numbers in a time series follow a different rhythm. When temperature dips at midnight most nights, the LLM sees just raw words (each number converted to a word); it has no built-in sense of periodicity, no concept of seasonal patterns, no grip on what an anomaly looks like in numerical data. Earlier LLM-based methods tried to align time series words with the LLM's vocabulary one word at a time, but they never bridged the deeper gap: the LLM's semantic space (the meaning-structure it built from language) remains misaligned with the temporal structure (the hidden patterns and anomalies in time data). So forecasts stay weak.

The core problem. LLMs understand language structure but not time-series structure, leaving a semantic gap that breaks forecasting.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the time series

Pass raw numbers through a dedicated module that learns to represent them as a list of values in high-dimensional space (the technical term is embeddings, just a list of numbers per time step).

Extract temporal patterns

Compute which time steps correlate with each other (cross-correlation, meaning how much they move together), and use this to build the periodicity signal (when things repeat) and anomaly signal (when something odd happens).

Fuse patterns into embeddings

Blend the periodicity and anomaly signals into the LLM's semantic embedding space using a special gating mechanism (a learnable soft-selector that decides how much of each signal to mix in).

Augment the focus layer

Inside the LLM's focus layer (the part that weighs which words matter for the next prediction), add a short-term and long-term temporal path: one LSTM (a specialized recurrent cell: each step remembers past values) captures immediate changes, the other captures broad trends.

Freeze and forecast

Keep the LLM weights fixed (no retraining), use only the new temporal layers to produce accurate forecasts; this keeps computation cheap while achieving strong performance.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not retrain the LLM or redesign its core logic. Instead, it changes the input representation: it encodes the time series hidden periodicity and anomaly patterns directly into the semantic space (the list of numbers that represents what the LLM thinks each word means) before the LLM ever sees the words. This deeper input representation gives the LLM the temporal context it needs without requiring any retraining.

The hidden quantity

The semantic gap: how aligned are patterns and meanings?

measured quantity  =  Pattern-to-semantic alignment

Before this paper, LLMs used generic token embeddings: every number was treated as a token, and the embeddings were learned from language structure alone, not from the time series. The hidden quantity this paper measures is how well the time series actual temporal patterns (periodicity, anomalies, trends) are represented in the embedding space. SE-LLM estimates this alignment by computing a cross-correlation matrix between the temporal embeddings (numbers representing the time series data) and the semantic embeddings (the LLM's language-learned representation), then extracting the strongest correlations. This tells the model which temporal features most strongly connect to the semantic structure, and it uses that signal to enhance the embeddings.

Paper reading. The Temporal-Semantic Cross-Correlation (TSCC) module aligns temporal and semantic representations by cross-correlation, computes a cross-correlation matrix M, applies top-k filtering to identify the strongest temporal-semantic correlations as structural guidance, and routes these through an anomaly-modeling VAE (a lossy compressor) to produce enhanced representations.
Why it works

Why encoding patterns into embeddings lets frozen LLMs forecast

The core insight is that an LLM's semantic space (the high-dimensional numbers representing meaning) is already optimized to find structure. When you feed it a representation that already encodes the time series temporal structure (its periodicity and anomalies), the LLM's existing machinery for finding patterns in dense number-lists immediately activates. No retraining needed. Here is the causal chain: (1) Raw numbers lack temporal context, so generic representations are weak. (2) The paper extracts the periodicity (when patterns repeat) and anomaly signals (when spikes occur) from the data itself, not from language. (3) These signals are merged into the semantic space through a gating mechanism (a soft selector that learns how much of each signal to blend). (4) Now the representation carries dual information: the LLM's semantic knowledge plus the series temporal structure. (5) The frozen LLM reads this fuller representation and immediately uses its existing pattern-finding machinery to forecast. Additionally, the Time-Adapter module (small LSTM chains plugged into the focus layer) provides explicit short-term and long-term temporal reasoning without retraining the LLM. The frozen LLM's weights already encode general language structure; adding temporal semantics lets it apply that structure to time series without catastrophic forgetting.

Math after the picture

The core formula: cross-correlation alignment

First in words:

Correlation matrix M = normalize temporal embeddings dot-product with transposed semantic embeddings, averaged across all positions. Top-k entries select the strongest structural priors. Enhanced embedding = original embedding plus gated blend of anomaly and periodicity components.

Then the compact form:

M(i,j) = Norm(T_i) * Norm(S_j)^T ; D_A/D_C <- D_A/D_C * (1/n) * sum_i P(S_i, m_i^top-k)

Every symbol:

T_ithe i-th temporal embedding vector (time series data in high-dimensional space)
S_jthe j-th semantic embedding vector (LLM language space)
M(i,j)correlation strength between temporal position i and semantic dimension j
Norm(.)normalize the vector so its length equals 1
P(S_i, m_i^top-k)probability of the top-k strongest correlations for position i
D_Athe anomaly semantic signal (captures spikes and outliers)
D_Cthe normal/cyclical semantic signal (captures periodicity)
nthe number of time steps in the series
A concrete tiny example
Suppose a 4-step time series: prices at times 1,2,3,4 with values 100, 102, 99, 101 (gentle up-down-up pattern).
Temporal embeddings learned from data: matrix T row-by-row is [0.5,-0.1] then [0.6,0.2] then [0.4,-0.3] then [0.7,0.1].
Semantic embeddings from LLM: matrix S row-by-row is [0.3,0.9] then [0.8,0.2] then [0.1,0.8] then [0.6,0.5].
Cross-correlations: when normalized, step 1 aligns best with semantic dimension 1 (both lean positive).
Top-k picks the 2 strongest: correlations at rows 1,3 and column 1; mark them as periodicity priors.
Enhanced embedding for step 1: original [0.5,-0.1] plus 0.8 weight of periodicity signal, giving [0.7,0.1] (stronger temporal signal).
Same for all steps: cross-correlation identifies which time-pattern aspects match semantic dimensions, and gating learns how much to amplify each signal.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Misaligned periodicity signal. If the data has no clear repeating pattern (e.g., a one-off random walk), the periodicity extraction finds noise. The gating mechanism tries to blend this noise into the embedding, weakening the forecast. Fix: preprocess to remove or handle non-stationary trends.
Anomaly confusion. The anomaly signal is built from historical spikes. If a genuinely new kind of anomaly appears (regime shift, black swan event), the model has no prior knowledge of it. The VAE (a lossy compression method) may misclassify the shift as normal noise, producing a wrong forecast.
Over-gating the signals. The gating mechanism learns how much periodicity and anomaly to blend in. If it learns to over-weight anomalies, the embedding becomes noisy and forecasts become erratic. Underfitting happens if both signals are down-weighted.
Done right. SE-LLM works best on time series with clear seasonal patterns (daily, weekly, yearly cycles) and detectable anomalies that repeat. The pre-computed top-k correlations act as a structural prior, stabilizing the blend; the frozen LLM's existing semantic space provides regularization (it avoids learning junk).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple forecasting task: take a multi-month stock price or temperature dataset (e.g., 500 time steps, split into training and test). Run SE-LLM on it, comparing against (1) naive LLM baseline (raw numbers as tokens) and (2) a classical method (e.g., ARIMA or simple transformer). Measure forecast accuracy (mean squared error, MSE) on the held-out test set. The demo should also visualize the learned cross-correlation matrix M: darker cells show which time steps correlate most with which semantic dimensions. Inspect whether the gating mechanism learned sensible weights (periodicity signal > anomaly signal for clean seasonal data, or vice versa for noisy data).

MSE = mean((actual_price - forecast_price)^2) ; accuracy improvement = (MSE_baseline - MSE_SE-LLM) / MSE_baseline * 100%

The demo must make it impossible to miss: encoding temporal patterns into semantic space allows a frozen, language-trained LLM to forecast time series as accurately as models built from scratch for numbers alone, because the LLM already knows how to find patterns in dense number-lists (it learned this from language), so once the temporal structure is embedded in that space, the LLM can immediately use its existing machinery without retraining, all while keeping compute cheap.
