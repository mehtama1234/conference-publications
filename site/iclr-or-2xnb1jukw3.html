AlphaAlign: Proactive Safety Reasoning Through Reinforcement Learning

AlphaAlign teaches language models to reason about safety dangers explicitly using reinforcement learning, rather than simply learning to refuse.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models have absorbed safety knowledge during training — they can tell the difference between safe and dangerous requests — but current methods teach them to refuse by rote, which breaks their helpfulness or makes them refuse harmless requests too. AlphaAlign treats safety like a solvable problem: the model thinks through why something is dangerous, then refuses or helps accordingly. It does this by rewarding two things at once: explicit safety reasoning that gives reasons for refusing harm, and high-quality answers to safe questions.

Zero assumption version

Models Learn to Refuse, Not to Reason

Language models have seen thousands of examples of harmful requests and safe responses during training. They have absorbed the pattern: this request is dangerous, that one is harmless. Yet when trained to be safe, models usually take shortcuts. Either they learn a shallow rule—'refuse anything that looks like it might be bad'—which makes them refuse harmless questions too (like 'how do I remove rust from my car?'). Or they need detailed examples of a human explaining the danger, which is expensive and doesn't teach them to think for themselves.

The beginner's naive approach: just show the model lots of good refusals and let it memorize the pattern. This works poorly because the model copies the surface-level trick (saying 'I can't help with that') instead of learning to spot actual danger—it's like memorizing the shape of traffic signs instead of understanding why they mean danger. It also breaks: a variant of the harmful request slips through, or the model refuses a benign question because it sounds vaguely similar.

The paper's one-sentence move. Instead of teaching refusal by example, reward the model when it thinks through the reason a request is dangerous, then refuses with that reasoning explicitly written.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

RL training loop starts

Model generates output: safety reasoning, then final answer (refusal or response).

Safety verifier checks output

Did the model refuse something dangerous, or answer something safe? Yes/no signal only.

Dual reward computed

Safety reward: +1 for correct decision (refuse harm, answer safe question). Format reward: +1 if structure is right.

Helpfulness bonus

For safe questions, add reward only if answer quality exceeds the average; this way the model learns to give better-than-usual responses, not mediocre ones, so it stays helpful while staying safe.

Nudge weights toward high-reward outputs

Adjust the weights (the model's internal numbers) to make high-reward outputs more likely next time, low-reward outputs less likely.

What the paper changes

The Single Thing That Changes

AlphaAlign changes one thing: the training objective. Instead of supervised classification (learn to label requests as safe/unsafe), the model now receives a scalar reward signal (the dual-reward sum) for each full output it generates. This reward incentivizes the model to produce two things together: explicit written reasoning about safety, and the correct final action (refuse or answer).

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: How Much the Model Reasons About Safety

measured quantity  =  how much the output mentions specific safety concerns

The paper measures a hidden quantity that determines whether the model is actually reasoning about safety or just mimicking a refusal template: How many words in the model's output are explicitly reasoning about the danger? A truly aligned model should mention specific harms, explain why something violates safety principles, or articulate the actual risk. A shallow-aligned model repeats 'I can't help' without explaining why—because it never had to develop understanding; it just learned the surface pattern.

AlphaAlign estimates this by tracking whether the model's generated output contains words related to understanding the harm (such as 'dangerous', 'harm', 'violate', 'risk', 'illegal', or reasoning phrases) versus generic refusal words. The paper counts this indirectly by observing that RL-trained models show more diverse, reasoning-heavy outputs than supervised models.

Why it works

Why the Dual Reward Works

AlphaAlign works by creating a situation where the only way to maximize reward is to reason. The safety reward gives +1 only when the model makes the correct decision (refuse harm or answer safe questions). A model that refuses everything scores 0 on safe questions—it loses the reward for those, so it learns refusing is not always right. A model that helps everything gets punished on harmful requests—it earns 0 reward instead of the +1 it could get by refusing, which means it gives up accumulated reward it could have earned, training it to do better next time. So both extremes leave reward on the table, and the model must learn to tell the difference between harmful and safe to maximize its total reward.

The format reward enforces structured output: safety reasoning block, then final answer. This forces the model to write out its thinking rather than hide it—it cannot claim to have reasoned if it skipped the reasoning block. The normalized helpfulness reward for safe questions is only positive if the answer exceeds average quality, so the model learns to give better responses (not just any response), which means it does not shortcut to lazy safe refusals.

The key difference: the model is not shown examples of 'correct safety reasoning.' There is no supervised dataset saying 'here is how an aligned model should reason.' The model must invent the reasoning itself to earn the reward. This is why it learns deep alignment: it has no choice but to develop its own understanding of harm.

Math after the picture

The Reward Formula

First in words:

total_reward = safety_reward + format_reward + helpfulness_reward, where safety_reward checks if refusal decision matches the true label, format_reward checks output structure, and helpfulness_reward is positive only when safe-question answers exceed average quality

Then the compact form:

R_total = r_s + r_f + r_h, where r_s = 1 if decision matches label else 0, r_f = 1 if format correct else 0, r_h = max(0, quality_score - avg_quality)

Every symbol:

R_totalthe total reward for this output
r_ssafety reward: +1 for correct refusal/answer decision
r_fformat reward: +1 if reasoning block is structured correctly
r_hhelpfulness reward: only non-negative for above-average safe answers
quality_scorehow good the answer is on a safe question (rated by LLM verifier)
avg_qualitythe average quality score across all safe-question answers so far
A tiny worked example
Scenario A: Harmful query, model refuses with reasoning. r_s=1 (correct), r_f=1 (format good), r_h=0 (not applicable). Total=2.
Scenario B: Safe query asking for cooking help. Model gives detailed recipe. Quality_score=8, avg_quality=5. r_s=1 (correct decision), r_f=1 (format correct), r_h = max(0, 8-5) = 3. Total=5.
Scenario C: Safe query, model gives mediocre answer. Quality_score=4, avg_quality=5. r_s=1 (correct decision), r_f=1 (format correct), r_h = max(0, 4-5) = 0. Total=2.
Policy gradient nudges weights to repeat Scenario B (reward=5), less so Scenario C (reward=2). Model learns to reason and provide quality help.

The verifiable reward removes the need for supervised examples of safety reasoning; the model develops it to earn reward.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad safety verifier. If the binary check (is this refusal correct?) is itself flawed, the model learns to game it. A model might refuse in an elaborate way that satisfies the verifier but does not reflect real safety understanding.
Weak incentive to reason. If the format reward is too lenient or the verifier accepts terse refusals, the model can still earn high reward without reasoning explicitly. The structure must genuinely enforce reasoning.
Over-correction on helpfulness. If the normalized helpfulness reward is too strong, the model might focus on giving excellent answers to safe questions and deprioritize safety reasoning. The balance between the two rewards must be tuned carefully.
Done right. The paper calibrates verifier strictness, format requirements, and reward weights through ablation. The combination of binary reward with structured output makes gaming and shortcuts detectable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should train a small language model on a safety task (a dataset with harmful and benign prompts, each labeled) using AlphaAlign's dual-reward RL objective for fewer than 200 steps. Measure three things: (1) accuracy on refusing harmful requests, (2) accuracy on answering safe requests (not over-refusing), (3) what kinds of words appear in the reasoning block and how long it is. Show that the model's reasoning mentions specific harms, not generic refusal. Compare against a baseline trained with supervised examples of refusals. The demo proves that explicit reasoning emerges without being taught.

alignment_quality = (accuracy_refuse_harm + accuracy_answer_safe) / 2, reasoning_depth = fraction_of_safety_reasoning_tokens_in_output

The core insight to make vivid: a model trained with verifiable reward for explicit reasoning develops safety understanding, not refusal shortcuts. Fewer than 200 RL steps suffice because the model already knows the concepts; the reward just tells it to show its thinking.
