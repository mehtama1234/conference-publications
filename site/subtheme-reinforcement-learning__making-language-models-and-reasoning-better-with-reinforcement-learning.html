Making language models and reasoning better with reinforcement learning

Using reinforcement learning techniques to help large language models reason through problems step-by-step, improve their reasoning chains, or align their outputs with human preferences.

What this is

Large language models can generate text, but they often make mistakes or stop reasoning too early. The problem is: a model trained just to predict the next word doesn't know when it's solving the problem right. It needs feedback that says 'yes, that reasoning chain was correct' or 'no, that answer is wrong.' Reinforcement learning can provide that feedback. Instead of asking a human to write the perfect answer for every task, researchers score each attempted solution — a chain of reasoning steps that led to a correct answer gets high score, a wrong answer gets low score. The model learns to repeat what gets high scores.

The trick is that language models think in sequences — one token (word chunk) at a time — and the reward for the whole chain comes only at the end. So the model has to learn which earlier steps contributed to the final success, not just copy the final answer. It's like learning to play chess by playing games and only knowing at the end whether you won, but having to remember which moves in the middle mattered. Papers in this area train models to generate reasoning chains step-by-step, then score the full chain, then adjust what the model learns to favor high-scoring chains.

This matters because it lets AI systems tackle harder problems. Instead of answering yes-or-no questions instantly, they can walk through multi-step math, trace logic, or plan actions. The reinforcement learning component keeps them from just memorizing text that looks like reasoning — it makes them solve the actual problem.

How the work breaks down

Language models need feedback to reason better because a model trained just to predict text doesn't know when it solved the problem correctly. The papers here split into four directions: (1) making reasoning steps generate step-by-step with RL feedback so the model learns which steps matter, which forces the model to commit to intermediate answers rather than gloss over hard parts; (2) scaling that training across multiple agents and policy variants so training stays stable at scale, because adding more data or more learning agents often breaks everything unless you coordinate carefully; (3) understanding how to shape rewards and values so the model can tell if it's on the right track sooner, which cuts down the feedback loop from end-of-sequence to mid-sequence; and (4) applying these ideas to robotics and game-playing where the environment is already structured so the feedback is unambiguous, which serves as a clean testbed for reasoning ideas.

Making language models reason step-by-step

Language models trained just to predict text don't learn when to stop thinking or how to backtrack when they get stuck. These papers tackle how to teach a model to reason by generating one step at a time and getting feedback on the full chain. The hard part is: the reward only comes at the end, but the model has to credit which middle steps mattered. It's like learning chess by playing full games—you win or lose at the end, but you need to know which moves in the middle made the difference.

RL of Thoughts does inference-time planning by training a tiny navigator (under 3,000 weights) to steer where the model should look next, letting small 7-billion-weight models match huge 100-billion-weight reasoning because the navigator learns to amplify the model's best reasoning paths. Entropy Regularizing Activation works differently—it boosts reasoning by adjusting how the model's internal neurons fire, getting a 37.4% score jump on a hard math benchmark just by changing activation entropy, not training new weights, which means the model's reasoning capacity was already there but activation patterns were blocking it. ARES also controls entropy but at the token level, learning which steps matter most for different problem types (math vs. logic vs. pictures), so it only spends computation where it helps, and CGPO uses the curve of the reward signal—steeper curves get more training—to reason better across math, code, science, and even creative writing, which amplifies the feedback signal when the model is learning fastest. Together these show that reasoning emerges when the model learns which steps were worth the computation.

Scaling reasoning across agents and policies

Training one language model to reason is hard; training many of them to reason together is harder. These papers ask: how do you coordinate multiple agents' policies? How do you learn from data where the expert's behavior is very different from your own (off-policy)? How do you pick which expert moves to imitate when the world is unpredictable?

Jackpot solves the off-policy problem: when the training data is stale (128 times older than the model's current policy), standard learning breaks down because the feedback signal is from old behavior that the model has moved away from, but Jackpot keeps the actor and policy output spread aligned and stable, gaining 20% on one benchmark and 8% on another without learning getting destabilized, which means the model can learn from old data safely. Multi-Agent Guided Policy Optimization proves it can improve monotonically when many agents contribute at once, validated on 43 different tasks, which is rare because adding more agents often causes instability from conflicting feedback. In-Context Learning for Pure Exploration addresses structured search—when you don't know the rules but can try things—and learns to explore efficiently without explicitly modeling what you're searching for, so the model saves computation by learning what questions to ask rather than building a world model. Enhancing Geometric Perception in VLMs adds multimodal reasoning: vision-language models trained to see shapes, angles, and spatial relationships better with RL jump 26.5% on in-domain tasks and even 39% on downstream reasoning, because RL's feedback helps models ground language in visual patterns rather than just learning text statistics.

Reward shaping and value learning

Knowing what gets a high score isn't enough—the model needs to estimate value (how much reward do I expect from here?) and shape that signal to speed up learning, because telling the model 'you won at the end' doesn't help it know which middle steps to repeat. These papers tackle how to build better value functions and make rewards tell the right story, so learning becomes faster and more stable, which means the model can learn from fewer examples and not crash into bad local optima.

Value Flows redesigns how distributional RL (tracking not just expected reward but the full spread of outcomes) can learn in continuous control, beating methods from before by 30% on success rate across 62 benchmarks because tracking uncertainty helps the model avoid tricky states where things go wrong unpredictably. Policy Newton Algorithm uses second-order calculus in reproducing kernel Hilbert space to get local quadratic convergence—rare in RL—using only kernel tricks, not neural networks, so it converges faster than standard first-order methods without extra computation. Scalable In-Context Q-Learning learns value functions that adapt in-context, working especially well when the data isn't optimal (you only have suboptimal rollouts), which is realistic because good training data is expensive. MVR: Multi-view Video Reward Shaping learns rewards from watching videos of the correct behavior (without action labels), and applies this on robot arm tasks and humanoid walking, so humans can just show solutions without annotating every action. Does Differentiable Simulators Give Better Policy Updates looks at physics models and finds the noise in learning updates depends on a recipe: learning rates, weight decay, batch sizes, and data augmentation mixed together—getting the recipe right is the whole game because small changes in the combination flip whether learning works. Look-ahead Reasoning with Learned Models in Imperfect-Information Games learns both the exact game rules and when to use a simpler model, showing that planning with incomplete information can beat hand-coded heuristics because adaptive models know when simplification helps.

Context, diversity, and world models

Some reasoning happens inside the model's world model—an internal simulation of the problem. These papers ask: when does a model learn to adapt on the fly (in-context learning)? How do you solve a new problem with no examples to learn from?

Context and Diversity Matter shows that in-context learning (the ability to learn from examples in the prompt) only works when the context window is long and the model has seen many different environments during training. Without diversity, the model overfits to one style. Solving General-Utility Markov Decision Processes in the Single-Trial Regime tackles the extreme case: you have one chance to solve a task, no replay, no second attempts. The paper shows you can plan with a learned world model even when you get only one trajectory, matching specialized baselines.
