Generalised Flow Maps for Few-Step Generative Modelling on Riemannian Manifolds

A method to teach generative models on curved geometric spaces to create samples in just one or two steps by learning a direct shortcut map instead of many small steps.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Generative models that create data on curved geometric surfaces (like protein shapes or Earth surfaces) normally need many steps of simulation to produce good samples, because they follow paths that bend with the shape. This paper introduces a fast shortcut: instead of taking many steps along the curve, the model learns to jump directly from random noise to a finished sample in one or two leaps, even when the space is curved.

Zero assumption version

Curved spaces are slow

When generative models work on curved surfaces (picture a sphere, a donut, or a twisted rope), they follow paths that bend with the shape. Traditional generative models like diffusion models take many tiny steps down this curved path—one hundred, two hundred, or more—because each step must be small enough to stay true to the curve and not poke through the surface.

A beginner might think: why not just sample random data and transform it directly to the shape we want, without all the intermediate steps? The catch is that on curved surfaces, you cannot just draw a straight line from noise to the target. The path must curve with the space itself. So if you skip steps, the model does not know how to land accurately.

The constraint. On curved spaces, each step of generation must stay on the surface, so many steps are needed to reach the final sample safely.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper teaches a neural network to learn a direct map (a function) that jumps from random noise to finished samples on curved geometric spaces. This map is called a flow map. Instead of changing how the model takes steps, the paper changes what the model is asked to learn: not a single step to repeat many times, but the full end-to-end transformation in one or two leaps.

the object it moves  =  flow map
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Path Efficiency

measured quantity  =  path efficiency

Path efficiency is the ratio of computation spent in slow steps to the quality gained from each step. When a model takes 100 steps, each step contributes a little progress toward the target. The question is: how much of that progress is wasted because steps are too cautious, or wrongly aimed? The paper does not measure this directly, but it implicitly optimizes it by training a flow map to condense all that progress into one or two big leaps.

The paper estimates path efficiency by checking whether the learned one-step or two-step samples match the quality (log-likelihood and sample fidelity) of 100-step samples. If the shortcut samples are as good, then the slow path was inefficient.

Why it works

Why the shortcut works

Diffusion models normally work like this: start with pure noise, then iteratively clean it up. On a curved space, each cleanup step must be a tiny nudge in the right curved direction, so you need many steps. But if you have a pre-trained slow model, you can ask it: what is the target sample for this noise? The answer is the end-to-end transformation. The paper trains a new fast model to mimic that answer in a single step or two.

Here is the trick: the fast model does not learn from ground truth samples. Instead, it learns from the slow model itself, in a process called self-distillation. The slow model (called the teacher) predicts what the target should look like. The fast model (the student) learns to match that prediction. But the student also gets its own guesses checked against the slow teacher, so it can correct itself. This means the fast model learns to compress the entire curved journey into a leap that lands in the same place.

The reason this works on curved spaces is that the paper extends the flow-matching mathematics to use the proper curved distance metric. Instead of measuring errors in a straight line, it measures them along the curve. This ensures that when the fast model learns its shortcut, it still bends with the shape of the space.

The equation

First in words:

The loss function teaches the fast model to match the slow model's predictions. For each point on the path between noise and target, the model learns to predict where that point goes. The prediction error is measured using the curved geometry's distance metric, not a flat line.

Then the compact form:

L_GFM(theta) = E[ d_g(X_theta(x), X_teacher(x))^2 ] where X_teacher is the pre-trained slow model, X_theta is the fast student network, and d_g is the curved distance metric.

Every symbol:

X_theta(x)the fast model's prediction of where input x goes
X_teacher(x)the slow model's prediction (the target the student learns from)
d_gthe distance measured along the curve, not in a straight line
E[ ]the average over many training examples
thetathe fast model's internal numbers (weights)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a point starts at angle 0 degrees on a circle (noise) and the true endpoint is 90 degrees.
The slow teacher predicts: endpoint is at (x=0, y=1), which is correct for a unit circle at 90 degrees.
The fast student first predicts: endpoint at (x=0.3, y=0.9), which is at angle ~72 degrees (wrong).
The curved distance between 90 degrees and 72 degrees is about 1.05 radians on the circle.
The loss is 1.05^2 = 1.10, telling the network its prediction was off.
After training updates, the student learns to predict (x=0.05, y=0.99), now at angle ~87 degrees.
New loss is (0.17)^2 = 0.03, much smaller, so the network improved.

The network learns to jump directly to the right endpoint by minimizing the gap between its own guess and the slow teacher's answer, measured along the curved surface.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Teacher is bottleneck. If the slow teacher model itself is inaccurate (e.g., trained on bad data), the fast student learns the teacher's errors. The shortcut is only as good as the teacher's guidance.
Metric must be right. The curved distance measurement must match the real shape. If the metric is wrong, the network learns to respect the wrong shape, and samples drift off the true surface.
Instability during training. Learning the shortcut can bounce between contradictory goals (staying close to the teacher while also reaching realistic data). The paper uses stop-gradient tricks (freezing part of the teacher) to stabilize it.
Done right. When the metric is correct and the teacher is good, the flow map learns a stable shortcut that produces samples as diverse and realistic as the slow model, but 50-100 times faster, because the network distills all the knowledge from the teacher's 100 steps into a single leap that lands at the same target.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of points on a sphere or torus (a curved surface). Train a slow diffusion model on it that takes 100 steps. Then train a flow-map shortcut model to jump directly to the target in one step, using the slow model as a teacher. The demo should show that both models produce samples in the same cloud, but measure how fast each one is.

Quality parity = (one_step_model_error / one_hundred_step_model_error); Speedup = (time_for_100_steps) / (time_for_1_step)

If the one-step model produces samples as good as the 100-step model (error within 10 percent), and runs 50 times faster, the demo proves that the shortcut compressed the path without losing precision, because it means the information from 100 careful steps fit into one bold jump that still lands in the right place.
