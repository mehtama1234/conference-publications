Small Language Models with Geometric Constraints for Taxonomy Expansion

Fine-tune small language models by pushing their token embeddings into hyperbolic space, which naturally fits hierarchies, making them outperform much larger frozen models at expanding scientific taxonomies.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Large language models like GPT-4o cost a lot to run and often invent taxonomy nodes that do not exist. A cheaper option is to fine-tune a small language model like DistilBERT (110 million weights), but small models struggle to preserve tree structure. SS-Mono solves this by reshaping how a small model encodes meaning using a curved mathematical space (hyperbolic space) that naturally fits hierarchies. The result: a small model that beats much larger frozen models on real taxonomy tasks.

Zero assumption version

The scaling trap: bigger is fragile, smaller is stuck

Scientific taxonomies are trees: a root (e.g., Biology), branches (Animals, Plants), sub-branches (Mammals, Birds), and leaves (Cat, Dog). To expand a taxonomy, a model must add new nodes at any level and put them in the right place—a task that requires understanding the hidden structure.

The naive approach: use a big frozen model. GPT-4o knows a lot, but it hallucinates (making up taxonomy nodes that do not exist) and costs money to run. A cheaper alternative is a small model like DistilBERT, which is cheaper because it has fewer weights to store and runs faster on consumer hardware; fine-tuning adapts its knowledge to taxonomy tasks at lower cost. But small models normally learn to encode meaning on a flat map—a grid where all directions are equal. Hierarchies do not fit on a flat map; they spread outward like cones. Small models trained on flat maps either forget the hierarchy or distort it.

The core problem: Small models trained normally encode hierarchies in flat space, which crumples the structure; big models hallucinate; nobody gets both cheap and correct.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Augment taxonomy locally

Use a big model to suggest new candidate nodes around existing leaves; keep only high-confidence ones to reduce hallucination.

Prepare training pairs

From the expanded taxonomy, build examples: (node description, parent name, depth in tree) as training targets.

Apply cone constraint during training

Add a cone-distance loss term: penalize word encodings that sit in flat space, reward those that fit the cone shape.

Fine-tune the small model end-to-end

Minimize both the task loss (predicting parent and depth) and the cone loss; word encodings bend toward the cone shape.

Calibrate big model outputs

Refine the candidates by running them through a consistency check using the big model; keep only candidates the big model re-affirms.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper reshapes how a small model encodes the meaning of each word (as a list of numbers inside the model). Instead of using a flat map, it bends this space into a cone shape, where distance grows much faster as you move outward—perfect for fitting trees, because trees naturally have a tight root and spreading branches, which matches the cone's tight tip and spreading base.

the object it moves  =  How word meanings are encoded in space
The hidden quantity

Cone distance: how far apart are word meanings in curved space?

measured quantity  =  Cone distance from the surface

On a flat map, distance measures steps on a grid. On a cone surface, distance measures steps where each step outward covers more ground the farther you go. A parent word meaning sits close to the root; child meanings sit farther out. The cone distance is much larger than flat distance would predict, giving the hierarchy room to separate.

The paper estimates cone distance by measuring how far each word encoding sits from the true cone surface. If an encoding is exactly on the surface, the constraint is satisfied. If it is off the surface (sitting in flat space), a loss term nudges it back onto the cone.

Plain reading. A curved cone surface is a different kind of map from a flat grid. On a cone, distances grow much faster as you move away from the tip, so small, tightly-packed clusters near the root can spread into large, separate clusters at the leaves, because the cone expands outward geometrically—the circumference grows as you move away from the tip, so nodes can spread farther apart without crossing each other. This is exactly how a scientific taxonomy looks.
Paper reading. The paper uses a mathematical model that embeds word encodings in a special curved space. Each encoding is pulled toward lying exactly on the cone surface during training, measured by how far it sits off the surface. This cost, called the geometric loss, teaches the model to encode hierarchies naturally.
Why it works

Why cone-shaped space fixes small models on hierarchies

A small model trained normally learns to encode meaning on a flat map, where all directions are treated equally. A node deep in a taxonomy sits just as far from its siblings as a root node does, because flat maps have no preferred center. When the model tries to place a new leaf node, it has nowhere unambiguous to put it.

By adding a cone-shaped constraint, the paper changes the cost of encoding a hierarchy. In cone space, the penalty for placing a non-root node near the root is very high; the penalty for spreading children outward is very cheap. The training loss now naturally prefers hierarchical encodings, because the cone shape makes hierarchy inexpensive and non-hierarchy expensive. When the model fine-tunes, it automatically stretches parent-child relationships and separates siblings. The small model learns the structure without being told to, because the cone surface makes hierarchy fit naturally. Larger gaps between levels emerge not from explicit penalties on depth, but from the cone shape itself: children simply do not fit close to parents.

The loss that shapes word encodings

First in words:

Total loss equals task loss plus a weighted cone-distance loss that penalizes encodings sitting in flat space.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_task + lambda * L_cone L_cone = sum_i (distance from encoding_i to cone surface) squared

Every symbol:

L_totalcombined training loss
L_taskloss for predicting the correct parent and depth (standard classification loss)
L_conecone-distance loss: how far encodings sit from the cone
lambdastrength of the cone-shaping constraint (higher turns up the shaping)
encoding_ithe list of numbers representing the i-th word or node
distance to surfacehow far the encoding sits from the true cone surface
A tiny worked example
Suppose a parent encoding sits 0.1 units from the cone surface and a leaf encoding sits 0.8 units away.
Cone-distance loss for parent: 0.1 squared = 0.01
Cone-distance loss for leaf: 0.8 squared = 0.64
If lambda is 0.5, contribution to total loss: 0.5 times (0.01 plus 0.64) equals 0.325
Training nudges both encodings back onto the cone; after a few steps, the leaf encoding is 0.1 from the surface too, reducing loss to 0.5 times (0.01 plus 0.01) equals 0.01.
The model learns that staying on the cone surface is cheaper than drifting into flat space.

The cone-distance constraint acts like a rubber band tied to the cone surface, gently pulling word encodings back into the tree shape.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Cone constraint too weak. If lambda is too small, the cone-distance loss barely influences training. Encodings drift back into flat space and hierarchy collapses. Prediction of depth becomes random.
Cone constraint too strong. If lambda is too large, training is dominated by the cone shape and ignores the actual task (predicting parent correctly). The model learns a perfect cone that has nothing to do with the real taxonomy.
Augmentation introduces noise. If the big model suggests incorrect candidate nodes during augmentation and they make it into the training set, the small model learns wrong hierarchical relationships. The cone constraint cannot fix bad data.
Non-leaf insertion is harder than leaves. Expanding nodes in the middle of the tree is harder than adding leaves, because the model must insert a node while preserving both parent and child relationships. The cone constraint helps because it guides the model to encode depth, but examples of non-leaf expansion are rare in typical taxonomies, so the model has fewer examples to learn from.
Done right. Lambda is tuned so that the model balances task accuracy and cone shape. Training data is carefully curated using the big model's best judgments. The fine-tuned small model then expands taxonomies with few hallucinations and strong depth awareness.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple scientific taxonomy such as a 4-level tree of physics topics: Physics includes Classical, Classical includes Mechanics, Mechanics includes Kinematics. Fine-tune a small model (such as DistilBERT) on examples of (node description, parent, depth). Train once with the cone constraint (lambda positive) and once without (lambda zero). On a held-out test set of new nodes to be inserted, measure two metrics: (1) how often the model predicts the correct parent, (2) how often the predicted depth equals the true depth. The constrained model should win on both.

Accuracy = (correct parent predictions + correct depth predictions) / (2 * total test examples)

The demo makes unmissable: without the hyperbolic constraint, small models confuse depth and mix up parents. With it, they rarely do.
