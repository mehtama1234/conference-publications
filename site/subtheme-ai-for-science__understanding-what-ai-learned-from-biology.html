Understanding what AI learned from biology

Building foundation models trained on vast amounts of biological data—DNA, proteins, cellular images, brain signals—so researchers can adapt them to new questions.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Over the last decade, biologists have sequenced the genomes of thousands of organisms, imaged millions of protein structures, recorded billions of DNA and RNA sequences, photographed millions of cells, and captured brain activity from thousands of subjects. This is raw material. What if we trained a model on all of that data, and it learned the deep patterns in how biology works? Then when a researcher has a new question — does this mutation cause disease, or will this protein fold correctly — they could ask the model instead of running expensive experiments from scratch.

The challenge is that biological data is messy, diverse, and often involves rare events. A mutation might occur in one person in a million. A protein might only fold correctly if three things happen in sequence. The data comes from different labs, different organisms, different measurement methods — so the patterns are noisy and contradictory. A model trained on all this data could pick up false patterns or memorize quirks of specific datasets. It also needs to transfer: a model trained on human proteins should say something useful about a mouse protein, even if it has never seen that exact sequence before.

This cluster builds and releases foundation models — large models trained on vast biological datasets — and explores what they learned. Some models are trained only on DNA sequences and learn to predict mutations or genetic disease. Others are trained on protein sequences and 3D structures and learn to predict function or engineering challenges. Still others combine vision and biology: trained on cell images to detect disease patterns, or on brain imaging to predict outcomes. The papers explore how to train these models so they do not just memorize, how to adapt them to new tasks with small amounts of data, and how to check whether the model is really learning biology or just picking up statistical artifacts.

How the work breaks down

When you train a model on vast amounts of biological data, it learns patterns that would take humans years to find by hand. The work here splits into five natural directions: brain models that decode what neural signals mean, protein models that predict how sequences fold and work, DNA models that find disease mutations and patterns, models that combine different measurement types (images, signals, sequences), and models that learn to adapt quickly to new tasks from small amounts of data.

Brain signals: decoding neural activity

Brains produce trillions of signals every second—electrical pulses, images from scanning, eye movements, muscle activity. The hard problem is that each person's brain is wired differently, each lab measures differently, and the signals are noisy. A model trained on thousands of subjects and many measurement types should learn what signals mean across all of them.

The Brain Graph Foundation Model paper (1) took 25,000 subjects across 27 neuroimaging datasets spanning 25 brain disorders, 2 atlas types, and 8 different ways of dividing the brain into regions. It trained once, then could adapt to new disorders and new datasets with just a few examples—even with zero examples. BioX-Bridge (2) solves a different puzzle: it takes biosignals like heartbeat, breathing, and brain waves from different devices and teaches models to understand one type by learning from another, without needing labeled data. Learning Brain Representation (8) focuses on reconstructing what someone sees by reading their brain activity using hierarchical visual embeddings—layering simple patterns into complex ones so the model can match brain regions to visual features. A Cognitive Process-Inspired Architecture (12) goes further: it can reconstruct videos of what someone is watching just by reading brain signals from a new person it has never seen, losing only 7% accuracy and taking 10 seconds with no retraining.

Brain signals: seizures and speech decoding

Beyond seeing and reconstructing images, models can extract specific information from brain signals. Some tasks matter clinically: detecting seizures before they occur. Others restore lost capabilities: helping paralyzed people communicate by reading their brain activity. The challenge is that each patient's brain is different, signals drift over time, and the lab equipment varies.

MVPFormer (15) combines multiple brain signal streams in parallel, achieving expert-level performance on tasks like detecting seizures and decoding speech, pitch, and volume from direct brain recordings because combining signals reveals patterns no single signal shows. Decoding Inner Speech (32) reduces errors from 24% to 10% by using an end-to-end brain-to-text interface that learns to translate imagined speech signals into words and can transfer what it learns across different tasks because end-to-end training learns richer intermediate representations. MEG-XL (51) learns from brain magnetic field recordings instead of electrical ones, matching supervised learning with a fraction of training data—learning from 1 hour instead of 50 hours because magnetic field patterns encode semantic information efficiently. NeuroCLUS (56) uses clustering to organize brain regions based on their function, improving accuracy and interpretability on speech and seizure tasks while discovering clusters that align with known neuroscience because functional grouping reveals which brain regions work together.

Protein structure: sequences and 3D shapes

Protein sequence models have been transforming biology, but they have limits. Language models that read proteins as sequences can run into repetition—generating the same amino acid over and over instead of real proteins. Models see only the chain of amino acids, not how they fold in 3D space or dock with other molecules. Learning good representations means capturing function, chemistry, and shape all at once without memorizing the training data.

Controlling Repetition (5) solves a key problem with protein language models: they generate too much repetition. This paper shows how to substantially lower repetition while keeping confidence scores high and outperforming simpler fixes like decoding penalties. Towards All-Atom Foundation Models (11) tackles the binding affinity problem—predicting how strongly a protein binds to a drug or another protein. Training on all-atom structure (including every hydrogen and charge), it achieves top performance across protein-ligand binding, drug-target binding, protein-protein interactions, and even antibody-antigen pairs, and identified mutations in the lab that boost binding, because including all atoms captures the precise geometric and chemical interactions. FlexProtein (37) jointly learns from sequences and structures by training on both at once. It outperforms existing methods on 12 downstream tasks because learning from both modalities captures information neither alone provides, with especially strong gains when predicting how mutations change protein behavior—exactly where structure-only and sequence-only models struggle separately. Greater than the Sum of Its Parts (40) shows that proteins contain substructures (domains, sheets, loops) and training models to detect and use these parts improves function prediction because function often depends on how substructures work together, revealing how global structure and substructures complement each other.

Protein representation: atoms, force fields, and compression

Beyond sequences and global structures, proteins are made of atoms arranged in precise 3D coordinates. Representing proteins at the atomic level requires compressing vast information without losing function. Force fields—which simulate how atoms interact—can teach models what makes stable protein structures.

Protein Structure Tokenization (63) compresses 3D structures into tokens with over 10x compression without losing information because finding the right information to keep lets large language models work at scale. Representing Local Protein Environments (66) learns representations from machine learning force fields that capture the chemical environment around each atom. These embeddings zero-shot generalize—they work on proteins the model never saw—and beat all competing methods on spectroscopy tasks, showing force fields are general-purpose learners for protein modeling because they encode the rules of chemistry directly. SLAE (70) reconstructs full 3D structures with high accuracy and beats existing methods on downstream tasks by learning strictly local all-atom environments, creating a latent space that is chemically informative because focusing on local chemical contexts preserves the rules that determine structure.

DNA sequences: learning from mutations and disease

DNA is a single molecule that can be billions of letters long, and models need to learn what patterns matter. The hard part: mutations can be rare (one in a million), effects can be buried in complex regulatory regions, and models trained on one part of the genome need to transfer to parts never seen. Most genomic foundation models are enormous, but can they learn actual biology or just dataset quirks?

Genomic Foundationless Models (6) challenges the assumption that pretraining helps. When trained on the same data, simple character-token models matched or beat much larger pretrained models on many tasks because the scaling benefit of pretraining depends on genomic patterns that current models may not exploit. The real finding: genomic foundation models fail to capture clinically relevant mutations, and pretraining provides modest, tokenizer-dependent improvements—not the leap models achieve in language or vision. Genome-wide DNA Methylation (13) predicts methylation patterns (chemical tags on DNA that control which genes turn on) at unmeasured locations across 1.6 billion CpG sites, generalizing to new samples it has never seen because methylation patterns follow biological rules that transfer across individuals. Adaptive DNA Sequence Modeling (17) uses specialized units inspired by how DNA actually works to model sequences with near-linear computation instead of quadratic, then proves theoretically why this design works because it mirrors the sequential nature of how genomes function. AntigenLM (20) shows that preserving genomic structure is critical—it forecasts how flu viruses mutate across regions and subtypes better than purely evolutionary models by training on sequences while keeping structural information because the 3D folding of genomic regions constrains which mutations can survive.

Tokenization: finding where DNA regions begin

How you divide a sequence into tokens matters as much as training itself. Fixed divisions are arbitrary and miss how biology actually works. Learned tokenization adapts to the task and can discover functional boundaries that biology obeys. Smaller models with smart tokenization can beat huge generic models.

BioToken and BioFM (23) use biologically informed tokenization, requiring 100x less compute than large-scale genomic models while outperforming them on pathogenicity and expression prediction because dividing sequences where biology actually changes (at functional boundaries) captures function better than arbitrary splits. DNACHUNKER (30) learns how to split DNA sequences into chunks instead of using fixed chunking. Its learned boundaries are mutation-resilient and reflect actual biology, improving over fixed-grid tokenization by up to 14 percentage points on five benchmarks because the model discovers where true functional units begin and end. LDARNet (43) adapts representations of genomic regions and learns to tokenize based on task needs, winning on 11 of 18 compact models on histone modification tasks (predicting which parts of DNA are accessible) because different tasks require different granularity. dnaHNet (84) is a scalable hierarchical model that beats large language models on zero-shot protein variant fitness and gene essentiality prediction because organizing DNA at multiple levels of abstraction mirrors how regulatory systems actually work, running over 3x faster.

Cross-modal learning: images, signals, and sequences

Biology produces many types of measurements: images from microscopes, electrical signals from sensors, RNA sequences, wearable data from smartwatches, brain scans in different magnetic fields. A model trained on one type can teach another type to recognize patterns because underlying biology is the same even if the measurement changes. The challenge: signals have different scales, timing, and noise, so the model must learn which patterns transfer and which are specific to one device or person because this determines which knowledge generalizes.

Breaking Scale Anchoring (3) solves the resolution problem: train a model on low-resolution images and it can make accurate predictions on high-resolution images never seen, because it learns frequency patterns that scale across resolutions. HiMAE (7) trains hierarchical masked autoencoders on wearable sensor data at multiple time scales, outperforming foundation models while being orders of magnitude smaller—so small it runs on smartwatch CPUs in less than a millisecond. Bimodal Masked Language Modeling (22) combines bulk RNA-seq and DNA methylation data by training on both at once, beating all competing methods on cancer classification and survival prediction while still working when one modality is missing. CDBridge (24) bridges across genomic measurements by training on RNA sequences, then using that learning to improve predictions from RNA expression levels and DNA structure, capturing long-range dependencies between exons.

Multi-modal brain imaging: combining scans and signals

Brain research combines many imaging types and signal measurements: structural scans, functional activity maps, genetic data, and clinical records. A unified model should integrate all of these to predict outcomes like disease progression. The hard part: different modalities need different preprocessing and some patients lack certain measurements.

Cross-Subject Modeling for Widefield Calcium Imaging (28) tokenizes brain imaging at multiple spatial scales and trains across subjects. It can then decode behavior from brain images of subjects it has never seen and reconstruct missing brain regions. Joint Adaptation (42) tackles Alzheimer's by adapting models trained on individual modalities—brain scans, genetic data, functional brain imaging, and clinical records—so they work together on diagnosis and predicting disease progression. M-IDoL (50) decomposes information from medical images across five imaging types into modality-specific and shared information, beating 20 foundation models on 21 clinical tasks. Scaling Vision Transformers for Functional MRI (72) observes strict power law scaling for brain scans—performance follows a predictable pattern as model size increases—and shows that a vision transformer trained on images can predict brain states at scale.

Rapid adaptation: few-shot and test-time learning

In practice, researchers have only a few examples of a new task or a new type of patient. A foundation model should learn from just a handful of examples without forgetting what it learned during pretraining because rapid adaptation unlocks practical use. The hard part: the new data is different from pretraining data (different lab, different disease, different measurement device), and models can overfit or fail to transfer because the shortcut patterns that work on old data do not work on new data.

CellDuality (4) enables language models to reason about single-cell biology using self-supervised feedback—the model learns from data about cell types and genes without needing an expert to grade every answer because the data contains implicit feedback (cell type relationships), bootstrapping reasoning ability from the data itself. Map as a Prompt (9) uses spatial maps as prompts to help the model generalize across different buildings and scenarios for wireless localization, beating baselines with zero examples on new scenarios because the map encodes the structural patterns the model needs to recognize. Test-Time Adaptation (10) predicts drug activity without seeing the source data it was trained on, adapting on the fly to new drug scaffolds, new proteins to target, and new assay types because updating during testing captures distribution shift without accessing original training data. Adaptive Test-Time Training (19) handles multi-center hospital data, keeping clinically relevant patient representations while adapting across different medical centers because representation-level adaptation preserves medical meaning.

Foundation model limits: classical methods still win sometimes

Foundation models are often assumed to help with scarce data, but findings show limits. When you have only a few examples to learn from, fine-tuning large models sometimes performs worse than classical methods built specifically for data scarcity because large models need more data to adapt well. Sometimes raw sequences are noise compared to what surrounding context reveals about function. Sometimes learning task-specific patterns from data outweighs what pretraining provided.

Are EEG Foundation Models Worth It? (21) exposes a gap: fine-tuned foundation models often fail to beat classical methods in data-scarce settings on brain signals, questioning whether pretraining helps when labeled data is tiny because the models lack enough examples to escape local optima despite pretraining. Genome-Factory (39) provides tools to tune genomic foundation models and interpret them, validating that they work on real prediction tasks and that their learned features are biologically sensible. Lost in Tokenization (49) shows that when asked biological reasoning questions, context about genes and proteins is more useful than raw sequences—raw sequences act as noise—revealing what models actually understand. Tokenizing Single-Channel EEG (78) learns time-frequency motifs (repeating patterns) instead of hand-designing them, improving diverse foundation models by up to 11% on benchmark tasks while generalizing across device types. Uni-NTFM (81) unifies EEG representation learning after training on 28,000 hours of brain signal data, beating existing models across nine distinct benchmarks.

EEG and ECG: heart and brain signals

Two specific biological signals—brain electrical activity (EEG) and heart electrical activity (ECG)—show how foundation models work on time-series data. Each has special structure: EEG has multiple sensors on the head, ECG is typically one channel but highly interpretable. Both are noisy and drift, but both reveal predictable patterns when read with the right method.

Spectrum Video for EEG (41) learns better spatiotemporal representations by using video understanding models as backbone, because treating brain signals as time-frequency maps lets vision models recognize spatial patterns. Learning Cardiac Latent Representations (44) beats ECG-space baselines on multiple tasks with better generalization, especially when testing on different types of patients because learning in a better coordinate space (vectorcardiogram space) aligns with how heart signals actually work. RECTOR (64) beats competing methods in EEG emotion recognition and brain signal task-engagement classification while handling missing channels and working across different sensor arrangements. Riemannian High-Order Pooling (67) improves EEG classification accuracy and speed across diverse benchmarks as a plug-and-play module compatible with modern foundation models. SGERA (68) uses Stein operators (geometric alignment tools) to align ECG signals and medical reports in latent space because matching signals to written descriptions teaches the model what different patterns mean.
