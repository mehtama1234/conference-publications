Beyond Majority Voting: Aggregating LLMs by Trusting the Surprising

When asking multiple AI models the same question, ignore majority vote and instead reward models when their answer matches others who rarely agree.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When you ask multiple AI models the same question, the simplest approach is majority voting: count votes and pick the most common answer. But this treats all models as equally reliable, which they are not. Some models are better calibrated (they know when they are guessing), and some models' answers correlate with each other (they make the same mistakes). This paper introduces a training-free method that detects which models tend to agree with the crowd and which stand alone, then reweights their votes so that independent dissenters have more power.

Zero assumption version

Why majority voting is naive

Imagine you ask ten AI models to diagnose a medical image. Five say cancer, four say benign, one abstains. Majority voting picks cancer. But what if those five models are a fine-tuned variant that was trained on biased data? What if the four dissenters are independent models that rarely agree with the first five, yet each time they do, they are right? Majority voting cannot know this.

A beginner might try: just ask each model how confident it is in its own answer. But models often do not know how to calibrate confidence honestly—they confabulate certainty even when uncertain. A more advanced beginner might try to cluster which models agree with each other and downweight the cluster, but without a principled metric, this becomes a guess.

The core problem. Majority voting ignores that models have latent reliability patterns: some are more trustworthy, some correlate with each other, and models that dare to be wrong when others agree often point to a real blind spot.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect votes

Ask all models the same question, collect their answers.

Step 2: Count model agreement

For each model, count: how often does it agree with majority consensus? (Popularity score.)

Step 3: Flip the weight

Models that agreed often are downweighted. Models that disagreed often are upweighted. (Inverse Surprising Popularity.)

Step 4: Measure first-order statistics

For the Optimal Weight variant, also measure per-model accuracy on the test set.

Step 5: Weighted re-vote

Re-aggregate using the learned weights. The final answer is now resilient to correlated errors.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the way votes are counted when combining multiple AI model answers. Instead of each model's vote counting equally, each vote is multiplied by a learned weight that depends on the model's historical pattern of agreement with the crowd.

the object it moves  =  vote weights per model
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Surprising Popularity

measured quantity  =  how often a model bucks the consensus

Surprising Popularity is a measurement of whether a model tends to agree or disagree with the majority consensus across many test questions. A model with high popularity has voted with the crowd often (it is a conformist). A model with low popularity has disagreed often (it is a rebel).

The paper estimates this by running all models on a batch of questions and recording, for each model, what fraction of the time it matched the majority answer. If a model matches the majority 95% of the time, its popularity is high. If a model matches only 40% of the time, its popularity is low. The Inverse Surprising Popularity method (ISP) then reverses this: it gives high weight to models with low popularity, treating the rebels as a tie-breaker vote.

Why it works

Why weighting by surprise works

Majority voting fails when multiple models make the same mistake together. This happens because models are not independent: a fine-tuned variant shares training data similarities with its parent, or multiple models studied the same web corpora and learned the same biases. When three models that were trained on overlapping data all vote red, majority voting treats it as strong evidence, but it is really one error repeated three times.

Here is the core mechanism: a model which disagrees with the crowd has already proven it does not share the crowd's correlated mistake. If Model X votes blue when everyone else votes red, and Model X is not in the main cluster, then Model X's blue vote is independent evidence. By upweighting such models, the algorithm trades away conformist redundancy for rare, uncorrelated signals. In domains with data bias (medical imaging, question answering), the low-popularity models are often the ones that caught a blind spot the majority missed.

The Optimal Weight variant adds a second layer: it also measures per-model accuracy on a side validation set. Models that are both independently-minded (low popularity) and individually accurate get the highest weight. Models that conform but are individually accurate get medium weight. Conformist, inaccurate models get low weight. This combines two dimensions of reliability into a single vote multiplier.

Math after the picture

The equation and worked example

First in words:

For each model i, compute popularity as the fraction of questions where model i agrees with the majority. Then assign weight as one divided by popularity. The final answer is computed as the answer that has the highest sum of weights across all models that voted for it.

Then the compact form:

w_i = 1 / popularity_i, where popularity_i = (count of agreements with majority) / (total questions) final_answer = argmax_a sum_i w_i * [model_i voted for a]

Every symbol:

w_ithe weight given to model i's vote
popularity_ithe fraction of times model i agreed with the majority consensus
model_i voted for aindicator: 1 if model i chose answer a, 0 otherwise
final_answerthe answer option that accumulates the highest total weight
A tiny worked example with four models and one new question
Setup: On 10 prior questions, Model A always agreed with majority (popularity_A = 10/10 = 1.0, weight_A = 1/1.0 = 1.0).
Setup: Model B agreed 8 times (popularity_B = 8/10 = 0.8, weight_B = 1/0.8 = 1.25).
Setup: Model C agreed 4 times (popularity_C = 4/10 = 0.4, weight_C = 1/0.4 = 2.5).
Setup: Model D agreed 3 times (popularity_D = 3/10 = 0.3, weight_D = 1/0.3 = 3.33).
New question: Models A, B, C vote Red (3 votes). Model D votes Blue (1 vote).
Majority says Red (3 vs 1). But Model D is a rebel.
Red total weight: 1.0 + 1.25 + 2.5 = 4.75 (includes the consistent rebel C).
Blue total weight: 3.33 (the rare rebel D).
Majority voting picks Red. Weighted voting also picks Red, but notices the dissent. If D is right, the weight difference (4.75 vs 3.33) narrows the margin, allowing the algorithm to recover faster on future cases where the rebels are correct.

Each model's vote is multiplied by its weight, which is high for rebels and low for conformists, so independent minority answers get a louder voice.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If all models are the same, there are no rebels. If all ten models are the exact same fine-tuned variant, they will all have high popularity and all get equal weight. The algorithm collapses to majority voting, which is correct but uninteresting. The method works best when models are diverse.
If the validation set is biased, weights are wrong. The popularity measurement is computed on a specific batch of test questions. If that batch has different bias than the deployment data, the weight assignments will be misleading. A model with low popularity on the validation set might actually be wrong in production.
Rebels can be wrong too. Upweighting independent models assumes their dissent reveals a blind spot. But sometimes they are just wrong. If three models vote blue and seven vote red, and all three blues are actually incorrect, boosting them hurts accuracy. The method works well when the minority catches real gaps; it fails when the minority is simply inferior.
Done right. The method works best when models are trained on different data, use different architectures, or are fine-tuned with different objectives—so that their mistakes are truly independent. In that regime, rebels are gold.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

The demo should show that on a dataset where models have correlated errors (e.g., several models trained on overlapping data), weighted aggregation outperforms majority voting, especially on edge cases where the minority was right. This works because the upweighting mechanism gives more vote power to the independent models, so when they dissent, their vote has enough weight to flip the final decision. A concrete setup: fine-tune the same base model three different ways, each picking up a different data bias. Ask all three plus two independent models to answer questions on a holdout set. Mark questions where the correlated trio all agree vs questions where one of the independents disagrees. Run both majority voting and ISP on these questions and compare accuracy, focusing on the disagreement cases.

accuracy_lift = (accuracy_ISP on disagreement cases) - (accuracy_majority_vote on disagreement cases)

The demo must show a case where the majority all agree and would pick wrong, but the low-popularity model picks right, and ISP correctly flips the final decision.
