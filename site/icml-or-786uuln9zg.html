Mixture of Latent Actions: From Video Imagination to Robot Hands

A robot can imagine the future by watching video, but only if it converts that imagination into actual physical actions through a special translation layer called MoLA.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots can now imagine the future using video generation models, much like you might picture yourself reaching for a cup. But imagined video is not the same as a command a robot hand can follow. This paper solves that mismatch by adding a translator in the middle: instead of sending raw predicted frames to the robot, MoLA first asks three specialized assistants to look at the video changes and vote on what action probably caused them, then combines those votes into one final action the robot can execute.

Zero assumption version

The gap between imagination and control

Robot tasks like picking up a cup or placing a block take many steps, so engineers want robots to plan ahead. A natural idea: show the robot's video generator a picture of the world today and ask it to imagine what comes next. The problem is that a video prediction model is trained to make the next frame look realistic to a human eye — so it gets very good at predicting blur and shadows and reflections, all the pixel details. But for controlling a robot hand, those details are noise. What matters is: did the arm move up or sideways? Did the fingers curl?

When engineers tried sending those predicted frames straight to a policy (the neural net that tells the hand what to do), the results were indirect and shaky. The robot would thrash around trying to match what it saw, rather than executing a clean action. It was like learning to cook from watching a YouTube video in bad lighting instead of following a recipe.

The real problem. Video imagination tells you what the world will look like, not what to do.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Video imagination

The video generator predicts the next frame given the current one.

2. Three specialists look

Three pre-trained inverse models independently analyze frame pairs to infer actions.

3. Semantic channel

One inverse model reads what moved (object recognition) and infers likely actions.

4. Flow and depth channels

Two more inverse models read motion direction and distance to infer complementary actions.

5. Mixture combines and executes

The policy receives the combined latent actions and outputs the robot control command.

What the paper changes

What changes

Instead of passing raw predicted video frames to the policy, MoLA inserts a translation step. This step takes the predicted visual changes and converts them into latent actions — abstract, control-friendly descriptions of movement, not pixels.

the object it moves  =  The interface between video and policy
The hidden quantity

Latent actions: The control-friendly abstraction

measured quantity  =  Latent actions (lists of numbers that describe movement)

A latent action is not a pixel. It is a numerical representation of what action probably caused the observed change in the video. For example, if the video shows the gripper opening, the latent action might be a vector like (0.9, 0.1, 0.05) encoding: 0.9 confidence in opening, 0.1 in upward motion, 0.05 in leftward shift. This is far closer to what a policy actually needs than a raw image.

MoLA estimates latent actions using three inverse dynamics models (models that learn the reverse mapping: from observed change back to action). Each model sees the frame transition and outputs its own latent action vote. The paper then mixes these votes into one consensus latent action that the policy network can use to execute a command. Because three different models each capture a different aspect (semantic, flow, depth), their mixture is more reliable and catches errors that any single view would miss.

Why it works

Why translating video through three expert eyes works better

The core idea is that a single video frame is ambiguous. The same pixel change could mean many things: maybe the gripper moved up because it is opening, or because the whole arm lifted, or because the camera jiggled. A human watching a robot sees motion in the world and instantly infers the cause. MoLA teaches the system to do the same by running three specialized inverse models in parallel, each trained to see one type of clue: semantic (what objects moved), flow (which direction pixels are streaming), and depth (how distances changed).

Each inverse model outputs a latent action. The mixture step then weights these votes: if all three experts agree, the weight is high; if they conflict, the vote is hedged. This mixture is much richer than raw pixels because it forces the system to name what actually happened (which action caused the change), rather than memorize pixel patterns. When the policy receives this latent action, it is already halfway to executing the command — it knows the motor cause, not the sensory consequence.

Why does this beat passing raw predicted frames? Because a policy trained on raw frames must learn to ignore all the perceptual detail (blur, lighting, reflections) and extract the true motor cause by trial and error. By doing that extraction before the policy sees the data, MoLA gives the policy a clean, action-centric input, so it can learn faster and generalize to new camera angles, lighting, and object shapes.

The mixture equation

First in words:

final latent action equals the weighted sum of latent action votes, where each inverse model's vote is scaled by a confidence weight

Then the compact form:

a_latent = sum_i ( w_i * a_i )

Every symbol:

a_latentThe final latent action the policy uses (a vector)
a_iThe latent action vote from expert i (semantic, flow, or depth)
w_iThe weight (confidence) assigned to expert i
sum_iSum across the three experts
A tiny worked example with three experts
Semantic expert votes: 'reach down' = latent action (0.8, 0.2, 0.1), weight w_sem = 0.5
Flow expert votes: 'move left' = latent action (0.1, 0.5, 0.4), weight w_flow = 0.3
Depth expert votes: 'come closer' = latent action (0.2, 0.3, 0.6), weight w_depth = 0.2
Final = 0.5 * (0.8, 0.2, 0.1) + 0.3 * (0.1, 0.5, 0.4) + 0.2 * (0.2, 0.3, 0.6)
= (0.40, 0.10, 0.05) + (0.03, 0.15, 0.12) + (0.04, 0.06, 0.12)
= (0.47, 0.31, 0.29)
Interpretation: 47% reach-down signal, 31% left signal, 29% closer signal — the policy sees this mixed action and decides on a smooth blended movement.

Each expert sees one hidden aspect of the video change; their mixture gives the policy a complete, actionable summary without forcing it to re-learn what pixels mean.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Inverse models give conflicting votes. If semantic, flow, and depth models disagree sharply (e.g., one infers left, one right), the final latent action is ambiguous. This can cause shaky or erratic control. Mitigation: use a learned weighting scheme (as the paper does) that learns which expert to trust in each situation.
The video prediction itself is wrong. MoLA can only extract the right action from the video if the video prediction is accurate. If the generator predicts a plausible but unrealistic motion (e.g., an object teleporting), the inverse models will vote for a false action. This is fundamental: garbage in, garbage out.
Latent actions do not transfer to new hardware. The inverse models are trained on one robot with a specific morphology (arm length, gripper speed). If you move to a very different robot, those latent action meanings may not transfer, and the policy will fail. You need inverse models trained on the new hardware.
Done right. When the three inverse models are well-trained, diverse, and their outputs are carefully weighted, MoLA yields clean, stable action representations that any policy can quickly learn to execute. This works consistently in both simulation and real robots because the latent action abstraction is physics-based (it comes from inverse models trained on actual dynamics) and modality-diverse (three independent views catch mistakes), so it generalizes better than pixel-based signals to new objects, camera angles, and lighting conditions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A convincing demo would take a trained video generator and a trained policy on a simple task (e.g., pick up a block on a table). Run two experiments in parallel: (1) Send raw predicted frames from the video generator directly to the policy, and measure success rate and motion smoothness over 30 trials. (2) Insert MoLA in the middle: run the predicted frames through three pre-trained inverse models, mix their latent actions, and send the result to the same policy. Measure the same metrics. Plot both results side-by-side as success-rate bar charts and video clips showing arm motion stability. The demo is successful if the MoLA version shows higher success rate (70%+ vs 40%+) and smoother, more confident hand movements, even when the video predictions are imperfect.

success_rate = (number of trials where task completed) / (total trials); stability = average smoothness of joint trajectories; generalization = success rate on new object shapes not seen during training

The point of MoLA is simple: do not make the video generator better; instead, make the policy's job easier by converting what is realistic into what the robot can actually do.
