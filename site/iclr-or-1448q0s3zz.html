BioX-Bridge: Lightweight Model Bridging for Cross-Modal Biosignal Knowledge Transfer

Instead of training a full-size model for each new body-signal type, train only a lightweight bridge that connects the old model's layers to the new model's layers, reusing both.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Human bodies send out multiple signals at once—heartbeats, brain waves, skin responses—and they are often correlated. A model trained on one signal (like heart-rate sensors) can help train a model on a different signal (like EEG brain-wave sensors) if you can translate between them, because both signals reflect the same underlying physiology. But foundation models (large pre-trained models) are too heavy to duplicate for each new signal. BioX-Bridge solves this by training only a tiny bridge network that sits between the old and new models, letting them share knowledge without retraining either one.

Zero assumption version

Why large models break the cross-modal problem

When you want a model to handle a new biosignal—switching from ECG (heart rhythm) to EEG (brain waves)—you have two choices. First: train a brand-new model from scratch on labeled EEG data. But labeled data is scarce and expensive to collect. Second: use knowledge distillation—run the old model (trained on ECG) as a teacher while training a new student model on EEG, teaching it to mimic the old model's outputs. This works, but it forces you to train a full-size new model alongside the teacher, doubling the compute and memory needed. When foundation models are large (this work uses a model with 90 million numbers that the network needs to learn), running both teacher and student becomes impractical on typical GPUs.

A beginner might hope to fine-tune just the new model on the old model's outputs, but without a way to align the internal representations—the intermediate activations buried inside both models—the signals get scrambled and performance collapses.

In one sentence. Instead of retraining a full student model per new modality, train only a small bridge network that connects two foundation models' internal layers.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pick alignment layers

Scan all combinations of old-model and new-model layers; find the pair whose internal signals are most similar using a correlation metric.

Build a tiny bridge

Create a bridge network with a learnable prototype set (a small set of reference vectors) and a low-rank approximation (two small matrices A and B) that together act as an adapter.

Pair up raw data

Collect examples of the same physiological moment captured in both signal types—e.g., heart and brain signals recorded at the same time.

Train bridge only

Feed paired data through both frozen models; align their intermediate representations at the chosen layers using the bridge as the adapter; only the bridge's numbers are adjusted during learning.

Use downstream task

The new-modality model (now guided by the bridge to the old model's knowledge) trains on the new task with far fewer labeled examples.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper swaps out the full student model (which could be 90 million parameters) for a bridge network that is only 1-13% of that size. The bridge sits at a fixed pair of internal layers and learns to translate one model's representation into the other's. All other parameters—in both the old and new foundation models—remain frozen.

The hidden quantity

Representation alignment error

measured quantity  =  Alignment gap between model layers

When you run an ECG signal through the ECG model, its internal layer 6 produces a list of 512 numbers (called a vector—just a sequence of numbers). When you run an EEG signal through the EEG model, its internal layer 4 produces a different list of 512 numbers. The alignment gap is how different these two lists are—measured as their distance in space. If they point in similar directions (low gap), knowledge transfers cleanly. If they point in opposite directions (high gap), the bridge has to work harder to interpret the EEG model's signals in terms the ECG model would understand.

The paper estimates alignment gap by computing a similarity score (centered kernel alignment, or CKA—a measure of how much two vectors point in the same direction) between the representations. It then uses this to pick which layers should be bridged. During bridge training, it minimizes the gap by adjusting the bridge's matrices A, B, and prototypes P so that EEG-model layer 4, after passing through the bridge, becomes as close as possible to ECG-model layer 6.

Why it works

Why the bridge transfers knowledge without retraining

Foundation models are pre-trained on enormous unlabeled datasets and learn representations that capture deep structure. The ECG model's layer 6 has already learned to represent heart rhythms in detail, capturing patterns like regular vs. irregular beats, fast vs. slow. The EEG model's layer 4 is learning to represent brain activity, capturing patterns like wakefulness vs. sleep. These representations live in spaces defined by 512 or more separate numbers per signal. They are not directly comparable: raw numerical differences between two lists of 512 numbers are meaningless.

The bridge solves this by learning a lightweight transformation that maps the EEG model's representation space into the ECG model's representation space. It does this with a prototype network: a set of learnable reference vectors (prototypes) and a low-rank approximation (two small matrices A and B that multiply together). When an EEG signal passes through the bridge, the bridge asks: which prototypes does this signal resemble? It then computes a weighted combination of those prototypes, and the result is a transformed representation that aligns with the ECG model's space. Because the bridge architecture is tiny (using low-rank factorization), it has far fewer parameters to train than a full student model would need.

Once the bridge is trained, the EEG model can learn the downstream task (e.g., detect sleep stage from brain signals) by leveraging the ECG model's pre-trained knowledge, because their representations are now aligned. Instead of teaching the EEG model from scratch via a teacher model, we teach it by aligning it to a frozen model that already understands the domain—a shortcut that works because the human body is interconnected and the patterns learned from heart signals help interpret brain signals.

The alignment loss

First in words:

minimize the distance between the ECG model's final layer and the bridge-transformed EEG model, averaged over all paired examples

Then the compact form:

L_align = (1/N) * sum_i distance(h_ECG_L[i], bridge(h_EEG_m[i]))

Every symbol:

h_ECG_L[i]the representation from ECG model's layer L, for example i
h_EEG_m[i]the representation from EEG model's layer m, for example i
bridge(·)the bridge network (prototypes + low-rank matrices A, B)
distance(·,·)cosine distance or mean-absolute-error between two representation vectors
Nnumber of paired examples in the batch
Tiny worked example with 3 paired signal samples
Example 1: ECG L6 = [0.5, -0.3, 0.8], EEG L4 (raw) = [0.1, 0.2, 0.9], bridge output = [0.48, -0.32, 0.79], distance = sqrt((0.5-0.48)^2 + (-0.3-(-0.32))^2 + (0.8-0.79)^2) = 0.026
Example 2: ECG L6 = [0.2, 0.6, -0.1], EEG L4 (raw) = [0.3, 0.5, 0.0], bridge output = [0.21, 0.58, -0.08], distance = sqrt((0.2-0.21)^2 + (0.6-0.58)^2 + (-0.1-(-0.08))^2) = 0.028
Example 3: ECG L6 = [-0.4, 0.9, 0.3], EEG L4 (raw) = [-0.5, 0.8, 0.2], bridge output = [-0.41, 0.91, 0.31], distance = sqrt((-0.4-(-0.41))^2 + (0.9-0.91)^2 + (0.3-0.31)^2) = 0.017
Total loss = (0.026 + 0.028 + 0.017) / 3 = 0.024. During training, a learning algorithm nudges the bridge matrices A and B to shrink this loss, aligning EEG representations toward ECG representations.

The bridge outputs a weighted blend of prototypes that minimizes the gap between EEG and ECG intermediate representations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bridging at the wrong layer. If you align at an early layer (e.g., layer 2 instead of layer 6), small errors in the bridge grow as signals propagate through the remaining layers. Aligning at the final layer captures all error and lets the bridge compensate.
Paired data is weak or misaligned in time. The bridge learns to translate one representation into the other. If the paired examples are not truly synchronized—ECG and EEG from different moments—the bridge sees contradictory signals and fails to align them.
New modality is too different from old modality. If the two biosignals reflect completely different physiological processes (e.g., skin temperature vs. heart rate), their representations may not have enough correlation for a bridge to align. Correlation matters.
Done right. Select layers greedily by correlation, keep the bridge low-rank, use paired data that is truly synchronized, and validate on a held-out test set before scaling to the full downstream task.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Collect paired biosignal data: record ECG and EEG from the same person at the same time. Split into train, validation, and test. Load two pre-trained foundation models (one for ECG, one for EEG). Use CKA to select the best pair of layers to align (e.g., ECG layer 6 and EEG layer 4). Initialize a bridge network with low-rank matrices (rank 32, for example) and a small prototype set (16 prototypes). Train the bridge on paired examples to minimize alignment loss. Finally, take the EEG model (now guided by the trained bridge) and fine-tune it on a downstream task (e.g., sleep-stage classification) using only a small labeled dataset. Measure accuracy and parameter count.

parameter_reduction = (full_student_parameters - bridge_parameters) / full_student_parameters; accuracy_gain = downstream_accuracy_bridge - downstream_accuracy_baseline

The demo must show that the bridge achieves 88-99% parameter reduction and maintains or improves accuracy compared to knowledge distillation, because a tiny bridge can translate between representations without the overhead of training a full new model, and this efficiency gain lets the EEG model spend its learning budget on the downstream task instead of on alignment—all while using 3-4x less training time on the same GPU.
