Probabilistic Scenarios for Time Series Forecasting

Instead of guessing the future with random samples, predict a small set of realistic futures plus their exact odds.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting (predicting what happens next in a stream of data like stock prices or weather) usually works by throwing many random guesses at the problem. This paper says: stop guessing. Instead, directly list the few futures that are actually likely, and give the exact probability for each one.

Why random guessing breaks down

Today's best forecasters work like this: the model draws one thousand random samples from its guess at what the future looks like, then reports the average or the spread of those samples. It sounds clever, but it fails in three ways. First: you get no actual probabilities, only a cloud of random guesses. Second: no one can prove the spread covers the right range of futures. Third: drawing a thousand samples is expensive — it burns computation time.

A beginner would naively say: just make the model output one probability for each possible future. The problem is that the future is continuous (infinitely many possible paths), so you cannot list every one. Sampling feels like the only way out.

The method in one sentence. The model predicts a fixed number of plausible futures and their exact probabilities, learned directly without drawing samples.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take the past

Feed historical data (past prices, past weather) into the model.

Predict futures

Model runs three separate linear calculations, each outputting one plausible future path.

Assign probabilities

Each calculation also outputs a confidence score, normalized into a true probability by dividing by the sum of all scores (so all probabilities add up to exactly one).

Return explicit pairs

Model outputs: Scenario-1 at 45% chance, Scenario-2 at 35% chance, Scenario-3 at 20% chance.

Measure error

Training adjusts the scenarios and their probabilities to match where the real future actually landed.

What the paper changes

The thing the paper changes

The paper replaces the sampling step. Instead of drawing random samples, the model directly outputs {scenario, probability} pairs that it learns from data.

the object it moves  =  sampling to direct scenario prediction
The hidden quantity

Coverage of plausible futures

measured quantity  =  Scenario coverage fraction

Most forecasters never know if they covered the actual future well. This paper measures coverage: the degree to which the predicted scenarios and their probabilities span the true outcomes that actually happen. A good forecast with 10% coverage is giving 10% of its probability to outcomes that actually occur.

The paper estimates coverage by training the model to predict real outcomes and then checking: when the real future falls into one of our scenarios, we assign it that scenario's probability. High coverage means the model was confident and correct; low coverage means it was either overconfident (real future was unexpected) or underconfident (real future was in a low-probability scenario).

Why it works

Why learning explicit scenarios works better

Sampling-based methods guess an infinite number of futures, then pick a random subset. The model never knows if its random picks actually cover reality. By contrast, Probabilistic Scenarios forces the model to commit: pick exactly K futures (say, three), assign each a probability, and train to maximize how often the real future falls into one of your scenarios with high confidence.

This works because the learning objective itself is clearer. With sampling, the model adjusts weights to make the sampled cloud look right, but it has no direct way to ask: 'Did I actually predict the right futures?' With explicit scenarios, every training step answers that question: 'The real future was Path-B, which I gave 35% chance. Good.' Or: 'The real future was Path-D, which I gave 5% chance. Terrible, adjust.' The model learns to find the few futures that matter and get their odds right, instead of blindly tuning a random generator.

Computation also drops, because three linear layers do a fixed amount of work (multiply by weights and sum), no matter what the data is. Drawing a thousand samples and computing their weights is much slower: the cost scales up with the number of samples, and sampling itself is inherently serial (each sample takes time to draw).

The learning formula

First in words:

Minimize the negative log-likelihood of the real outcome under the predicted distribution. For each time step, the model predicts K scenarios with probabilities, and the loss penalizes how unlikely the true future was under those predictions.

Then the compact form:

Loss = -sum_t log(P_t(y_true_t)) where P_t(y_true_t) is the probability the model assigned to the actual outcome at time t

Every symbol:

y_true_tthe actual future value at time t
P_t(y_true_t)the probability the model assigned to that true value
sum_tadd up the penalty over all time steps in the forecast
log(...)natural logarithm; smaller probability gives bigger penalty
A tiny worked example
At time step 1: real future is 52. Model predicted Scenario-A (value 50) at 40% and Scenario-B (value 55) at 60%.
Scenario-B is closest, probability 60%, so log(0.60) = -0.51. Loss contribution: 0.51.
At time step 2: real future is 48. Model predicted Scenario-A at 60% (closer match). Loss: log(0.60) = 0.51.
Total loss over two steps: 0.51 + 0.51 = 1.02. Training lowers loss by adjusting scenarios to assign more probability to futures that actually happen.

The loss directly rewards assigning high probability to futures that actually occur, forcing the model to identify plausible scenarios and their true odds.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fixed scenario count doesn't adapt. If you commit to predicting exactly three futures, some real datasets have five plausible futures. The model learns to crowd them or ignore tail risks.
Scenarios can collapse to duplicates. Without constraints, all three linear layers might learn to predict the same scenario. Add a penalty that rewards each layer for predicting something different, forcing them to explore different futures.
Rare futures are hard to capture. If a highly unlikely but catastrophic event (e.g., a stock market crash that happens once every ten years) occurs, the model sees just one example and may ignore it as noise.
Done right. Choose scenario count based on the data (cluster real outcomes to find how many natural regimes exist), add a penalty that pushes scenarios apart, and weight rare futures upward in training.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple forecast on a real time series (temperature over a month, traffic counts, or synthetic data). Train the model to predict the next day. Compare two versions: (A) sampling baseline that draws fifty random futures, and (B) Probabilistic Scenarios with three explicit futures. Measure three metrics: (1) the model's average log-likelihood on held-out test data (how confident it is about outcomes that actually happen); (2) the actual probability assigned to the test outcomes (are its 70% predictions right 70% of the time?); (3) wall-clock time to make one forecast.
