Tracing how models compute step by step

Instead of treating models as black boxes, these papers zoom into the circuits and wires inside—finding which parts perform specific tasks and how they talk to each other.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A large language model processes text in layers. The first layer might extract simple patterns like 'this token is a noun.' A middle layer might combine patterns: 'this noun is the subject.' Deep layers might encode abstract reasoning. But how do these stages connect? Which earlier computations feed into which later ones? Can we find modular circuits — small groups of neurons that collaborate to solve one sub-problem? This line of research treats the model as an actual circuit: neurons are nodes, connections have weights, and information flows from input to output.

The difficulty is scale and entanglement. Even a small model has millions of connections. Most neurons participate in multiple tasks. A single neuron might fire for dozens of unrelated reasons. Researchers use techniques like activation patterns, attention weight analysis, and causal intervention — toggling parts of the model to see what breaks — to trace these circuits. The payoff is a mechanistic model: a picture of how the model actually computes, not just a statistical description. When you understand the circuit, you can predict failure modes and sometimes redesign it.

How the work breaks down

Understanding how neural networks compute means finding the hidden wiring: which neurons fire for which inputs, how heads that filter information route data, what circuits perform specific reasoning steps. The work splits into four directions. First, researchers zoom into heads and their roles—finding heads that identify grammatical positions, track entities across sequences, or focus information flow. Second, they trace circuits end-to-end, discovering that multiple overlapping paths can solve the same task. Third, they build tools to intervene—toggling parts of the computation to watch which break the model or change the answer. Fourth, they certify their findings, because tiny circuits are fragile and small changes reveal different mechanisms.

Attention heads: finding what they do

Attention heads are like selective readers: each head learns to focus on different parts of the input sequence. Understanding which heads do what unlocks the model's computation. The challenge is scale—a model has hundreds of heads—and many heads participate in multiple tasks at once.

The work on Decoupling Positional and Symbolic Attention shows that heads split their job by frequency: some attend to nearby tokens (local positions), others to distant semantic matches. By controlling which frequencies each head sees, the researchers could steer the model's behavior. Expert Heads identifies specific heads that reliably surface evidence for retrieval tasks and multi-hop reasoning, outperforming dense retrievers on QA benchmarks because it directs the model to use the right evidence. The SLASH paper sharpens structural attention by redirecting attention weights away from useless patterns (so-called attention sinks) toward real structure, which improves performance on graph and molecular tasks because the model can now devote more attention capacity to the actual relationships in the data. Identifying and Evaluating Inactive Heads measures that over 12% of attention heads fire randomly and can be deleted without hurting accuracy, showing models learn redundant capacity they don't need. Towards Understanding the Nature of Attention uses sparse decomposition to find rare subtoken induction heads that operate at the character level, enabling cleaner circuit discovery than earlier methods because character-level heads are more interpretable units. Finally, Localizing Task Recognition and Task Learning pins down two complementary head types: some heads align hidden states to task subspaces (recognition), others rotate within that subspace to shift predictions (learning).

Functional circuits: finding which neurons solve sub-problems

A circuit is a minimal set of neurons and connections that performs one sub-task. The problem is that circuits are not unique—the same output can come from multiple different paths through the network. A second problem is scale: even a tiny circuit is hard to isolate in a model with millions of weights. Researchers use causal intervention (ablating parts) and spatial analysis to find these sub-problems.

All Circuits Lead to Rome warns that mechanistic explanations are non-canonical: the same reasoning task may use multiple disjoint circuits with low overlap. This fundamentally changes how we interpret circuit discovery results—there is no single "the circuit" but rather a family of equivalent solutions. Certified Circuits puts circuit discovery on formal ground, yielding circuits with provable stability guarantees and 56% higher accuracy than baselines under ablation because formal proofs ensure the discovered circuits actually matter. Circuit Insights goes beyond activation analysis to reveal circuit dynamics—how information transforms as it flows through layers—making mechanistic analysis more reliable when tested on new data because understanding the transformation steps catches changes that activation-only views miss. From Directions to Regions uses local spatial structure (not single points) to decompose activations, showing that concepts live in regions of activation space rather than single directions, outperforming sparse autoencoders on steering because regions capture the stability that points miss. Formal Mechanistic Interpretability establishes the first principled guarantees for circuit discovery, proving which parts are truly necessary for a task so claims hold under mathematical scrutiny. Function Induction and Task Generalization reveals that induction heads—a known mechanism for copying patterns—appear at higher abstraction levels as a reusable algorithm across synthetic and real reasoning tasks, suggesting the model discovers fundamental problem-solving strategies.

Steering and intervention: changing what models compute

Steering is the inverse of tracing: if you understand the circuit, you should be able to flip switches and change the computation. The hard part is doing this reliably—small interventions often fail or break the model in unexpected ways. Researchers use causal intervention, activation patching, and weighted addition of internal representations to surgically modify model behavior.

Path Channels and Plan Extension traces planning in a simple RNN on Sokoban, revealing implicit bidirectional planning in model-free reinforcement learning—showing that even simple models learn multi-step planning strategies. Scaling Reasoning Hop identifies a critical bottleneck in multi-step reasoning: a simple lightweight intervention (redirecting attention) consistently improves generalization across LLMs and tasks because fixing one bottleneck unblocks the downstream reasoning. Task Vectors Learned Not Extracted shows that hand-crafted task directions (weighted combinations of model weights, or vectors) can be learned end-to-end, outperforming extracted ones by steering specific key attention heads and rotating early representations toward task-relevant subspaces, which means learned vectors adapt to the model structure better. Faithful Bi-Directional Model Steering achieves stable, interpretable steering by deriving steering factors from how data activates the model rather than hyperparameter tuning, enabling both to-true and to-false flips on behavioral attributes because data-driven factors reflect what the model actually computes. How Reasoning Flows traces information pathways through attention, enabling targeted reinforcement learning that focuses learning signals on tokens routing information toward correct answers so learning updates flow where they matter. Reforming the Mechanism shows that circuit reshaping (editing patterns within identified circuits) improves logical reasoning across multiple difficulty levels because fixing circuits directly targets the broken computation.

Tracing computation across layers: how information transforms

Information flows through a model layer by layer. Early layers build simple features, middle layers combine them, late layers make high-level decisions. Understanding this pipeline requires tracing which computations happen where and how representations morph. This work often reveals surprising facts: that some layers are redundant, that computation happens earlier or later than expected, or that multiple mechanisms compete.

Transformers Don't Need LayerNorm at Inference shows that LayerNorm—a normalization operation—barely affects GPT-2 XL (only +0.03 loss increase when removed), enabling cleaner mechanistic interpretability research by removing a confounding component. How Do Transformers Learn to Associate Tokens matches learned weights to theoretical predictions based on corpus statistics, revealing how each component captures semantic associations grounded in training data. How Language Models Process Negation shows that negation is handled by competing mechanisms in late-layer attention modules; ablating these modules greatly improves negation accuracy, exposing a fragile handling strategy. How Transformers Represent Hierarchies uncovers a local-to-global parsing mechanism achieving logarithmic depth that matches trained Transformer behavior on structured sequences. Language Models Use Lookbacks identifies belief-tracking via lookback mechanisms—heads that revisit earlier tokens to maintain state for character tracking and Theory of Mind tasks. The Mechanistic Emergence of Symbol Grounding traces the layers where words ground to meaning: this happens via aggregate mechanisms in middle layers across Transformers and state-space models.

Hardening interpretability: validation and stability

Mechanistic interpretability findings are fragile. Small changes to training, architecture, or the specific input can reveal different circuits or break claimed mechanisms. Researchers address this by measuring stability (whether findings hold under perturbation), proving theoretical guarantees, and showing when findings transfer across models. This work shifts interpretability from exploratory to empirical science—grounding claims in data.

Mechanistic Interpretability as Statistical Estimation warns that small perturbations yield vastly different circuits, emphasizing the need for statistical rigor because single findings from one model run can be misleading. Provably Tracking Equivalent Mechanistic Interpretations provides the first theoretical guarantees relating a model's algorithmic interpretations, circuits, and representations, laying formal foundations for evaluation so interpretability claims can be proven. Quantifying LLM Attention-Head Stability establishes cross-instance consistency as a prerequisite for oversight—showing that weight decay substantially improves consistency, and middle layers show the strongest variability between runs, which means middle layers are where the model's behavior becomes most changeable. Towards Understanding Subliminal Learning shows that hidden-bias transfer is fragile to prompt paraphrasing and depends on masking divergence tokens—demonstrating fine-grained mechanisms can collapse under small changes, so mechanisms are sensitive to subtle variations. Weight-sparse transformers have interpretable circuits proves that sparse models develop circuits with unprecedented human understandability and that sparsity-interpretability trade-offs improve at larger scale because sparsity forces each component to specialize. When Thinking Backfires reveals that reasoning-induced misalignment correlates with activation entanglement between reasoning and safety circuits—identifying a mechanistic source of catastrophic forgetting because both systems compete in the same neurons.
