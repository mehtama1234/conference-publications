SYNC: Measuring and Advancing Synthesizability in Structure-Based Drug Design

A classifier that predicts whether a designed drug molecule can actually be made in a lab, then uses that to guide molecule design toward synthesizable solutions.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When a computer proposes a new drug molecule, there is a hard gap between what looks good on screen and what chemists can actually build in a lab. Traditional ways to judge synthesizability are inconsistent and slow. This paper builds a fast, consistent system called SYNC that learns to spot which designed molecules are really buildable, and then steers the generation process toward molecules that bind to disease targets AND can be synthesized.

Zero assumption version

Designed molecules that cannot be made

Drug design now uses AI to generate 3D shapes that stick tightly to disease proteins. The problem: many AI-generated molecules cannot actually be synthesized in a chemistry lab, even though they look perfect on a computer. Synthesizing means taking simple, available chemicals and gluing them together following real chemistry rules — a process with strict constraints.

A beginner might check each proposed molecule against old synthesizability rules (lists of forbidden patterns, scoring functions like Synthetic Accessibility Score). The problem bites immediately: different rules give opposite verdicts for the same molecule. Rule A says it is easy to make; Rule B says it is impossible. This inconsistency makes them useless for steering design. Also, running these rules is slow, so they do not scale to screening thousands of candidates.

In one sentence. SYNC learns to predict synthesizability directly from a molecule's 3D shape using one consistent, fast model.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train SYNC

Learn a model that scores how synthesizable a 3D molecule is, using known examples.

Plug into generation

Feed SYNC scores into the diffusion sampler as a reward signal during molecule building.

Steer toward high scores

The generator adjusts its steps to favor high-synthesizability paths while keeping binding intact.

Direct Preference Optimization

Rank pairs of generated molecules by their SYNC scores, then retrain to prefer the synthesizable ones.

Final check

Screen the outputs; SYNC also ranks them so the chemist gets the best candidates first.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of running eight inconsistent hand-written scoring rules, SYNC replaces them with one learned classifier. This one model, trained on examples of real synthesizable and hard-to-make molecules, produces consistent, interpretable scores.

The hidden quantity

Synthesizability score: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Synthesizability score

A synthesizability score is a number between 0 and 1 that answers: given this 3D molecule's shape, atoms, and bonds, how easy is it for a chemist to build it from simpler pieces? A score near 1 means easy; near 0 means very hard or impossible. The paper trains SYNC to predict this number by learning patterns in where atoms are positioned in 3D space — patterns that match up with which real lab designs turn out buildable.

SYNC computes the score using an SE(3)-invariant architecture, which means it sees the same structure no matter how you rotate or flip the molecule in 3D space — just like a chemist recognizes the same molecule whether it sits upright or sideways. The paper measures success by checking if SYNC's scores match a database of known synthesizable vs. difficult molecules, and by testing if scores correlate with how readily a new molecule can be made.

Why it works

Why SYNC makes better drug design

The core reason SYNC works: old scoring rules were built by hand, listing forbidden substructures or applying simplistic formulas. Each rule was a separate engineer's guess, so they conflict. SYNC learns from real examples of molecules that were actually built or actually failed to build. It sees the actual positions and arrangement of atoms in 3D space — not just abstract counts and patterns. This spatial arrangement carries the real information about whether a molecule is synthetically feasible, because real chemists look at a drawn structure and mentally simulate which reactions are possible given how the atoms physically sit.

When SYNC is plugged into the generation process, it acts as a reward. During diffusion (which builds molecules step by step by gradually adding atoms), the model checks the SYNC score at each step and nudges its next step toward higher-scoring shapes. Separately, after generating candidate molecules, Direct Preference Optimization ranks pairs by their SYNC scores and retrains the generator to produce molecules that score high. This dual steering — reward-guided diffusion plus preference ranking — moves the generator away from synthetically impossible regions without erasing the binding affinity that was already learned.

Math after the picture

The score formula

First in words:

The synthesizability score of molecule M is produced by the SYNC classifier as a function of its 3D geometry. During guided diffusion, the score is used to weight each diffusion step; during preference optimization, pairs are ranked by score difference and used to retrain the generator.

Then the compact form:

score_SYNC(M) = f_theta(geometry_3D) where f_theta is the learned SYNC model and geometry_3D encodes atomic positions and bond types using SE(3)-invariant features. During generation: loss_gen = binding_affinity(M) + lambda * score_SYNC(M), steering toward both high binding and high synthesizability.

Every symbol:

Ma 3D molecule (atoms and bonds in space)
geometry_3Dthe 3D coordinates and atomic types encoded as an invariant graph
f_thetathe SYNC neural network classifier
score_SYNC(M)predicted synthesizability, a number from 0 to 1
binding_affinity(M)how tightly the molecule fits the protein pocket
lambdathe weight balancing binding vs. synthesizability
SE(3)-invariantthe encoding stays the same under any rotation or flip
A tiny worked example
Suppose a proposed molecule A has SYNC score 0.8 and binding score 0.7.
Molecule B has SYNC score 0.4 and binding score 0.9.
With lambda = 1.0, molecule A's total = 0.7 + 1.0 * 0.8 = 1.5.
Molecule B's total = 0.9 + 1.0 * 0.4 = 1.3.
Molecule A ranks higher even though it binds less tightly, because it is much easier to synthesize.
A chemist can make A and run binding experiments; B would sit on a shelf as theoretically perfect but impractical.

The classifier learns a single, consistent number reflecting where atoms sit in 3D space; that number steers generation and ranking toward practical, buildable solutions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Training data bias. SYNC is trained on known synthesizable and difficult molecules from chemical databases. If the training set over-represents certain molecule families (e.g., drug-like compounds), SYNC will mispredict on unfamiliar families (e.g., exotic materials never seen during training).
Binding vs. synthesizability trade-off. Turning up the synthesizability weight in the loss function can push the generator to make molecules that are easy to synthesize but no longer bind tightly to the target protein. If lambda is too large, binding power collapses.
No guarantee that predicted synthesizable is actually buildable. SYNC predicts synthesizability from how atoms are positioned in space, but real synthesis also depends on reagent availability, reaction kinetics, and lab skill. A molecule with a high SYNC score might still fail in the real lab for reasons the model never saw.
Rotation and flip invariance assumed. SYNC uses rotation-flip-safe features, which is correct for 3D atom positions. However, if the 3D conformer (the actual spatial shape the molecule adopts) is wrong or unstable, SYNC scores a phantom form that will never exist in the lab.
Done right. Validate SYNC scores against a held-out set of real synthetic attempts. Collect diverse training data across chemical space. Tune lambda by testing generated molecules with both computational binding and actual lab synthesis. Use SYNC as a preliminary filter, not a guarantee.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that SYNC-guided generation produces molecules that score higher on both synthesizability and binding than molecules from the baseline generator (one without SYNC steering). Ideally, it should also show that at least one of the top-ranked SYNC-high molecules is actually synthesizable when attempted in a real chemistry lab, or that chemists rate them as more feasible than baseline candidates.

Improvement = (SYNC_score_with_SYNC_steered - SYNC_score_baseline) + (Binding_affinity_with_SYNC - Binding_affinity_baseline). A successful demo shows both terms positive: higher synthesizability and maintained or improved binding.

The hardest lesson to miss: one model trained on real lab outcomes beats eight conflicting hand-written rules, and using that model to steer generation produces molecules that both stick tightly to disease targets and actually can be synthesized by chemists.
