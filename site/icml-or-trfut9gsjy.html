Sparse Attention for Weather Forecasting

A weather model uses sparse attention and learned perturbations to preserve fine-scale weather details that compression normally erases.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Weather forecasting models trained on machine learning often blur fine-scale details — clouds, storms, rain patterns — because they compress data into tight files and train by predicting the average outcome instead of the variety. Mosaic fixes both problems: it uses attention (a way of picking which distant data matters for each prediction) that only looks at nearby regions efficiently, and it learns to add small random variations so forecasts capture the natural spread of possible futures.

Zero assumption version

Weather models lose fine details in two ways

A weather forecaster's job is to predict which values (wind speed, rain, temperature) will happen at thousands of grid points across the globe. Machine learning models do this by learning from past weather data, but they run into two steady traps. First, they train on the average outcome: given today, predict the mean temperature tomorrow. This erases the natural scatter — the fact that on similar days, real weather varies. A model trained this way learns to predict the safest average and misses the details that distinguish one storm from another. Second, to fit huge weather maps into memory, models compress data by combining nearby grid points or dropping high-frequency details. This bottleneck makes it hard to recover fine-scale features later, because once you merge two grid points into one number, the separate information is destroyed and cannot be recreated by any later layer.

A naive first attempt: train a bigger model or use a normal attention mechanism (where every grid point can look at every other grid point). This fails because it either runs out of memory or becomes so slow that forecasting takes days. The model needs to be both memory-efficient and able to reach distant weather patterns.

In one sentence. Mosaic recovers fine-scale weather details by using efficient sparse attention and learned random perturbations instead of compression.

What the paper changes

The paper moves two things

First: it removes the compression step so the model processes uncompressed weather data at its native resolution, allowing fine-scale details to enter and flow through every layer without being erased by merging. Second: it replaces the training goal (predict the mean) with a dual training: a base network predicts the central tendency, and a separate learned perturbation network learns how to shift that forecast to match the variability seen in real meteorological ensembles, because a single point estimate cannot capture the fact that identical starting conditions lead to different outcomes.

The hidden quantity

Spectral fidelity: how much detail survives

measured quantity  =  Spectral fidelity

Spectral fidelity is a measure of whether the model's forecast preserves all the scales of weather variation — from global wind patterns (slow, large-scale wiggles) to local storms (fast, small-scale wiggles). In signal-processing terms, it asks: does the model's output contain energy at all the frequencies present in the real data? A forecast with poor spectral fidelity is blurry and averages out storms into smooth fields. A forecast with high fidelity preserves sharp edges and small features.

The paper measures spectral fidelity by computing a power spectrum (breaking the forecast and real data into their fine-scale and coarse-scale components, like a piano keyboard) and comparing how much energy the model puts at each frequency. They check that even the fastest wiggles (smallest weather features) are represented, not erased by averaging or compression.

Why it works

Why sparse attention and perturbations recover detail

Compression destroys detail because packing a high-resolution grid into fewer numbers forces the model to forget small wiggles. Sparse attention solves this: instead of compressing, the model processes the full-resolution grid but smartly skips expensive attention links. It attends closely to nearby grid points (cheap because they are local) and learns which distant grid points matter (expensive but rare). This is like a person reading a book: you skim nearby sentences in the current paragraph, but jump to distant chapters when you recognize they matter. The cost grows linearly (proportional to the data size) instead of squared (all-to-all), so high-resolution weather is tractable.

Training to predict the ensemble mean erases variety because one number (the average) squashes all the possible futures into a single forecast. Mosaic adds learned perturbations: the model learns a function that, given the same input, outputs small random shifts. These shifts are added to the base forecast to create ensemble members — a family of plausible futures. The training sees real ensembles (where meteorologists run many simulations and average) and learns which perturbations make synthetic ensembles look like real ones. This way the model captures not just the center but the spread of possible outcomes.

How sparse attention costs less

First in words:

For each location in the weather grid, sum the weighted average of nearby locations plus learned distant locations. The weight for each other location depends on how similar their patterns are. Limit attention to K nearby blocks plus R learned long-range connections, so computation scales as O(N times (K plus R)) instead of O(N times N).

Then the compact form:

output_i = sum over j in Neighbors(i) and LongRange(i) of attention_weight(i,j) times value_j

Every symbol:

output_ithe prediction at location i
Neighbors(i)K nearby grid points to location i
LongRange(i)R distant grid points the model learned to attend to
attention_weight(i,j)learned score: how much location j influences location i
value_jthe weather data (temperature, wind) at location j
O(N times (K plus R))computation time: linear in data size N, not quadratic
A tiny worked example
Weather grid has 1000 locations. All-to-all attention: 1000 times 1000 = 1,000,000 weight multiplications. Sparse: each location attends to K=4 neighbors plus R=2 long-range = 6 connections. Total: 1000 times 6 = 6,000 multiplications. Speedup: 167 times faster.
Model learns: location at North Pole cares about location at Equator when a global wave pattern appears. Sparse attention includes this link because it matters, not because of distance.
Result: high-resolution forecast (no compression) computed in acceptable time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Sparse pattern must match weather physics. If the learned sparse attention pattern ignores a region where real weather matters (e.g., a distant jet stream), the model cannot reach it. Training must see enough examples that the model learns which distant locations matter.
Perturbation magnitude must match true ensemble spread. If learned perturbations are too small, ensemble members collapse to near-identical forecasts and miss real variability. Too large, they become noise that washes out the signal.
High-resolution input requires hardware support. Sparse attention is efficient in theory but needs specialized hardware (modern GPUs with structured sparsity support) or it reverts to dense computation.
Done right: learned sparse patterns adapt to data. The model learns which distant connections matter by exposure to many weather scenarios. Ensemble perturbations are calibrated on real ensemble data, so spread matches nature.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a small weather prediction model on precipitation or temperature at a specific region. Split training into two versions: (1) old way: compress high-resolution data to low resolution before training, train to predict the mean only; (2) Mosaic way: keep full resolution, use sparse attention with a small number of learned long-range connections, train to predict both a base forecast and learned perturbations around it. Evaluate both models at their native resolution and measure spectral fidelity (for each scale of detail, check how much energy the forecast retains compared to real data) and ensemble realism (run the perturbation function multiple times and measure whether the resulting ensemble spread matches the spread of an actual meteorological ensemble).

Spectral fidelity = (sum of energy in forecast at each frequency) divided by (sum of energy in truth at that frequency), reported per scale

The demo must make impossible to miss that sparse attention preserves fine details that compression would lose, and that learned perturbations capture the natural spread of weather futures.
