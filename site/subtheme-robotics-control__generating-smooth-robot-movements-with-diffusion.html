Generating smooth robot movements with diffusion

Papers applying diffusion models and flow matching to produce natural, smooth robot trajectories and policies—these generative models learn distributions of good movements from data.

What this is

In recent years, generative models called diffusion models have shown that they can produce high-quality images and videos by starting with noise and gradually refining it into something structured. The same idea can be applied to robot motion. Instead of asking 'what is the one best action,' you can ask 'what does a smooth, natural sequence of actions look like?' and let a generative model produce a sequence of good motions sampled from patterns in the training data. The advantage is that the model learns from the data what good movement looks like, without needing to optimize a complicated reward function. If the robot has seen a thousand hours of successful manipulation, the model learns to generate movements that look similar to those, because the model copies successful patterns from the training data—and movements that look natural to humans often work better in practice because humans have evolved to move in efficient, energy-conserving ways.

The challenge is that robot actions are constrained by physics and the shape and size of the robot body. A robot cannot move its arm backward through its own torso. It cannot apply forces larger than its motors can produce. Generating motions that are smooth, natural-looking, and physically feasible is much harder than generating images, where any pixel value is valid. Also, a diffusion model generates sequences of actions from scratch; it does not naturally connect to the current state of the robot. How do you tell the model 'start from this current position and generate a sequence that reaches this goal'? Conditioning the generation process to respect current state and goals is not straightforward.

Papers here use diffusion models as a policy—a way to generate action sequences in real time. Some condition the model on the current observation and the goal, so it generates an appropriate action. Some use diffusion for planning: the model generates a sequence of future states, the robot picks the first action in that sequence, and repeats. Some train the diffusion model on human demonstrations so it learns to imitate good behavior. The insight across these approaches is the same: if you have access to a good model of what successful motion looks like, you can generate new motions that are likely to succeed, without needing to optimize a complicated reward or planning problem.

How the work breaks down

The work in this subtheme splits into five natural directions: using diffusion models to learn smooth motion from video and human demonstrations (because raw data contains motion patterns the model can copy), planning trajectories that satisfy safety constraints and physics (so the robot does not crash or overextend its motors), adapting the model to new embodiments and environments (which lets a model trained on one robot work on a different one), combining multiple policies to handle diverse tasks (so one system can do many different jobs), and making the model run faster (so robots can execute these models in real time without lag).

Learning motion from video and imitation

A core challenge in robot control is that you need motion that looks natural and works in the real world. The simplest approach is to learn from examples: if you have videos of humans or robots moving smoothly, train a generative model to reproduce that pattern. This avoids the need to hand-code a reward function; the model just learns 'what does good movement look like?' from data.

Cosmos Policy takes video foundation models (trained on internet videos of how things move) and fine-tunes them for robot control by showing the model pairs of images and the actions between them. This works because video models have already learned how motion flows from watching millions of videos, so the robot learns to generate movements that fit natural-world physics. The result is a single model that can do manipulation with both arms on real robots with 67% success on complex real-world tasks. ViPRA (Video Prediction for Robot Actions) improves on this by adding a smooth prediction step: the model predicts the next frames as the robot moves, and that prediction is used to refine the action selection. This produces smoother continuous control and lets the robot run at 22 Hz (much faster than typical discrete action policies). Adversarial Dual On-Policy Distillation trains a teacher policy and transfers its knowledge to a smaller, faster student policy by having them compete against each other, which works better when the training data is noisy or limited.

Safe and constrained planning

Real robots live in constrained spaces. They cannot pass through walls, cannot apply forces beyond their motor limits, and must reach goals without crashing. Generating motion that is both smooth and satisfies hard constraints (like 'stay within this region') is much harder than generating unconstrained paths.

SafeFlowMatcher wraps a diffusion model with control barrier functions, which are mathematical tools that guarantee a trajectory stays in the safe region. During generation, the model checks each sample: if it would hit an obstacle, that sample is rejected and the model learns to prefer paths that never leave the safe region. Because unsafe samples are filtered out early, the model produces faster, smoother paths than standard diffusion while guaranteeing safety. SAD-Flower (Safe, Admissible, and Dynamically Consistent Planning) uses flow matching (a faster alternative to diffusion) and adds another layer: it ensures the generated trajectory respects not just collision-free regions but also the dynamics of the robot itself—so the accelerations and velocities the model predicts are actually achievable by the robot's motors. Both methods show that generative models can encode hard constraints without becoming much slower, which means robots can plan safely at high speed.

Hierarchical and long-horizon task composition

Most real-world tasks are not single actions; they are long sequences of subtasks. Pick and place has two phases: reach-grasp and place-release. Furniture assembly has dozens of small steps. The challenge is that a diffusion model trained on short clips of motion does not naturally extend to tasks that take minutes or hours.

H3DP (Triply-Hierarchical Diffusion Policy) solves this by creating three levels of hierarchy: it breaks the task into phases, then sequences of actions within each phase, then low-level motor commands. Each level learns its own diffusion model and they work together: the top level decides which phase to do next, the middle level decides what action sequence to do in that phase, and the bottom level decides the exact motor commands. Because each level specializes in one aspect, the model can handle tasks with dozens of steps and achieves 27.5% better results on complex manipulation than non-hierarchical approaches, which struggle when forced to generate everything at once. HDFlow (Hierarchical Diffusion-Flow Planning) uses a similar idea for tasks that can be decomposed spatially: plan a coarse path through space at the top level, then refine each segment with detailed motion at the lower level. This works well for furniture assembly where the robot needs to position itself, align a part, and then slide it in with precise force control, since each stage requires different types of motion control.

Part understanding and embodiment generalization

Robots come in many shapes and sizes. A humanoid has different body proportions than an industrial arm. A task learned on a two-fingered gripper may not transfer to a three-fingered hand. The challenge is that motion that works for one embodiment may be infeasible for another, and the model needs to understand what parts of the object matter and how they move.

PA3FF (Part-Aware Dense 3D Feature Field) explicitly learns which parts of the object the robot is manipulating and builds a 3D model that represents part locations and properties. This lets the model generalize better to new objects and new grippers because it understands what each part looks like in 3D space rather than memorizing specific examples. Motion Latent Guidance (From Language to Locomotion) solves the embodiment problem by training a humanoid on diverse motion descriptions and learning a latent space where similar motions are close together. Then at deployment, the model can take a language command or music and generate locomotion that fits that semantic meaning, and it automatically adapts to different humanoid bodies because the latent space works for any leg proportions. This cuts deployment time by half and produces smoother, more natural gaits.

Speed and real-time execution

Diffusion models run slow. They start from noise and refine it over tens or hundreds of steps, which can take seconds even on a fast GPU. But robots need to react to their environment in tens of milliseconds to stay stable, which means a slow model will miss the moment to act. Making these models run faster without losing quality is critical for real-world deployment, because a model sitting in the lab works on a schedule but a robot on a factory floor must act now.

Block-wise Adaptive Caching works as a plugin to any existing diffusion policy: it caches intermediate results and reuses them across multiple timesteps so the model does not recompute the same features each time it generates a new action, which means the same computation can serve multiple timesteps. This gives a 3x speedup with no retraining. Mixture of Horizons in Action Chunking learns to adaptively choose how many action steps to generate ahead at each call: sometimes the model should predict 1 step, sometimes 5 steps, based on what it is confident about, so the model spends computation only on uncertain decisions, which means it can execute more steps overall. This delivers 2.5x higher throughput. Sparse ActionGen uses structured pruning to skip unnecessary computations during generation, achieving up to 4x speedup because not every computation contributes equally to the final answer. VITA (Vision-to-Action Flow Matching Policy) uses flow matching instead of diffusion, which requires only one forward pass instead of dozens of refinement steps, and so it is 1.5x-2x faster than diffusion policies while matching or exceeding their accuracy on both simulation and real robots. These four papers show the consistent message: if you remove unnecessary computation (caching, pruning, fewer refinement steps) and let the model choose its own timing, you can run diffusion policies at real-time speeds without sacrificing performance because speed and quality are not fundamentally at odds if you target only necessary computation.
