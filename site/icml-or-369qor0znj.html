CoopEval: Benchmarking Cooperation-Sustaining Mechanisms and LLM Agents in Social Dilemmas

Test which game rules can make self-interested language-model agents cooperate instead of defect.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Stronger reasoning does not automatically make agents more cooperative because a capable agent can still see that defection maximizes its own payoff under the base rules. In one-shot social dilemmas, capable agents often choose the action that maximizes their own payoff even when mutual cooperation would be better for the group, because the game rewards defection at the individual level. CoopEval studies four classes of game rules that can change this outcome: repeated interaction, reputation, third-party mediation, and contracts that pay based on results.

Zero assumption version

Why capable agents still defect

In a social dilemma, each player can often gain by defecting even though both players would be better off if both cooperated. If an agent only tries to maximize its own immediate reward, defection is an attractive move. Recent language-model agents show this pattern clearly in one-shot settings.

This is a safety problem for multi-agent systems because future agents will often interact with other goal-directed systems, not just people. CoopEval asks a practical question: which external rules can sustain cooperation even when each agent is acting rationally with respect to its own payoff?

The paper's move in one sentence. Benchmark several mechanism-design tools across multiple social dilemmas and compare which ones make cooperation stable for language-model agents.

The hidden quantity

Cooperation incentive: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  How attractive cooperation is relative to defection

The key quantity is the incentive gap between cooperation and defection. If defecting gives a better personal payoff in equilibrium, capable agents will keep choosing it because that is the payoff-maximizing move under the rules they face. A mechanism works when it changes the payoff structure enough that cooperation becomes stable or at least much more attractive.

CoopEval estimates this hidden quantity indirectly through observed play across different mechanisms and game settings. High cooperation rates under a mechanism suggest that the incentive gap has moved in cooperation's favor.

Why it works

Why this works

Mechanisms work by changing incentives, information, or enforcement. Repetition helps because a player can punish future defection, reputation helps because a bad history affects future interactions, mediation helps because a trusted third party can coordinate decisions, and contracts help because they directly shift payoffs toward cooperative outcomes.

The experiments show that not all of these tools are equally dependable. Repetition can break down when co-players vary, because the future relationship is less predictable. Mediation and contracts perform better because they reshape the immediate decision more directly.

Math after the picture

A cooperation condition

First in words:

Cooperation is stable when the expected payoff from cooperating under the mechanism is at least as large as the expected payoff from defecting, taking into account future rounds, reputation effects, mediator choices, or contract payments.

Then the compact form:

cooperate if E[payoff_coop | mechanism] >= E[payoff_defect | mechanism]

Every symbol:

E[payoff_coop | mechanism]expected personal payoff if the agent cooperates under a given mechanism
E[payoff_defect | mechanism]expected personal payoff if the agent defects under the same mechanism
mechanismthe added game rule, such as repetition, reputation, mediation, or a contract
cooperatethe action that helps the group outcome
defectthe action that helps the agent immediately at the group's expense
A tiny worked example
In a one-shot game without extra rules, suppose cooperate gives 3 and defect gives 5.
Then 3 < 5, so a self-interested agent defects.
Now add a contract that pays 3 extra units when both players cooperate.
Cooperate now gives 6 while defect still gives 5.
Since 6 >= 5, cooperation becomes individually rational under that contract.

The paper studies several richer games, but the core point is the same: a mechanism succeeds when it changes expected payoffs enough that cooperation is no longer dominated by defection.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

A mechanism can depend on unrealistic assumptions. A contract or mediator may be effective in the benchmark but harder to deploy in an open environment because enforcement or trust may be missing.
Results can change across game families. A rule that helps in a prisoner's dilemma may work less well in a public-goods setting because the strategic structure is different.
Observed cooperation is not the same as genuine alignment. Agents may cooperate only because the mechanism made it profitable, not because they learned a broader cooperative norm.
Done right. Test multiple game families, vary co-players, and compare mechanisms by whether they change equilibrium incentives in a dependable way.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small prisoner's dilemma and public-goods simulator with two or more language-model agents. Run four settings: no mechanism, repeated play, mediation, and a simple contract with outcome-based payment. For each setting, record cooperation rate and average personal payoff over many runs.

The demo should prove that some mechanisms sustain cooperation because they alter expected payoff directly, while others help only in narrower conditions because their effect disappears when the interaction pattern changes.

cooperation_rate = cooperative_actions / total_actions ; stable_if cooperation_rate remains high across opponent changes
