Direct Doubly Robust Estimation of Conditional Quantile Contrasts

Estimate a treatment effect at different outcome ranks directly, so the result stays readable and does not have to be recovered by a hard inversion step.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

In treatment-effect work, people often want more than one average number. They want to know how a program changes the lower end, middle, and upper end of the outcome range for people with the same background traits. The conditional quantile comparator gives that kind of view, but the usual estimator reaches it indirectly: first estimate a difference between two outcome curves and then invert that result. This makes the target hard to inspect and hard to shape with domain knowledge because every modeling choice is filtered through an intermediate step. The paper builds a direct estimator instead. That helps because the object of interest is written down explicitly, so one can model it, constrain it, and study its error in terms of the actual target rather than in terms of a two-step detour.

Zero assumption version

Why the usual route hides the quantity people care about

Suppose a job program affects low earners and high earners differently. An average effect can hide that difference because it compresses the whole outcome range into one number.

The conditional quantile comparator aims to keep that richer view. It compares treated outcomes at different ranks while conditioning on the untreated outcome and the person's covariates. The trouble is that the usual estimator does not model this comparator directly. It first estimates outcome curves and then solves an inversion problem to recover the target. That indirect route obscures the final estimate because each modeling choice acts through a hidden intermediate object.

The paper's move. Write the target directly and estimate it directly, because a visible target is easier to interpret and easier to control than a target recovered from a two-step inversion.

What it actually does

Define the rank-specific treatment target.

State the conditional quantile comparator itself as the main object, so estimation focuses on the quantity of interest from the start.

Fit helper pieces for treatment and outcomes.

Estimate the treatment assignment and the untreated outcome behavior, because those pieces correct for confounding and missing counterfactual outcomes.

Build a direct estimating rule.

Combine the observed data and the helper pieces into a score whose zero point identifies the comparator directly.

Solve for the direct target.

Estimate the comparator without first recovering an entire outcome curve, so the model stays tied to the final quantity because it never has to pass through a larger intermediate object.

Read rank-specific effects.

Inspect how the treatment shifts low, middle, and high outcome ranks because the direct form keeps that structure visible.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  rank-specific treatment shift after conditioning on untreated outcome and covariates

The hidden quantity is not an average gain. It is the treatment shift at a chosen outcome rank for people who share the same background traits and the same untreated-outcome position.

That quantity matters because two groups can have the same average gain while having very different gains near the bottom or top of the outcome range. The direct estimator makes this quantity visible because the model is written for that exact target instead of for an intermediate curve whose meaning is harder to explain.

Why it works

Why the direct route helps

The usual two-step route spends effort on a broad intermediate object and only later converts that object into the target. Any error in that first stage is then filtered through an inversion, which makes interpretation and error analysis harder.

The direct estimator avoids that detour. It writes an estimating condition for the comparator itself, so each fitted degree of freedom is spent on the final target. This helps because model complexity now tracks the quantity people want to read rather than a larger object that exists only for the sake of the old procedure.

The paper also keeps a double-protection property: if either the treatment-assignment helper or the outcome helper is correct, the final estimate remains valid. That matters because observational data rarely give both helper pieces perfectly, so keeping validity under one good helper model reduces sensitivity to misspecification.

Math after the picture

Direct estimating rule

First in words:

Choose the comparator value so that a corrected weighted residual has average zero. The correction uses treatment-assignment and outcome helper pieces, so one correct helper is enough for validity.

Then the compact form:

mean[ score(observed data, comparator, helper pieces) ] = 0

Every symbol:

score(observed data, comparator, helper pieces)the corrected residual built from the observed sample, the target comparator, and the helper fits
comparatorthe rank-specific treatment quantity being estimated
helper piecesthe treatment-assignment and untreated-outcome fits used for correction
mean[ ... ]the sample average over people in the data
A tiny worked example
Pick the 25th outcome rank for people with the same background profile.
Suppose a trial comparator value of 3 makes the corrected residual average to +0.8.
That positive average says the trial value is too small, so move the comparator up.
Suppose a trial value of 5 makes the corrected residual average to -0.2.
Now the value is slightly too large, so move it down.
The estimate is the value where the corrected residual centers near zero, because that is the point the direct estimating rule asks for.

The method does not need to invert a full estimated outcome curve, so the fitted target stays directly interpretable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Both helper pieces can be poor. The double-protection property does not rescue the estimate when both the treatment-assignment fit and the untreated-outcome fit are wrong, because then the correction term is misaligned in two places.
Extreme ranks can be data-thin. Very low or very high outcome ranks may have little local support, so the direct target can vary a lot because only a small amount of information identifies those tails.
Conditioning variables can be misspecified. If the analyst omits an important confounder, the estimated treatment shift can still be biased because the direct estimator cannot repair information that is absent from the data.
Done right. With careful helper fits and enough support across the outcome range, the method yields a readable rank-specific effect because the direct estimating rule stays tied to the quantity of interest from start to finish.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic program-evaluation setting where age changes how much treatment helps at low and high income ranks, because that creates a clear rank-specific pattern the estimators must recover. Compare an indirect inversion-based estimator against the direct estimator on the same sample sizes and helper-model errors.

The demo should report rank-specific estimation error at several outcome ranks and under cases where only one helper fit is correct. A convincing result shows the direct estimator staying accurate and easier to inspect because the target is modeled explicitly rather than reconstructed after a detour.
