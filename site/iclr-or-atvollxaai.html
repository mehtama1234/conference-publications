SPRINT: Sparse-Dense Residual Fusion for Efficient Diffusion Transformers

Drop most tokens in deep layers while keeping all tokens in shallow layers, then train short-term on full tokens to fix the gap.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Diffusion models (which learn to reverse noise) built on transformers work well but cost a fortune to train—the cost squares as sequence length grows. SPRINT splits the work: shallow layers keep all details, deep layers throw away 75 percent of pieces (the chunks of data the model processes) for speed, and they glue together with simple residual connections. A two-part schedule (long pretraining with dropped pieces, then brief retraining on full pieces) closes the mismatch between training and real use, cutting training compute 10 times without hurting quality.

Zero assumption version

Why normal transformer training is expensive

Diffusion transformers (the models that turn noise into images step by step) work beautifully but cost huge amounts to train because they look at every token (piece of data) in every layer. Early on, the model only needs to know fine local texture—is this pixel corner sharp or smooth? Later, the model only cares about big global meaning—is this a dog or a cat? Yet standard transformers force the deep layers to process every single tiny local detail too, burning compute on information those deep layers throw away.

A beginner's first fix: drop tokens everywhere equally. Drop 75% of the least important tokens from each layer. But this breaks: shallow layers are starved of the fine texture they need, and deep layers have to guess what the missing tokens meant. The model forgets how to handle full-token inputs at test time.

In one sentence. SPRINT keeps all tokens in shallow layers and drops 75% in deep layers, then retrains briefly on full tokens to close the training-test gap.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

SPRINT modifies the token flow itself: shallow layers in a diffusion transformer process every token, while deep layers receive a token-dropped version (25% of the original). Rather than globally applying a fixed drop ratio, SPRINT applies depth-aware pruning—the depth of each layer determines its sparsity.

The hidden quantity

The layer specialization gap

measured quantity  =  wasted computation in deep layers

Every transformer layer has a job: shallow layers learn local texture (are pixels sharp or smooth?), deep layers learn global structure (dog or cat?). Yet they process the same tokens. SPRINT measures how much computation deep layers waste on fine-grained detail they discard anyway—this is the hidden quantity. If 75% of deep-layer attention and feed-forward compute never influences the final answer, that is pure waste.

SPRINT estimates this by design: drop tokens before deep layers and watch the quality loss shrink over training steps. If quality recovers quickly during the brief fine-tuning phase, then deep layers adapted successfully, confirming they can model global structure on sparse data.

Why it works

Why two-stage training closes the gap

The trap: if you train on sparse tokens only and test on dense tokens, the model breaks because deep layers never learned to handle dense input. SPRINT sidesteps this via a two-stage schedule. First, long pretraining (1–4 million steps, 75% drop) is cheap and teaches deep layers global structure on sparse data. Then, short fine-tuning (50–200 thousand steps, full tokens) wakes up deep layers to the full input stream. Since pretraining is 95 percent of the work and pretraining loss is already low, fine-tuning only needs 2.5 percent as many steps to close the training-testing gap.

Why it works mechanically: deep layers in the pretraining phase only receive 25 percent of tokens, so they learn to extract maximum signal from sparse data. During fine-tuning, they see full tokens but their already-learned sparse-adaptation strategy generalizes—they ignore or aggregate the noise correctly. Shallow layers stay dense throughout, feeding detailed local information to deep layers even during pretraining via residual fusion (a simple addition). The model never forgets how to process full inputs because fine-tuning refreshes deep-layer weights in the full-input regime quickly.

The fusion equation

First in words:

output = shallow layer output plus deep layer output on sparse tokens, residually combined; deep layer weights updated via standard diffusion loss during stage 2

Then the compact form:

y_final = y_shallow + y_deep_sparse (stage 1 pretraining) y_final = y_shallow + y_deep_dense (stage 2 fine-tuning, full tokens)

Every symbol:

y_shallowlearned representation from shallow layer on all tokens
y_deep_sparselearned representation from deep layer on dropped-token input during pretraining
y_deep_denselearned representation from deep layer on full-token input during fine-tuning
y_finalblended output sent to next stage
A tiny worked example
Suppose an image has 256 tokens. Shallow layer processes all 256.
Before deep layer, drop 192 tokens (75%), leaving 64.
Deep layer during pretraining: processes 64 tokens, makes a compact representation (y_deep_sparse).
During fine-tuning, deep layer retrains: processes all 256 tokens, refines representation (y_deep_dense).
At every stage, output = shallow (dense, detailed) + deep (global structure), residually fused.
Cost: 75% fewer operations in deep layers during pretraining (1M–4M steps); 2.5% cost overhead during fine-tuning (50K–200K steps). Total: 9.8× fewer training FLOPs.

Residual means each layer adds its output to the previous, so both shallow detail and deep structure flow forward without erasing each other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Skipping fine-tuning. If you only do pretraining with sparse tokens and skip the brief fine-tuning, deep layers never adapt to full-token input. At test time, the model degrades because it was trained on sparse but runs on dense tokens. You must retrain on full data, even if briefly.
Dropping too early. If you drop tokens before shallow layers, you starve them of local detail. They cannot pass detailed information to deep layers via residual fusion. Only drop after shallow layers extract dense features.
Weak residual fusion. If shallow and deep outputs are not properly fused (e.g., no residual connection), the model becomes two separate streams and loses coherence. The residual add is essential to blend local texture and global structure.
Done right. Stage 1: long pretraining with 75% drop in deep layers only. Stage 2: brief retraining on full tokens. Residual fusion at every step. Test time uses full tokens, full compute.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal diffusion transformer (a model that learns to turn noise into small images). Implement SPRINT: shallow layers process all tokens, deep layers get 75% dropped tokens during pretraining. Train for 1000 pretraining steps with sparse inputs. Measure quality (how realistic the images look). Then fine-tune for 25 steps on full tokens. Measure quality again. Compare against a baseline that either trains on full tokens (expensive) or trains on sparse tokens everywhere (broken at test time).

quality_gain = (quality after fine-tuning) minus (quality after pretraining alone) divided by (quality of full-token baseline)

The demo makes impossible to miss: brief retraining on full pieces recovers quality to baseline level, proving the training-testing gap closes fast. Pretraining cost is 75 percent cheaper because deep layers process only one quarter of the tokens (the rest are dropped), cutting their compute four-fold; and pretraining is 95 percent of all work, so the overall saving compounds to 10 times faster. Total wall-clock time is 10 times faster because deep-layer compute dominates, and dropping 75 percent of that work cuts elapsed time proportionally.
