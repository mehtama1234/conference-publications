Agentic Collaboration as an Information Bottleneck

Multi-model systems work better when you measure how much information the smaller model keeps when compressing raw context, then use that to pick which model pair to use.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Language model systems built for complex workflows often chain two models together: a small one squashes raw documents or code into compact text, and a big one reads that summary to answer the user. These two models need to coordinate, but today they are paired by guess and check. This paper asks: how much information actually survives the squeeze? If you can measure that, you can predict whether a system will work well, independent of what task you throw at it.

Zero assumption version

Picking compressor-predictor pairs is an expensive guessing game

Today, when engineers build multi-model systems (a small compressor squashing raw context, then a large predictor reading the summary), there is no principled way to choose which models to pair. They resort to running exhaustive tests across all combinations, which is slow, expensive, and specific to each task.

A naive engineer might think: 'Bigger models are always better, so use the biggest compressor and biggest predictor.' This almost always wastes money because the compressor often becomes the bottleneck. Or they might think 'Smaller is cheaper, so squeeze everything into a 2B model.' This fails because the tiny compressor throws away critical details that the predictor needs, and no amount of scaling the predictor can recover what was lost in the squeeze.

In one sentence. You can measure how much useful information survives compression and use that to pick model pairs without exhaustive testing.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Frame the system as information flow

Treat the compressor as a noisy channel: raw context goes in, compressed text comes out, and information can be lost.

Measure mutual information

Calculate how much of the original context details are recoverable from the compressed output, measured in information units per compressed word.

Test across datasets and models

Run the measurement on five different datasets and three model families to see if mutual information predicts performance universally.

Compare scaling paths

Compare scaling the compressor versus scaling the predictor and see which gives more bang for the computational buck.

Apply to real systems

Show that a 3B compressor keeps 99% of the accuracy of a frontier model's system while cutting API costs to 26%.

What the paper changes

The quantity the paper measures

The paper focuses on one specific thing: the mutual information (a numerical score measuring information preservation) between raw context and the compressor's output. This is the single metric that predicts whether a compressor-predictor pair will work well or fail.

the object it moves  =  Mutual information in compressed text
The hidden quantity

Mutual information: how much detail survives compression

measured quantity  =  Mutual information (information units per word)

Mutual information is a number that measures how much you can learn about the original context by reading the compressed version. If the compressor copies the context word-for-word, mutual information is high (all details survived). If the compressor replaces everything with a single generic sentence, mutual information is near zero (all details were lost). It is measured in 'bits per token' (how many bits of original context each compressed token represents).

The paper estimates mutual information by checking: given only the compressed text, how well can you predict what the original context said? High performance at that prediction task means high mutual information.

Plain reading. Think of squeezing a sponge: clear water comes out (high mutual information) or dirty, cloudy water (low mutual information). The clarity of what drips out tells you if you squeezed hard enough to destroy the useful stuff.
Paper reading. Formally, mutual information between X and Y measures the reduction in uncertainty. It can be calculated as: the detail-level of X minus the detail-level of X given Y. Here X is the original context, Y is the compressed text, and detail-level (a measure of information richness) quantifies how much detail or uncertainty is present in the text.
Why it works

Why measuring information predicts system performance

Here is the causal chain: the compressor reads raw context and writes a short summary. That summary is lossy—it throws away details. If the compression throws away critical information (low mutual information), then the predictor has no way to recover it, no matter how capable the predictor is. The predictor works with only what the compressor passed along. Therefore, mutual information directly limits the predictor's ceiling performance. Any dataset, any task—higher mutual information in the compression means the predictor can do better because it has more material to work from.

Why does scaling the compressor work better than scaling the predictor? A small compressor has limited reasoning capacity to figure out what to keep and what to discard, so it drops important details. A larger compressor can understand context more deeply and preserve information more selectively, passing along more information per word. Once that bottleneck is cleared, the predictor has the material it needs. Scaling the predictor without first fixing the compressor does not help because the predictor can only work with information it receives—if the compressor threw away critical details, no larger predictor can recover them.

Math after the picture

Mutual information equation

First in words:

Mutual information between original context X and compressed text Y equals the entropy of X minus the entropy of X given Y. High mutual information means the compressed text removes little uncertainty about the original.

Then the compact form:

I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)

Every symbol:

I(X;Y)mutual information between original context and compressed text (bits per token)
H(X)entropy of the original context (how much uncertainty or detail is in the original)
H(X|Y)entropy of original given the compressed text (how much uncertainty remains after reading the summary)
Xthe raw context
Ythe compressed output from the compressor
A tiny worked example
Imagine an original medical document with 8 bits of information (diagnoses, measurements, treatments).
After compression, you can still recover 6 bits by reading the summary (the compressor kept the critical details).
Remaining uncertainty: 8 - 6 = 2 bits were lost to the squeeze.
Mutual information I(X;Y) = 8 - 2 = 6 bits. That compressor preserved 75% of the original detail.
A worse compressor that kept only 3 bits would have I(X;Y) = 8 - 5 = 3 bits (only 37% preserved).
The paper shows: systems with mutual information of 6+ bits per token answer questions correctly; systems with 3 bits per token fail most tasks.

The higher the mutual information, the more original context survives the squeeze. This improves the predictor's performance because the predictor has more accurate and complete information to work with.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Compressor optimizes for brevity, not information. If you train the compressor only to be short (minimize token count), it will drop important details to save tokens, leaving low mutual information and poor final performance. The compressor must be trained to preserve information, not just shrink text.
Predictor cannot recover lost information. No amount of scaling the predictor can recover details the compressor threw away. If the compressor bottleneck is too tight, the system fails no matter how smart the predictor is. You must fix the compressor first.
Task-specific demands break the prediction. If a rare task requires context details that the compressor never learned were important, mutual information from other tasks will not predict performance on that task. The framework works well across common patterns but breaks on tasks with unforeseen requirements.
Compressor and predictor mismatch. A 7B compressor trained to preserve information for Qwen-class predictors may not work as well with a fundamentally different predictor (e.g., a sparse model or a specialized domain model). Information is task-agnostic, but model architecture fit still matters.
Done right. Measure mutual information on representative data. Use it to pick compressor scale based on the information bottleneck, not cost alone. Pair with a predictor sized to consume that information rate efficiently.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a multi-model system where you can swap compressor models (1B, 3B, 7B) and measure mutual information for each. Then on a held-out test dataset, run the compressor-predictor pair on a new task the mutual information measurement never saw. The demo must show that the compressor with the highest mutual information predicts the highest final accuracy on that new task, even though the mutual information was measured on different data.

Mutual information (bits per token) on calibration data → Task accuracy on held-out test data. Rank compressors by MI; verify they rank the same on test accuracy.

The key claim is that mutual information predicts performance independent of task. The demo must show the ranking by information quality matches the ranking by accuracy on completely new ground truth data the information measurement never touched.
