Federated Quantile Learning with Privacy

When hospitals or clinics keep data private and learn together, this paper shows how to estimate the middle-to-edge values of outcomes fairly across all of them.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Imagine 100 hospitals trying to understand what a typical patient outcome looks like — not just the average, but the full picture of who does best and worst. They cannot share raw patient records (privacy rules forbid it), and each hospital has slightly different kinds of patients. This paper solves that problem: a method that lets each hospital add noise to its local updates (to hide individuals) and combine those noisy results into one honest estimate of where outcomes sit across the whole population.

Zero assumption version

Learning from split data without revealing individuals

Suppose 100 hospitals want to work together to understand patient outcomes. Each hospital has data (ages, treatments, results), but they cannot share it because privacy law forbids it. A naive approach: each hospital trains a local model, and then someone averages the final numbers. But this breaks in two ways. First, if you average outcomes from hospitals with very different patient populations — one treats wealthy patients, one treats poor patients — the average is nonsense; it hides the edge cases (worst outcomes) that matter most for planning. Second, even if you average carefully, a clever attacker can often reverse-engineer original patient details from the shared updates.

The problem is harder for quantiles (like: 'the median' or 'the 90th-percentile outcome') than for averages. Quantiles measure where individual points sit; they have sharp corners in their math, not smooth curves. That makes them stubborn — standard techniques that work for smooth averaging do not work here.

The core challenge. Multiple sites with private data and different patient mixes must jointly estimate quantile values (like medians or the tail) without revealing anyone's record and without averaging away the differences.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

Most federated learning papers move the average (the mean outcome). This paper moves quantile values — specific points in the outcome distribution (the pattern of how often each outcome occurs, like the median or the 90th percentile). The quantile is found by solving an optimization problem (finding the value Q that minimizes a special loss function), and the paper adjusts how local gradient steps are taken and noise is added so that heterogeneous (different-sized, different-kind) sites all contribute fairly to that one estimate.

the object it moves  =  Quantile estimation across heterogeneous sites
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: bias from the noise

measured quantity  =  Noise-induced bias and how to cancel it

When you add random noise to hide individuals, you introduce a side effect: the noisy updates point in slightly the wrong direction. In typical federated learning, the server averages the noisy updates directly and the noise washes out in the limit. But quantiles are sharp; naive averaging leaves residual error.

This paper names that error and invents a trick to cancel it: each hospital includes in its update a self-normalizer (a sum that measures local data scale). The server uses that sum to estimate and subtract out the noise-bias exactly, leaving a clean quantile estimate. The hidden quantity is not just noise, but the interaction between noise and the non-smooth shape of quantile loss.

Why it works

Why noise-correction works for quantiles

In standard federated averaging, each site sends noisy updates and the server averages them. When data is smooth and the loss is convex, noise averages to zero. But quantiles lose that property: the objective function has kinks. Noisy updates from the kink region send the estimate slightly off track, and averaging does not fix it automatically.

This paper solves it by having each site compute a self-normalizer: a sum (the L2 norm, a measure of how much data is at that site) sent along with the noisy gradient. The server uses this sum to estimate the exact bias that the noise and non-smoothness introduce, and subtracts it out algebraically. This works because the self-normalizer captures the scale of each site's data without needing the raw data or a separate estimate of the Hessian (a matrix used in smoothness arguments). The result: a clean quantile estimate that still respects privacy.

Math after the picture

The estimator and its correction

First in words:

The quantile Q is found by minimizing: sum over hospitals k of (weight p_k times local quantile loss for that hospital). The loss has a kink, so gradients are not smooth. Each hospital sends a noisy gradient plus a self-normalizer V_k (the scale of its data). The bias correction works by subtracting an estimate of noise-induced drift, which is proportional to the variance of the local updates, measured via the self-normalizer.

Then the compact form:

Q_hat_T = Q_previous - (1/T) * sum_t sum_k (p_k * (noisy_gradient_k_t + bias_correction_t))

Every symbol:

Q_hat_Tthe final estimated quantile after T rounds
p_kweight of hospital k in the global pool
noisy_gradient_k_tprivacy-protected gradient update from hospital k at round t
bias_correction_tthe adjustment to remove noise-induced drift
Ttotal number of communication rounds
A tiny worked example
Suppose the true quantile is Q=50. Hospital A sends gradient 0.8 with self-normalizer 100, Hospital B sends gradient 0.3 with normalizer 80, both with privacy noise added (random ±0.1).
Hospital A's noisy gradient observed: 0.75. Bias estimate for A: 100 * 0.04 / (100 + 80) = 2.2 (small, because A has larger scale).
Hospital B's noisy gradient observed: 0.25. Bias estimate for B: 80 * 0.04 / (100 + 80) = 1.8 (a bit larger, because B is smaller).
After bias correction: effective update from A is 0.75 - 2.2 = -1.45, from B is 0.25 - 1.8 = -1.55. Averaged: -1.5 per round.
After 10 rounds, Q moves from 50 by roughly 10 * 1.5 = 15 units. True quantile value for this population holds, despite noise.

The self-normalizer lets the server estimate and cancel noise-drift without asking hospitals to send their raw Hessians, which would leak information.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Heterogeneous privacy budgets mismatch. If one hospital insists on very high privacy (lots of noise) and another accepts lower privacy (less noise), the correction becomes unbalanced. The fix: let each site choose its own privacy level upfront, and the server weights aggregation by how much noise each site added.
Extreme outliers in quantile targets. If one hospital wants the 1st percentile (the worst outcome) and another wants the 99th percentile (the best), the shared quantile becomes meaningless. The fix: communicate quantile targets upfront, or use one shared global quantile level and let each hospital estimate its local version separately.
Too few rounds or high noise. If the privacy budget is tiny (lots of noise added), or if training stops early, the bias correction is imperfect and convergence slows. The fix: run more rounds or relax privacy guarantees slightly, trading off privacy for accuracy as the application requires.
Done right: coordinate privacy budgets and quantile targets ahead of time. Ensure all sites agree on the quantile level being estimated (e.g., 'median' for all), and each site sets its privacy budget to match its risk tolerance. Run enough rounds for convergence and the bias correction to take effect.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should simulate a multi-site scenario: generate data from 3-5 different distributions (the patterns of how often each outcome occurs at each site; e.g., one site has mostly young patients, another old). Run the federated quantile algorithm with privacy noise enabled. Track two things: (1) the true quantile at each site (computed from full data), and (2) the estimated global quantile recovered by the algorithm. Also measure privacy loss (how much a privacy budget decreased per round) and show that privacy noise adds less than 5% error to quantile estimates compared to the noiseless version.

accuracy_loss = abs(true_quantile_site_i - estimated_global_quantile) / true_quantile_site_i, averaged over sites. privacy_cost = sum of privacy budgets spent. Trade-off: achieve <5% accuracy loss while maintaining privacy cost < 1.0 eps.

The core insight the demo must make clear: adding noise to hide individuals does not ruin the quantile estimate if you measure and subtract out the noise-bias using the self-normalizer. Privacy and accuracy are not enemies here.
