BOCLOAK: Adversarial Attacks on Bot Detectors via Optimal Transport

Use optimal transport math to design adversarial attacks that fool graph-based bot detectors while respecting real-world constraints.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Social media bots threaten public discourse, so platforms use graph-based detectors—models that look at how accounts connect—to find them. But attackers adapt, and the old ways of testing these detectors ignore the real constraints attackers face (limited friend slots, temporal order matters). BOCLOAK treats bot behavior and human behavior as two different probability clouds, learns the shortest path between them via optimal transport, and converts that into sparse, realistic edge edits that evade detection.

Zero assumption version

Why existing attacks fail on real bots

Platforms detect bot accounts using graph neural networks—models that learn patterns from the entire social network, not just one account alone. They look at friend counts, follow patterns, and how accounts interact. Attackers want to turn a detected bot into an undetected one by editing the friendship graph.

Naive attacks change edges randomly or greedily, which works in toy labs but fails in reality. Real attackers face strict constraints: you cannot befriend thousands of accounts in one day (temporal ordering), you can only add so many friends before humans notice (sparsity), and the specific people you befriend matter for believability. Existing attack methods ignore these constraints entirely, so they are useless for testing whether detectors actually work in the real world.

The gap: attacks that ignore real-world constraints. BOCLOAK learns where bot and human neighborhoods differ, then constructs minimal, plausible edge changes that move a bot's footprint to look human.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Model two clouds

Measure the spatio-temporal neighbor patterns of all known bots and all known humans.

Learn the transport path

Find the minimal-cost set of edge changes that would move a bot's neighborhood pattern to match a human's.

Respect constraints

Restrict the transport plan to changes that obey real-world rules: sparse additions, temporal ordering.

Decode to edge edits

Convert the optimal plan into actual friend additions or removals for the target bot.

Test detector

Run the graph-based detector on the modified graph; measure if the bot now passes as human.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the edges in the social graph—specifically, which accounts a bot is connected to. It adds or removes friendships to change how the bot's local neighborhood looks to the detector.

the object it moves  =  graph edges (friendships)
The hidden quantity

Transport cost: the distance between bot and human neighborhoods

measured quantity  =  Transport cost (neighborhood dissimilarity)

The paper measures how far apart a bot's neighborhood is from a human neighborhood in a high-dimensional space. This is not computed manually; it is learned automatically as an optimal transport plan. It is the minimal amount of edge-moving work needed to transform one bot's friend list into a human-like one.

The paper estimates this by setting up a probability measure (a statistical summary) over all the neighbor features (follow counts, temporal order, mutuals, etc.) for all known bots and humans. It then computes the optimal transport cost—a single number measuring how much effort (measured in sparsity and realism constraints) is required to move bot neighborhoods toward human ones. Sparse edits have low cost; wild changes have high cost.

Why it works

Why optimal transport solves the problem

The core insight: bot and human neighborhoods are geometrically different in a high-dimensional neighbor-feature space. Optimal transport is a mathematical technique for finding the cheapest way to morph one distribution into another. Here, the 'cost' of an edge change is not just binary (did you add an edge?); it includes realism penalties—adding a friend to a bot when real bots never befriend that person is expensive, as is violating temporal order.

The paper sets up the transport problem so that only changes consistent with real-world constraints have low cost. Adding five accounts the bot befriended at realistic times is cheap. Suddenly friending five thousand accounts or breaking temporal order is infinitely expensive (forbidden). The solver then finds the minimal path through the space of plausible edits. Because optimal transport is geometric, it naturally finds sparse solutions and ignores redundant changes.

Math after the picture

The optimal transport equation

First in words:

Cost of moving bot distribution to human distribution, minimizing over all possible transport plans, subject to sparsity and temporal constraints.

Then the compact form:

min_P sum_{i,j} C(i,j) * P(i,j) subject to: P >= 0, sum_j P(i,j) = rho_bot(i), sum_i P(i,j) = rho_human(j), card(P) <= k_max, temporal-order preserved

Every symbol:

P(i,j)how much transport mass flows from bot neighborhood feature i to human feature j
C(i,j)cost of moving from bot feature i to human feature j (includes realism penalty)
rho_bot(i)how often bot neighborhood feature i appears in the data
rho_human(j)how often human neighborhood feature j appears
k_maxmaximum number of edges we are allowed to change (sparsity constraint)
Tiny worked example
Bot has 100 friends: 80 follow-backs, 15 newly-followed, 5 old-follows.
Human average: 20 follow-backs, 50 newly-followed, 30 old-follows.
Transport step 1: reduce follow-backs from 80 to 20 (cost = 60 deletions × 1 per edge = 60).
Transport step 2: add 35 newly-followed (cost = 35 × 1 = 35).
Transport step 3: add 25 old-follows (cost = 25 × 1 = 25).
Total transport cost = 120 edge operations. Sparsity constraint says k_max = 50. Solver must choose only the 50 cheapest moves, leaving bot partially transformed but detectably different from both bot and human norms.

The solver minimizes total cost while respecting the limit on how many edges can be touched, so only high-impact changes are made.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatch between learned and real constraints. If the optimal transport model underestimates how quickly humans notice temporal anomalies, the transport plan may produce edits that still look suspicious in practice, even if they pass the constraint check.
Incomplete neighborhood features. If the detector uses information about a bot's followers (reciprocal friends, followers of followers) that the transport model does not include, the edits may not fool the detector.
Detector adapts to sparse edits. If platform operators learn that attackers are making minimal edge changes, they can retrain detectors to flag bots with specific sparse edit patterns, negating the advantage.
Done right. Model realistic constraints explicitly (temporal windows, friend-request limits, mutual-account availability), include all neighborhood features the detector uses, and assume platforms will retrain detectors after an attack—test the attack again on updated detectors.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic social graph with 200 humans and 50 bots, each with hand-crafted neighborhood patterns (bots cluster, humans spread). Train a simple graph neural network bot detector on this graph. Then run BOCLOAK on 10 target bots with realistic constraints (add or remove at most 20 edges per bot, respect temporal order). Measure how many of the bots now evade detection (attack success rate). Compare against a random-edge baseline and a greedy baseline.

Attack success rate = (number of bots that evade after BOCLOAK) / (total target bots). Memory usage = GPU VRAM consumed during attack planning.

The demo must show that BOCLOAK achieves >80% attack success with <100 MB GPU memory, while random edits either fail the detector less often or consume gigabytes of memory.
