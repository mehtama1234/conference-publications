Learning Distributions over Permutations with Factorized Representations

Instead of forcing permutations into hard constraints, recode them into simple numbers that a neural network can learn like any other data.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Permutations (rearrangements of items) are tricky to learn because reordering is a discrete problem without smooth nudges. Existing methods use expensive tricks like mixtures or diffusion models. This paper rewrites permutations using three alternative codes—Lehmer, Fisher-Yates, and Insertion-Vectors—that turn any permutation into a list of unconstrained numbers a normal neural network can learn directly.

Zero assumption version

Why permutations break normal neural networks

A permutation is just a rearrangement: if you have items A, B, C, one permutation is (B, A, C). The number of possible permutations explodes—for 10 items there are 3.6 million; for 20 items, more than 10 to the 18th power.

Normal neural networks learn by smoothly nudging numbers up and down. But permutations are discrete: swapping two items does not create a smooth path to a better solution, because the space jumps discontinuously—there is no "almost right" permutation between (A, B, C) and (B, A, C). If a neural network tries to output "item 2 should go in position 1," a small nudge of 0.1 does not mean "move it 10% of the way," it just breaks the answer. Traditional methods handle this by relaxing (stretching permutations into continuous space and then rounding) or mixing (combining multiple simple models), both expensive.

Existing approaches use variational inference (a slow probabilistic trick) or diffusion models (many noisy intermediate steps), costing tens to hundreds of forward passes just to train or inference once.

The core trick. Recode each permutation as a sequence of plain numbers where any choice at each step naturally produces a valid permutation when decoded back.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pick a recoding scheme

Lehmer (count inversions), Fisher-Yates (shuffle draw at each step), or Insertion-Vector (insert into sorted list).

Encode the target permutation

Turn the goal rearrangement into a sequence of plain numbers using that scheme.

Train a neural network on the coded sequence

Use autoregressive (one number at a time) or masked language modeling (predict multiple at once) like text models.

At inference, sample numbers from the network

Let the model propose each coded choice, or sample multiple in parallel if speed matters more than perfect expressivity.

Decode back to a permutation

Follow the recoding scheme in reverse. Every coded sequence guaranteed to produce a valid rearrangement.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the representation: from a discrete locked permutation to an unconstrained sequence of numbers. Instead of outputting "position of item A," the network outputs "at step 1, choose the 3rd item from what is left; at step 2, choose the 1st item from what is left," etc. This is automatically a valid permutation when you follow the recipe.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Factorization level

measured quantity  =  How much the network samples in parallel (the independence trade-off)

Normally you build a permutation one choice at a time: the network picks an item, removes it from the pool, picks the next item from what remains. This is autoregressive—each choice feeds into the next.

But the paper shows you can also sample multiple choices in parallel if you accept a loss. If the network tries to pick items 2, 3, and 1 all at once (using a masked language model), it treats those choices as independent of each other, even though they are not. This is a loss in modeling power, because the network can no longer capture that certain items must be chosen together, but it saves compute because one forward pass picks three items instead of three, cutting the compute cost per training step by 3x.

The paper measures this trade-off by testing different recoding schemes and observing which ones allow parallel sampling. Lehmer and Fisher-Yates can be factorized (sample multiple draws in parallel) because each draw only depends on what was sampled before, not what comes after. Insertion-Vectors are trickier because the insertion logic mixes past and future choices. The hidden quantity is: how many elements can the network sample independently in one pass without breaking the guarantee that the result is valid?

Why it works

Why this works: Bijection and unconstrained learning

The core insight is a bijection: a one-to-one map between permutations and sequences of numbers. Lehmer codes map each permutation to a sequence of counts (how many items are skipped at each step). Fisher-Yates maps to a sequence of shuffle draws (which position to swap at each step). Insertion-Vectors map to insertion positions.

Because the recoding is a bijection, every sequence of valid numbers produces exactly one valid permutation when decoded, and every permutation comes from exactly one coded sequence. The network does not need to worry about constraints: it just learns to output numbers. The decoding is deterministic and always valid.

This unlocks standard neural network training: autoregressive next-token prediction (like language models) where each step predicts the next number conditioned on the previous ones, or masked language modeling (like BERT) where the network fills in blanks. No expensive variational tricks, no relaxation and rounding errors, no diffusion steps. Training nudges flow cleanly through the learning process on the coded numbers, and validity is guaranteed by the bijection.

Math after the picture

The bijection equation

First in words:

Lehmer code: for each position in the permutation, count how many items to the right are smaller. Fisher-Yates: at each step, record which position was swapped. Insertion-Vector: record where each item was inserted into the growing sorted list.

Then the compact form:

Perm <-> Code: for Lehmer, code_i = count of items in Perm[0:i] that are smaller than Perm[i]; Lehmer[i] in range [0, n-i] always produces valid Perm when reversed.

Every symbol:

Perm[i]the item at position i in the permutation
code_ithe coded number at position i
nthe length of the permutation
bijectiona one-to-one mapping: every code has one permutation, every permutation has one code
Lehmer code example: permutation (B, C, A)
Position 0, item B: count items to the right smaller than B. A is smaller. code_0 = 1.
Position 1, item C: count items to the right smaller than C. A is smaller. code_1 = 1.
Position 2, item A: no items to the right. code_2 = 0.
Lehmer code: [1, 1, 0]. Reverse: code_0=1 means skip the first item (A), pick second (B). code_1=1 means from remaining (A,C) skip first, pick second (C). code_2=0 means pick remaining (A). Result: (B, C, A). Valid and deterministic.

Every sequence [a, b, c] where a in [0,2], b in [0,1], c in [0,0] produces a unique permutation and vice versa—the bijection guarantees the network's learned numbers always decode to a valid rearrangement.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Factorization loses expressivity. If you sample too many items in parallel, the conditional independence assumption breaks the model's ability to capture dependencies between choices. The network cannot express rankings that require global coherence. Autoregressive sampling has full expressivity but needs many forward passes.
Decoding complexity. Lehmer and Fisher-Yates have fast decoding, but Insertion-Vectors require a more complex algorithm. If the decoding is buggy or slow, the whole speedup vanishes. The paper provides a theorem and batched algorithm to fix this.
Single ground-truth benchmarks are misleading. Jigsaw puzzles have one correct answer. Traditional permutation models cannot learn that a wide range of reorderings are equally good (e.g., movies can be ranked many ways and still be close to correct). The paper adds cyclic permutations and movie ranking to expose this limitation.
Done right: choose the recoding scheme to match your task. Autoregressive for maximum expressivity. Factorized for speed. Lehmer for classic puzzles. Fisher-Yates for cyclic permutations. The bijection guarantees validity no matter which you pick.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small toy permutation task: unscramble a 10-letter word (a scrambled permutation). Train a simple autoregressive network on the Lehmer codes of the correct unscrambles. At inference, sample Lehmer codes from the network and decode them back to permutations. Show that the network learns to unscramble, and that every sampled code decodes to a valid permutation (no crashes, no invalid rearrangements). Compare one autoregressive forward pass (our method) to running a diffusion model for 50 steps on the same task.

Unscramble accuracy = fraction of sampled permutations that spell a real word; Compute cost = forward passes per inference; Invalidity rate = permutations that violate the bijection (should be 0%)

The demo must make it impossible to miss: training the coded numbers with standard methods is fast, direct, and guarantees valid outputs—no relaxation rounding errors, no diffusion noise, no expensive variational tricks.
