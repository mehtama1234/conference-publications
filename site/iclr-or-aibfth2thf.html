Robust Fine-Tuning from Non-Robust Models: Epsilon-Scheduling

When you train a model not originally built for attack-resistance to handle adversarial examples, the early-training stress blocks task learning — epsilon-scheduling gradually increases the adversarial challenge to let both happen.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Modern pretrained models are not designed to resist adversarial examples (tiny, imperceptible tweaks to images that fool the model). When you try to add resistance to such attacks via fine-tuning on a new task, the training conflict is brutal: the attack-resistance training signal pushes weights one way to withstand perturbations, while the task signal pushes another way to do the job. The result is often worse than just using the original model. This paper shows why and proposes a simple fix: start training with very small adversarial attacks and gradually increase them, letting the model adapt to the task first.

Zero assumption version

The attack-resistance training trap

Fine-tuning is the standard move: take a big pretrained model and train it on your specific task. It works because the model already knows general patterns. But resistance to adversarial attacks — the ability to resist tiny-but-crafted image tweaks — requires weights to move in very specific ways. If your pretrained model was never trained to resist such attacks, and you suddenly force it to during fine-tuning, the training pulls in two opposite directions at once: one signal says move weights to do the task, another says move them to handle perturbed inputs. The model gets stuck.

A beginner would try: 'Just use an attack-resistant training objective from the start.' But experiments show this does not work — the adversarial stress is so strong it prevents the model from learning the downstream task at all. The weight changes needed for attack resistance dominate, and task adaptation fails. In hard cases, performance can be worse than if you had just used the original model without any adversarial training.

In one sentence. Adding resistance to adversarial attacks during fine-tuning blocks the model from learning the new task, because the two goals pull weights in opposite directions.

What the paper changes

The adversarial perturbation schedule

The paper changes one key thing: the strength of the adversarial attacks during training, which is usually called epsilon. Instead of using the same epsilon from the start of training, the paper uses a schedule — epsilon starts tiny and grows over the training run. This single change breaks the conflict between task learning and attack-resistance.

the object it moves  =  adversarial perturbation schedule
The hidden quantity

Expected attack-resistance: averaging across attack strengths

measured quantity  =  expected attack-resistance

The paper introduces a new way to measure model quality: instead of asking 'does it resist this one specific attack strength?', it asks 'what is its average performance across a range of attack strengths?' This is called expected attack-resistance. Think of it as averaging the model's score at many different perturbation levels (weak attacks, medium attacks, strong attacks) rather than picking one worst-case number. It gives a fuller picture of the accuracy-tradeoff when adding attack-resistance.

The paper estimates expected attack-resistance by testing the model at many different epsilon values and averaging the results. This reveals whether a model works well across the board or only at one specific attack strength and fails at others.

Why it works

Why scheduling the adversarial pressure prevents transfer failure

During training, the model receives two competing signals: the task loss (learn to classify correctly on the new task) and the adversarial loss (learn to predict correctly even when the input is slightly perturbed). If both signals are equally strong from the start, the adversarial signal is so intense that it prevents the model from moving weights toward the task goal at all — the model gets trapped trying to satisfy the attack-resistance constraint before it even understands the task.

Epsilon-scheduling breaks this deadlock by starting with an almost-zero adversarial constraint. Early in training, the task signal dominates, and weights move freely toward solving the task. Once the model understands the task and has made substantial progress, the adversarial pressure gradually turns on. Now the weights have a good "anchor" — they are already in a region that solves the task — so the gradual increase in adversarial stress refines them for attack-resistance without pulling them off the task cliff. The schedule acts as a curriculum: first do the easy thing (learn the task), then do the hard thing (add attack-resistance on top).

Math after the picture

The training loss with epsilon-scheduling

First in words:

total loss = task loss plus epsilon(t) times adversarial loss, where epsilon(t) is a schedule that grows over training time t

Then the compact form:

L(t) = L_task(theta) + epsilon(t) * L_adv(theta, delta)

Every symbol:

L(t)total loss at training time t
L_task(theta)task loss: error on classifying the downstream task correctly
L_adv(theta, delta)adversarial loss: error when input is perturbed by delta
epsilon(t)the adversarial perturbation strength at time t; starts small, increases over time
deltaa small perturbation added to the input to simulate an attack
thetathe model weights
A tiny worked example
At epoch 1: epsilon(1) = 0.01, L_task = 0.5, L_adv = 2.0 -> total = 0.5 + 0.01 * 2.0 = 0.52. Task loss dominates.
At epoch 50: epsilon(50) = 0.25, L_task = 0.1, L_adv = 1.5 -> total = 0.1 + 0.25 * 1.5 = 0.475. More balanced.
At epoch 100: epsilon(100) = 1.0, L_task = 0.08, L_adv = 0.8 -> total = 0.08 + 1.0 * 0.8 = 0.88. Attack-resistance refinement phase.
The schedule epsilon(t) grows from 0.01 to 1.0, shifting the emphasis from task first to attack-resistance refinement.

The schedule epsilon(t) is the single knob: turn it up slowly, and task learning gets a head start before attack-resistance pressure arrives.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Schedule ramps too slowly. If epsilon grows so slowly that attack-resistance training barely starts before the end, the model will not resist attacks well. The schedule must actually reach a substantial epsilon by the end of training.
Starting epsilon is too high. If the initial epsilon is not small enough, the adversarial signal still drowns out task learning early. The schedule must truly start at a negligible perturbation.
Very difficult downstream task. On tasks so hard that the model struggles to learn even without adversarial pressure, the schedule may not recover the loss. The method assumes the pretrained model can solve the task reasonably well.
Done right. With a well-tuned schedule (small initial epsilon, smooth growth, reaching full epsilon by mid-to-late training), the model learns the task first and refines attack-resistance second, avoiding the all-or-nothing failure.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple classifier on a standard dataset (CIFAR-10: ten classes of small images). Start with a pretrained model not trained for attack-resistance. Run two training runs side by side: one with constant adversarial pressure (epsilon starts at 0.8 from epoch 1), and one with epsilon-scheduling (epsilon grows from 0.01 at epoch 1 to 0.8 at epoch 100). Measure task accuracy and attack-resistance at many epsilon values for each trained model.

expected attack-resistance, computed as mean of accuracy across all tested epsilon levels: accuracy at epsilon 0.0, plus accuracy at epsilon 0.1, plus continuing through epsilon 0.8, then divide by number of levels tested

The demo proves that epsilon-scheduling produces a model that works across a range of attack strengths, while naive constant-epsilon training produces either good task accuracy with no resistance, or vice versa — but not both.
