CO3: Contrasting Concepts Compose Better

When a diffusion model draws a multi-concept image, it drifts toward regions where single concepts dominate; CO3 steers sampling away from those regions so all concepts stay balanced.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Text-to-image models struggle when you ask them to draw multiple things at once—sometimes one concept vanishes, fades, or crashes into another in an ugly collision. CO3 fixes this without retraining the model. It works by detecting when the sampling process starts drifting toward a region of image-space where a single learned concept takes over, and gently pushes back toward territory where all concepts can coexist with equal visual weight.

Zero assumption version

Multiple concepts collide in a single image

Text-to-image diffusion models are trained on millions of (picture, text label) pairs. Each pair teaches the model: this text snippet points to this visual pattern. When you ask the model to draw a single concept like 'a cat', it works well—it has learned that pattern deeply.

But when you ask for a multi-concept prompt like 'a cat and a clock', something breaks. The model was trained on images that had both cats and clocks, yes, but it was also trained on images with ONLY cats and images with ONLY clocks. During sampling (the process of drawing a new image), the model can drift into a region of image-space where one of those single-concept patterns dominates. The result: one concept vanishes, turns faint and washed-out, or gets mangled into the other. The model has collapsed into a learned 'attractor'—a region where the math pulls it strongly toward one concept alone.

A naive fix would be to retrain the model on more multi-concept images, but that is expensive and slow.

In one sentence. CO3 detects when sampling drifts toward single-concept attractors and pushes the image back toward the balanced multi-concept region.

What the paper changes

What changes: the sampling path

The model itself stays the same. CO3 changes the path the sampling algorithm takes through image-space. Instead of moving in the direction that the standard guidance points, it adds a corrective nudge that steers away from single-concept attractors.

the object it moves  =  sampling trajectory
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: concept balance

measured quantity  =  concept imbalance score

Concept balance is a number that measures how evenly the model's learned patterns are represented in the image so far. If the cat pattern is firing at strength 80 and the clock pattern at strength 5, the imbalance is high. If both are firing at strength 50, the imbalance is low.

CO3 estimates this by looking at how strongly each single-concept pattern (learned during training) activates when you show it the image being generated. If any one pattern is much stronger than the others, sampling has drifted into that concept's attractor zone because the sampling algorithm's math pulls it toward regions where learned patterns are strongest—and a single strong pattern creates a sharper pull than a balanced mixture. The corrective guidance uses this signal to steer back toward low-imbalance territory.

Why it works

Why it works: the attractor trap and the escape

During training, the diffusion model learned millions of associations: this text goes with this image pattern. Some of those associations are for single concepts (just cats, just clocks), some for multi-concept combinations. When sampling, the process is trying to find an image that matches the prompt. The math pulls it toward regions of high probability—regions where learned patterns are strong.

Here is the trap: the single-concept regions are often sharper and more well-defined than the multi-concept regions because there are more training examples of single concepts (a million images with only cats) than of balanced pairs (far fewer with equal cat-and-clock presence). A sharper, more well-defined region creates a stronger pull on the sampling algorithm. So the attractor for 'pure cat' is stronger than the attractor for 'cat and clock' because the training data packed more density into single-concept territory. When sampling wanders near both, it gets pulled into the single-concept zone.

CO3 detects when imbalance is rising (one concept growing stronger than others) and applies a corrective nudge that points back toward the balanced zone because this rising imbalance is a signal that sampling is drifting into single-concept territory, and a nudge away counteracts that drift. The nudge is small enough not to break the model's ability to generate realistic images, but large enough to overcome the pull of the single-concept attractor. Think of it as a guardrail that keeps the sampling process from straying too far into lopsided territory.

Math after the picture

The equation: corrected guidance step

First in words:

next_image = current_image + step_size * (normal_guidance + correction_weight * balance_gradient)

Then the compact form:

x_{t-1} = x_t + alpha * (grad_prompt + lambda * grad_balance)

Every symbol:

x_tthe image at step t (earlier means more detailed)
x_{t-1}the image at the next step (slightly more denoised)
alphahow big each step is
grad_promptwhich direction to move to match the text (standard guidance)
grad_balancewhich direction to move to balance concepts (the new part)
lambdahow much to weight the balance correction
A tiny worked example
Suppose current image has cat-strength 75, clock-strength 20 (imbalanced).
Normal guidance says: move +8 in cat direction, +3 in clock direction.
Balance gradient says: move -5 in cat direction, +4 in clock direction (reduce cat, boost clock).
With lambda=0.5: next step = 8 + 0.5*(-5) = 5.5 in cat direction, 3 + 0.5*4 = 5 in clock direction.
Result: both concepts get roughly equal nudges, keeping them balanced.

The correction term scales with imbalance, so only kicks in when one concept is drowning out the others.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Correction is too weak. If lambda is too small, the nudge back toward balance is not strong enough to overcome the pull of the single-concept attractor. One concept still drowns out the other.
Correction is too strong. If lambda is too large, the corrective term fights against the text-prompt guidance too hard, producing unrealistic images that match neither concept well.
Unstable weight regimes. For some prompt pairs, the guidance weights themselves can become numerically unstable—very large or oscillating—which amplifies any correction and makes imbalance worse. CO3 stays within safe weight regimes where this does not happen.
Three or more concepts. CO3 is designed for pairs of concepts. With many concepts, defining balance becomes complex, and the corrective term would need to track more attractor zones and their boundaries.
Done right. CO3 works best on prompts where concepts are visually distinct (cat and clock) and the model has seen both in training. It requires no model retraining and works as a plug-in to standard sampling.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate images from multi-concept prompts (at least 10 diverse pairs: animals and objects, colors and shapes, etc.) using standard diffusion sampling, then with CO3. For each image, measure the visual presence of each concept using a simple classifier (a pre-trained image model that can score 'how much does this look like a cat'). Count how many generated images have both concepts clearly visible, how many have one missing or faint, and how severe the imbalance is (largest-concept-strength minus smallest-concept-strength).

concept_coverage = (images_with_all_concepts_visible / total_images) * 100%; concept_imbalance = max(concept_strengths) - min(concept_strengths)

CO3 should increase concept coverage and decrease imbalance, with the effect larger for visually distinct prompts where single-concept attractors are strongest.
