HALO: Unified Vision-Language-Action Model with Embodied Chain-of-Thought

The paper shows that a robot can reason step-by-step by thinking aloud in text, picturing what the world will look like after its next move, and then predicting the action to take—all in one model with specialized experts for each job.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot that can manipulate objects needs three skills at once: understanding what it sees, imagining the future consequences of its move, and deciding what to do next. The paper proposes a single model that does all three by adding an explicit reasoning step: first generate words describing the plan, then visually predict the outcome, then output the action. The key innovation is using specialized experts that work together, each focusing on one skill so they do not interfere with each other.

Why robot reasoning breaks on long tasks and new situations

Today's robot models see an image and directly predict the next action—grab, push, or place. But this fails when the task needs more steps or when the scene looks different from training. A robot cannot think, so it cannot plan or recover from surprises. It just pattern-matches and often picks the wrong move.

A beginner might think: just train on more data. But the core problem is that the model has no mechanism to reason backward from a goal (where do I need the block to end up?) or forward through time (what happens if I move my hand that way?). Without a structured thinking step, the model is blind to the causal chain of the task.

The paper's move: Add a reasoning step where the robot writes out its thoughts, then explicitly predicts what the scene will look like after the next move, then uses both to pick the action. Use separate expert models for thinking, seeing-the-future, and acting so each does its job well without distractions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Reasoning expert processes input

Takes the image and writes a step-by-step reasoning trace explaining what the goal is and what is in the scene.

Visual prediction expert looks ahead

Takes the image, reasoning, and imagines a refined intermediate goal: a picture of the scene after the next action succeeds.

Action expert combines both

Takes current image, reasoning trace, and predicted next image to output the actual robot command.

Experts share context via cross-expert connection

Each expert can read the outputs of the others (a mechanism called attention: scoring which parts of the other expert's output to focus on) so decisions are coordinated and consistent.

Train on synthetic multi-step data

An automated pipeline builds training data by generating reasoning + subgoal + action triples, then fine-tunes all three experts end-to-end.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces an intermediate layer of reasoning and visual prediction. Instead of mapping image directly to action, it inserts two steps: a text reasoning trace and a predicted future image. Both are learned outputs the model must produce, and both guide the final action choice.

the object it moves  =  Reasoning and predicted subgoals
The hidden quantity

The embodied reasoning trace

measured quantity  =  Explicit step-by-step text reasoning

The key hidden quantity is the text reasoning trace—the words the model writes to explain its plan. In a traditional robot model, this reasoning is buried inside hidden layers and invisible. HALO makes it explicit: the model must generate human-readable text describing the task structure, the current scene, and the goal. Making reasoning visible forces the model to organize its thinking into coherent words before it acts, because words cannot compress or omit details the way that hidden numerical weights can. The model must make choices about what matters.

The paper builds this trace by training on synthetic data where reasoning is automatically generated from task descriptions and scene graphs (structured representations of what objects are where). The model learns to predict the trace, then uses it to guide both the visual prediction and the final action.

Why it works

Why explicit reasoning and future prediction work together

The core insight is that reasoning and action planning operate on different modalities (text, visual, spatial) and benefit from different inductive biases. By splitting them into separate expert models, each can focus on what it does best.

The reasoning expert learns to map images to language, which forces abstraction and structure. Language is sequential and symbolic; the model cannot compress meaning into silence and must name what matters and what to do, so it must explain in words why a move makes sense. The prediction expert learns to map images and reasoning to realistic future images, combining visual grounding with language hints. Finally, the action expert takes both signals and outputs precise robot commands. Each expert sees the outputs of the others, so their decisions are coordinated. A reasoning trace like 'stack red on blue then retreat' guides the predictor to imagine the red block moved to the blue position, which in turn guides the action expert to output the correct grasp and motion.

This works because language is a bottleneck that forces lossy, generalizable reasoning. When a robot is trained to say what it is doing, it cannot overfit to pixel details or memorize specific situations. And when it must predict a realistic future image, it learns the physics and constraints implicitly. The action expert benefits from both guides: it sees what the robot decided to do (reasoning) and what physics would result (prediction), so it can correct course if reality diverges.

The combined training objective

First in words:

Total loss is the sum of four pieces: error on predicting the reasoning text trace (how wrong are the words), error on predicting the subgoal image (how wrong is the future picture), error on the final action (how wrong is the robot command), and a bonus for consistency: if two different reasoning traces lead to the same action, penalize them equally (so multiple good explanations are allowed, but the action stays consistent).

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_reasoning(text_pred, text_true) + L_vision(image_pred, image_true) + L_action(action_pred, action_true) + lambda * L_consistency

Every symbol:

L_reasoningcross-entropy loss between predicted text trace and ground-truth trace
text_predtext sequence generated by reasoning expert
text_truegold-standard reasoning text from synthetic data
L_visionimage reconstruction loss (e.g., pixel or feature distance)
image_predpredicted scene image after next action
image_trueground-truth image observed after action was executed
L_actionaction loss (e.g., L2 on coordinate commands or classification on discrete moves)
action_predpredicted action vector from action expert
action_trueground-truth action that was actually taken
lambdaweight balancing consistency penalty
L_consistencypenalty when same input maps to different actions via different reasoning paths
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model predicts reasoning text with 10% word error: L_reasoning = 0.10.
Predicted future image differs from true image by average pixel distance 15 units: L_vision = 15.
Predicted action is off by 0.5 cm in x and 0.3 cm in y: L_action = sqrt(0.5^2 + 0.3^2) = 0.58.
Two different reasoning traces lead to same action 2 times: L_consistency = 2.
With lambda=0.1: L_total = 0.10 + 15 + 0.58 + 0.1*2 = 15.88.
This is higher than any single loss term because all three expert modules contribute; no expert is ignored during training.

The three experts are trained jointly, so error from all three losses flows backward through each expert; each learns to output signals that are useful to the other experts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reasoning text hallucination. The model may generate plausible-sounding reasoning that does not actually match the image or constraints. If the text is wrong but confident, the downstream action expert may be misled. This is mitigated by training the reasoning expert on ground-truth traces from the synthetic pipeline.
Predicted image unrealism. The visual prediction expert might output an image that has correct structure but violates physics or is impossible given the gripper state. If the action expert trusts this false prediction, the action will fail. The paper addresses this by training on real observation data and adding auxiliary physics-consistency losses.
Expert disagreement. The reasoning expert and the action expert might suggest contradictory moves. Without careful training, they could oscillate. The consistency loss penalizes conflicting outputs from the same input, forcing coordination.
Synthetic data mismatch with real world. The reasoning and subgoal data are automatically generated from scene graphs; they may not match real-world visual and physical complexity. The paper mitigates this by fine-tuning on a mix of synthetic and real demonstrations. Real data corrects how common each reasoning pattern and predicted outcome actually is in practice.
Done right. When the reasoning is grounded in the actual image, the prediction is physically plausible, and the action expert sees both, the three experts work in concert. The reasoning serves as a semantic abstraction that the other experts can reason about. Generalization works because a new scene can be reasoned about in familiar language (the text 'stack X on Y' is the same regardless of where Y is), predicted visually via learned physics patterns (the visual expert sees how objects typically move), and acted on via those two guides, rather than memorizing specific pixel configurations.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple simulation with a robotic arm and a few objects on a table. Set up a task sequence: pick the red block, place it on the blue block, then retreat. Run both the baseline (image to action directly) and HALO on the same scene, then on a modified scene with the objects in new positions and slightly different lighting. Measure success (did the blocks stack?) and record the reasoning text the model outputs. Also show the predicted image after each step.

Task success rate = number of completed tasks / total tasks. Generalization = success rate on held-out new configurations / success rate on training configurations. Reasoning quality = human rating of whether the text trace is sensible given the image (1-5 scale).

HALO succeeds on new object positions and lighting changes because the reasoning step forces abstraction: the text 'put red on blue' is the same no matter where blue physically is. The prediction step grounds that abstraction in physics by learning realistic motion and deformation patterns. The baseline has no abstraction layer and must memorize specific pixel patterns, so it breaks when the scene changes even slightly.
