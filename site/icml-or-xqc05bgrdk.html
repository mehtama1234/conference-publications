Overcoming the Incentive Collapse Paradox

When AI does the work, paying people by accuracy fails—so audit them instead to force effort at fixed cost.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

In AI-assisted work, a human and a machine collaborate: the human checks or refines the machine's output, but their effort is hidden. Paying by accuracy seems natural, yet it breaks catastrophically—as the AI gets better, the human needs more and more money to stay motivated, eventually requiring infinite payment. This paper solves that by switching from payment-by-accuracy to payment-by-detection: randomly audit the human's work and pay based on whether they were truly trying.

Zero assumption version

The accuracy trap

Imagine a company uses AI to tag photographs. A person reviews each tag before it ships: does the AI's label make sense? The person's work is effortless when the AI is bad (they catch obvious mistakes easily) but becomes grueling when the AI is already very good (spotting the rare remaining errors takes focus). Yet the company wants to pay fairly: it offers money proportional to correctness—more correct output, more pay.

This creates a trap. When the AI is bad, the human catches most errors and both the work and pay feel reasonable. But as the AI improves, two things happen: (1) the work becomes harder (fewer obvious mistakes to catch means more careful focus needed), and (2) the pay per correct output stays the same or shrinks (because the AI's contribution grows). The person becomes unmotivated and stops trying. To fix this, the company raises the pay per correct tag—but this never ends. As AI accuracy approaches human-level, the human needs infinite payment to stay engaged at all. This is the incentive collapse paradox.

The core problem in one sentence. Paying by accuracy creates a perverse incentive: the better the AI, the less humans try (because catching rare errors is effortful, and the pay per correct output shrinks as AI improves), and the more you must pay to keep them engaged—with no upper limit.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Identify the collapse

Accuracy-based payment breaks as AI improves (humans need infinite pay because the pay-per-mistake shrinks while the effort-to-catch-one rises), so humans need infinite pay to stay motivated.

Introduce audits

Randomly sample and grade the human's real effort, independent of task difficulty or AI accuracy.

Design the payment

Fix the base pay; dock it if audits reveal shirking. The human knows they might be caught.

Set audit rate

Choose how often to sample (5%? 10%?) to balance cost of audits against the effort you need.

Optimize sampling

Spend your audit budget wisely: focus audits on harder tasks where slacking is most tempting.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the payment rule from accuracy-based to auditing-based. Instead of paying proportional to how correct the final output is, it pays a fixed amount and randomly audits the work, penalizing insufficient effort. The human's incentive shifts from a direct link to output quality (which collapses) to a detection-avoidance motive (which stays constant).

The hidden quantity

What the paper measures: effort level

measured quantity  =  Human effort level

The hidden quantity is the human's actual effort—how hard they are trying on each task. It is not directly visible; you observe only the output they produce and what the AI says. Effort can be high (careful review, thoughtful corrections) or low (rubber-stamping the AI, guessing, skipping careful checks).

The paper estimates effort by auditing: when sampled, an auditor carefully grades whether the human actually tried, independent of whether the final answer was correct. An auditor can tell if a tag was reviewed with care or rubber-stamped, even if both happen to be correct.

Why it works

How auditing forces effort at finite cost

When you pay by accuracy alone, the human faces a math problem: as AI accuracy climbs, the marginal benefit of their effort (catching one more rare mistake) shrinks to zero, so they stop trying. Raising the payment does not solve this—it just creates a race to the top with no finish line.

Auditing breaks this trap by rewiring the incentive. When the human knows they are randomly sampled (say, 5% of their work), the cost of being caught shirking (a penalty or job loss) becomes a constant threat, independent of AI quality. Whether the AI is 90% or 99% accurate, the human faces the same detection risk. This makes sustained effort rational because the expected loss from an audit (probability of being caught times the penalty) stays constant regardless of AI accuracy, while shirking remains risky.

The paper formulates this precisely: design an audit rate (how often to sample) and an audit budget (how much auditing costs), then prove that you can enforce strictly positive, controllable human effort at a cost that stays finite even as AI accuracy approaches perfection. Audits measure effort directly, not accuracy indirectly, so they sidestep the collapse entirely—human motivation no longer depends on task difficulty or AI quality.

Math after the picture

The payment formula

First in words:

Total cost is the fixed payment plus the audit cost times audit rate, plus the expected penalty if audit finds low effort.

Then the compact form:

total_cost = base_pay + audit_cost * audit_rate + penalty * prob_caught_low_effort

Every symbol:

base_payFixed hourly or per-task payment
audit_costCost to inspect one sample (auditor time)
audit_rateFraction of work actually sampled (e.g., 0.05 for 5%)
penaltyDocked pay if caught not trying (or termination cost)
prob_caught_low_effortProbability effort is below minimum given current payment
A tiny worked example
Suppose base_pay = $50 per task, audit_cost = $10 to inspect one output, audit_rate = 5% (sample 1 in 20).
Penalty for caught shirking = $200 (job loss risk / docked pay).
If human effort is low (prob_caught = 0.8), then:
Audit cost per task = $10 * 0.05 = $0.50
Expected penalty = $200 * 0.8 = $160 (amortized per undetected task, weighted by risk)
Total per task ≈ $50 + $0.50 + (negligible if well-incentivized) = $50.50.
Key: the base pay is fixed at $50, independent of AI accuracy. The human stays motivated because the audit risk ($160 * 5% = $8 expected loss per task if lazy) outweighs the temptation to slack.

As AI accuracy rises, the accuracy-based payment would spike to infinity, but this formula stays constant—the human's effort is no longer tied to task difficulty.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Audits too cheap to deter. If auditing costs almost nothing, you will audit often, but the overhead adds up. If detected shirking has no real penalty (easy to rehire), humans still slack because the expected loss is negligible.
Audits too expensive to afford. If auditing is very costly (hard to assess true effort), you audit rarely, giving the human many undetected chances to slack. The mechanism breaks if the audit budget runs out.
Effort is hard to define. If it is unclear what counts as genuine effort (some tasks are inherently harder to review), auditors may disagree on whether the human tried, leading to inconsistent penalties and eroding trust.
When tuned right. With a credible audit (cheap enough to sample often, consistent in grading), a fixed penalty (clear to the human), and clear effort thresholds (audit looks for verifiable review steps), the mechanism holds: humans stay motivated and the company pays a predictable, finite amount regardless of AI quality.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should simulate a hiring task (or image-labeling, or document-review) where an AI system completes most of it and a human refines it. Set up a fixed payment for the human, then sweep the audit rate (0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%) and track: (1) the human's observed effort level, (2) the total cost (payment + audits + penalties), and (3) the final task accuracy. Show that as you increase audit rate, human effort rises and levels off at a steady, positive level (because the detection risk discourages shirking), while total cost stays bounded (audits are expensive but limited by the rate you choose). Compare this to an accuracy-based payment scheme (without audits) and show that accuracy-based payment requires unbounded increases to sustain effort as AI accuracy improves (because the pay-per-error collapses as AI gets better).

total_cost = base_pay * num_tasks + audit_cost * (num_tasks * audit_rate) + penalty * (num_caught_shirking)

The demo must make impossible to miss: auditing enforces effort at finite cost (because the audit mechanism measures effort directly rather than inferring it from accuracy), while accuracy-based payment fails catastrophically as AI gets better (because the marginal benefit of human effort shrinks as AI improves).
