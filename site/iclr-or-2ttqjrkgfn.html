Adaptive gradient descent on Riemannian manifolds

A method that tunes learning step sizes automatically when optimizing over curved spaces, without needing expensive searches.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Most optimization algorithms nudge values in a straight line, but sometimes the right space is curved like a rubber sheet. This paper introduces RAdaGD, a family of methods that automatically picks good step sizes on these curved spaces without expensive line searches. It gives the first convergence guarantee for fitting probability patterns without smoothness assumptions, and shows competitive performance in practice.

Zero assumption version

Curved spaces need adaptive step sizes, but existing methods use expensive line searches

In ordinary machine learning, you nudge weights along straight paths. But some problems—like fitting probability distributions using Gaussian variational inference—live on curved spaces. Imagine a rubber sheet: taking a step of size 1 on the sheet does not mean walking distance 1 in the world.

A beginner tries using straight-line adaptive methods (which pick step sizes based on past gradient sizes) on curved spaces anyway. The problem: these methods do not know about the curvature. They often take huge steps that overshoot, or tiny steps that crawl forward. Worse, the standard way to fix this is to run expensive line searches—testing many step sizes until one works—which makes training slow.

In one sentence. RAdaGD automatically picks good step sizes on curved spaces without line searches, and converges reliably even when the space has extreme curvature.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: learning rate adaptation

The paper changes how step sizes are chosen. Instead of a fixed constant or one that only looks at gradient magnitude, RAdaGD picks the step size by combining information about both the gradient size and the local curvature of the space.

the object it moves  =  adaptive step-size rule
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: geodesic smoothness

measured quantity  =  geodesic smoothness constant

Geodesic smoothness is a number that measures how much the curvature can change as you move around the space. On a sphere, curvature is constant everywhere, so this number is small. On a bumpy surface with wildly changing slopes, this number is large.

RAdaGD estimates this number from the data as it learns, without needing to compute it by hand. The paper proves that as long as geodesic smoothness and convexity hold, the algorithm converges predictably—and for Gaussian inference, it converges even without the smoothness assumption under additional technical conditions.

Why it works

Why the method works: curvature-aware step scaling

The core idea: if you know how curved the ground is underneath you, you can pick step sizes that are neither too big nor too small. On a steep curved surface, take tiny steps so you do not overshoot, because a large step would skip past the goal or slide sideways. On a flat surface, take bigger steps because the ground is even and will not surprise you.

RAdaGD does this by adjusting the learning rate based on estimates of the gradient size and the curvature. Technically, it scales each step by dividing by an estimate of the local curvature, similar to how Newton's method works. This automatically gives bigger steps in flat regions and smaller steps in steep curved regions. No need to test many step sizes; the adaptation does it automatically.

Because the method accounts for curvature, it does not require the terrain to be globally smooth in the classical sense. This is why it can handle fitting probability distributions, where the target function has unusual properties that break straight-line adaptive methods.

The core equation: adaptive step on a curved space

First in words:

new_position equals old_position minus learning_rate divided by curvature_estimate times gradient. The learning rate is fixed; the curvature estimate varies based on observed changes in gradients.

Then the compact form:

x_{k+1} = Exp_{x_k}( -alpha / sigma_k * grad f(x_k) )

x_kcurrent position on the curved space

A tiny worked example
Suppose gradient magnitude is 2.0 and curvature estimate is 1.0. Step size = 0.1 / 1.0 * 2.0 = 0.2 units.
Same gradient but curvature estimate rises to 4.0 (steeper). Step size = 0.1 / 4.0 * 2.0 = 0.05 units (smaller).
In steep regions, the method automatically cuts step size by 4x, preventing overshoots.

The curvature estimate keeps the method from taking steps that are too large for the local terrain, while the base learning rate ensures steady progress downhill.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Curvature estimate too noisy. If the gradient varies a lot from step to step (high randomness), the curvature estimate becomes unreliable and step sizes oscillate.
Non-convex or highly curved spaces. The convergence guarantee applies only to geodesically convex spaces. On more complex terrains, the method may get stuck in local minima.
Very high-dimensional spaces. In thousands of dimensions, the curvature estimate itself becomes expensive to maintain, offsetting savings from avoiding line searches.
Done right: tuned base learning rate. Choosing a good base learning rate alpha matters. The paper's theory assumes the rate is small enough; practitioners should validate convergence on a held-out test case.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic problem on a curved space: fitting a Gaussian to data where the optimization happens on the space of covariance matrices (a curved surface). Run RAdaGD, a naive adaptive method (ignoring curvature), and a method using line search. Plot the loss over steps. RAdaGD should match line-search performance without the extra cost, and beat the naive method by converging much faster, because curvature awareness avoids both overshoots and tiny-steps oscillation.
