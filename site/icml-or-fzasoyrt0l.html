Robust Assortment Optimization

When picking items to sell, assume the world might not behave as your data predicted — and find the choice that keeps profit safe even in the worst case.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

A store manager looks at past sales data and decides which products to stock. But tomorrow's customers might behave differently than yesterday's. This paper solves the assortment problem — which items should you offer to maximize revenue — when you cannot trust the patterns in your historical data. It finds the choice that makes the most money even if the real world is as far from your data as the allowed math says it could be, because you set a bound on how much the real behavior can deviate before you give up.

Zero assumption version

Why picking items when the future is uncertain is hard

Every store sees patterns in what customers buy. From this data, a manager learns what price each customer will accept, what colors they prefer, and how much they will pay for an extra feature. Then the manager picks a set of items to offer — an assortment — to make the most money.

But customers tomorrow may not follow yesterday's patterns. Maybe a new competitor opened. Maybe the weather changed. Maybe the data was biased and never showed what a certain group actually wants. A model built from yesterday's data may be dead wrong about tomorrow.

A naive approach: pick the assortment that looks best based on the historical data. But if the real customer behavior is even slightly different, that choice could be terrible — people might refuse everything, or buy only the cheap items, erasing profit.

The trick: Find the assortment that makes the most money even if the real world is as far from the data as it is mathematically allowed to be.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: define worst case

Draw a circle around the historical data. Real behavior sits somewhere inside or near that circle.

Step 2: check each assortment

For a given set of items, compute the worst revenue that could happen if the world sits anywhere in that circle.

Step 3: pick the winner

Choose the assortment whose worst-case revenue is highest — the one that keeps you safe.

Step 4: use duality

A mathematical shortcut (duality) makes this computation fast instead of slow.

Step 5: output the assortment

Tell the manager: offer these items and get this guaranteed minimum revenue.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Normally, assortment optimization assumes the customer behavior you measured is the truth. This paper changes a single thing: it assumes instead that the real behavior sits in a bounded region around what you measured — a region you define using divergence (a math distance between two probability descriptions). The algorithm now searches over assortments by asking not 'what is the best revenue if this data is truth' but 'what is the best revenue that is guaranteed even in the worst corner of that region.'

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: worst-case revenue

measured quantity  =  Worst-case revenue under data shifts

The paper measures a new quantity: the guaranteed minimum revenue. It is the lowest revenue an assortment could earn if the true customer model sits anywhere inside the ambiguity set (the bounded region of allowed deviations from your measured data). This is not a random variable or an average — it is the rock-bottom number you can promise to stakeholders.

The paper estimates this by applying duality theory. Instead of checking every possible customer behavior in the ambiguity set (which is infinite), duality rewrites the problem so you only need to solve a smaller optimization problem — one you can run in polynomial time.

Why it works

Why it works: the power of duality

The paper's mechanism hinges on mathematical duality, which is a clever rewrite of a hard problem into an easier one. In this case, the hard problem is: for a given assortment, search over infinitely many possible customer behaviors inside the ambiguity set, and find the worst one. Duality flips this by swapping the order of the search: instead of searching over behaviors (infinitely many), you search over how far you allow the behavior to diverge from the data (a finite number), and the math guarantees that this reordered search finds the same worst-case answer. This works because both orders produce the same minimum value — they are mathematically dual to each other. The swap transforms an infinite search into a finite optimization that runs in polynomial time.

Once duality is in place, you know the worst-case revenue for any assortment instantly. So you iterate through candidate assortments (or search over them) and pick the one with the highest worst-case revenue. The algorithm is guaranteed to be fast because the duality-rewritten problem has a special structure — convexity — which means any local optimum is also the global best.

Math after the picture

The guarantee: an equation that finds worst-case revenue

First in words:

For an assortment S, worst-case revenue is the minimum over all assortments of the maximum over all customer models P inside the ambiguity set (defined by divergence D and radius epsilon) of the expected revenue from S given P.

Then the compact form:

max_S min_P: D(P, P_hat) <= epsilon E_P[R(S)]

Every symbol:

Sthe assortment (set of items to offer)
Pa customer behavior model (how likely they buy what)
P_hatthe historical data model (your initial estimate)
D(P, P_hat)divergence between two models — how different they are
epsilonthe ambiguity radius — how far we allow them to differ
E_P[R(S)]expected revenue from assortment S under model P
min_P subject to D(...) <= epsilonthe worst customer model in the allowed region
A tiny worked example with three items and one divergence bound
Historical data says customers will pay: item A (80 units), item B (60 units), item C (40 units).
We allow total-variation divergence epsilon = 10 units of shift (roughly 12% tolerance).
Assortment {A, B}: expected revenue from historical data = 80 + 60 = 140. But in worst-case world (10 units moved away), A drops to 70, B drops to 50 -> worst revenue = 70 + 50 = 120.
Assortment {A, C}: expected from data = 80 + 40 = 120. In worst case, A -> 70, C -> 30 -> worst revenue = 70 + 30 = 100.
Assortment {B, C}: expected from data = 60 + 40 = 100. In worst case, B -> 50, C -> 30 -> worst revenue = 50 + 30 = 80.
Best safe choice is {A, B} with worst-case revenue 120, even though {A} alone might be higher if the data never changes.

The equation finds the assortment whose worst-case revenue is highest, using duality to compute that worst-case fast.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong divergence choice. Total-variation (TV) divergence is loose and conservative, chi-square (χ²) is tight, KL divergence is in between. Picking the wrong one makes your guarantee either useless (too loose) or unachievable (too tight). The paper gives algorithms for all three, but the practitioner must choose wisely.
Ambiguity radius too small. If epsilon (the allowed divergence budget) is set too conservatively, the algorithm is nearly safe but leaves money on the table. If epsilon is too loose, the safety guarantee vanishes.
Assortment cardinality constraints ignored. The paper solves the case where you can offer any subset of items. Real stores have limits: display space, stocking cost, or a fixed number of SKUs. Adding these constraints requires reworking the algorithm.
Works correctly when divergence is chosen by domain knowledge. If you know the realistic range of customer behavior drift from pilot tests or competitor benchmarks, you can set epsilon appropriately and the algorithm provably guarantees safety within that range.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small simulation: generate historical customer behavior from one model, fit a choice model (a multinomial logit model — a formula that predicts how likely a customer is to buy each item) to it, set an ambiguity radius epsilon, and run the safe algorithm. Then sample a new customer model from within epsilon of the historical one (a realistic drift). Compare the revenue of the safe assortment chosen to the naive assortment chosen on historical data. The safe choice should maintain revenue; the naive choice should collapse. Repeat with several different drifts to show the safe assortment's worst-case guarantee in action.

worst_case_revenue = min over all drift models in the ambiguity set of revenue from the robust assortment minus revenue from naive assortment. This difference should be positive and stable across drifts.

The demo must show that the safe assortment gives up some easy-case profit for a guarantee that holds across many realistic ways the world could diverge from the data.
