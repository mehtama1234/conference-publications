The Deleuzian Representation Hypothesis

Extract interpretable concepts from neural networks by clustering activation differences, not spending money on autoencoders.

Neural networks contain interpretable concepts somewhere inside their layers, but finding them is hard. Sparse autoencoders, the usual method, are expensive and need hand-labeled examples. This paper clusters the differences between activation patterns (the numbers each neuron produces when the network runs) to pull out concepts without any labels or high cost.

Zero assumption version

Why finding concepts inside networks is stuck

A neural network is a black box. When you ask it to classify an image or translate a sentence, the layers do billions of tiny calculations that nobody can read. Inside those hidden layers, concepts seem to form—edges, colors, words, ideas—but you cannot point to them or explain them.

The old way to find concepts uses sparse autoencoders, a machine that learns to compress and decompress the network's inner numbers. It works, but it is slow and expensive, and it needs you to tell it which concepts matter most. A beginner might try to just look at which neurons fire the most for each concept, but that assumes each neuron represents one pure idea—and it usually does not. Concepts are tangled across many neurons.

In one sentence. Find hidden concepts by clustering the differences between activation patterns, not by fitting expensive autoencoders.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take two inputs

Run two different examples through the network and watch the layer light up differently.

Subtract activations

Compute the difference between the two activation patterns (neuron by neuron).

Cluster the differences

Group similar differences together; each group is a candidate concept.

Score by skewness

Upweight clusters with uneven, lopsided activation patterns, because real concepts fire only a few neurons sharply, so spiky patterns indicate genuine features.

Map back to concepts

Each cluster maps to one interpretable feature the network learned to encode.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of fitting an expensive supervised autoencoder to find concepts, the paper clusters the differences between activation patterns directly. No labels, no fitting, no bottleneck layer.

the object it moves  =  activation differences
The hidden quantity

Activation skewness: how uneven a concept is

measured quantity  =  activation skewness

When a network encodes a clear concept, its activation pattern is usually uneven—some neurons fire a lot, others barely fire. We call this skewness: how lopsided the pattern is. A uniform, flat pattern (all neurons equally active) signals noise or a weak concept; a spiky, skewed pattern signals a real, crisp idea.

The paper measures skewness (how spiky the activations are) for each cluster of differences. It then boosts the importance of high-skewness clusters during assignment, because crisp, uneven patterns signal real learned concepts—they show that a few neurons strongly encode the idea—while flat patterns signal random noise spread thinly across many neurons.

Plain reading. Skewness is how uneven a set of numbers is. Imagine neurons as a crowd: if everyone talks equally, it is flat; if a few people shout and most stay silent, it is spiky and high-skewness.
Paper reading. The paper formalizes this within discriminant analysis: activation differences are points in a space, and the paper finds clusters that maximize between-cluster variance while shrinking within-cluster variance. Skewness weights push toward crisp, high-contrast clusters.
Why it works

Why clustering differences finds real concepts

A concept is something the network learned to distinguish. When two examples differ in that concept—say, one has a cat and one has a dog—the network's inner activations shift in a coordinated way. The activation of concept-detecting neurons changes together. This shift is the difference pattern.

By clustering these differences, the paper groups together shifts that happen the same way. Each cluster represents one stable shift—one concept the network reliably encodes. The clustering finds these concepts automatically without needing labels, because differences naturally separate into patterns that repeat.

Skewness weighting refines this: true concepts create sharp, uneven activation shifts, while noise creates flat, spread-out shifts. By upweighting spiky clusters, the method favors genuine concepts over random noise. This is why the extracted concepts align with human intuition.

Math after the picture

The clustering equation

First in words:

for each pair of examples, subtract the activations and cluster the differences by minimizing within-cluster scatter and maximizing between-cluster scatter; weight each cluster by how spiky (skewed) its activation pattern is.

Then the compact form:

maximize sum_c w_c (mu_c - mu_global)^2 - sum_c sum_i (x_i - mu_c)^2, where w_c = skewness(c)

Every symbol:

ca concept cluster
x_ithe activation difference for example i
mu_cthe center (mean) of cluster c
mu_globalthe center of all differences
w_cthe weight of cluster c, based on how uneven it is
skewness(c)how spiky the activation pattern of cluster c is
A tiny worked example
Imagine two clusters of differences: Cluster A (concept-cat) has activations [12, 1, 0, 0, 2] (spiky, skewed). Cluster B (noise) has [4, 5, 4, 3, 5] (flat).
Cluster A skewness is high (one big spike); Cluster B skewness is low (all similar).
Skewness weights: w_A = 4.0, w_B = 1.0 (A gets 4x more weight).
Within-cluster scatter for A: small (all close to mean 3). Within-cluster scatter for B: small (all close to mean 4.2).
Between-cluster scatter: large (mean 3 vs mean 4.2).
Score for A: (4.0 * large between-scatter) - small within-scatter = high. A is a good concept.
Score for B: (1.0 * large between-scatter) - small within-scatter = low. B is noise; ignored.

The skewness weight boosts clusters with crisp, uneven activation patterns, filtering out flat noise, because when a network encodes a real concept, only a handful of neurons fire strongly for it, creating a spiky pattern, whereas noise activates neurons randomly and evenly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Skewness is too aggressive. If activation patterns vary wildly per layer or per model family, a single skewness threshold might over-favor noise in some contexts or under-favor genuine but flat concepts.
Clustering sensitivity. The quality of concept clusters depends on the distance metric and cluster-count hyperparameters. Bad choices scatter a concept across multiple clusters or merge unrelated concepts.
Activation differences are tangled when examples differ in multiple concepts. When two examples differ in more than one concept at once, their activation difference encodes multiple changes mixed together. Clustering then assigns the difference to one cluster, losing the information about the other concepts.
Done right. Cross-validate cluster assignments on held-out data, measure concept stability across multiple runs, and steer the model to verify that extracted concepts causally affect its behavior.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train or load a pretrained image classifier (ResNet or similar). For each pair of images that differ in a labeled concept (e.g., presence of a cat vs. absence), compute the activation difference at one layer. Cluster the differences with and without skewness weighting. Compare the resulting concepts: do they match human concepts? Do high-skewness clusters align with crisp, interpretable ideas (e.g., cat-shape, sky-blue)? Do low-skewness clusters capture noise?

concept_quality = fraction of extracted clusters that align with human-labeled concepts; concept_diversity = number of distinct concepts extracted.

A good demo makes it impossible to miss: the skewness-weighted clusters map to crisp human concepts because only the spiky, lopsided activations correspond to learned features, while ignoring unweighted, flat noise because flat patterns lack the crisp, coordinated neuron firing that signals a genuine concept.
