The Deleuzian Representation Hypothesis

Instead of teaching a neural network to compress its own activations, look at how activations change across examples and cluster those differences to find interpretable concepts.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

A neural network hides its reasoning inside lists of numbers (these flow through the network as it processes an input). To understand what the network thinks, you need to extract human-readable concepts from these lists. The old way uses sparse autoencoders—a separate neural network that learns to squeeze these lists into a sparse code. This paper proposes something simpler: cluster the differences between these lists across examples, weighted by how lopsided the pattern of change is, to pull out interpretable concepts without training an extra network.

Zero assumption version

Neural networks hide their reasoning

A trained neural network stores its knowledge in thousands of numbers (we call these lists weights and inner states). When the network processes an image or sentence, it updates these inner states—lists of numbers that represent what it currently thinks. But these numbers are meaningless to humans. A beginner might try the old way: train a separate neural network (called a sparse autoencoder) to compress these lists into a small, sparse code. The problem: this extra network is slow to train, requires hand-tuning sparsity penalties, and sometimes captures noise rather than real concepts.

The core challenge: you need a fast, unsupervised method (no manual labeling) that finds genuine, diverse concepts—not just dense approximations.

The insight. Look at how a model's numbers change from example to example, not at the raw numbers themselves—and weight the changes by how lopsided the pattern is.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect numbers across examples

Run many images through the network; record the numbers at one layer.

Compute differences

For each neuron, compute how much its activation changes between example pairs or groups (e.g., cat vs. dog).

Weight by skewness

Measure how imbalanced each neuron's changes are; upweight neurons with rare, extreme jumps.

Cluster weighted differences

Use K-means clustering to group neurons by their weighted difference patterns; each cluster is one concept.

Verify and steer

Test whether each concept cluster actually controls model behavior when you flip it on or off.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the sparse autoencoder (a neural network that learns to compress the lists) with unsupervised clustering of differences in these lists, weighted by how lopsided the pattern of change is.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: skewness of activation differences

measured quantity  =  Distribution imbalance (skewness)

When a neuron responds to a specific image feature, its activation changes a lot in response to that feature—but only sometimes. For most examples, the activation is quiet; for the examples containing that feature, it jumps. This imbalance is called skewness—the difference distribution is lopsided. A neuron that fires equally on everything has zero skewness; a neuron that rarely fires but then fires hard has high skewness.

The paper measures skewness of the differences (how much each neuron changes from one example to the next) and uses it as a weight: neurons with lopsidedness are chosen first during clustering, so rare, sharp patterns stand out. A cluster of high-skewness neurons forms a coherent concept because they all respond decisively to the same specific thing.

Why it works

Why it finds genuine concepts

Raw numbers mix everything the network sees—background, lighting, the target object, all at once. But differences isolate the change. When you compare the numbers on a photo of a face versus a photo of random pixels, the difference lights up only the neurons that care about faces. Lopsided (imbalanced) patterns of these differences are even better: they single out the most decisive neurons, the ones that barely respond most of the time but spike hard on their trigger—because a neuron that spikes at random or constantly has a balanced change pattern, while a neuron that spikes only on one thing has wild imbalance.

Clustering groups these decisive, imbalanced neurons by their response patterns. If neurons always spike together, they form one cluster—one concept. This works without training an extra network, so it is faster and does not introduce extra hyperparameters. The method also preserves lossless steering: because you are not projecting into and out of a learned code, you can flip a concept up or down and see an exact reversal in the model's behavior, proving the concept is real and causal.

Math after the picture

The clustering equation

First in words:

Weighted difference clustering: for each neuron, compute the difference in activation between example groups, scale by the imbalance (skewness) of that difference distribution, then cluster neurons by their scaled-difference patterns using K-means.

Then the compact form:

weighted_diff_i = diff_i * skewness_i; cluster K-means over [weighted_diff_1, weighted_diff_2, ..., weighted_diff_N]

Every symbol:

diff_ithe difference (change) in neuron i's activation across examples
skewness_ihow imbalanced the distribution of diff_i is (rare spikes score high)
weighted_diff_ithe difference scaled by its imbalance; unbalanced neurons matter more
Ntotal number of neurons in the layer
A tiny worked example
Neuron 1 (whisker detector): activates strongly on 10% of cat images, barely on others. Difference = +5 on cats, 0 on non-cats. Skewness = 0.8 (very imbalanced). Weighted diff = 5 × 0.8 = 4.0.
Neuron 2 (color sensor): activates moderately on 50% of images (balanced). Difference = +2 always. Skewness = 0.1 (balanced). Weighted diff = 2 × 0.1 = 0.2.
Same absolute change (+5 vs +2), but neuron 1 is weighted 20× higher because its responses are rare and decisive. Clustering groups neuron 1 with other rare-spike neurons (forming the whisker concept), not with the color neuron.

Skewness is the weight: it makes the clustering algorithm pick neurons that carry rare, strong signals over generic background responses.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong choice of difference groups. If you cluster on differences that do not correspond to real categories (e.g., random noise vs signal), the concepts will not align with human-readable attributes. The method is unsupervised, so it cannot know which differences capture real features.
Skewness is not always the right weight. A neuron can be imbalanced because it detects something rare and real, or because it is a fluke outlier. The method assumes imbalanced responses come from real features; this usually holds in vision and language but may not hold for all domains.
K-means requires choosing the number of concepts. You must pick how many clusters (concepts) to extract. Too few and you miss fine distinctions; too many and you oversplit coherent ideas. The paper shows this is less sensitive than SAE sparsity, but it is still a choice.
Done right: concepts are causal. Because steering is lossless (no projection in/out), you can flip a concept on and off and see exact reversals in model behavior. If the concept stays causal after many runs, it is a real lever on the network.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment: extract concepts from one layer of a vision model (DinoV2 or a small classifier) on a dataset with clear categories (e.g., cat vs dog photos). Use K-means to cluster the weighted differences. For each discovered concept, compute a probe loss: train a simple logistic classifier to predict a known attribute (e.g., has_whiskers, has_ears) from that one concept cluster. Compare probe loss to sparse autoencoders on the same task. Then steer the concept by adding/subtracting its mean vector and show that the model's prediction flips (e.g., the network stops detecting cats when you remove the face concept).

probe_loss = cross-entropy loss of logistic classifier predicting ground-truth attribute from concept activation; steering_reversibility = whether adding +alpha then -alpha recovers the exact original activation

The demo must show that (a) unsupervised clustering beats supervised SAE baselines on concept quality, and (b) steering a concept predictably changes the model's output, proving the concept is a real, causal lever—not a statistical artifact.
