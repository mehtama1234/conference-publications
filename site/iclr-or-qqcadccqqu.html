FRIEDA: Cartographic Reasoning Benchmark

A benchmark that tests whether vision-language models can read maps the way people do: by understanding symbols, measuring distances, and reasoning across multiple maps.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Maps are nothing like charts. They use special symbols (squiggly lines for rivers, colored regions for countries, tiny numbers for elevations), compass arrows for orientation, and numbers for scale to encode meaning. When you read a map, you have to connect all these pieces: Which river is on the north side? How far apart are these two towns? What area did the earthquake shake? This is called cartographic reasoning, and it matters for disaster response and urban planning. But current vision-language models (the kind that look at images and answer questions) have barely been tested on it. FRIEDA is a benchmark—a test set with real maps from geology reports, urban planning documents, and environmental assessments—that exposes this gap.

Zero assumption version

Maps look like charts, but they are not

When you read a chart, you look for bars or dots and read off a value. When you read a map, you have to decode a visual language: colored regions mean different things in different maps, symbols stand for physical features (a river, a road, a building), arrows point north, and numbers at the bottom tell you the scale (how many miles per inch). A chart is a direct picture of a data table. A map is a translation—it squashes the three-dimensional Earth onto a flat page and uses symbols to say what each piece means.

So far, vision-language models have been tested mostly on charts and infographics—simpler tasks where you just read the numbers directly. Some researchers tried testing them on maps, but only simple ones, one at a time, or focused on specific domains like satellite imagery. This left a huge gap: Can a model read real maps the way a person does—understanding the layer of symbolic meaning, comparing multiple maps from the same document, and reasoning across them to answer multi-step questions?

In one sentence. FRIEDA is a benchmark with real maps from actual documents, designed to test whether vision-language models can truly read and reason about maps.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect real maps

Source actual maps from geology, urban planning, environmental documents.

Identify spatial relations

Tag each map for topological (inside, border, touching), metric (distance), and directional (north, south) questions.

Design questions

Write multi-step questions, some single-map, some requiring cross-map reasoning and retrieval.

Evaluate models

Run 11 leading vision-language models in two modes: direct (map given) and contextual (find the map first).

Measure the gap

Compare model accuracy to human performance and categorize errors by reasoning type.

What the paper introduces

FRIEDA is a benchmark dataset: a collection of real maps paired with carefully designed questions that force models to do authentic cartographic reasoning. The object is not a model or an algorithm. It is a set of test cases that expose whether models actually understand maps.

The hidden quantity

The cartographic reasoning gap

measured quantity  =  model-to-human performance drop

This is the chasm between what a trained vision-language model can do and what a human can do on the same map-reading task. Humans are trained by years of looking at maps in school, work, and daily life; they internalize the symbolic language automatically. Models have seen maps only in their training data, and not the full complexity of real-world cartography. The paper measures this gap by running each model on the same questions and comparing the accuracy.

How big is the gap? The strongest models (Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5-Think) achieve about 38% accuracy. Humans achieve 85%. That is a 47-percentage-point gap. This gap is the hidden quantity the benchmark reveals. It exists because the benchmark includes the full texture of real maps: multiple symbolic layers, orientation cues, scale calculations, and cross-map reasoning. Simpler benchmarks hide this gap because they test only one layer at a time.

Why it works

Why the benchmark exposes the gap

A benchmark is only as good as the questions it asks. If you ask simple questions, even weak models will pass and you learn nothing. If you ask questions that require deep understanding, weak models fail and you see where they are stuck.

FRIEDA asks hard questions because it sources real maps—not synthetic ones, not simplified ones. Real maps have layers: a river shown with a blue line, a road with a red line, a town boundary with a dotted line, all labeled with tiny text. A legend explains what each symbol means. A compass arrow points north. A number says 5 miles per inch. A person reading the map integrates all of this. They translate symbols into concepts (What does the blue line represent?), they use spatial reasoning (If this point is north of that line, and this region is to the east of the compass line, then ...), and when the question spans two maps, they retrieve the right maps and fuse the information.

The benchmark tests this by requiring multi-step reasoning—where you have to think through several connected ideas before you can answer. A simple question is "Is city A north of city B?" A hard question is "Using the elevation map and the geological map, which rock formation is in the valley to the north of the town?" That question forces the model to understand symbols, read text, calculate bearings, retrieve both maps, and combine their information.

inputReal map image(s), question text, legends and scales
stepDecode symbols: what do colors, lines, hatching mean?
stepRead text labels and extract numbers (distances, elevations, coordinates)
stepReason spatially: apply topological, metric, or directional logic
stepMulti-map fusion (if needed): retrieve the other map and integrate its information
outputAnswer: a spatial fact (yes/no, a location, a distance, a direction)

How to measure the reasoning gap

First in words:

accuracy_model = (correct_answers) / (total_questions), gap = accuracy_human - accuracy_model

Then the compact form:

gap = A_human - A_model, where A is the fraction of correct answers

Every symbol:

A_humanfraction of questions a human answers correctly
A_modelfraction of questions the vision-language model answers correctly
gapthe cartographic reasoning gap we want to measure and close
A tiny worked example
Suppose a benchmark has 100 map questions.
Humans answer 85 correctly. So A_human = 85/100 = 0.85 = 85%.
The best model answers 38 correctly. So A_model = 38/100 = 0.38 = 38%.
The gap = 0.85 - 0.38 = 0.47 = 47 percentage points.
This means models are 47 points behind on map reasoning—a massive gap compared to other tasks (where gaps are often 5–10 points).

The larger the gap, the more progress is needed. FRIEDA shows a 47-point gap, proving that cartographic reasoning is a frontier for vision-language models.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Symbol confusion The model assigns the wrong meaning to a symbol. For example, confusing a hatched region for "unstable ground" with a hatched region for "urban area" because it only sees the pattern, not the legend.
Scale misreading The model ignores or misinterprets the scale bar. It guesses a distance without checking the scale-to-mile ratio, leading to off-by-factor-of-ten errors.
Orientation blindness The model does not properly use the compass arrow or north indicator. It answers "north" when the feature is actually "south" or confuses relative directions.
Cross-map retrieval failure In the contextual setting (where the model must find the right maps first), it retrieves the wrong map or misses a map it needs, then reasons correctly on incomplete information.
Done right A model that decodes the full symbolic layer, integrates spatial cues, accurately measures using the scale, and for multi-map questions, finds and fuses the relevant maps.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple map-reading system that a person can test interactively. Load one real map, present a question that requires decoding a symbol plus calculating a distance plus using an orientation cue (multi-step), and ask the model to answer. Show the human the model's reasoning (which symbols it identified, what scale it used, what direction it inferred) so the errors are visible. Then repeat with a cross-map question: provide two related maps and a question that requires information from both, and show how the model does at retrieving the right map and fusing the data.

accuracy = number_correct / total_questions, broken_by: (topological, metric, directional) x (single_map, multi_map_direct, multi_map_retrieval)

The demo must make it impossible to miss that the model struggles hardest on multi-step spatial reasoning and cross-map retrieval, and that mistakes are not random—they follow patterns of symbol confusion, scale misreading, or orientation error.
