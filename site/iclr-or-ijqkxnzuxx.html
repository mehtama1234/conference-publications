Phantom-Data: Breaking the Copy-Paste Problem in Video Generation

Stop video generation models from blindly copying backgrounds by training on subjects photographed in different contexts.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Video generation models today often fail at a simple task: when you ask them to put a person into a new scene with a new caption, they copy the person's original background instead of the caption's context. This is called the copy-paste problem. The root cause is how models are trained: they learn from pairs where the reference image and target video come from the same video, so background details get tangled up with identity. Phantom-Data breaks this habit by training on one million identity-consistent pairs where reference and target come from completely different videos, forcing the model to learn what identity really means separate from background.

Zero assumption version

Background Entanglement

Today's video generation models are trained on pairs where a reference image (the subject you want to animate) and the target video come from the same video. A naive approach: show the model 'a person' and ask it to generate 'the person playing basketball'. The model learns from examples where the person's original background is always there, so it gets confused about what is the person versus what is the background.

The copy-paste problem emerges: the model learns to replicate not just the person, but the surroundings, lighting, and scene context from the reference image, even when the text instruction asks for something completely different. The model is stuck because background variation in training data is so limited—the subject only ever appears in a few contexts.

The root cause: Training pairs from the same video lock subject identity and background together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Detect subjects

Vision-language model finds candidate people/objects in millions of videos and images.

Step 2: Retrieve across contexts

For each detected subject (e.g., a specific person), pull images of them from 53 million videos and 3 billion images, in different poses, backgrounds, viewpoints.

Step 3: Verify identity consistency

Use face/object encoders and human checkers to confirm each pair actually shows the same person or object, even under context change.

Step 4: Build cross-pair dataset

Assemble one million validated pairs where reference and target come from different videos.

Step 5: Train video generation model

Model learns on these pairs; forced to recognize identity independent of background, solving copy-paste problem.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes where the reference image comes from: instead of sampling it from the same video as the target, pull it from a completely different video. This one change—swapping in-pair for cross-pair sampling—forces the model to learn identity as something separate from background.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Identity-Background Entanglement

measured quantity  =  entanglement score (how much model conflates identity with background)

Every video generation model has an invisible score: how much does it treat the subject's identity as fused with its background? In in-pair training, this score is high—the model learns to glue them together because they never separate. In cross-pair training, the model forces this score down because the same subject keeps appearing in different contexts.

The paper estimates this quantity indirectly by measuring text alignment (does the output match the caption?) and identity consistency (does the person still look like the reference?). High entanglement means poor text alignment and high background copying. The cross-pair dataset shrinks entanglement by showing the model thousands of examples where identity and background are decoupled.

Why it works

Why It Works: Breaking the Confound

In in-pair training, every subject appears in only a handful of backgrounds. The model has no way to know: is this person the same across contexts, or are these different people? So it takes a shortcut: this visual pattern means this context. When you ask it to put the person in a new context, the model does not know how to untangle them.

Cross-pair training reveals the truth by repetition. The same person appears 10, 50, 100 times in the training data, always in different backgrounds and poses. The model must learn: 'The thing that stays constant is the face/body shape. The thing that changes is the background.' This forces a separation. Once learned, when text says 'person in a boxing ring', the model knows to keep the face stable and regenerate only the background to match the new caption.

The three-stage pipeline amplifies this: (1) subject detection isolates individuals and objects reliably, (2) cross-context retrieval ensures high variation (different videos, poses, lighting), and (3) identity verification gatekeeps the dataset so false pairs do not confuse the model. The result: the model learns a clean factorization—identity versus context—rather than a tangled blob.

Math after the picture

The Training Loss

First in words:

loss = diffusion_loss_on_video + identity_preservation_weight * identity_loss

Then the compact form:

L = L_diffusion(video_pred, video_target) + lambda * L_identity(reference_emb, generated_emb)

Every symbol:

L_diffusionhow wrong the generated video is (pixel-level)
video_predthe generated video
video_targetthe real target video
lambdahow hard to preserve the subject identity (weight)
L_identityhow much the generated subject differs from the reference subject
reference_embnumerical fingerprint of the reference face/object
generated_embnumerical fingerprint of the generated face/object
A tiny worked example
Say reference person is 'face fingerprint [0.8, 0.1, 0.9]' (three numbers representing face shape).
Generated video produces person fingerprint [0.79, 0.12, 0.88]—almost identical.
Difference in fingerprints: squared distance = (0.8-0.79)^2 + (0.1-0.12)^2 + (0.9-0.88)^2 = 0.0001 + 0.0004 + 0.0004 = 0.0009.
If lambda = 100, identity loss contributes 100 * 0.0009 = 0.09 to total loss.
Model learns: keep that fingerprint close or pay 0.09 penalty. Background is free to change.

The cross-pair dataset lets the model learn identity embeddings (numerical fingerprints of faces/objects) reliably because the same person appears under so many contexts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

False identity matches. If the identity verification step fails and the dataset includes pairs of different people who happen to look similar, the model gets confused about what identity means. Prior-guided verification (using pre-trained face encoders) reduces this.
Subjects outside common categories. The dataset has one million pairs, but covers mostly common object categories (people, products, animals). An uncommon subject not well-represented in training may not appear enough times to break the entanglement.
Extreme pose or viewpoint changes. If the reference and target show the same person in wildly different body poses or viewing angles, the model may struggle to recognize identity, even with cross-pair training.
Done right. With a large, diverse, verified cross-pair dataset, the model learns identity as an invariant feature that persists across backgrounds, poses, lighting, and scenes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a test set of 50 identity-subject pairs where each person appears in three reference images (different backgrounds). For each pair, generate five videos with captions describing different scenes (boxing ring, beach, office, concert, forest). Measure: (1) does the generated video show the same person as the reference? (2) does the background match the caption, not the reference image? Compare outputs from a model trained on in-pair data versus Phantom-Data.
