Hierarchical Policy Learning via Spectral Decomposition

A robot policy learns coarse motion first, then adds fine-grained corrections, by decomposing action sequences into low-frequency and high-frequency parts.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot motions contain two layers: a rough plan of where to move, and precise tweaks that refine it. Current learning methods treat every movement equally and miss this structure. This paper proposes splitting action sequences into slow, global movements and fast, local corrections, then learning them separately so the robot gets the big picture right before worrying about precision.

Why robot learning gets precision wrong

When a human teleoperates a robot to stack blocks or press a key, the demonstration contains two completely different types of movement. First, the arm swings to roughly the right spot. Then, the hand makes tiny adjustments to line up perfectly with the target. Current imitation learning methods treat both the big swing and the tiny adjustment the same way: they just try to copy every motion equally.

This fails on precision tasks. The big swing is stable and easy to learn. But the tiny adjustments are sensitive to timing, hand position, and contact—they depend heavily on exactly where the arm ended up after the swing. When a learning algorithm learns both at once without understanding their relationship, it gets confused: it learns corrections that only work if the arm arrived in one specific place, or it learns corrections that fight the original motion. Real-world robot data makes this worse, since network latency and execution delays blur the timing of precise movements into noise.

Naive approach and why it breaks: Learn all actions at once with uniform training. This treats the initial swing and final precision tweak identically, causing the model to either learn corrections that misalign the overall motion or corrections that never fire at the right moment.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard imitation learning takes action data and learns a single policy p(action | observation, language) that predicts everything at once. This paper factors that into two sequential models: one for coarse motion, one for fine correction. The coarse model sees only observation and language. The fine model sees observation, language, and the actual low-frequency motion the coarse model produced.

The hidden quantity

The spectral frequency content

measured quantity  =  spectral energy partition (which components matter at which frequencies)

When you take a motion sequence and decompose it mathematically (using the discrete cosine transform: a way to break any motion into a sum of oscillations at different speeds), most of the energy concentrates in the slowest, lowest-frequency oscillations. For robot manipulation, the paper observes that you can capture the overall task motion (the swing) in roughly the lowest 30 percent of frequency components, and the precise adjustments live in the remaining 70 percent of high-frequency values.

The paper estimates this split empirically: for each task in the demonstrations, compute the spectral components, average how much power each frequency band carries, then find the cutoff where the lower frequencies account for 90 percent of total energy. That cutoff becomes the boundary between coarse and fine.

Why it works

Why hierarchical prediction fixes precision

Imitation learning works by measuring the gap between what the model predicted and what the human actually did, then nudging the model to shrink that gap. When all actions are lumped together, the learning signal for fine corrections gets drowned out by large coarse motions. The correction 'adjust left by 2mm' looks equally wrong whether the arm is 1cm away or 50cm away, because the training loss does not encode which arm position would make that adjustment useful; the model gets equally punished for missing a 2mm tweak regardless of context, so it learns corrections that are too small to matter or corrections that only work in one specific scenario instead of adapting to where the arm actually lands.

By predicting coarse and fine separately, the learning algorithm can optimize each layer independently. The coarse model learns 'reach the general area' and its error signal is strong and clear. Then, given the actual coarse motion the coarse model made, the fine model learns 'given where we actually landed, adjust by this amount to align perfectly.' Now the correction is conditioned on concrete, observed arm state, so the learning signal is precise and the model can learn which micro-adjustments work after which arrivals.

In real robot teleoperation, network delays and timing jitter corrupt the precise movements worst. By explicitly separating the coarse and fine layers, the coarse layer absorbs delays (since big motions change very little if the timing is off by a few milliseconds) and the fine layer learns corrections that react to where the arm actually is, not a noisy transmission of what the operator sent.

Math after the picture

The factorized policy equation

First in words:

The policy factorizes the full action distribution into coarse and fine. Coarse is predicted from observation and language alone. Fine is predicted from observation, language, and the coarse motion that actually happened.

Then the compact form:

p(a | o, l) = p(a_coarse | o, l) * p(a_fine | o, l, a_coarse)

Every symbol:

athe robot action (position, velocity, or force commands)
othe robot observation (camera images, joint angles, tactile feedback)
lthe language instruction describing the task
a_coarsethe low-frequency action components (global motion)
a_finethe high-frequency action components (precision tweaks)
p(...)probability: the model outputs a distribution over possible actions
Tiny numeric example: stacking a block
Suppose the robot must move from position 20cm to target 50cm, then adjust ±2mm for alignment.
Low-frequency model learns: from this observation and 'stack the block', output a trajectory going from 20cm to 48cm (the rough arrival). Its error is 2cm — large but clear.
Now the fine model sees: observation + 'stack the block' + the fact that coarse moved us to 48cm. It learns: output +2cm correction to hit exactly 50cm.
Error in fine model: 0cm, perfect. If both trained together without factorization, the coarse model might learn to arrive at 49.9cm (a compromise), and fine learns to output ±0.1cm (too small, washed out by noise). Factorization lets each layer optimize its own job.

The two models multiply their probabilities, which means they contribute independently; coarse commits to the overall motion shape, and fine refines without interfering with the coarse structure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Coarse model is wrong from the start. If the low-frequency predictor sends the arm to the wrong zone (far from any valid approach), the fine model cannot recover, because fine-grained corrections are only a few millimeters and cannot bridge large gaps—the target is on the table in front but coarse sent the arm upward and backward, so no 2mm correction can fix that error. The factorization only helps if coarse gets the general neighborhood right, since the fine module depends on starting near the target to add only small, context-specific adjustments.
Frequency cutoff is poorly chosen. If you set the boundary between coarse and fine at the wrong place (e.g., put alignment details into coarse), the factorization loses its benefit. The paper uses a data-driven heuristic (90 percent energy threshold), but for unfamiliar tasks this might need tuning.
High-frequency model sees stale observation. The fine model receives the original observation and the coarse trajectory, but not real-time feedback of where the arm actually is after coarse executes. If execution delays are large, the fine model's conditioning information is out of date.
Done right. Train coarse to convergence on the big picture, then train fine conditioned on realistic coarse outputs. Use feedback of actual arm state (if available) when training fine, so corrections learn against real trajectory errors, not hypothetical ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple manipulation task: move a single block from starting position A to goal position B, then align it precisely to a target orientation on the table. Record ten human teleoperation demonstrations (each person fumbles differently). Train three models: (1) a standard baseline that learns all actions together, (2) a naive 'two-stage' model that trains coarse and fine but not causally (both see full observation), and (3) this paper's CSP with causal factorization (fine conditioned on coarse output). Test each on fifteen fresh goal positions. Measure: (a) fraction of trials where the block reaches within 1cm of the goal (coarse success), and (b) fraction where the block aligns to within 5 degrees of the target orientation (fine success).

fine_accuracy = number of trials aligned to within 5 degrees / total trials; coarse_success = number within 1cm / total trials

The key metric is the interaction: coarse success should rise (because coarse model ignores fine noise), and fine success should be higher than the baseline (because fine model is conditioned on where coarse actually landed, not a uniform prediction). A naive two-stage model should show intermediate results—better than baseline on coarse, but worse than CSP on fine, because its fine module does not learn against realistic coarse trajectories.
