How Models Learn From Examples: The Information Removal Inside

When language models learn from a few examples, they succeed by selectively erasing irrelevant information from their internal representations, filtering noise to find the task.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Language models can learn new tasks from just a handful of examples shown to them. This happens inside the model's hidden layers. Without examples, the model generates random guesses because it holds information for every possible task at once, muddled together. With examples, something filters away the noise, because the examples act as a signal showing which information matters and which to throw away. This paper discovered what that something is: specific switches inside the model (called Denoising Heads) that use the examples to identify and delete information that would steer the model toward the wrong task. With the noise erased, the final layer produces the right answer instead of a random guess.

Zero assumption version

The puzzle inside in-context learning

When you show a language model a few examples and ask it to solve a new task, it works. A model sees two positive movie reviews and two negative ones, then classifies a new review correctly. But if you ask the same model with zero examples, it fails completely — accuracy near zero. This is strange because the model already knows what positive and negative reviews look like from its training. What changes when you add examples?

A naive guess: the examples just remind the model of the task it learned long ago. But the paper reveals something deeper. In zero-shot mode, the model's internal layers contain information about all possible tasks mixed together in one tangled representation. The model looks at a review and internally produces a number-list (called a hidden state) that could answer any question: Is this positive or negative? Is it long or short? Does it mention a director? All answers are baked in. With no signal pointing to the right answer, the model picks randomly.

The problem in one sentence. The model holds all information at once, so without examples it cannot know which question to answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper identifies and isolates the specific switches (called Denoising Heads) that delete task-irrelevant information. These switches, found in middle-to-late layers of language models, work like selective erasers, blanking out representations that do not bear on the task at hand. When you remove these switches, few-shot learning collapses.

the object it moves  =  Denoising Heads (specific switches)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: task-relevance in the mix

measured quantity  =  Task-relevant information ratio

Inside the model's hidden layers, a representation (a list of numbers) carries multiple kinds of information at once. Some bits are specific to the task you want solved; others are noise. The paper measures how much of the hidden state is task-relevant versus task-irrelevant. It does this by training a tiny two-layer filter (on top of the model's saved outputs) that learns to pull out just the task information, discarding the rest. The filter's encoder-decoder learns which directions in the representation space point to task-relevant patterns.

Formally, they train a low-rank filter with rank 8 on zero-shot examples, then use it as a measuring stick. They project the hidden state onto this task-focused direction and calculate what fraction of the variance (the spread of values) aligns with task information versus noise. Heads that reduce this noise are marked as Denoising Heads. They measure this ratio across all layers and all examples to find the pattern.

Paper reading. The paper defines task-relevant information via covariance analysis. The hidden states (the internal number-lists at a specific layer and position) are processed by two learned filters called an encoder and a decoder. These filters project the state down to rank 8 (only 8 numbers summarizing task information) and back up. The ratio measures the variance (spread) of the filtered version divided by the spread of the original, telling us what fraction of the state's information aligns with the learned task space.
Why it works

Why it works: the information removal pipeline

When a zero-shot input arrives, the model's early layers encode it into a representation holding information for every possible task. This representation is contaminated with irrelevant noise. In middle-to-late layers, specific switches called Denoising Heads are positioned to clean it. These switches attend to the examples you provided (if any) and compare them against the input. The examples act as a target signal: here is what the right task looks like.

Each Denoising Head is wired to delete information that conflicts with the examples. Mechanically, the head attends to the example tokens (the positive and negative review demonstrations), reads their signal, and constructs a mask or erasure pattern. This pattern travels down the residual stream (the main information highway in a transformer model), where it subtracts out the irrelevant components. The result: a representation that is narrower, more focused, carrying mainly information needed to classify a review as positive or negative.

This filtering happens layer by layer. Early layers do not filter much (they process low-level features like words and syntax). By layer 9 or 10, the Denoising Heads have erased most noise. Later layers receive a cleaner signal and can output the correct class. Without examples, there is no target signal, so the heads do not erase, and the output is random. This is why zero-shot fails catastrophically: the model is answering all questions at once instead of just one.

Math after the picture

The filtering equation

First in words:

task-relevant-ratio equals variance-in-aligned-space divided-by total-variance

Then the compact form:

task_relevance_ratio = Var(H_l,k * W_enc * W_dec) / Var(H_l,k)

Every symbol:

H_l,kthe hidden state at layer l, position k (a list of numbers)
W_encencoder: a matrix that projects the state into task-aligned space
W_decdecoder: a matrix that projects back to original space
Var(X)variance; how spread out the values are
task_relevance_ratiofraction of the representation that aligns with task information (0 to 1)
A tiny worked example
Suppose H_l,k (the hidden state) has variance 100 (very spread out, lots of noise).
After projection through W_enc and W_dec (the task-aligned filter), the variance becomes 20 (more concentrated on task-relevant patterns).
task_relevance_ratio = 20 / 100 = 0.2, meaning 20% of the original state aligns with task information.
When Denoising Heads reduce noise, this ratio stays high (0.8+) in their influence; other heads show ratio 0.2 or below.
Higher ratio in a head's output = that head is a strong Denoising Head.

The encoder-decoder pair learns what true task information looks like; the ratio measures how well a head preserves it while removing the rest.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unseen labels in examples. If the few-shot examples do not include the correct label (the model never sees what correct looks like), the Denoising Heads have no signal to guide erasure. The paper shows accuracy collapses severely in this case, confirming the heads rely on having seen the target label to know what information to keep.
Early layers do not denoise. Denoising Heads only emerge in middle-to-late layers (layers 8+). Early layers process raw linguistic features and do not filter for task relevance. If task information is lost in early layers, later denoising cannot recover it.
Wrong head attribution. Not every switch that looks important by one metric is a true Denoising Head. The paper measures multiple properties (how clustered the representation is, how the information flow changes, and which examples each switch looks at) to confirm. Blindly removing high-variance switches may remove important non-denoising processing.
Done right. The paper validates Denoising Heads through three independent signals: (1) a geometric metric measuring how aligned the representation is with task information, (2) attention patterns showing which heads attend to the label examples, and (3) ablation experiments demonstrating that removing those heads causally degrades performance.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal reproduction: take a small language model (500M–1B parameters), load a sentiment-classification dataset (e.g., SST-2 with positive and negative reviews), and run three experiments. First, run zero-shot inference and measure accuracy (should be near random, ~50%). Second, add four few-shot examples (two positive, two negative) and measure accuracy again (should jump to 70–90%). Third, identify the Denoising Heads using the paper's method (train the rank-8 filter on the hidden states, compute the task-relevance ratio for each head), then ablate all detected Denoising Heads and run few-shot inference again (accuracy should drop back to 40–50%, nearly as bad as zero-shot). The demo proves that removing the specific filtering heads breaks the few-shot learning ability.

accuracy_few_shot_with_heads - accuracy_few_shot_without_heads

The gap between the two few-shot accuracies (with and without Denoising Heads) should be 30+ percentage points, proving these specific heads are the mechanism that makes few-shot learning work.
