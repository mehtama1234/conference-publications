A Spectral-Grassmann Wasserstein metric for operator representations of dynamical systems

To compare two dynamical systems from their trajectories, represent each as a list of its own eigenvalues and directions, then measure distance via optimal transport.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you have trajectory data from two different moving systems—say a pendulum and a spring—you want a fair way to ask how similar they are. Koopman operators let you turn any nonlinear system into a linear one by looking at its eigenvalues (the special frequencies it rings at) and eigenvectors (the directions it oscillates in). This paper builds a metric (a distance measure) between two systems by treating those eigenvalues and directions as data points, then using optimal transport to find the cheapest pairing between them. The metric ignores how fast the data was sampled, works efficiently on real systems, and lets you interpolate smoothly between different dynamical systems.

Zero assumption version

Comparing systems that move over time

You have real-world data from two dynamical systems—maybe sensor readings from two robots or atmospheric measurements from two locations. Each system evolves according to its own rules. A naive approach: just compute the Euclidean distance between the trajectories. But this breaks immediately: if one trajectory is sampled every 0.1 seconds and the other every 0.2 seconds, the sampled points sit in different places even though the underlying system is identical. A second try: extract features like the mean or variance of the trajectory. But that loses the important structural information—the system's natural oscillation modes and how they interact.

The deeper problem is that dynamical systems have internal structure: the frequencies they naturally ring at and the directions they prefer to move. Existing distance measures either ignore this structure or are expensive to compute and break down with sampling variations.

What the paper does in one sentence. Represent each system as a collection of data points where each point is one eigenvalue and its direction, then use optimal transport (an assignment algorithm) to compute a fair, sampling-invariant distance between two systems.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Extract Koopman operator

From trajectory data, compute the linear operator that best fits the data.

2. Find eigenvalues and directions

Decompose the operator to find its natural frequencies and oscillation shapes.

3. Collect them as a list

Create a list of points where each point is one frequency-direction pair.

4. Apply optimal transport

Solve the assignment problem: match points from system A to system B with minimum total cost.

5. Return one distance

The cost is the distance. It is invariant to how fast the original data was sampled.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces naive trajectory-based distances (which break under sampling-rate changes) with a new distance that operates on the Koopman operator—the linear approximation that captures the system's internal frequencies and directions.

the object it moves  =  Koopman operator distance metric
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spectral cost

measured quantity  =  Spectral cost of matching

The hidden quantity is the total cost of pairing up one system's eigenvalues and directions to another's. Each eigenvalue pair has a distance in frequency space, and each direction pair has a distance in shape space (measuring direction distance correctly in high dimensions requires curved space, not flat distance). The paper computes this cost as if solving a shipping problem: how much does it cost to move all of system A's spectral features to match system B's?

The paper estimates this by first extracting the Koopman operator from each trajectory using finite-sample data, then finding the minimum-cost matching via optimal transport (an algorithm that solves assignment problems). The final metric is the square root of this cost—which makes it a true distance that obeys the triangle inequality, because taking the square root of the optimal transport cost (which is always nonnegative and satisfies triangle inequality on its own) preserves these properties.

Why it works

Why it works

The mechanism has three parts. First, Koopman operators capture the natural frequencies and modes of a system regardless of how fast the data is sampled, because the operator is defined on the continuous dynamics, not the discrete samples. Second, by treating eigenvalues and eigenvectors as data points and measuring distance via optimal transport (an algorithm that solves assignment problems), the metric automatically handles the fact that eigenvalues may be in different orders or slightly shifted: the algorithm pairs them optimally rather than using a fixed index-based comparison, which would be brittle. Third, because optimal transport solutions satisfy the triangle inequality and other distance axioms, the final number is both mathematically sound and practically interpretable.

The efficiency comes from a key insight: instead of comparing trajectories point-by-point (which scales with trajectory length), the method compares the spectral signatures, which are fixed-size summaries. And the paper proves that with finite samples, the estimated distance converges to the true distance at a known rate, so you can trust results even on moderately-sized datasets.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

The Spectral-Grassmann Wasserstein distance between two Koopman operators is the Wasserstein distance between their spectral measures, where each spectrum is represented as a point cloud of eigenvalues and their corresponding spectral projectors, with cost computed in the product space of frequencies and Grassmann geometry.

Then the compact form:

d_SGW(A, B) = sqrt(min_pi sum_{i,j} pi_{ij} * (||lambda_i^A - lambda_j^B||^2 + d_Grass(V_i^A, V_j^B)^2))

Every symbol:

A, Bthe two Koopman operators being compared
lambda_i^Athe i-th eigenvalue (frequency) of operator A
V_i^Athe i-th eigenvector (direction) of operator A
pi_{ij}the fraction of mass moved from A's i-th feature to B's j-th feature
||...||^2squared distance in frequency space
d_Grass(...)squared geodesic distance on the Grassmann manifold, measuring how far apart two directions are
min_pichoose the assignment that minimizes total cost
sqrt(...)take square root to make it a proper distance metric
Tiny worked example
Suppose system A has eigenvalues [2.0, 5.0] with directions v_1, v_2.
System B has eigenvalues [2.1, 4.9] with directions u_1, u_2.
Option 1: pair (2.0→2.1, v_1→u_1) and (5.0→4.9, v_2→u_2). Cost: (0.1^2 + 0.05) + (0.1^2 + 0.03) = 0.28.
Option 2: pair (2.0→4.9, v_1→u_2) and (5.0→2.1, v_2→u_1). Cost: (2.9^2 + 0.4) + (2.9^2 + 0.4) = 17.92.
Optimal transport chooses option 1. Final distance = sqrt(0.28) ≈ 0.53.

The equation pairs up frequencies and directions to minimize total movement cost; the Grassmann term measures direction distance using curved space, which is correct for high-dimensional directions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Koopman estimation fails on short trajectories. If the data is too brief, the estimated Koopman operator is unreliable, and so is the distance, because the Koopman decomposition requires enough trajectory samples to estimate the operator's eigenvalues and eigenvectors accurately. The paper provides convergence guarantees, but they require enough data.
High-dimensional dynamics are expensive. If the system lives in a very high-dimensional space, extracting and decomposing the Koopman operator becomes slow, because the computation scales with dimension cubed. The spectral method wins over naive comparison on trajectory length, but loses on state dimension.
Noise in trajectories corrupts the Koopman decomposition. Measurement noise or model mismatch (the system is not perfectly governed by a linear Koopman operator) biases the eigenvalue and eigenvector estimates, because the estimation algorithm assumes the data came from a linear model and noise corrupts that assumption. Applying a smoothing filter to the data helps by reducing noise, but is not automatic and requires careful tuning.
Done right, the metric is stable. When you have sufficient clean data and the system is approximately Koopman-linear, the metric gives consistent, interpretable distances that respect sampling-rate changes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a small Python script that generates two synthetic nonlinear dynamical systems (e.g., two different pendulum configurations or a Lorenz system with slightly different parameters). Sample each system at two different rates (say, 10 Hz and 5 Hz for the first system). Estimate the Koopman operator for all four trajectories, compute the Spectral-Grassmann Wasserstein distance between all pairs, and verify that: (1) the distance between the same system at different sampling rates is near zero, and (2) the distance between truly different systems is noticeably larger. Display the distances in a table to show the invariance.

distance = sqrt(min-cost spectral matching) ; sampling_invariance = |distance(A at 10Hz) - distance(A at 5Hz)| should be < 0.01 for truly identical systems

The demo makes it impossible to miss that the metric ignores sampling rate (because it operates on the Koopman operator, which is defined on continuous time) yet still distinguishes different dynamics (because the spectral structure changes between genuinely different systems).
