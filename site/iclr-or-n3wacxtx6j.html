TAPTRv3: Point Tracking in Long Videos

To track a marked point across a long video, look at its surroundings (spatial context) and remember which past frames were honest (visibility-aware), not just the last frame.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

A camera follows a soccer player across 300 frames. A tiny marker on the player's knee should stay on the knee, but over time the tracker drifts: by frame 200 it is tracking the ball instead. TAPTRv3 solves this by adding two facts the old tracker ignored: what surrounds the point (spatial context to spot fakes), and which past frames actually saw the point clearly (visibility-aware memory to reject ghost signals).

Zero assumption version

The old tracker loses the point in long videos

Tracking a single marked point across a video is harder than it looks. The old TAPTRv2 looked for the point by matching a snapshot of it to every frame. As frames pile up (100, 200, 300), the point moves, rotates, tilts, gets shadowed. The snapshot from frame 1 becomes useless by frame 150.

A naive fix: keep the last match and search nearby in the next frame. This works for 20 frames. But by frame 100, if the point bounces behind an obstacle, the tracker locks onto the wrong thing (a similar-looking texture nearby). Once locked, it never lets go. By frame 200 it is tracking something else entirely. The signal has drifted.

In one sentence. Old trackers ignore context around the point, and they treat all past frames as equally trustworthy even when some frames see the point clearly and others see only shadows.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Extract neighborhoods

Around where the tracker thinks the point is, grab the local surroundings (16x16 pixels) plus the full frame.

Step 2: Score matches with context

Instead of matching just the point, score based on local texture + broad surroundings together (this rejects fake matches).

Step 3: Check visibility from the past

Look back at every frame seen so far. Did the point appear clear, or was it shadowed? Mark frames as honest or unreliable.

Step 4: Weight past matches by visibility

Build memory of where the point was, but trust frames when it was visible (clear signal) more than frames when it was guessing.

Step 5: Predict next frame

Combine the weighted memory (honest past) with local scoring to find the point's new location.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes two things: (1) how the tracker scores a potential match for the point (add local context to catch fakes), and (2) how it remembers past frame matches (weight by whether the point was visible or in shadow).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: visibility

measured quantity  =  visibility (how clear the point appeared)

Visibility is a confidence score for each frame: did the point appear with a strong signal, or was it dark, blurry, or occluded? In frame 50 the point is in bright sunlight (visibility high). In frame 87 it moves behind a player's arm (visibility low). In frame 150 it re-emerges (visibility high again).

TAPTRv3 estimates visibility by looking at how consistent the point was across nearby frames. If the point jumps around erratically, visibility is low (the signal is noisy). If it moves smoothly, visibility is high (the signal is honest). The algorithm then uses these scores as weights: high-visibility frames pull the tracker's memory forward strongly, low-visibility frames are ignored or downweighted.

Why it works

Why it works: context catches fakes, visibility rejects ghosts

A point tracker must answer: where is the point in this frame? The old method said: find the image patch that best matches the snapshot from frame 1. Problem: by frame 150, many patches look like the point (the ball, a logo, a player's tattoo). The snapshot is a weak signal alone.

TAPTRv3 adds spatial context. Instead of matching just a 5x5 pixel patch, it also looks at the 32x32 neighborhood around it. The neighborhood is unique: the sky, the grass, the player's jersey all form a fingerprint. A fake match (the ball) will have different surroundings. The context filter rejects it. This is the first anchor.

But even with context, the tracker can get confused over 300 frames. The second anchor is visibility: TAPTRv3 keeps a history of where the tracker thought the point was in past frames. But it does not treat all history equally. Frames where the point was clear (bright, in focus, moving smoothly) get high weight. Frames where the point was in shadow or motion-blurred get low weight. The logic: if the point was murky in frame 50, any guess about frame 50 is unreliable, so do not let it pull the current prediction away from the bright-frame consensus. This two-anchor design (local context + trusted history) prevents the drift that plagued TAPTRv2.

The core equation

First in words:

For each frame, score a candidate match based on both the local patch and its surroundings (spatial context score), then blend that score with a weighted average of past predictions, where past frames are weighted by their visibility.

Then the compact form:

score_t = alpha * context_score(I_t, neighbor_t) + (1 - alpha) * sum_i visibility_i * pos_i

Every symbol:

score_tconfidence in the current prediction
context_score(I_t, neighbor_t)how well the patch at frame t and its surroundings match the target
alphathe blend weight (how much to trust current context vs. past)
visibility_iconfidence in frame i (high if point was clear, low if shadowed)
pos_itracked position from frame i
sum_i visibility_i * pos_iweighted average of all past positions
A tiny worked example with three past frames
Frame 50 was bright, visibility = 0.9, tracked position was x=100 pixels.
Frame 100 was shadowed, visibility = 0.2, tracked position was x=102 pixels.
Frame 150 was bright, visibility = 0.9, tracked position was x=105 pixels.
Weighted past = 0.9 * 100 + 0.2 * 102 + 0.9 * 105 = 90 + 20.4 + 94.5 = 204.9, divided by total weight (0.9 + 0.2 + 0.9 = 2.0) = 102.45 pixels.
Current frame context score = 0.7 (moderately confident match).
Alpha = 0.5 (split evenly). Final score = 0.5 * 0.7 + 0.5 * 102.45 = 0.35 + 51.225 = 51.575.
The prediction is pulled toward 102 because bright frames (50, 150) outweigh the shadowed frame (100).

The formula balances current local cues (context) against the collective memory (weighted by visibility), anchoring the tracker to honest signals and rejecting ghost detections.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fast appearance changes (object rotates sharply). If the point rotates 180 degrees in a single jump, the neighborhood fingerprint changes completely. Context may not recognize it. The paper mitigates this by looking at broad spatial regions, not just tight patches, but sharp deformations can still fool it.
Entire scenes in shadow or motion blur. If many frames have low visibility (a long shadow, a camera shake), there are few high-confidence anchors to trust. The tracker has to guess, and guesses can drift.
Two near-identical objects close together. If the tracked person is a twin, or a logo is identical to another marking, spatial context alone may not disambiguate. The tracker could jump between them.
Done right. TAPTRv3 is most reliable when the point has a unique neighborhood (like a person's face) and the video has enough bright frames to establish honest history. It still drifts less than TAPTRv2 even in hard cases.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple tracking test: mark a point on a moving object in a 300-frame video. Run both the old tracker (TAPTRv2) and TAPTRv3. Measure the distance between the predicted point and the true point in each frame. Plot the error over time.

drift = error_at_frame_300 - error_at_frame_50 (how much the error grew over 250 frames)

TAPTRv3 should show a small drift (error grows by 5-10 pixels); TAPTRv2 should show large drift (error grows by 50+ pixels). If the lines are close, the experiment needs a longer video or faster point motion, because a short video or slow motion does not give the snapshot enough time to become stale, so even the weak old method does not fail.
