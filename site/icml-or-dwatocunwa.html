Reinforced Sequential Monte Carlo for Amortised Sampling

Train a reusable sampler with reinforcement learning while correcting it with particle-based search so the learned proposal stays broad and accurate on difficult target laws.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Sampling from an unnormalized density means producing draws from a target law when we can score points only up to an unknown constant. Two standard tools address this in different ways. Amortized samplers learn a reusable policy that can draw quickly after training, but they may miss parts of a multi-peak target. Sequential Monte Carlo explores with many weighted particles, but it pays substantial cost at test time because every new task needs a fresh particle run. This paper combines the two. It views the neural sampler through a reinforcement-learning lens, uses Sequential Monte Carlo to generate stronger off-policy training data because particle reweighting rescues branches the policy would miss, and learns proposal and twisting functions together so the reusable sampler keeps improving toward the target law because broader training experience corrects repeated blind spots.

Zero assumption version

Why learned samplers and particle methods each leave money on the table

A learned sequential sampler is attractive because, after training, it can draw candidates quickly with one forward pass per step. The risk is that it may concentrate on one region and fail to cover all important parts of the target law. That failure is especially harmful for multi-peak targets because missing one peak means the learned approximation is wrong in a structural way.

Sequential Monte Carlo offers the opposite tradeoff. It keeps many weighted particles alive, resamples toward promising regions, and often explores hard targets better. But it is expensive because the particle procedure must run again each time we want high-quality samples. The paper asks whether SMC can be used as a teacher during training so that the final learned sampler gains its exploratory strength without carrying full particle cost forever.

The main change. Train the neural sequential sampler with reinforcement learning, but use SMC trajectories and weights as stronger experience because the particle system explores hard targets better than the current learned policy alone.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Write sampling as a sequential decision process

Treat each proposal step as an action whose quality depends on how well the full trajectory matches the target law.

Define proposals and twists

Learn both the forward proposal and an auxiliary reweighting function that sharpens particle importance weights.

Run SMC with the learned proposal

Use the current learned sampler inside SMC so particle exploration reflects the model's current behavior while still correcting it.

Train off-policy from particle experience

Update the sampler and twist function from SMC trajectories because those trajectories visit useful target regions the policy might otherwise miss.

Reuse old high-value trajectories

Keep historical samples in a replay buffer and weight them carefully so rare high-value trajectories still help later training because corrected weights stop old data from overwhelming the current objective.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the role of SMC from a standalone test-time sampler into a training-time corrective teacher for an amortized sampler. It also changes replay from plain storage into weighted reuse tied to annealed importance corrections, which matters because old trajectories are helpful only when their contribution is adjusted so that the update still matches the current objective.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  coverage of high-probability regions under the learned proposal

The key hidden quantity is how much of the target's important mass the learned proposal can actually reach with usable probability. A sampler can look stable during training yet still ignore a secondary peak, which makes its final approximation wrong.

SMC improves this quantity because particles can branch, reweight, and resample into regions the current proposal underexplores. Off-policy training then feeds that broader experience back into the proposal, so future direct samples cover the target better by increasing probability on steps that previously reached the missed region.

Why it works

Why the combination works

A neural sampler can be written as a policy that proposes one hidden variable at a time. In that view, training seeks a policy whose induced trajectory law matches the target law after reweighting. The problem is that policy learning depends on the trajectories it already visits. If those trajectories miss important regions, the policy receives weak or biased guidance.

SMC supplies a stronger behavior policy because it runs many particles with the current proposal, then rescales and resamples them according to how well they agree with the target. That procedure discovers high-value branches the policy alone may not visit often enough. Off-policy reinforcement learning can then update the proposal from those branches, which improves future direct sampling because the proposal learns from trajectories that better reflect the target.

The twist function sharpens this further by changing incremental weights so that the particle system spends more effort on trajectories that are likely to matter later. Replay adds another gain because rare but useful trajectories can still train the sampler after the iteration that first found them, provided their weights are corrected carefully.

Math after the picture

The weighted trajectory objective

First in words:

Update the sampler by maximizing expected return over trajectories drawn from a broader behavior distribution, then correct the mismatch with importance weights.

Then the compact form:

J(theta) = E_(tau ~ b) [w(tau) * R_theta(tau)]

Every symbol:

thetaparameters of the learned proposal
taua full sequential sampling trajectory
bthe behavior distribution produced by SMC and replay data
w(tau)the correction weight that accounts for the difference between the behavior distribution and the learned proposal
R_theta(tau)the training signal assigned to trajectory tau under the current objective
A tiny worked example
Suppose the current sampler directly visits mode A often and mode B rarely.
SMC produces ten useful trajectories in mode B because particle resampling keeps them alive.
Each of those trajectories receives a positive correction weight, so they matter during the policy update.
After the update, the learned proposal assigns more probability to the steps that reach mode B, so future direct samples cover both modes better.

The paper uses a more detailed maximum-reward-with-randomness derivation, but this expression captures the practical point because stronger exploratory data reveals missed regions and correction weights keep the update statistically valid.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Importance weights can become unstable. If correction weights vary too much, a few trajectories dominate the update, so training becomes noisy even when the particle search is doing useful work.
Replay can become stale. Historical trajectories help only when their weights still match the current target tempering schedule and proposal, because otherwise old data can push the sampler in the wrong direction.
Particle cost can remain high. If the target is extremely hard, training may still need many particles, so the method can inherit much of SMC's computational burden even though the final sampler is fast.
Done right. Adaptive tempering, stable joint training of proposal and twist functions, and careful replay weighting matter because they keep the corrective particle signal strong without letting variance dominate the update.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create a two-dimensional target law with several separated peaks. Train one neural sequential sampler directly and train a second with the reinforced SMC procedure. Keep particle count moderate so the difference is easy to inspect.

Measure held-out divergence to the target, the fraction of peaks visited by the sampler, and variance of the training signal across iterations. The demo should show that the reinforced version covers more peaks because particle search finds underexplored regions and replay keeps those discoveries available for later policy updates.

peak_coverage = count(peaks_visited_with_enough_mass) / count(all_true_peaks)

If peak coverage and target fit rise together, the learned sampler is gaining the right kind of exploration rather than only extra noise.
