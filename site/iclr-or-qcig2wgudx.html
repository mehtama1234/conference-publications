Mapping Post-Training Forgetting in Language Models at Scale

The paper measures forgetting sample by sample, so you can see exactly which facts disappear after post-training instead of hiding gains and losses inside one task average.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Large language models are now improved mainly through post-training: domain-continued pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and model merging. Those steps often raise benchmark scores, but the rise can hide two opposite effects. A model may forget some facts it knew before while gaining other abilities elsewhere. If you average over a whole task, the two movements cancel numerically and the actual knowledge change stays invisible. This paper fixes that measurement problem by tracking each sample before and after post-training. The main idea is simple: count when a sample flips from correct to incorrect, and count when it flips from incorrect to correct. That separation matters because forgetting and backward transfer are different events with different causes.

Zero assumption version

Why task averages hide actual knowledge change

Suppose a model answers one history question correctly before post-training and misses it afterward. Suppose it also learns one new coding question it previously missed. Task accuracy can stay unchanged even though one fact was lost and another was gained. From the average alone, you cannot tell whether the model stayed stable or swapped one piece of knowledge for another.

This is a serious problem for studying post-training, because the practical question is not only 'did the score go up?' It is also 'what was forgotten, what was gained, and at which training stage?' Standard benchmark averages answer the first question but erase the other two. They compress all sample transitions into one number.

The measurement fix. Track each sample's correctness before and after post-training, then count losses and gains separately instead of averaging them away.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Evaluate paired model versions on the same samples

The framework keeps the sample identities fixed so each answer can be compared directly before and after post-training.

Record binary transitions

Each sample is tagged as stayed correct, stayed wrong, changed from correct to wrong, or changed from wrong to correct.

Separate forgetting from backward transfer

The two flip directions are counted independently, because they reflect different changes in the model.

Adjust for random guessing on multiple-choice tasks

Chance-correct answers are subtracted in expectation, so accidental hits do not inflate the measured gains or losses.

Compare stages, scales, and model sizes

The transition counts are repeated across post-training regimes, which reveals when a stage mostly preserves knowledge and when it rewrites it.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the unit of analysis. Instead of treating a benchmark as one average score, it treats each sample as its own memory item and measures how its state changes across training.

the object it moves  =  the measurement unit for knowledge change
The hidden quantity

The actual quantity of interest is transition mass

measured quantity  =  sample-wise correctness transitions

The hidden quantity is not final accuracy by itself. It is the mass of samples moving between states. A 1 to 0 transition means a previously correct item was lost. A 0 to 1 transition means a previously wrong item became correct. These transitions are the direct evidence of memory change.

That quantity matters because the same final score can come from very different transition patterns. One model may keep almost everything and add a few new wins. Another may lose many items and gain the same number elsewhere. Average accuracy treats those outcomes as equal, but anyone studying pretrained knowledge should not.

Why it works

Why transition counting reveals what averages miss

The method works because forgetting is directional. A sample that goes from correct to incorrect is not interchangeable with one that goes from incorrect to correct. Task averages throw away the direction of change, but direction is exactly what determines whether post-training preserved or rewrote existing knowledge.

The chance adjustment matters for multiple-choice tasks. If a model guesses among four options, some answers will flip into correctness by luck and some will flip out by luck. The paper subtracts the expected contribution from random guessing, so the measured transition counts are closer to deliberate model change rather than answer noise.

Running the same transition analysis across many post-training regimes then exposes distinct patterns. Continued pretraining tends to produce moderate forgetting with limited compensating gains, because it keeps updating the model on a new data mix for many steps. Supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning-style tuning can produce substantial backward transfer with little forgetting, because they often teach new response behavior without heavily rewriting broad factual structure. The paper also finds mixed effects at larger scales for later post-training on instruction-tuned models, which shows why a sample-wise audit is needed before drawing simple conclusions.

Math after the picture

The forgetting rate

First in words:

Count the fraction of samples that were correct before post-training and wrong after it. That is the direct forgetting rate.

Then the compact form:

forgetting = count(pre = 1 and post = 0) / count(pre = 1)

Every symbol:

prethe sample correctness state before post-training, with 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect
postthe sample correctness state after post-training
count(pre = 1 and post = 0)how many previously correct samples were lost
count(pre = 1)how many samples were correct before post-training
A tiny worked example
Suppose the base model answered 200 samples correctly.
After post-training, 30 of those 200 are now wrong.
The forgetting rate is 30 divided by 200, which is 0.15.
Suppose 50 previously wrong samples became correct.
Then forgetting is 0.15 while backward transfer is measured separately as 50 divided by the number of previously wrong samples.
Two models with the same final accuracy can still have very different values here, because one may lose more old items and gain more new ones.

The key point is separation: losses and gains are not netted out before they are counted.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Binary correctness can miss partial degradation. A model may still answer a sample correctly while becoming much less certain or much more fragile. This framework will not see that change unless the answer actually flips.
Chance adjustment depends on task format. If answer options are uneven or generation is open-ended, the simple multiple-choice correction may not capture the full amount of random answer noise.
Sample identity must stay comparable. If evaluation prompts or scoring rules change between checkpoints, a measured transition may reflect a benchmark change rather than memory change in the model.
Done right. The audit is most useful when the same samples are measured across a clean training timeline, because then each transition can be attributed to a concrete post-training stage.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take two checkpoints of the same language model and evaluate them on a small benchmark with stable sample IDs. Build a table with one row per sample and columns for pre and post correctness. Report forgetting, backward transfer, and net accuracy change. Then create a second checkpoint pair with the same final accuracy but different transition counts. The demo should show that the transition view distinguishes the two cases immediately while the average score does not.

hidden_swap = count(pre = 1 and post = 0) + count(pre = 0 and post = 1)

The demo must show that a constant average score can still hide substantial memory movement, because losses and gains may cancel numerically while remaining very different scientifically.
