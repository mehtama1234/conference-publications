HDFlow: Hierarchical Diffusion-Flow Planning for Long-horizon Tasks

A robot plans long assembly tasks by splitting work into two layers: a slow diffusion model sketches out strategic waypoints, then a fast flow model fills in the smooth connecting movements.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Long-horizon robotics tasks (like furniture assembly) require planning hundreds of steps ahead, but generative models that plan step-by-step are slow and struggle with sparse rewards. HDFlow splits planning into two levels: a high-level diffusion planner that generates strategic intermediate goals in a learned abstract space, and a low-level rectified-flow planner that generates smooth dense trajectories between them. The hybrid approach exploits the strengths of each model—diffusion's exploratory power and flow's speed—to plan real furniture assembly tasks efficiently.

Zero assumption version

Why planning long robot tasks is stuck

A robot doing furniture assembly has a thousand-step journey: reach the wood, grasp the bolt, guide the nut, thread it, tighten. Step-by-step planning works (train the robot on what comes next), but it is slow—each step requires computing what the world looks like after that move, then deciding the next one. Scaling to a thousand steps multiplies the slowness.

Generative models promise a shortcut: ask the model to imagine the full sequence all at once. But the common generative approach (diffusion) iteratively denoises a random cloud into a coherent plan. That iteration is expensive. And in sparse reward settings (the robot only knows the goal succeeded or failed, not how close each step is), the model flails.

A beginner tries to train a single diffusion model to do it all—sketch the goal, fill in the middle, execute smoothly. It fails because a single model cannot specialize: what makes good waypoints (spreading exploration widely) is the opposite of what makes smooth trajectories (staying close to a known path).

In one sentence. Split long planning into two stages: a slow model to pick strategic intermediate checkpoints, a fast model to glue them together smoothly.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Train a world model

Encode observations (camera images) into an abstract space (a list of numbers) using contrastive learning, learning what progress looks like.

Step 2: Train high-level diffusion planner

A diffusion model learns to generate sequences of waypoints in that abstract space that lead to success, guided by an energy function that rewards good plans.

Step 3: Train low-level flow planner

A rectified-flow model learns to fill in smooth, dense trajectories between each pair of consecutive waypoints.

Step 4: Retrieve and project successful paths

At planning time, find nearby successful past waypoint sequences, project them onto a learned subspace, and use that to guide the high-level diffusion model.

Step 5: Execute: diffusion then flow

Generate waypoints with the guided diffusion planner, then generate smooth motion segments between them with the flow planner, and send commands to the robot.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of one model planning the whole sequence, HDFlow uses two. The high-level planner—a diffusion model—picks strategic intermediate goals (in a learned abstract space). The low-level planner—a rectified-flow model—generates smooth motion between each pair of goals. By splitting the job, each model specializes and the overall pipeline runs faster.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Waypoint quality

measured quantity  =  Energy of a plan

Every potential plan (sequence of waypoints) has an energy score: low energy for paths that succeed, high energy for paths that fail. The paper trains an energy-based model to learn this hidden signal from data. When training data is sparse (most attempts fail), the energy function turns sparse success/failure into a dense learning signal that guides the diffusion model to generate better waypoints, because the energy scores let the planner learn from even failed attempts—what made them fail—not just from successful ones.

At execution time, the energy function also acts as a filter: successful waypoint sequences from past trajectories are retrieved, and their low-energy structure is used to steer the diffusion model away from implausible paths. By projecting these successful examples into a learned subspace (a common geometric direction), the diffusion model is nudged to stay within the region of feasible solutions, because staying near past successes reduces the chance of generating physically impossible plans.

Why it works

Why splitting into two stages works

Diffusion and flow do different things well. Diffusion iteratively cleans up random noise, which is slow but flexible—it can explore many possible futures and pick the one that looks successful. Rectified flow (an ODE-based trajectory model) is faster because it directly traces a path from one point to another without iteration, but it stays close to the training distribution—it does not explore. HDFlow assigns each task to its strength: let the slow, exploratory diffusion model pick abstract waypoints, then let the fast, focused flow model smooth the motion between them.

The mechanism works through three layers. First, observations are encoded into an abstract space (a list of numbers) using a world model (a recurrent neural network trained to reconstruct observations and predict the next state). Second, the high-level diffusion planner operates in that abstract space, generating waypoint sequences. The energy-based model learned during training scores these sequences, and retrieval of past successful paths (found by nearest-neighbor search) further constrains diffusion to the successful region of the space. Third, the low-level flow planner conditions on pairs of consecutive waypoints and generates the smooth, dense trajectory connecting them. Because each planner specializes, neither is forced to do work it is poorly suited for.

Math after the picture

The flow-matching equation

First in words:

The low-level planner trains to predict the direction from one latent waypoint to the next. It learns a vector field (a function that assigns a direction to each point in space) by minimizing the squared difference between what it predicts and the true direction.

Then the compact form:

L_flow = E[ || v_θ( (1-u)τ_0 + u*τ_1, u, context ) - (τ_1 - τ_0) ||^2 ]

Every symbol:

v_θA neural network that predicts the direction to follow
(1-u)τ_0 + u*τ_1A point partway between two waypoints (u ranges from 0 to 1)
τ_0The current waypoint
τ_1The next waypoint
uHow far along the path (0 = at start, 1 = at end)
contextThe latent representation of the task context
(τ_1 - τ_0)The true direction from current to next waypoint
A tiny worked example
Suppose waypoint A is at latent position (10, 5) and waypoint B is at (12, 8).
The true direction from A to B is (12-10, 8-5) = (2, 3).
At u=0.5 (halfway), the model predicts direction (2.1, 2.9).
Error: ||(2.1, 2.9) - (2, 3)||^2 = 0.1^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.02. Small error, model is learning correctly.
At u=0.2, the model predicts (1.5, 1.0), error = 0.5^2 + 2^2 = 4.25. Large error, training nudges the model to improve.

The flow planner learns to trace straight-line-like paths in the latent space from waypoint to waypoint, avoiding iteration while staying smooth.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Waypoints too close or too far apart If the high-level planner generates waypoints spaced inconsistently (some tiny gaps, some huge jumps), the low-level flow planner will struggle to interpolate smoothly. The method assumes the diffusion model learns a reasonable waypoint spacing; if not, dense trajectories will be jerky or implausible.
Energy function misleads planning The energy-based model is trained on past data. If successful trajectories in training share a quirk that does not generalize (e.g., always grasping from the left), the energy function will mislead the diffusion planner to repeat that quirk on new tasks where it fails.
Abstract space does not capture structure The world model encodes observations into a learned abstract space. If the encoding is poor (e.g., nearby points in that space do not correspond to nearby physical states), then both the diffusion planner (which works in that space) and the retrieval of successful paths (which uses nearest-neighbor search) will fail.
Done right A well-trained hierarchy with a learned abstract space that captures task structure, a good energy function trained on many trajectories, and waypoints spaced so the flow model can interpolate smoothly, enables fast planning on long assembly tasks because each planner can focus on what it does best without conflicting constraints.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple furniture assembly simulator (e.g., a 2D bin with objects to stack in order). Train HDFlow on a small dataset of successful and failed attempts. At test time, run the two-stage planner and measure success rate, execution time, and the distance between generated waypoints and the ground-truth subgoals from successful trajectories.

Success rate = (tasks_completed / tasks_attempted) × 100%; Execution time = wall-clock seconds from planning to completion; Waypoint alignment = average distance between predicted waypoints and observed waypoints in successful reference trajectories.

A successful demo shows that the two-stage hierarchy completes harder assembly tasks faster than a single-model baseline because splitting the specialization into two models removes the bottleneck of a single model doing everything at once, and that the high-level planner discovers waypoints close to those in real successful demonstrations because retrieval and energy guidance pull it toward the learned pattern of success.
