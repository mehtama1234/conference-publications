LiveMoments: Restore Reselected Live Photos with Motion Alignment

Restore lower-quality video frames to the photo's level by matching motion and copying structure from the original high-quality photo.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

A Live Photo captures a high-quality photo plus a short video clip around the moment. Users often want to swap in a different frame from the clip as the key photo because it has better expressions or timing. But video frames are lower quality than photos—the camera applies less cleanup when recording video. This paper proposes LiveMoments, which takes the reselected low-quality frame and a two-channel restoration pipeline that copies structure and texture from the original photo while tracking how pixels moved between the two frames.

Why reselected frames are blurry or corrupted

A Live Photo stores two things: a perfectly sharp photo (because the camera's image processing pipeline applies heavy cleanup and denoising), and a one-second video clip (where video processing trades quality for speed, so it skips some cleanup steps). When users pick a different frame from the video clip as the key photo, they get a frame that is grainier, blurrier, or has color shifts compared to the original photo.

A beginner might try a simple approach: take any existing image-repair tool and run it on the low-quality frame. But those tools do not know that a high-quality reference photo is available right there. They treat the blurry frame as if it were a random damaged photo, without any guidance. The result is worse—the tool may invent details that do not match the original photo, or smooth away textures that should be preserved.

In one sentence. LiveMoments guides restoration by matching motion between frames and copying fine details from the high-quality original photo.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Compress both images

Convert the reselected frame and original photo into compact compressed forms that preserve all the important information.

2. Estimate optical flow

Track motion: for each pixel in the reselected frame, compute where it came from in the original photo.

3. Motion-guided fusion

Inject motion as a bias into the fusion step so the two networks focus on matching regions.

4. Patch correspondence retrieval

During restoration, locate which patches from the original photo should guide each region of the reselected frame.

5. Decode back to high quality

Convert the fused, restored representation back into a clean, sharp photo.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

LiveMoments changes how information flows between the high-quality reference photo and the low-quality reselected frame during restoration. Instead of treating them as separate signals, it creates two parallel neural networks: one that analyzes the reference photo to extract structure and texture, and one that restores the reselected frame. The key innovation is injecting motion information (computed from optical flow, a technique that tracks how pixels move frame-to-frame) into the fusion step that combines the two branches, and adding a patch retrieval stage that looks up the best matching regions from the reference photo.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: alignment coherence

measured quantity  =  Alignment coherence between reselected and reference regions

Alignment coherence is a measurement of how well the patches (small tiles of pixels) from the reselected low-quality frame line up with the same content in the original high-quality photo. If the two frames were shot at slightly different moments, pixels moved—the camera panned or the subject blinked. LiveMoments must know exactly where that motion happened, so it does not accidentally copy texture from the wrong part of the photo.

The paper estimates this by computing optical flow (the direction and speed each pixel moved) and using it as a signal in the fusion step. During the cross-connection operation that fuses the reference branch and restoration branch, motion is encoded as an additive bias—a small adjustment that tells the network 'look at this location in the reference photo, not that one.' The higher the coherence, the fewer artifacts appear in the output.

Why it works

Why it works: the two-branch dance

The core insight is that image restoration is easier when you have both a degraded version and a clean reference. Instead of asking one network to hallucinate the missing sharpness from scratch, LiveMoments splits the job: the Reference branch (processing the high-quality original photo) learns what details and textures the camera should have captured; the Restoration branch (processing the low-quality reselected frame) learns how to match those details without drifting away from the low-quality version's true content.

The two branches use attention—a mechanism that lets one part of the network decide what to look at—to communicate. But attention alone would confuse them if the frames do not align: if the camera tilted between shots, pixels in the reselected frame might point to the wrong parts of the reference photo. That is where motion comes in. By computing optical flow (the direction each pixel moved), LiveMoments tells the attention mechanism exactly where to look. The motion signal is encoded by a lightweight neural network and added as a bias to the attention calculation, so every fusion step is spatially aware. This ensures structure, texture, and color come from the right place in the reference photo, not from misaligned regions that would create ghosts or blur.

Math after the picture

The restoration equation

First in words:

Cross-attention output = softmax of (query scaled by reference key and motion bias) applied to (reference value combined with restoration value)

Then the compact form:

CrossAttn = Softmax( Q [ K, K_ref + M_bias ] / sqrt(d) ) [ V, V_ref ]

Every symbol:

QQuery: feature from the restoration branch at this step
KKey: feature from the restoration branch (what is already known)
K_refKey from reference branch (structure from high-quality photo)
M_biasMotion bias: alignment guidance encoded from optical flow
dDimension: how many numbers are in each feature
VValue: restoration branch features to update
V_refValue: reference branch details to copy over
A tiny worked example
Suppose Q = [0.5, 0.3, 0.8], K = [0.2, 0.4, 0.1], K_ref = [0.9, 0.7, 0.6], and M_bias = [0.1, 0.1, 0.1] (motion steering).
Combined key = [0.2 + 0.9, 0.4 + 0.7, 0.1 + 0.6] + [0.1, 0.1, 0.1] = [1.2, 1.2, 0.8].
Scaled dot product: [0.5, 0.3, 0.8] · [1.2, 1.2, 0.8] / sqrt(3) = 1.36 / 1.73 ≈ 0.79.
Softmax(0.79) ≈ 0.69 (normalized attention weight, now the network looks at reference more than restoration alone).
Output blends V and V_ref weighted by 0.69, pulling in high-quality details while staying anchored to the reselected frame's true content.

Motion bias shifts the attention weights toward spatially corresponding regions in the reference photo, so high-quality details come from the right place.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Large motion or occlusion. If the subject moved a lot between the original photo and reselected frame (fast hand gesture, blink, camera pan), optical flow may fail to find correct correspondences. Then the motion bias steers attention to the wrong region and texture copies incorrectly, creating ghosting or blur.
Insufficient reference detail. If the original high-quality photo was underexposed or had motion blur, there is no clean texture to copy. The restoration branch then amplifies the reference branch's noise, making the output worse than input.
Extreme resolution mismatch. Patch correspondence retrieval assumes the two frames have similar resolution and overlap. If the crop or zoom changes a lot, the patch lookup fails and alignment coherence drops, leading to blurry or incoherent output.
Done right. When motion is small, reference photo is clean, and both frames are similar in crop and zoom, LiveMoments copies texture and structure without any visible errors, producing output nearly indistinguishable from the original photo's quality level.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo takes one Live Photo (original sharp photo + one grainy video frame), runs LiveMoments, and displays the restored frame side by side with the input and the reference. It should measure two metrics: PSNR (a numerical score of pixel-level closeness to the reference photo; higher is sharper) and a perceptual quality score that reflects whether the restored frame looks natural and artifact-free. The demo should include at least one case with noticeable motion between the original and reselected frame to show that motion-guided alignment prevents ghosting. A second test case with minimal motion should show near-perfect texture copying. Code should be under 200 lines: load frame pair, compress both with VAE, run reference and restoration branches with motion bias, decompress, and compute metrics.

PSNR_improvement = PSNR(restored, reference) - PSNR(input, reference)

A successful demo makes impossible to ignore: the restored frame's sharpness approaches the original photo, and even in motion-heavy scenes, no ghosting or misalignment artifacts appear.
