DRBench: Enterprise Deep Research Benchmark

A benchmark for testing AI agents on realistic company research tasks that require combining public web facts with private company documents.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When companies need to answer complex questions (like how to change a product based on new rules), they must dig through both public information and internal files. This paper introduces DRBench: 100 real research tasks that test whether AI agents can solve these multi-step puzzles by finding relevant facts from both places, checking accuracy, and writing a useful report.

Zero assumption version

Testing AI agents on real company research is impossible today

Right now, AI agents are tested on toy questions: 'What is the capital of France?' or 'Find a URL about climate.' These are too simple. Real company work is harder. A VP of sales might ask: 'What do we need to change in our pricing to meet this new regulation, and which customers will be affected?' That requires combining facts from three places at once: public web (the new regulation text), email (what customers said earlier), and company databases (current pricing). No existing test measures whether an agent can do that.

When researchers try to build these systems, they have no agreed way to measure if they work. Some agents fail silently (they skip searching the company files entirely). Others pull facts from the right places but mash them together wrong. Without a real benchmark with realistic tasks and a scoring rule, it is impossible to know which agent approach actually works and which is just luck.

The problem in one sentence. We have no standard benchmark for testing AI agents on realistic multi-source company research tasks.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a real question

Each task is written by humans to match a real company job (sales manager, security officer, compliance officer).

Agent searches all sources

The agent must query the public web, internal files, emails, and chat to find supporting facts.

Collect the facts

The agent writes down which facts matter and where they came from.

Build a report

The agent writes a coherent answer that connects the facts in a way that solves the original question.

Score against the ground truth

Humans grade how many right facts the agent found, whether they are accurate, and whether the report makes sense.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

DRBench introduces a new kind of test dataset: 100 tasks where each task has facts scattered across multiple sources (web, files, emails, chat), and the ground truth answer is written by humans who know the company and the real regulation or situation. This is the first benchmark where you can only solve the task if your agent actually searches inside a company's private data, not just the public web.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Multi-source recall

measured quantity  =  multi-source recall

Multi-source recall is the fraction of relevant facts the agent finds across all sources combined. If a task needs 10 facts (3 from the web, 4 from company files, 3 from email) and the agent finds 8 of them, the recall is 80%. The reason this is hidden: agents often fake it by returning lots of text. DRBench measures what fraction of the actually-useful facts made it into the agent's search results.

The paper estimates multi-source recall by having humans mark each ground-truth fact, then counting how many the agent retrieved. It also measures factual accuracy (did the agent get the facts wrong?) and report coherence (is the final answer clear and well-structured?). Together, these three hidden quantities reveal whether an agent is just talking a lot or actually solving the research task.

Why it works

Why a multi-source benchmark matters

The mechanism is simple: if you want to measure whether agents can do real company research, you have to give them real company research problems. When you only test on public web questions, agents learn they can ignore internal data. With DRBench, the question literally cannot be answered from the web alone — the agent must search company files, emails, and chat. This forces the agent to actually learn how to navigate and extract from multiple document types.

The second part of the mechanism: human-graded ground truth. When you measure only whether the agent retrieves some document, you miss the hard part: did it find the actually-useful facts? By having humans label which facts matter and checking whether the agent found them, you reveal whether the agent is doing real reasoning or just string-matching keywords. The three-part scoring (recall, accuracy, coherence) creates pressure on every dimension: an agent that retrieves a lot but mixes in garbage will score low on accuracy; one that is accurate but only finds half the facts will score low on recall.

Scoring formula

First in words:

The overall score is a weighted sum: (number of relevant facts found divided by total relevant facts) times 0.5, plus (fraction of found facts that are correct) times 0.3, plus (clarity rating from 0 to 1) times 0.2.

Then the compact form:

score = 0.5 * (facts_found / facts_total) + 0.3 * (correct_facts / facts_found) + 0.2 * clarity_score

Every symbol:

facts_foundnumber of relevant facts the agent successfully retrieved
facts_totaltotal number of relevant facts in the ground truth answer
correct_factsnumber of retrieved facts that are accurate (not hallucinated or wrong)
clarity_scorehuman rating of report structure (0 = incoherent, 1 = clear and well-organized)
0.5, 0.3, 0.2weights showing that recall matters most (0.5), then accuracy (0.3), then clarity (0.2)
A tiny worked example
Task: 'How must we change our pricing given the new tax rule, and who is affected?'
Ground truth: 10 relevant facts (4 about the tax rule from web, 3 about current pricing from company files, 2 about customer impact from email, 1 from chat).
Agent retrieves 7 facts: 3 tax-rule facts (correct), 2 pricing facts (1 correct, 1 hallucinated), 2 customer facts (both correct).
Scores: recall = 7 / 10 = 0.7. Correct = 6 / 7 = 0.86. Clarity = 0.8 (report is mostly clear).
Final score = 0.5 * 0.7 + 0.3 * 0.86 + 0.2 * 0.8 = 0.35 + 0.26 + 0.16 = 0.77.

The formula prioritizes finding facts (recall 0.5) over correctness (0.3) and clarity (0.2), because in enterprise research a missed fact is worse than a typo.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Agent hallucinates facts. The agent retrieves documents but invents details that are not actually there. DRBench catches this via the accuracy score (fraction of retrieved facts that are correct). Low accuracy even with high recall signals hallucination.
Agent ignores one source. Some agents learn to ignore email or chat because they are harder to parse. DRBench forces multi-source recall by making every task require facts from at least two sources. A web-only agent will score poorly.
Agent retrieves but does not synthesize. The agent finds all the facts but writes an incoherent report that does not answer the original question. DRBench measures this via clarity and also by human evaluation of whether the report solves the problem.
Done right. The agent learns to search all sources, extract facts carefully, check them for accuracy, and write a report that actually addresses the original question. On DRBench, it scores high on all three dimensions: recall, accuracy, and clarity.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a system that takes one of the 100 DRBench tasks and runs an agent through it. The agent should search a mock company database (5-10 documents), a mock email archive (3-5 emails), and the live web. The ground truth answer should be pre-scored by a human. After the agent finishes, show the recall score (how many facts found), accuracy score (how many are correct), and a side-by-side of the agent's report and the ground truth to let a human see whether the answer makes sense.

overall_score = 0.5 * (facts_found / 10) + 0.3 * (facts_correct / facts_found) + 0.2 * clarity_grade

The demo must make it impossible to miss: if your agent does not search the company files, it will score very low on the recall dimension, even if it aces the web search.
