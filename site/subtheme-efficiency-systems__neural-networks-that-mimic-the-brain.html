Neural networks that mimic the brain

These papers use spiking neural networks inspired by biology—neurons that fire only when needed—to save power and memory compared to traditional neural networks.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

The human brain is incredibly efficient: it uses about 20 watts of power while performing tasks that would need megawatts of electricity in a computer. Neurons are the key: they do not fire all the time. A neuron stays quiet unless it gets strong enough input to cross its threshold, at which point it spikes. This is sparse — not every neuron fires every time step. Spiking neural networks try to mimic this behavior. Instead of continuous values flowing through the network, neurons accumulate charge over time and fire only when the voltage crosses a threshold. The spike — a discrete event — then propagates.

Why is this more efficient? Because computation only happens when a neuron fires. If most neurons stay quiet, you do way less math. Additionally, spikes are discrete — you just need to send which neurons fired, not numbers — so communication between chips or parts of the brain can be compressed. The tradeoff is that spiking networks are harder to train and predict. Traditional neural networks use calculus-friendly continuous math. Spikes are discrete events that do not play well with standard learning algorithms. Researchers have developed new training methods for spiking networks, but they are still not as easy to optimize as regular networks.

These papers explore different neuron models (different threshold and charging rules), training algorithms, and applications. They measure power consumption, latency, and accuracy. Spiking networks are particularly interesting for edge devices — phones, embedded cameras, robots — where power is scarce and you want to run inference locally without draining the battery. The brain inspiration is not perfect — biological neurons are far more complex — but even a simplified spike-based model can get significant efficiency gains.

How the work breaks down

The work on brain-inspired spiking networks breaks into several natural directions: how to train them better and deeper so they work on hard tasks, how to adapt attention-based networks (which compute similarity between all pairs of inputs—expensive at scale) to work with spikes, how to run them on language tasks, and how to deploy them efficiently on small edge devices. Each direction tackles a core constraint because spikes are discrete and discrete math is hard to optimize, attention-based networks need huge compute overhead so you need to cut the math, language models burn massive power, and edge devices have tiny memory and batteries.

Training Deep SNNs Stably

The hardest part of building spiking networks is training them. Traditional neural networks use smooth math that calculus loves. Spikes are all-or-nothing events—a neuron fires or it does not. This discrete behavior breaks standard gradient descent. One core problem is memory: when you train a deep spiking network, you need to store the state of every neuron at every time step, which explodes memory for networks with 1000 layers. A second problem is instability: training deep SNNs without special tricks causes the signal to explode or vanish as it moves through layers.

The gradient-checkpointing paper tackles memory head-on by storing only some intermediate states and recomputing the rest, achieving up to 8 times better memory efficiency with only a 20% slowdown and no accuracy loss. The training-without-normalization paper solved instability differently: it shows that deep SNNs can actually train stably without the tricks used in regular neural networks—it successfully trains 1000-layer SNNs by carefully managing how information flows, outperforming networks that use normalization layers. The timestep-scaling paper fixes a different stability issue: when you adjust the number of time steps the network runs for (how many discrete moments the simulation runs), performance gets worse instead of better, which is puzzling in the field. This paper achieves better or equal accuracy than existing methods on both time-series and event-based tasks while solving that scaling problem, because it changes the neuron model itself to handle different numbers of time steps without collapsing. Together, these three papers address the three core obstacles to scaling SNNs: memory consumption, training instability, and timestep sensitivity.

Spiking Attention Networks for Efficiency

Attention-based networks have become the standard architecture for language and vision, but they are computationally expensive: standard attention is quadratic in the sequence length, meaning longer sequences take four times as much compute. Spiking attention networks try to run attention blocks with spike-based neurons to gain energy savings. The challenge is that these networks were designed around continuous values; plugging in discrete spikes breaks the math.

SmoothSpike improves on baseline spiking attention networks by adding a learnable Hadamard transformation—a fixed-structure math trick that lets the network learn how to mix spike values more efficiently—and gains 8.2 percentage points average accuracy improvement on language tasks while keeping energy low. Efficient-Transformer-Attention for SNNs uses a different simplification: instead of the full quadratic attention computation, it uses Hadamard math to reduce computation from quadratic to linear—from N² operations to just N—which cuts the communication cost from N² to N as well, achieving 96 percent accuracy on CIFAR-10 with much less compute. The Otters paper takes a hardware angle: it uses optical time-to-first-spike encoding, a technique from hardware sensors that measures exactly when light arrives, to make spiking attention networks even more energy-efficient. It achieves better or equal accuracy than other methods on seven language benchmarks and uses 1.77 times less energy than the previous best spiking attention network, because the optical encoding integrates naturally with neuromorphic hardware. Positional-Encoding for Spiking Transformers fixes a gap in the architecture: these networks rely on positional encodings to understand word order, but spiking networks had no good way to do this. This paper adds positional encodings specifically designed for spike-based networks, outperforming other SNN methods on 13 language benchmarks. These four papers collectively show that attention-based networks can work with spikes if you redesign attention, add the right positional tricks, and match them to hardware realities.

State-Space & Sparse Models

Beyond attention-based networks, researchers are exploring other efficient architectures for spiking networks. State-space models and sparse networks offer alternatives that might be even leaner than attention-based designs.

The harmonic-resonate-and-fire state-space model paper presents an entirely different neuron design: instead of the standard leaky-integrate-and-fire neuron, it uses a harmonic oscillator metaphor where the neuron's internal state changes like a vibrating system, and it fires when the oscillation crosses a threshold. This model eliminates matrix multiplications—the expensive operation in neural networks—and achieves better accuracy than attention-based networks on 17 time-series datasets using much less compute, because it processes sequences by internal oscillation rather than by computing attention over all pairs of positions. The hybrid-Mamba-Transformer for point clouds combines spiking neurons with Mamba—a newer, simpler architecture than attention networks—and achieves better or equal accuracy than other spiking methods on 3D shape classification and part segmentation while using much less computational energy, because Mamba reduces quadratic dependencies. Cannistraci-Hebb training makes spiking networks ultra-sparse: instead of all neurons connecting to all neurons, it uses biology-inspired learning rules to create very sparse connection patterns—keeping only a small fraction of connections—yet maintains performance comparable to fully connected networks, which means less compute and memory because fewer multiplications happen. These three papers explore alternatives to the standard neuron model and architecture that can deliver efficiency gains in different domains: time-series, 3D vision, and structured sparsity.

Spiking Large Language Models

Language models are among the most power-hungry AI systems. A goal of recent work is to run large language models with spiking neurons to cut energy consumption dramatically. The tradeoff is that language is continuous and spiking is discrete; bridging that gap requires new techniques.

The phase-coding paper for spike-driven LLMs converts text to spikes using a method called phase coding—encoding information in the timing and phase of spike patterns—and achieves the lowest perplexity (a measure of language quality) while running on neuromorphic hardware 42 percent faster than standard methods. This works because phase coding maps continuous learned word vectors (embeddings represent each word as a list of numbers) into spike timing patterns that hardware accelerators can process directly without expensive multiplications. SpikingLM goes end-to-end: it creates a fully spiking language model that reduces energy consumption by 57.9 percent compared to traditional language models, and it gets competitive accuracy on standard language benchmarks. The trick is Spike2Max, a hardware-efficient bit-shift operation that removes multiplication overhead so each spike computation is just a few logic gates instead of floating-point arithmetic. TEFormer improves spiking attention networks by adding structured bidirectional temporal enhancement—a way to let the network look both backward and forward in time to understand context better—and gets higher or equal accuracy than both standard SNNs and earlier versions of spiking attention networks across benchmarks, because bidirectional context prevents the network from missing information that comes later in the sequence. Together, these papers show that language models can shrink their power footprint substantially if you think carefully about encoding and temporal modeling; the catch is this only works if hardware has special support for neuromorphic operations.

Adaptation & Deployment on Edge

The ultimate goal of spiking networks is to run real tasks on real edge devices—phones, sensors, robots—where power is limited and you cannot phone home to a server. This requires both algorithmic tricks to adapt SNNs to specific tasks and engineering to run them efficiently on commodity hardware.

The adaptive-and-asymmetric surrogate gradients paper improves training stability across both CNN and attention-based network variants and consistently boosts accuracy and energy efficiency on image classification and segmentation, providing a solution that works across many model types because the asymmetric gradients match how spikes actually propagate in both forward and backward directions through the network. The frequency-matching paper targets a specific application—millimeter-wave sensing for radar—and shows that if you tune the neuron's bandwidth to match the frequencies in your sensor data, you get 3.64 times lower energy use and 6.22 percentage points higher accuracy than traditional neural networks on that task because you are only computing the frequencies that matter. Fault-tolerance via learnable fragmentation makes spiking networks resilient to hardware faults—bit flips or failed components—by learning how to split information into pieces so that losing one piece does not break the entire output, validated on actual FPGA hardware where real faults occur. SAFA-SNN enables few-shot learning on edge devices: it learns new tasks from just a few examples while keeping the network sparse and energy-efficient, because it adapts the network structure itself rather than just updating weights, so adding a new class takes less memory. Spik4lite refactors how sparsity is exploited in SNNs on real edge processors: most SNNs have built-in sparsity—many neurons do not fire—but edge chips do not fully use that sparsity, so this paper maps SNN computations to edge hardware more intelligently, improving accuracy and cutting energy and computational cost by matching algorithm structure to what hardware can do fast. These five papers together form a full pipeline from lab research to products that actually ship on devices: they show better training methods that work across many architectures, tuning networks for specific tasks like radar sensing, making networks fault-resistant so they work even if hardware breaks, learning from just a few examples on small devices so you do not need huge datasets, and finally adapting the code to run well on real chips where every watt counts.
