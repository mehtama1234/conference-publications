MicroVerse: Video Models for Microscale Biology

Building a video model that respects the laws of biology by training it on a carefully curated dataset of microscale phenomena.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Video models can generate smooth, coherent videos of everyday scenes. But when you ask them to simulate what happens inside a cell or inside an organ, they fail badly—making things that violate the laws of physics or biology. This paper shows why that happens and builds a specialized video model that can accurately simulate microscale biology.

Zero assumption version

Video models trained on humans fail on cells

Modern video generation models are trained almost entirely on human-scale videos: people walking, driving, playing sports. They learn patterns like how light behaves, how clothes move, how faces change expression. But cells and organs follow completely different rules—they are often transparent, move with diffusion and flow rather than rigid motion, and obey different timescales.

A naive approach is to take a pre-trained video model and just ask it to generate microscale scenes. It fails. The output violates laws of physics (objects appear and vanish, momentum goes wrong, diffusion spreads backwards). It is temporally inconsistent (the same cell looks different frame to frame). And it simply does not match what microscale phenomena actually look like.

The core problem: Models trained on everyday videos lack the biological knowledge to simulate what is actually inside a cell.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect candidate videos

Download 8,000 YouTube videos tagged with microscale biology (organ, cell, molecule).

Build evaluation rubric

Use an LLM to generate 459 criteria (does it obey physics? match biology?). Experts vote and refine.

Filter and verify

Automatic filters remove corrupted clips; experts remove unphysical ones. 9,601 verified videos remain.

Generate captions

A multimodal LLM describes each video in detail (150 words); experts verify each description.

Fine-tune a video model

Train a video diffusion model on this biology-grounded dataset with the rubric guiding the training objective.

What the paper changes

What the paper changed

The paper changes one thing: the training data and the training objective. Instead of training a video model on generic internet video, the authors train it on 9,601 expert-verified videos of actual microscale phenomena, paired with biology-aware captions and constrained by physics-respecting evaluation criteria.

The hidden quantity

Physics constraint satisfaction

measured quantity  =  how much a generated video violates the laws of physics

The paper introduces a hidden quantity: how often does a generated video break the laws of physics and biology? This is not just a single number; it is a multi-dimensional measurement. The benchmark includes 459 expert-annotated criteria grouped into dimensions: scientific fidelity (does the biology stay correct?), visual quality (is it clear and artifact-free?), and instruction-following (does it match the description?).

The paper estimates this quantity by having experts score each generated video on the rubric. A general-purpose video model scores poorly (high violations). MicroVerse scores much higher because it was trained on videos that already respect these physical laws.

Why it works

Why training on the right data works

The mechanism is simple: models learn from what they see. A video model trained on everyday human-scale videos learns patterns like rigid objects, lighting from above, familiar physics. When asked to generate a cell, it falls back on those learned patterns—and they are wrong for biology.

By training a video model on 9,601 real videos of cells and organs, the model learns the actual patterns of microscale biology: how diffusion spreads (not rigid motion), how transparency changes (not like solid objects), how timescales work (millions of atoms move together). The expert-verified captions teach the model which description matches which biology. The rubric-guided training objective rewards the model for generating videos that satisfy the 459 physics and biology criteria.

This works because machine learning models are data-driven: the patterns in the training data become the patterns the model generates. Biology-grounded data produces biology-grounded output.

The training objective

First in words:

minimize the difference between the model's noise prediction and the actual noise injected into videos, weighted by how well the output satisfies biology criteria

Then the compact form:

L = E[ ||epsilon - epsilon_theta(L_t, t, E)|| ^ 2 ]

Every symbol:

L_tthe compressed video representation (made smaller to save memory) with noise added, at step t
epsilonthe random noise that was added to corrupt the video
epsilon_thetathe model's guess of what noise was there
twhich denoising step (0 means pure noise, T means the original clear video)
Ethe compact encoding of the text description
A tiny worked example
Suppose a video frame of a mitosing cell is encoded into 256 numbers (a compressed representation).
At step 50 of the denoising process (halfway through), random noise is added, corrupting the representation.
The model tries to predict what noise was added: it guesses 256 noise values.
The true noise was different; the error is large.
The model's weights are nudged to predict more accurately next time.
After many iterations on many videos, the model learns that 'mitosis' in the text description should predict a certain pattern of noise at each step.
When answering a new question, the model is given pure noise and a text prompt, then predicts and removes noise 50 times to reconstruct a biology-consistent video.

The equation is standard diffusion training. The difference is training it on biology-verified data with a rubric-guided objective.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Prompt too vague. If the text description does not specify a clear biological mechanism, the model generates plausible-looking but incorrect dynamics. The rubric catches this because the output will not match domain expertise.
Dataset too small for uncommon phenomena. The dataset has 9,601 videos across three scales (organ, cell, molecule). Very uncommon or specialized biological scenarios may not appear in training because they are so rare, so the model has never encountered the patterns needed to generate them and produces unreliable or incorrect results.
Imbalance in training videos. If the training videos are biased toward certain organisms or cell types, the model has seen those much more often and will be accurate on them, but will fail on organisms or cells it rarely or never encountered. The paper does not report which organisms appear most in the dataset.
Done right. Pair any video generation model with a curated, expert-verified, physics-grounded dataset and a rubric-based training objective. The model will learn to generate videos that respect the laws of the domain it sees.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a microscale simulation demo: download a video generation model (Wan2.1), fine-tune it on the MicroSim-10K dataset (or a subset), and generate videos of a cell undergoing mitosis or a protein folding. Compare its output to ground-truth videos on the rubric dimensions: does the output obey conservation of mass? Do organelles stay visible and move smoothly? Does the description match the generated dynamics?

biology_fidelity_score = (expert_rubric_score_finetuned - expert_rubric_score_pretrained) / expert_rubric_score_pretrained × 100%

The demo makes it impossible to miss: a model trained on everyday video generates cells that flicker and vanish because it never learned the right patterns; the same model fine-tuned on biology-grounded data generates cells that behave like actual cells because it has now seen how real biology works.
