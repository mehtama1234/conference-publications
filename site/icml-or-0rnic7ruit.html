Exposing Vulnerabilities in Explanation for Time Series Classifiers via Dual-Target Attacks

Time series classifiers can be fooled to make wrong predictions while their explanations stay plausible, revealing that explanation stability does not guarantee the predictions will stay correct.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When we train a deep learning model to classify time series data (like heart rate patterns or stock price movements), we often ask the model to also explain its reasoning by pointing to which parts of the signal mattered. It seems safe to assume that if the explanation stays consistent, the model is making sound decisions. But this paper reveals a dangerous gap: an attacker can craft a small, hidden modification to the signal that makes the model output a wrong class while the explanation points to a different, decoy part of the signal. The attack is called TSEF (Time Series Explanation Fooler), and it simultaneously steers two outputs — the prediction and the explanation — toward completely different targets.

Zero assumption version

The false trust trap

Time series classifiers (models that label sequences of numbers like sensor readings) make mistakes. To debug them and build trust, we attach an explanation layer that highlights which time steps and which signal features the model considered most important. In practice, if the explanation is temporally consistent — meaning it keeps pointing to the same region of the signal across similar inputs — we treat that as a sign the model will keep making correct decisions.

But there is a hidden vulnerability: the prediction pathway and the explanation pathway inside the model are not perfectly coupled. A naive attacker might add noise to fool the classifier, but they would also smear the explanation across the whole signal. A smeared explanation would immediately look suspicious. The gap that no one has studied: what if an attacker could separately steer the prediction and the explanation to two completely different destinations, keeping each one plausible on its own?

In one sentence: Attackers can craft a small modification that makes the time series classifier predict the wrong class while its explanation points to a different, innocent region, both appearing honest separately.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the time series input signal itself — not by adding random noise everywhere, but by selectively perturbing specific time steps in a sparse, connected pattern and shaping those changes in the frequency domain so they mimic natural signal variation.

the object it moves  =  the input signal perturbation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Temporal vulnerability

measured quantity  =  Temporal vulnerability (which time windows matter for the dual goal)

The paper measures a new quantity called temporal vulnerability: which contiguous region of time steps, if perturbed, makes it easiest for the model to simultaneously achieve both adversarial goals (wrong prediction and matching a decoy explanation). This is different from importance — it is not about what the model considers salient, but about which region is softest to attack, because time series models respond most sensitively to localized temporal patterns rather than scattered noise across all time steps.

The paper estimates temporal vulnerability by optimizing a learned mask Mt over the input, learning through repeated training which time steps minimize the joint attack loss. The mask is a sparse, binary indicator of which time steps are vulnerable. By learning this mask, the paper discovers which regions are truly exploitable — the ones where you can change both the prediction and the explanation simultaneously without triggering the dense-perturbation failure mode.

Why it works

Why the attack works: Decoupling two separate optimization paths

The core insight is that a time series classifier has two semi-independent pathways: one that outputs the class prediction, and one that outputs the explanation (which time steps and channels matter). An attacker can exploit this separation by pulling them in opposite directions.

Here is the mechanical flow: First, the attacker identifies which time steps and channels, if perturbed slightly, would best push the classifier toward a wrong target class. This is a standard adversarial attack problem, solved by nudging the numbers in the direction that increases the classification error. Second, simultaneously, the attacker optimizes a separate objective: make the explanation layer point to a different region of the signal (a decoy), one that looks innocent and plausible. The two objectives compete. Naive joint optimization would cause them to interfere with each other, spreading the perturbation across the whole signal and making the explanation noisy. The paper's insight is to separate the where from the how: use a learned sparse temporal mask to choose which time steps to perturb, then separately use frequency-domain filtering to choose how to perturb them (in a way that looks like natural signal variation). This separation lets each objective optimize independently within its own dimension — the classifier gets to move the prediction via a sharp, focused perturbation, while the explainer gets steered to the decoy region without the two goals fighting over the same numerical knobs. Finally, the method clips the total perturbation to stay within a norm budget, ensuring the attack is not easily detectable by magnitude alone.

Why it succeeds: Time series models respond to temporal structure and patterns, not individual timestep values. By learning a sparse, connected mask and shaping it in frequency space, the attack mimics natural signal variation while maintaining the mathematical grip on both outputs.

Math after the picture

The dual-target loss

First in words:

Total loss equals classification loss plus weighted explanation loss, minus regularization for sparse temporal mask

Then the compact form:

J = L_cls(f(X_perturbed), y_target) + lambda * L_exp(H_E(X_perturbed), A_decoy) + R(M_t)

Every symbol:

JTotal attack loss to minimize
L_clsClassification loss (cross-entropy toward wrong class)
f(X_perturbed)Classifier output on perturbed signal
y_targetWrong target class (not the true label)
lambdaWeight balancing how hard to push the explanation
L_expExplanation loss (alignment to decoy region)
H_EExplanation function (what the model says matters)
A_decoyDecoy explanation (reference mask, top 10% salient region on clean input)
R(M_t)Regularization keeping mask sparse and connected
M_tLearned temporal mask (which time steps to perturb)
A tiny worked example with concrete numbers
Suppose a classifier outputs class probabilities [0.1, 0.8, 0.1] for true class 1 (confidence 0.8).
Attack target: flip to class 0, so target probabilities become [0.8, 0.1, 0.1].
Classification loss L_cls = cross-entropy(model_output, [0.8, 0.1, 0.1]) = large penalty to push prediction.
Explanation before attack: highlights time steps 5-9 as important (salient region).
Attacker chooses decoy: reference explanation A_decoy highlights time steps 2-4 instead (innocent region).
Explanation loss L_exp = misalignment between current explanation and decoy = penalty to force explanation to match decoy.
With lambda = 0.5, total loss = 1.2 * L_cls + 0.5 * L_exp + R(M_t), steering both outputs at once.

The dual loss reveals why the attack works: the classifier and explainer compete for control, but by learning where to perturb, the attacker can minimize interference and achieve both goals with a single, small perturbation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Dense perturbations are detectable. If the attacker spreads the perturbation across all time steps uniformly, the explanation becomes noisy and inconsistent, immediately signaling an attack. TSEF avoids this by learning a sparse mask.
High-dimensional optimization is unstable. Time series have a high-dimensional attack surface (many time steps times many channels). Naive nudging spreads the attack energy too broadly. TSEF decouples by learning which region to hit, then shaping the hit independently.
Temporal incoherence betrays the attack. Random noise changes from moment to moment, but real signals have patterns. If the perturbation is not temporally smooth, it looks fake. TSEF applies frequency-domain filtering to make the perturbation look like natural signal variation.
Done right with TSEF. The attack learns a sparse, connected temporal mask that identifies the softest region, then applies a smooth, frequency-shaped perturbation that mimics natural patterns while achieving both goals within a norm budget.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple time series classifier on a standard dataset (e.g., ECG heartbeat classification or stock price trend classification). Train it with an attached explanation method that highlights which time steps and channels matter. Then implement TSEF and run it on test examples: First, craft a small perturbation using TSEF. Second, feed the perturbed signal to the trained classifier and record the prediction (verify it changed to the wrong class). Third, display the explanation for the perturbed signal (verify it points to a decoy region, not where you actually perturbed). Fourth, measure the norm of the perturbation (confirm it is within the allowed budget). Fifth, measure explanation consistency on the perturbed signal (show that it remains high even though the prediction is now wrong).

Dual success rate = fraction of clean test examples where TSEF achieves BOTH targeted misclassification AND decoy explanation alignment

The demo must make it impossible to ignore: explanation consistency and whether predictions stay correct are not the same thing. A model can pass the explanation consistency check and still be vulnerable to adversarial attack.
