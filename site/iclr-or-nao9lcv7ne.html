Emergent Dexterity Via Diverse Resets and Large-Scale Reinforcement Learning

A robot learns to do complex hand tasks by training from many different starting positions instead of a single fixed one.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot to pick and manipulate objects is hard because long sequences of moves need to explore many different ways things can go wrong. Current methods need humans to carefully design training schedules and reward signals, and even then they fail on tasks like inserting drawers or screwing in objects. This paper shows that simply starting each training trial from a randomly chosen realistic position—instead of always the same starting pose—lets the robot learn complex manipulation tasks at scale without any curriculum design or human guidance.

Zero assumption version

Why robots fail at complex manipulation

In simulation, a robot learns by trial and error (called reinforcement learning): it tries a move, gets feedback on how close it got to the goal, and adjusts. The problem: if you always reset to the same starting position and slowly move toward harder goals, the robot's learned behavior gets stuck exploring the same tiny part of the space of possible moves. It never sees the messy variety of ways objects can get stuck, tipped, or misaligned in the real world.

Long sequences of moves (like open gripper, move to object, adjust grip, lift, rotate, insert) need the robot to have seen many different combinations of 'what went wrong so far.' A standard training run keeps resetting to one comfortable starting pose, so the robot never builds this diversity of skills. It ends up like someone who has only practiced piano in one room with one tuning—frozen when faced with variety.

The method in one sentence. Train the robot by randomly resetting it to different realistic starting positions in each trial, forcing it to master many possible situations and not get stuck on one narrow path to success.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Identify reset zones

Offline, sample many realistic starting positions grouped by type: near the goal, near the object, in contact with object, or far away.

Validate and store them

Check that each reset is physically valid (no collisions, no flying objects) by stepping the simulator a few frames.

Start each trial randomly

During training, pick a random reset zone and a random valid starting position within it for every trial.

Train one RL policy

A single policy (called PPO, proximal policy optimization) learns from all these diverse starting points using a fixed reward and fixed hyperparameters.

Distill and transfer

Convert the proprioceptive (joint-angle) policy into a vision-based policy and send it to the real robot with no additional training.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves: starting positions

Instead of always resetting the robot and object to the same comfortable starting configuration, OmniReset varies the starting configuration across hundreds of hand-crafted but algorithmically valid reset zones. The shift is simple but changes everything: the learning algorithm no longer navigates one narrow ravine toward the goal; it learns to climb from many different foothills, building a richer toolkit of skills.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: interaction diversity

measured quantity  =  Contact and state diversity covered

The paper does not measure this explicitly, but it is the core lever: how much of the space of possible robot-object contacts and relative positions has the training process actually visited? When a robot always starts from the same pose, it explores a tiny slice of these interactions. By resetting from many zones, the algorithm encounters and learns from vastly more ways the gripper can touch, push, or reorient the object.

OmniReset estimates this by organizing resets into semantic regions (reaching distance, near object, in contact, grasping) and sampling uniformly across them. The paper does not compute a diversity score, but the hypothesis is clear: more diverse starting states mean the learned policy has seen more of the interaction space and thus generalizes to real-world variation.

The paper notes that advanced exploration methods (curiosity-driven, count-based, demonstration-based) add algorithmic complexity and do not scale well to massively parallel simulation. OmniReset instead trades algorithmic complexity for simplicity and scale: more interaction variety is achieved by covering diverse starting positions, not by redesigning the learning algorithm.
Why it works

Why learning from diverse starts works

Standard reinforcement learning finds policies by following gradient signals (nudges in the direction that reduces error). When the robot always starts from the same position, every gradient signal points toward the same narrow solution—there is one easy path downhill. The algorithm converges fast to that path, but the resulting policy fails the moment the real world starts the robot from a different pose.

By resetting to many different starting positions, every trial launches the robot from a new location with a new relationship to the goal and objects. The gradient signals now point in many different directions. The algorithm cannot converge to a single narrow behavior; instead, it must discover general skills that work from many angles. This forces the learned policy to work from many situations, not just one. Adding more compute (more parallel trials) directly translates to covering more of the starting-position space. Covering more space drives higher final performance and breaks the typical ceiling where policies plateau trapped in a narrow local solution.

Math after the picture

The training objective

First in words:

Maximize the expected sum of rewards across many trials, where each trial starts from a randomly sampled position across diverse reset zones.

Then the compact form:

max E[sum_t gamma^t * r_t | reset ~ Diverse Zones]

Every symbol:

E[·]the average over many trials
sum_tadd up all rewards across the full task sequence
gammadiscount factor (0.99 typically): future rewards matter slightly less than immediate ones
r_treward at time step t (e.g., +1 for reaching the goal)
reset ~ Diverse Zonesthe starting configuration is randomly sampled from multiple pre-defined zones, not always the same pose
A tiny worked example
Suppose a gripper-positioning task has four reset zones, each offering 50 valid starting positions (200 total).
Trial 1: reset sampled from zone A, position 17. Robot explores moves from this new angle. Learns: 'when at this pose, rotate then lift works.'
Trial 2: reset sampled from zone C, position 3. Different situation. Learns: 'when starting from here, lift first then rotate.'
Trial 3–N: each from a random zone and position. The gradient accumulates learnings across all these starting points.
Result: one policy that has internalized strategies for reaching from A, B, C, and D, not just the one 'easy' reset. Generalization emerges from the forced diversity.

The equation shows the standard RL objective, but the key difference is that the reset (starting position) varies across trials instead of staying fixed, forcing the policy to learn multiple pathways to success.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Invalid resets cause instability. If the offline reset-generation phase does not properly validate that a starting position is stable (no objects interpenetrating, no gripper flying through space), training will spend compute on broken trials. Solution: step the simulator forward a few frames and use collision checking.
Uninformed reset zones miss key interactions. If the human designing the reset zones guesses wrong about where the robot needs to start to learn the task, coverage remains incomplete. A drawer-insertion task might need resets where the gripper is already in light contact with the drawer; if the zones only cover reaching from far away, the robot never learns in-contact manipulation. Solution: include semantic zones for the task family (reaching, near-object, grasping, inserting).
Extreme reset imbalance ruins generalization. If 90% of resets come from one zone and 10% from others, the policy will over-specialize to that zone despite having diverse resets available. The gradient signal is dominated by the common zone. Solution: sample uniformly across zones.
Done right. Carefully define semantic reset zones (far, reaching, near-object, grasping), validate each one for stability, and sample uniformly at training time. Compute scales the coverage: more trials cover more of each zone, strengthening the generalization.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple simulation of a robot arm pushing a block on a table. Set up four semantic reset zones: one where the arm starts far from the block, one where it is reachable distance away, one where it is near the block, and one where it is already in contact. Train two policies side by side: one always resets to the same 'easy' starting position, the other randomly resets across all four zones. After training, test both on a held-out set of random starting positions that neither saw during training.

Generalization gap = (success rate on training resets) - (success rate on new resets)

The fixed-reset policy should show a huge generalization gap (succeeds on its one training reset, fails elsewhere); the OmniReset policy should show a small gap (mediocre performance everywhere, but consistent). Demonstrate that larger compute (more parallel environments) improves the OmniReset policy further—because more trials cover more starting positions—while the fixed-reset policy plateaus because it has already converged to its narrow solution.
