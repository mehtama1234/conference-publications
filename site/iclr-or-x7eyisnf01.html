IDEA: Shift GNN Explainers to Representation Space

Explain graph neural networks by matching structure in a richer space, not just predicting the same label.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When you ask a graph neural network why it made a decision, current explainers find a small subgraph and check if the model gives the same answer on just that subgraph. But that check only works in prediction space, which is weak at capturing structure. This paper instead builds the explanation in a richer space—the internal vector representation that the network naturally learns—which captures what parts of the graph actually matter structurally.

Zero assumption version

Current explainers are blind to structure

When a graph neural network (a model that learns patterns in networks of connected nodes) makes a decision about a graph, we want to know which part of the graph caused that decision. Current methods find a compact subgraph and ask: does the model predict the same label on this smaller piece? If yes, that piece is the explanation. But this label-matching approach is brittle.

The problem: a label is a single number (or a class name like cat or dog). Two very different subgraph structures can produce the same label. The method cannot tell the difference. Imagine a friendship network labeled toxic. One toxic subgraph might be a tight clique of 5 people who reinforce each other. Another might be a loose chain of 20 people spreading rumors. Both predict toxic, but the structures are opposite. A label-based explainer would miss this distinction entirely.

The method in one sentence. Instead of checking if a subgraph predicts the same label, build the explanation in the model's internal representation space—a richer vector space where structure information lives.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Disentangle

A graph tokenizer (a tiny sub-model that breaks the graph into pieces) learns which parts of the graph are structurally important for the explanation.

Stage 2: Collect prototypes

The important parts are packed into prototypical vectors—archetypal examples of what an explanation looks like in the model's vector space.

Stage 3: Extract subgraph

Pick the smallest subgraph whose vectors match the prototypes.

Stage 4: Align pattern spaces

Ensure the full graph and the small subgraph live in the same region of the model's internal vector space, not drifting apart.

Stage 5: Optimize

Iteratively adjust both the subgraph and the prototypes until they form a coherent explanation.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts the explainer's goal from working in label space to working in representation space. It swaps the optimization target from matching predictions to matching internal vectors.

the object it moves  =  the optimization target space
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Representational alignment

measured quantity  =  representational discrepancy between graphs

Representational discrepancy is how different two graphs look when you measure them in the model's internal vector space (not as a label, but as the full list of internal numbers the model computes). When the full graph and the explanation subgraph have a small discrepancy, it means they activate the model's internal patterns in similar ways.

The paper measures this discrepancy as the distance between two patterns of which internal numbers light up. It uses a technique called wasserstein distance, which measures how far apart two patterns are by comparing how the full graph and the subgraph each light up regions in the representation space. A small distance means a good explanation.

Why it works

Why representation space works better than labels

A label is a single output, so it compresses all the graph structure into one number. Many different graphs can map to the same label. The model's internal representation is a vector—a list of hundreds or thousands of numbers learned during training. This vector captures subtle structural patterns: which nodes are central, which edges form bridges, which clusters are tightly knit. When you match representations instead of labels, you are forcing the explanation to preserve those structural patterns.

The mechanism has two parts. First, a graph tokenizer uses a disentanglement loss (a penalty that pushes the model to separate structural signals) to identify which subgraph pieces are actually driving the decision. It does not just ask does this subgraph give the same label, but rather which subgraph components light up the same internal patterns. Second, alignment ensures the full graph and the explanation live in the same region of representation space, so no drift happens during optimization. Together, these lock down the explanation to what the model actually saw structurally.

Math after the picture

The optimization formula

First in words:

The total loss combines three pieces: the distance between the representational distributions of the full graph and the subgraph, the disentanglement penalty that forces structural separation, and a size penalty that prefers smaller explanations.

Then the compact form:

Loss = Wasserstein(G_full, G_explain) + lambda_dis * Disentangle_Loss + lambda_size * |SubgraphSize|

Every symbol:

G_fullthe vector representation of the complete input graph
G_explainthe vector representation of the extracted subgraph
Wasserstein(...)the distance between two distributions in representation space
Disentangle_Losspenalty that pushes explanatory and non-explanatory parts to different vector regions
lambda_disweight controlling how strongly to enforce disentanglement
lambda_sizeweight controlling the preference for smaller subgraphs
|SubgraphSize|the number of nodes and edges in the explanation
A tiny worked example
Suppose the full graph representation is at vector position (0.8, 0.6, 0.4), and the current subgraph representation is (0.5, 0.8, 0.3).
Wasserstein distance = 0.4 (moderately far apart, so representational discrepancy is 0.4).
Disentangle_Loss = 0.05 (low because structure is reasonably separated), lambda_dis = 2 → penalty = 2 * 0.05 = 0.1.
Subgraph has 8 nodes, lambda_size = 0.1 → size penalty = 0.1 * 8 = 0.8.
Total loss = 0.4 + 0.1 + 0.8 = 1.3. The optimizer nudges the subgraph vectors closer (shrinking Wasserstein) and removes nodes (shrinking size), hoping to lower this sum.

The three terms work together: representation matching keeps fidelity, disentanglement ensures the explanation is structurally coherent, and size keeps the explanation small.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatch between training and test graph patterns. If the graphs at test time have different structural patterns from training, the prototypes learned on training data will not align well. The method assumes the explanation space stays stable across different kinds of graphs.
Tokenizer overcomplicates the structure. If the graph tokenizer is not designed carefully, it might fragment important structures into too many tokens, making disentanglement noisy and the prototype matching unreliable.
Wasserstein distance is computationally expensive. For very large graphs, computing how the model's internal patterns compare between the full graph and subgraph scales poorly, slowing down explanation generation.
Done right. The method works well when the model's representation space naturally encodes structural patterns and when the test graphs look similar to the training graphs.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple demonstration on a synthetic graph classification task (for example, classifying small molecular graphs or citation networks). Train a basic graph neural network on this dataset. Then compare two explainers side by side: one that optimizes in label space (the old way) and IDEA (representation space). Extract explanations for 5 test graphs from each method. Measure how faithfully each explanation preserves the model's actual decision on the full graph versus the subgraph. Additionally, verify that IDEA's explanations are structurally recognizable—can a human see why the structure matters—while the label-space method produces tangled, hard-to-interpret subgraphs.

faithfulness = accuracy_on_full_graph - accuracy_on_subgraph; lower is better (high faithfulness = subgraph alone is almost as accurate as the full graph)
