PINFDiT: Physics-Informed Diffusion Transformers for Time Series

Add physics laws as a steering mechanism during inference so diffusion models generate time series that both fit the data and obey the physics.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Time series from science—climate, fluids, biology—come with a hidden constraint: they must follow physical laws. But most models learn only from data, so they can generate nonsensical sequences that violate conservation or other rules. This paper shows how to take a trained diffusion model and guide its samples toward physical correctness without retraining, using a technique called Langevin dynamics that gently nudges the generated values toward solutions that satisfy physics equations.

Zero assumption version

Physics-Respecting Generation is Hard

Scientific time series come from systems governed by physical laws—heat flowing through a rod, fluid swirling, molecules bouncing. A naive approach: train a neural network (a pattern-matcher) on historical data and ask it to fill in missing values or forecast the future. It will learn rough patterns from the data, but those patterns alone do not guarantee the output obeys the physics. The model might generate a sequence that looks plausible but violates energy conservation or momentum balance.

Real-world data makes this worse. Scientific datasets are often small (expensive to measure), contain missing values (a sensor breaks), are sampled unevenly (10 readings one hour, 100 the next), and span multiple phenomena at once. A pattern-matching model trained on such messy data has almost no hope of discovering the underlying physics on its own.

In one sentence. Models trained on data alone generate samples that fit the data but can violate physical laws; we need a way to steer generation toward physics-compliant solutions without retraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train normally

Learn a diffusion model (a noise-to-data converter) on raw time series, including masking for missing values and uneven sampling.

At inference time, start with noise

Initialize a random noisy sequence and begin the standard diffusion step-by-step refinement.

Insert physics guidance

After each refinement step, compute how well the current sequence obeys physical equations (e.g., energy balance, momentum conservation).

Apply a small correction

Use Langevin dynamics—a random-walk technique that nudges the values slightly toward solutions with fewer physics violations, much like water flowing downhill.

Repeat and output

Complete the diffusion loop; the final sample is both data-faithful and physics-compliant, ready to use.

What the paper changes

The Thing That Changes

The paper modifies the inference process—the moment when a trained model generates new sequences. Specifically, it adds a physics-guided correction step into the loop. The model architecture and weights stay unchanged; only the generation recipe changes. This is like upgrading a car's GPS to re-route around bad roads, without rebuilding the car's engine.

the object it moves  =  inference trajectory
The hidden quantity

Physics Violation: The Hidden Quantity

measured quantity  =  physics violation score

At any point during generation, a sequence can be scored for how badly it breaks physical laws. For example, if the physics is energy conservation, you run the sequence through the differential equations and measure how much energy the equations say it gained or lost; that mismatch is the violation. The paper does not invent new scores—it uses the residual of the governing equations themselves as a measure of failure.

The paper computes this violation continuously during inference. The lower the violation, the more physically consistent the sample. Langevin dynamics then uses that score to steer: it nudges the sample in the direction that reduces violation, like a ball rolling downhill until it stops rolling.

Why it works

Why This Works: The Step-by-Step Steering

Standard diffusion (noise-to-data conversion) works like sculpting: you start with a random blob and refine it step by step to look more like data. But there is no invisible sculptor ensuring the final piece respects physics.

This paper inserts a second sculptor—Langevin dynamics—that pulls on the sample toward physics compliance at each step. Langevin dynamics is a random walk (like a drunk person staggering) biased by a force: the force here is the direction that most steeply reduces the physics-violation score. A high-violation sample feels a strong pull; a low-violation sample is barely nudged. Over 50 or 100 correction steps, these small nudges accumulate, steering the trajectory away from physically nonsensical regions of the space.

You do not retrain. The diffusion model already learned to generate plausible data. Langevin correction just channels that plausibility toward the subset of plausible outputs that also obey physics. Think of it as a guardrail on a bridge—it does not change how you drive; it just keeps you on the road.

Math after the picture

The Langevin Steering Equation

First in words:

For each sample, add a tiny random step in the direction of decreasing physics violation, scaled by how badly the laws are broken.

Then the compact form:

x_{t+1} = x_t - gamma * grad_x V(x_t) + sqrt(2*gamma*eta) * noise

Every symbol:

x_tthe time series values at iteration t
gammastep size (how fast to move toward law-obeying region)
grad_x V(x_t)the slope of physics violation: which direction makes things worse
etatemperature (controls randomness in the walk)
noiserandom numbers drawn from a Gaussian (normal) distribution
A tiny worked example
Sample starts with violation V(x) = 25.0 (energy conservation broken badly).
The gradient at this point says: nudging in direction d would reduce V to 24.2 per step.
Set gamma = 0.1 (small step), eta = 1.0 (mild randomness).
Langevin update: x_new = x - 0.1 * (slope=-0.8) + sqrt(0.2) * 0.5 = x + 0.08 + 0.22 = x + 0.30.
After one step, violation drops to 24.8. After 50 steps, it settles near 5.0 (physical laws mostly respected).
The same model, no retraining, just one inference loop now has an internal steering mechanism.

Langevin dynamics balances two forces: the physics gradient (deterministic) pulls toward law-obeying regions, and noise (random) prevents premature lock-in, keeping the walk exploratory.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Physics equation is wrong or incomplete. If the governing equations are misspecified (missing a term, using wrong values for settings like friction or elasticity), Langevin will steer toward wrong solutions. The output will be internally consistent with the false equations, but physically meaningless. Always validate the equations independently.
Physics violation signal is weak. If the physics equations provide no directional signal (e.g., they are satisfied almost everywhere in the region the diffusion model explores), Langevin steering has nothing to pull on. It degenerates to random noise, wasting computation.
Conflicting constraints. If the data-fit goal and the physics goal point in opposite directions (e.g., the training data is systematically biased by measurement noise that breaks physics), steering will oscillate or get stuck. Clarify which constraint matters more and weight it accordingly.
Done right. Validate equations with domain experts. Use Langevin steering only when you have high-confidence physics and can verify the gradient signal is informative. Monitor the violation score during inference: it should monotonically decrease, not plateau or spike.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small forecasting task: take a known physical system (e.g., a pendulum, heat diffusion, or predator-prey population dynamics). Generate synthetic data by simulating the true equations, then corrupt it (add noise, delete samples, unevenly resample). Train a diffusion model (transformer architecture) on the corrupted data. Run inference with and without Langevin physics steering. Measure two metrics: how well the predictions match the held-out true future (data fidelity), and how well they satisfy the conservation or differential equations (physics compliance). Plot both metrics vs. the strength of the Langevin steering (the step size gamma). Show that you can trade off data-fit for physics-fit, and that moderate steering maximizes downstream utility (e.g., predictions that are both accurate and usable by downstream scientific models).

physics_compliance = 1 - (residual_of_equations / max_possible_residual); data_fidelity = RMSE(predicted - true_future)

The demo must make it impossible to miss: steering works smoothly, increasing gamma trades fit-for-compliance without breaking the model, and the 'sweet spot' often beats pure data fitting on real scientific tasks.
