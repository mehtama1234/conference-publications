TFMixer: Joint Time and Frequency for Irregular Time Series

Use both the time-domain view and the frequency-domain view at once to forecast messy, unevenly-sampled time series data.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Real sensor data comes in at irregular intervals and with missing values. Standard forecasting models assume data arrives at evenly spaced ticks, so they break on reality. TFMixer solves this by looking at the data two ways at once: as patterns unfolding over time (local), and as repeating frequencies (global). A learnable transformation lets it read spectral (frequency) patterns directly from the timestamps, without padding or interpolation.

Why standard forecasting fails on messy data

Imagine a heart-rate monitor that samples every 1 second, then skips 5 seconds, then samples every 2 seconds. Or a weather station that logs readings at odd times because sometimes the network is slow. Most forecasting models assume data arrives like a metronome: every heartbeat at exactly tick 1, tick 2, tick 3. When reality sends irregular, unevenly-spaced samples, those models break.

A beginner might try to smooth the data: insert fake measurements between the real ones (called interpolation), then run a standard model. But this hides the truth. The model learns to forecast the fake data, not the real world. Worse, standard models work in the time domain only: they see today, yesterday, the day before. They miss the bigger pattern: that heart rate, like many real processes, repeats in cycles (it rises when you exercise, falls when you rest). When data is irregular, those periodic cycles get obscured by the gaps.

The core problem. Standard forecasting models need evenly-spaced data and miss the cyclical patterns hidden in irregularly-sampled measurements.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Normalize the raw input

Scale measurements to a fair range, masking missing values.

Frequency branch: learnable Fourier transform

Read the raw timestamps directly, compute spectral (cycle) patterns without fake interpolation.

Time branch: patch mixing

Cluster measurements into chunks, use learnable spot lights to focus on the dense parts.

Fusion: combine both branches

Blend the frequency insight (what repeats) with the time insight (what changes now).

Inverse transform and denormalize

Convert the forecast back to the original data range.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how a model reads spectral patterns (the mathematical fingerprint of repeating cycles) from data with irregular timestamps. Instead of forcing data into evenly-spaced boxes, it uses a learnable Non-Uniform Discrete Fourier Transform (NUDFT) that directly ingests the raw, uneven timestamps and extracts frequency-domain features without any padding or fake data.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Information density imbalance

measured quantity  =  Information density (samples per time window)

In irregular time series, some time windows are packed with measurements and others are sparse. If you chop the data into fixed-size chunks (called patches), one chunk might contain 10 samples while the next has only 1. The model wastes computation on empty patches and drowns real signals in filler.

TFMixer estimates this imbalance by monitoring how many real measurements land in each patch. It then uses learnable query tokens (a kind of soft spotlight) that selectively attend to the densely-sampled patches and skip the empty ones, concentrating learning power where the data is rich.

Why it works

Why both views matter: time and frequency together

Time-domain patterns (what happens next based on trends) and frequency-domain patterns (what repeats cyclically) live in different spaces. A trend-only model sees: I went up yesterday, so I go up today (extrapolation). A cycle-only model sees: I always spike on Thursdays. For real data, you need both. If your heart rate has a slow rising trend AND a minute-by-minute rhythm, missing either one hurts accuracy.

TFMixer's learnable NUDFT is the key: it directly transforms irregular timestamps into frequency coefficients without interpolation (which corrupts the true pattern). The spectrum encoder then pulls out which cycles matter. Meanwhile, the time module clusters real samples into patches and uses learnable spotlight tokens to focus on the densely-sampled segments. Finally, the output module fuses both views: it takes the cycle prediction from the frequency branch and the trend prediction from the time branch, blends them, and adds seasonal corrections computed by inverting the frequency transform. This dual pathway captures both long-range periodicity (global) and short-term dynamics (local).

Math after the picture

The learnable transform at the heart

First in words:

loss equals forecast error plus a penalty term. The penalty is a reconstruction term that trains the frequency branch to capture real periodicities, not noise.

Then the compact form:

Loss = MSE(forecast, true_future) + lambda * MSE(reconstructed, original)

Every symbol:

forecastpredicted next value from both time and frequency branches
true_futurethe actual measured value in the test period
reconstructedthe input data reconstructed by the inverse NUDFT
originalthe true raw input measurements
lambdaregularization strength: how much to penalize frequency noise
A tiny worked example with real numbers
Suppose we forecast heart rate for 5 steps ahead. The true values are [72, 74, 73, 75, 76].
TFMixer predicts [71, 75, 73, 76, 76] using its fused time+frequency view.
Forecast error (MSE) = ((71-72)^2 + (75-74)^2 + (73-73)^2 + (76-75)^2 + (76-76)^2) / 5 = (1+1+0+1+0) / 5 = 0.6
The frequency branch also reconstructs the past input. If lambda = 0.5 and reconstruction MSE = 0.4, then penalty = 0.5 * 0.4 = 0.2.
Total loss = 0.6 + 0.2 = 0.8. The penalty weight (lambda) steers the frequency module toward real cycles and away from fitting random noise.

The reconstruction penalty acts as a guardrail: it prevents the frequency module from overfitting to random jitter and forces it to capture genuine periodic structure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatch between learnable transforms and true frequencies. If the data contains a 7-day cycle but the model has not seen enough data to learn it, the NUDFT may capture spurious patterns. The reconstruction loss helps by penalizing noise, but if the training set does not contain a full 7-day cycle, the model cannot learn it at all—no penalty weight can extract information from a gap in the data.
Patches too large or too small. If patches are huge, you lose fine-grained temporal dynamics. If they are tiny, information density becomes meaningless and the spotlight mechanism becomes noise.
Extreme missingness destroys both branches. If 95 percent of data is missing, even dual modeling cannot recover the full pattern. The frequency branch will see only fragments of cycles, and the time branch will have no dense patches to attend to.
Done right: tune the penalty and collect enough data. Tune lambda (the penalty weight) to encourage real cycles without over-constraining learning. Ensure training data spans at least 2-3 full cycles of the dominant frequencies, and that missingness is below 50 percent.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic time series: a sine wave (24-hour cycle) plus a slow rising trend, sampled at random intervals (e.g. samples drop 30 percent of the time). Split it into train and test. Train a time-only baseline and train TFMixer. Measure forecast accuracy on the test period. TFMixer should forecast the cyclical part accurately (by reading frequency patterns), while the baseline should either lag the trend or miss the cycle entirely. Then remove the frequency module from TFMixer and retrain: it should degrade, proving that both branches pull their weight.

accuracy = 100 * (1 - sqrt(MSE(forecast, true)) / std(true)); higher is better

The demo must make it impossible to ignore: time and frequency are two languages for the same data, and speaking both fluently beats speaking one.
