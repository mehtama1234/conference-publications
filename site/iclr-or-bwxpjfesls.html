Universal Sound Source Localization with Flexible Geometry and Physics

A system that pinpoints where a sound is coming from by learning how audio patterns change with different microphone arrangements, without needing to retrain for each new setup.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Imagine hearing a bird chirp and wanting to know exactly which direction it came from. A microphone array (several mics placed around a space) can do this by comparing the tiny time and volume differences between when the sound reaches each mic. But today's neural network methods are brittle: train on mics arranged in a line, and they fail on mics in a circle. This paper shows how to build a model that learns the core patterns once and adapts to any mic arrangement without retraining.

Zero assumption version

Why today's sound localization breaks

To locate a sound, you need microphones. A trained neural network learns to read the audio patterns and say which direction the sound came from. The problem: a network trained on one array shape (say, mics in a line) fails when you put mics in a circle or a triangle.

A beginner might think: just train on all shapes at once. But that bloats the model with separate branches for each shape, and it still breaks on unfamiliar arrangements. The root cause is that the model memorizes the geometric layout baked into its weights, instead of learning the universal principle: sound takes time to reach different mics, and those time differences encode direction.

The method in one sentence. Learn how sound physics works by understanding audio patterns and mic positions together in one unified space, because the underlying wave behavior never changes even if mics are rearranged.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most sound localization networks treat the microphone array as a fixed design detail and bake it into the model architecture. This paper decouples the core sound-understanding logic from where the microphones sit, letting audio patterns and microphone positions flow through a unified shared space so either can change without breaking the model.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: phase alignment patterns

measured quantity  =  Which frequency bins carry direction information

Sound direction is encoded in phase differences between microphones—how much the wave at one mic lags behind the wave at another. Different frequency bands carry phase information at different strengths. The paper measures this by learning to emphasize the frequencies most useful for the current microphone arrangement.

The learnable non-uniform discrete Fourier transform (LNuDFT) assigns higher importance to frequency bins where phase shifts clearly signal direction, and lower importance to bins where phase is noisy or uninformative. Instead of chopping up the spectrum into evenly spaced bins, it concentrates bins where they matter for the current mic arrangement.

Why it works

Why it works: physics meets learning

Sound localization obeys physics laws that do not change based on where microphones sit. When a sound comes from the left, the wave always reaches the left-side mic first—regardless of whether mics are in a line or a circle. The time differences and phase shifts always encode direction the same way.

The paper exploits this by learning two things: (1) which frequency regions are most sensitive to time differences between mics (the learnable Fourier part), and (2) how to represent microphone positions so the model understands their layout (relative microphone positional encoding). Once the model learns these physics patterns from one set of mic arrangements, it can apply them to any new arrangement because the underlying wave behavior never changes.

The model processes audio and mic positions in a unified computational space (a shared representation space where audio numbers and position numbers all flow together). The model learns that the same direction signal looks different on a linear array versus a circular one, but the physics underneath is identical. By representing both in one space, it finds the parts that never change regardless of array shape, and ignores the array-specific details.

The core equation

First in words:

Direction score equals a learned combination of audio features weighted by how important each frequency bin is, conditioned on the microphone positions.

Then the compact form:

direction_score = sum_k weight_k(geom) * feature_k(audio, freq_bin_k)

Every symbol:

direction_scorethe predicted confidence that sound comes from a specific angle
weight_k(geom)how much to trust frequency bin k for this microphone arrangement
feature_k(audio, freq_bin_k)the extracted audio signal at the k-th learned frequency bin
geomthe relative microphone positions
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 3 mics and 4 learned frequency bins. Mic arrangement: line (mics at x=0, 2, 4).
Bin 1 (sensitive to phase at 500 Hz): feature=0.8, weight for line arrangement=0.9 -> contribution = 0.9 * 0.8 = 0.72
Bin 2 (sensitive at 1000 Hz): feature=0.3, weight=0.7 -> contribution = 0.7 * 0.3 = 0.21
Bin 3 (noisy): feature=0.2, weight=0.1 -> contribution = 0.1 * 0.2 = 0.02
Bin 4 (noisy): feature=0.1, weight=0.05 -> contribution = 0.05 * 0.1 = 0.005
Total direction score = 0.72 + 0.21 + 0.02 + 0.005 = 0.955 (high confidence in this direction).
On a circle arrangement, weights adapt (say, 0.8, 0.8, 0.2, 0.1), but the same audio feature extraction logic reuses learned physics.

The weight for each frequency adapts to the microphone arrangement, while the audio analysis stays consistent, encoding the sound physics once.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Severe mic damage or removal If a microphone is removed or broken after deployment, the relative encoding breaks because it encodes all mic positions. Retraining on the new subset is necessary.
Extreme changes in mic spacing The model learns to handle diverse arrays, but if mics move very far from the training range (e.g. from 1 meter apart to 10 meters apart), the learned position encoding may not work—the fundamental physics of how phase shifts with distance can change.
Reverberation and room echoes Learnable Fourier bins and mic encoding assume relatively clean phase relationships. In highly reverberant rooms where sound bounces many times, the direct-path phase signal gets buried in reflections.
Done right: train on diverse array shapes If the model trains on many different mic layouts and room conditions, it learns sound physics that works well even on unfamiliar configurations.
