SARL: Bridging Perception and Action in Aircraft Conflict Resolution

A multi-agent learning system for aircraft that scales to different numbers of planes and enforces real aviation safety rules.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When airplanes fly near each other, they need to avoid collisions by coordinating their turns and climbs. Traditional learning systems break when the number of planes changes, because they were built to handle a fixed number of inputs. SARL solves this by measuring how well the model learns which planes are close enough to matter and the structure of air traffic instructions, then forces its decisions to follow actual aviation rules.

Zero assumption version

Aircraft need to coordinate without crashing, at any scale

When two airplanes are on a collision course, an air-traffic controller gives them instructions: one climbs, one descends, or both turn. If a learning system trains on scenarios with exactly five planes, it learns to look at exactly five position reports and output five move commands. When a scenario has seven planes, the system breaks — it has nowhere to put the extra inputs, so it ignores them or crashes.

A naive approach tries to feed all positions directly into a learning model and hope it scales. This fails because each airplane's instruction depends only on nearby planes, not all of them — the model wastes effort comparing planes on opposite sides of the airspace. Also, the learned moves often violate real aviation rules: a plane might be told to climb beyond its limit or turn too steeply.

The method in one sentence. Build a graph of which planes matter to each other, route decisions through experts that handle different move types, and check every command against real aviation rules before executing it.

What it actually does

Encode relationships

Physics rules say which planes are close enough to matter to each other; build a graph.

Graph network learns

Each plane looks only at nearby planes using a network that focuses on neighbors; scales to any number.

Route by move type

Three separate experts decide: should this plane climb, turn, or descend.

Combine decisions

Mixture-of-experts weights the experts; one usually leads, keeping moves stable.

Check against rules

Every command is tested against real aviation limits; violations are rejected.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

Instead of a single neural network that reads all plane positions and outputs all moves at once, SARL splits the decision into two pieces: a graph network that learns local proximity patterns regardless of how many planes exist, and separate route-choosing mixture-of-experts that each specialize in one type of move (climb, turn, descend). This way the model can handle five planes or fifteen planes without retraining.

the object it moves  =  graph-based routing with specialized move experts
The hidden quantity

Alignment between model structure and domain rules

measured quantity  =  Structural alignment score

The paper measures a hidden quantity called structural alignment: how well the model's architecture matches the actual rules of air traffic control. A poorly aligned model might treat all planes equally and ignore distance, or might learn moves that no real aircraft can perform. A well-aligned model encodes distance rules upfront (so nearby planes matter more) and restricts moves to physically valid ones.

The paper estimates alignment by checking two things: (1) does the model's perception scale correctly when plane count changes (via the graph attention mechanism, which processes each local neighborhood the same way), and (2) are the model's actions always within aviation limits (via the safety shield, which rejects invalid moves). High alignment means both are true.

Plain reading. Structural alignment is how closely a model's design mirrors the rules of the real world it is trying to control. A graph network aligns with air traffic (distance matters), and a mixture-of-experts aligns with real moves (only certain commands are valid).
Paper reading. The paper calls this alignment the bridge between perception (which planes see which) and action (which moves are legal). It is achieved by encoding physics into the graph (PERG: Physics-Encoded Relational Graph) and enforcing constraints at inference time (KSS: Kinematic Safety Shield).
Why it works

Why it works: structure and constraints guide learning

SARL works because it makes the learning problem much easier by hand-coding the hard parts. Instead of asking a generic neural network to figure out that distance matters, the graph attention mechanism bakes in the idea that nearby planes should influence each other. This is like giving the model a huge head start: it no longer wastes training time on the stupid idea that a plane five miles away should affect this plane's move. The model only learns the details of how to respond to nearby threats.

The mixture-of-experts does something similar for moves. Instead of one neural network trying to learn all three move types at once (climb, turn, descend) and potentially confusing them, three separate experts each specialize in one type. During training, the model learns which expert to trust in each situation. This keeps the move-space simple: a climb expert only learns climb patterns, a turn expert only learns turn patterns. When you ask for a move, usually one expert dominates, so the output is stable and interpretable.

Finally, the safety shield enforces real aviation rules at decision time. Even if the learned model briefly tries an illegal move (such as a climb steeper than the airplane can perform), the shield catches it and picks the closest legal alternative. This means the model never has to learn that an illegal move is bad — the shield prevents it from ever happening in data. Over time, training converges faster because the model only sees valid moves.

Routed decision with safety constraint

First in words:

total move score is the weighted sum of each expert's score, plus a safety penalty for any move that violates aviation limits

Then the compact form:

move_action = safety_filter( sum_k w_k * expert_k(graph_state) )

Every symbol:

move_actionthe final climb, turn, or descend command sent to the aircraft
safety_filter( )a function that checks each move against real aviation limits and rejects illegal ones
w_kthe weight learned by the system for expert k (how much to trust that expert)
expert_k()expert k's score for what move to make, given the current local state
graph_statethe positions and velocities of only the nearby planes that matter
sum_kadd up the contributions from all three experts
Tiny example: three planes in close formation
Nearby state: two planes ahead, one to the left, all 2 km away.
Climb expert sees upward space: scores +0.8 (safe climb available).
Turn expert sees left-turn space: scores +0.3 (moderate turn available).
Descend expert: scores -0.2 (descending worsens the formation).
Learned weights: w_climb=0.7, w_turn=0.2, w_descend=0.1.
Mixture score = 0.7*0.8 + 0.2*0.3 + 0.1*(-0.2) = 0.56 + 0.06 - 0.02 = 0.60.
Climb is strongest. Safety shield checks: is a 500-foot climb legal? Yes.
Final move: climb 500 feet. Model never learns that a 50,000-foot climb is bad because shield blocks it.

The safety filter is not a penalty in the loss function; it is a hard rule at decision time. Because illegal moves never actually happen in the real world the model sees, it never has to learn that they are bad — the shield prevents them before they occur, so training converges faster on only the valid move space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Experts overspecialize and ignore rare situations. If the climb expert trains only on normal-altitude scenarios, it might fail when planes are at sea level. The mixture-of-experts weight can become too focused on one expert, and the system stops working in unfamiliar conditions.
Graph edges are set by fixed distance rules, missing some relationships. If two planes are 2.5 km apart and the graph rule is to connect planes within 2 km, they look independent when they are actually close. A rigid distance threshold can miss dynamic threats.
Safety shield is too restrictive, rejecting all moves as illegal. If aviation limits are set too tight, the shield blocks even reasonable commands, and the model gets stuck unable to act.
Done right: graph rules match real threat distances, experts are trained on diverse scenarios, and safety limits are calibrated to real aircraft performance. The system works when the inductive biases (graph distance, move types) actually capture the domain, and safety rules reflect achievable physics.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that SARL handles three scenarios the traditional system cannot. First, train on five planes and test on seven planes; the demo should show that success rate stays above 85 percent. Second, gradually add planes up to twenty; the demo should show that the per-plane decision time does not explode (because graph attention scales with local neighbors, not all planes). Third, intentionally ask for an illegal move (such as a 60-degree turn when aircraft can only turn 30 degrees) and show that the safety shield rejects it and picks a legal alternative instead.

success_rate = (collision_avoidance_episodes_succeeded) / (total_episodes) and per_plane_time_ns = nanoseconds_per_decision / plane_count and illegal_move_rejection_rate = (shield_blocked_moves) / (total_expert_suggestions)

The key proof is that success rate is almost as high at ten planes as at five, proving the model truly scales.
