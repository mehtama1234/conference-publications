DecompGAIL: Decomposed Multi-Agent Imitation Learning for Traffic

Teaching a self-driving car to behave like real traffic by judging each agent separately from its map and neighbors, not from tangled interactions that confuse training.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When you train a self-driving car to copy real traffic behavior from video, the standard approach fails in crowded scenes. The problem: when the training system judges whether a car is behaving realistically, it gets confused because it looks at all the other cars too—and if a neighbor is doing something weird, the car you are actually training gets punished for it, even if the car itself is driving perfectly. This paper shows how to fix it by separating what is realistic about one car from what is realistic about its neighbors.

Why Multi-Agent Imitation Learning Breaks

Behavior cloning (the naive approach: just memorize what the expert drivers do) fails in traffic because every car's next move depends on where it started, not where it is now. A car that was turning left in the video might be turning left again in a new scene, even though that is wrong. This is covariate shift: the contexts change between training and real driving, so pure memorization fails.

Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) fixes this by learning a reward: a scoring function that judges whether a behavior is realistic. A policy (the car's brain) learns to act in ways that the reward scores highly. But in multi-agent traffic, the reward sees all agents at once. If one neighbor does something unrealistic, the reward penalizes the whole group—and the agent you are training gets blamed for its neighbor's mistake. This tangled feedback destabilizes training: the car's policy bounces around trying to fix things it cannot control.

The core problem: When a reward judges all agents together, a car gets penalized for its neighbors' unrealistic moves, even though the car itself is driving correctly.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decompose realism into two independent signals

The reward splits into ego-map realism (is this car realistic with the road?) and ego-neighbor realism (is this car realistic with each other car?).

Judge the car separately from each neighbor

Instead of one reward looking at all agents, use pairwise checks: does the car look real to this neighbor? Does it look real to that one?

Omit the tangled interactions

Do not score neighbor-to-neighbor or neighbor-to-map interactions; they confuse the signal and are not the car's responsibility.

Augment with social rewards

Add a soft constraint: the car wins points for being realistic but loses a little if nearby cars become unrealistic (distance-weighted).

Train with PPO

Use the decomposed rewards to train the policy with proximal policy optimization (a stable method that learns from reward signals).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces a single reward function with a decomposed reward function. A standard GAIL system uses one neural network that judges all agents at once. DecompGAIL uses two separate neural networks: one that scores ego-map interactions, one that scores ego-neighbor pairs. The policy then learns from this structured feedback instead of the tangled signal.

The hidden quantity

Interaction Misguidance: The Hidden Problem

measured quantity  =  irrelevant interaction misguidance

Irrelevant interaction misguidance is the amount of wrong feedback a policy receives because neighbors are behaving unrealistically. Imagine the policy is driving well, but a neighbor cuts it off. A standard GAIL reward sees the whole scene and says the whole group is unrealistic, so it penalizes the policy even though the policy did nothing wrong. This is misguidance: the reward is giving a false signal.

DecompGAIL measures this by checking: does the car look realistic with the map (yes/no)? Does it look realistic with each neighbor (yes/no)? If the map score is high and the neighbor score is low because one neighbor is weird, the decomposed system ignores that bad neighbor signal and trusts the car's own behavior. The paper estimates misguidance by visualizing the per-agent reward colors in the learned driving video: darker means the reward is penalizing that agent more.

Plain reading. Misguidance is blame for something you did not do. In traffic, if a neighbor runs a red light, you should not get punished for it—but a standard GAIL reward punishes the whole group.
Paper reading. The paper hypothesizes that the discriminator overuses weakly relevant neighbor-neighbor and neighbor-map interactions, which leads to training instability because these tangled signals force the policy to chase false feedback. DecompGAIL suppresses these irrelevant higher-order interactions by design, leaving only clean ego-focused signals.
Why it works

Why Decomposition Stabilizes Training

A standard GAIL reward is a black box that sees all agents and outputs one signal: realistic or not. If any neighbor behaves strangely, the reward flags the whole scene as unrealistic, even if the ego agent is driving perfectly. This gives the policy's learning algorithm a false signal, so the policy updates in the wrong direction.

Decomposed GAIL breaks the reward into two independent checks. First: does the ego car's motion match the static map context (same lane, same speed as the road allows)? This signal is clean because it only depends on the car and the map, not on neighbors—so a weird neighbor cannot poison it. Second: for each neighbor individually, does the ego car's motion make sense relative to that one neighbor (maintaining distance, not cutting it off)? These signals are also clean because each neighbor check is independent. Because the checks are independent, a weird neighbor does not poison the feedback. The policy learns from clear signals: stay realistic with the map, stay realistic with each neighbor, and you will succeed. This separates good learning signals from noise, so training converges smoothly because the learning algorithm is no longer chasing false signals.

A second mechanism: social rewards. After the decomposed discriminator scores the car, the training system adds a soft constraint—the car gains points for its own realism but loses a little if nearby cars become unrealistic because of its driving. This distance-weighted bonus encourages cooperation because a car that causes collisions or blocks traffic gets penalized, without forcing it to be responsible for neighbors that are already misbehaving far away.

Math after the picture

The Decomposed Reward Equation

First in words:

social reward for agent i equals the decomposed realism score from the discriminator, plus a bonus term that is the distance-weighted sum of other agents' realism scores.

Then the compact form:

R_i = D_ego_map(i) + D_ego_neighbor(i) + lambda * sum_j w(dist_ij) * D_ego_neighbor(j)

Every symbol:

R_ithe total reward for agent i
D_ego_map(i)the discriminator score for how realistic agent i is with the map
D_ego_neighbor(i)the discriminator score for how realistic agent i is with its neighbors
lambdahow much to weight the social bonus
sum_jadd up over all other agents j
w(dist_ij)a distance weight: closer neighbors matter more, farther ones less
D_ego_neighbor(j)the realism score of each neighbor j
A tiny worked example
Suppose agent A has ego-map score 0.9 (realistic with the road), ego-neighbor score 0.8 (realistic with neighbors).
Nearby agent B has ego-neighbor score 0.6 (unrealistic). Farther agent C has ego-neighbor score 0.7.
If lambda = 0.1, w(A-B) = 0.8 (close), w(A-C) = 0.2 (far):
Social reward = 0.9 + 0.8 + 0.1 * (0.8 * 0.6 + 0.2 * 0.7) = 1.7 + 0.1 * (0.48 + 0.14) = 1.7 + 0.062 = 1.762
Agent A is rewarded most for its own realism, slightly less for making nearby agents look good.

The social term is optional and distance-weighted, so a car is not responsible for distant or severely misbehaving agents, but it does incentivize cooperative, collision-free driving.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

A neighbor is genuinely unrealistic and the car gets stuck trying to avoid it. If one neighbor is doing something impossible (e.g., teleporting), the ego-neighbor score will never be high no matter what the ego car does. The social reward is distance-weighted, so distant agents matter less, but a close neighbor in an unrealistic state can still trap the policy. The paper addresses this by design: the social term is soft, not hard—the car is encouraged but not forced to account for bad neighbors.
The ego-map scorer is poorly trained and gives misleading signals. If the discriminator's ego-map component never sees diverse roads or weather, it may penalize realistic behaviors on unseen roads. This is a data quality issue, not a method issue, but it matters: garbage in, garbage out. The paper uses the WOMD (Waymo Open Motion Dataset), which is large and diverse, so this is mitigated.
The policy learns to be realistic with the map but callous to neighbors. If the social reward weight (lambda) is too low, the policy will drive realistically with the road but ignore collision risk. If lambda is too high, the policy over-accommodates neighbors and loses efficiency. The paper introduces a combination of PPO loss and behavior cloning loss to keep the policy grounded in expert demonstrations.
Done right. The method works by splitting a hard problem (judge all agents realistically) into two easier problems (is this agent realistic with the map, and is this agent realistic with each neighbor). Simpler signals lead to stable training.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple multi-agent traffic simulator with a few cars, a road, and some lane boundaries. Train a policy with standard multi-agent GAIL on one subset of expert driving data, and train DecompGAIL on the same data. Run both policies for 100 steps in a new scenario. Record three metrics: average training loss per step (lower is better, shows stability because smooth loss means the policy is getting consistent feedback), average collision count (lower is better because decomposed rewards reduce false blame for neighbor mistakes), and a realism score (how often the policy does things experts do in similar contexts).

stability = mean absolute change in loss per 10 steps; collisions = count of pairwise car overlaps; realism = fraction of actions matching the expert distribution conditioned on the state

The demo makes it impossible to miss the difference: standard GAIL should show wild loss swings because the policy gets tangled feedback, and poor realism because it never settles. DecompGAIL should show smooth loss because decomposed signals are clean, and high realism because the policy learns what actually matters.
