Grounding Generative Planners in Verifiable Logic

Teach LLM planners to build safe plans by having a logic tutor explain why each unsafe move is dangerous, then let the planner fix it.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When a language model plans actions for a robot in a real house, it can suggest harmful moves—like placing a hot pan where a child could grab it. Simply saying no is not enough, because the planner has no information about what would be better and will likely make the same mistake again. This paper introduces VIRF, a system where a strict logic tutor (grounded in real safety rules) talks to the language model like a teacher coaching a student, explaining each problem and letting the planner repair its own plan with that knowledge.

Zero assumption version

Language models make dangerous moves when planning for real robots

Language models (the kind that generate text one word at a time) have become good at writing out step-by-step plans. A robot reads these plans and tries to execute them. But language models are random—they guess the next word based on patterns, not formal rules. This means they can suggest moves that sound reasonable but are physically dangerous: put the pot on the edge of the counter (it falls), leave the cleaning fluid open (the child drinks it), stack heavy boxes on the fragile shelf.

Current safety systems either reject bad plans outright, telling the planner nothing about why, or ask another language model to check the first one—which is like asking a guess-machine to catch another guess-machine's mistakes. Neither teaches the planner how to think differently. The planner just tries again, makes the same mistakes, and gets rejected again.

In one sentence. VIRF makes a tutor-planner team where the tutor explains each safety rule violation and the planner learns to fix it, rather than just rejecting plans.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

LLM generates a plan

Planner writes steps as a sequence of actions.

Logic tutor checks each step

Deterministic (non-random) safety engine scans the plan against a database of real-world safety rules.

Tutor explains the violation

If a step violates a rule, tutor states which rule, why it matters, and how the planner could fix it.

Planner repairs the plan

LLM reads the feedback and rewrites only the conflicting parts, using the tutor's explanation.

Repeat until safe

Dialogue loops until all steps satisfy all rules, or planner declares the goal unreachable.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts the safety checker from a passive rejector into an active teacher. Instead of another language model that might be wrong, it uses a deterministic logic system grounded in a formal safety ontology (a structured database of real-world safety rules, hand-curated and machine-checked). Instead of just saying no, the tutor explains the causal chain: why placing a hot object near a child's reach height is unsafe, what the rule protects against, and what specific change would fix this step.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: causal feedback signal

measured quantity  =  Pedagogical feedback fidelity

Most safety systems produce a binary signal: pass or fail. VIRF measures and optimizes something deeper: how much of the real causal reason for failure does the feedback convey? A good feedback signal names the specific rule that broke, explains why that rule exists (to protect a particular person or object), and describes the minimal repair. A poor signal just says reject without explanation.

The paper operationalizes this by building a knowledge-base from real safety documents (home-safety guides, product warnings, building codes) and parsing each rule into a machine-readable form: if-condition (which states and objects matter), then consequence (what bad outcome the rule prevents), and remedy (what change to the plan fixes it). The tutor's feedback is then scored by how completely it conveys this structure to the planner.

Plain reading. The tutor gives honest, causal explanations, so the planner learns to reason about safety like a human does: it learns that certain people (the child) and situations (things within reach height) matter, so it rewrites plans to keep dangerous items away from them.
Paper reading. The feedback signal is grounded in a formal safety ontology derived from real documents, making it verifiable (not a guess) and causal (it states why the rule exists). This causal structure allows the LLM to perform abductive reasoning—when told a rule (high-heat objects must be out of reach), the planner can invert it (put the heat somewhere out of reach) by applying the rule backward to find a revision.
Why it works

Why teaching, not rejection, works

Language models learn by adjusting patterns based on feedback. If you only reject a bad plan, the planner has to guess what went wrong and try again—like throwing darts with no feedback on where they land. If you explain the rule and the cause (the child can reach this height, the pot is hot, therefore the pot cannot be at this height), the planner can apply that rule to rewrite its plan. It has learned a constraint.

The second reason is efficiency and goal satisfaction. A simple rejector keeps asking the planner to start over, which wastes time and often fails to find any plan. A tutor says: your goal was to cook a meal, but your first sequence put a child at risk. Here is the rule you broke. Now rewrite only the steps that conflict with it. The planner can then creatively repair the plan (e.g., move the cooking to when the child is asleep, or use a pot the child cannot lift) rather than throwing away all its work.

Math after the picture

The feedback-revision loop

First in words:

feedback_quality = rule_match + consequence_clarity + remedy_applicability; revision_success = plan_satisfies_all_rules AND goal_not_lost

Then the compact form:

F_t = sum(rule_j * indicator(violated_j) * feedback_quality_j) + (goal_remains_reachable ? 1 : 0)

Every symbol:

F_tSafety and success score after iteration t
rule_jThe j-th safety rule from the formal ontology
violated_jIndicator: does the current plan step violate rule j?
feedback_quality_jHow well the tutor explained rule j (rule name, why it matters, how to fix it)
goal_remains_reachableDoes the revised plan still achieve the original objective?
A tiny worked example
Draft plan: [heat pot on counter], [child reaches counter]. Violations: 1 (pot in reach of child).
Tutor feedback: Rule=keep-heat-away-from-reach, consequence=burn-risk, remedy=move-heat-to-high-shelf.
Planner revision: [heat pot on high shelf], [child reaches counter]. Violations: 0. Goal=cook? Yes, still true.
F_t = (1 rule * 0 violations * quality=0) + (goal reached? yes=1) = 1.0. Plan is safe.

The score rewards explaining rules completely and preserving the planner's original goal—not just safety for its own sake.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incomplete safety ontology. If the tutor's database of rules does not cover a real danger (e.g., a new product the database never saw), the planner will miss it because the tutor has no rule to flag it with feedback. Building the database from real documents is better than guessing, because real documents encode safety lessons learned over time; however, even real documents cannot predict every situation that did not exist when they were written.
Planner does not understand the feedback. Language models sometimes misread or misapply textual explanations. If the tutor says move-the-pot-to-the-high-shelf, but the planner revises by just saying high-shelf in the goal state without planning intermediate actions, the pot never actually moves.
Contradictory rules. If the safety database has two rules that conflict (e.g., keep-the-pot-warm and never-leave-heat-unattended), the planner may find no satisfying solution, and the process loops without converging.
Done right. When the database is curated from real documents, the planner is reliable, and rules are consistent, the tutor provides honest causal feedback that the planner can understand, leading to safe, efficient revisions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A language model plans a home task (preparing a meal, helping a child get ready). The first draft has hazardous actions—a hot pot within a child's reach. VIRF's tutor examines the plan, finds the rule violation, and explains the causal mechanism: the pot is hot, the child can reach this height, so the two states conflict. Because the planner now understands the reason (the mechanism), it can revise the plan locally—move the heat to a high shelf—while keeping the rest of the task intact. The result: zero hazardous actions, goal accomplished, in one or two feedback cycles. A bare rejector, by contrast, would force the planner to restart or abandon the goal because the planner learned nothing about how to fix the problem.

The essential proof: zero hazardous actions and goal achieved in one or two iterations, because explaining a rule's causal reason (not just saying no) lets the planner repair intelligently, whereas rejection forces restart or failure.
