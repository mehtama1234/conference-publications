Checking How Well Models Handle Multimodal Content

Papers develop tests for how models work with mixed inputs like text plus images, videos, audio, tables, and graphs — ensuring they can coordinate information across different types of data.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Real-world information is messy and mixed. A news article might come with photos. A scientific paper has text, equations, graphs, and images. A video has sound and motion and visuals. A table of data is a different kind of input than a paragraph. This subtheme is about building tests for how AI models handle information that mixes different types together—multimodal content.

The challenge is that handling mixed types requires coordination. A model has to read text, understand images, parse graphs, listen to audio, and then combine all of that to answer a question or complete a task. It is not enough for a model to be great at images and also great at text if it cannot connect them. A model might see a photo and read a caption but completely miss how they relate. It might misunderstand the scale of a graph or fail to notice that the text contradicts the visual data. Building tests for this coordination is hard because you have to measure whether a model genuinely integrated information across modes or just processed each mode separately and guessed a combined answer.

Researchers build multimodal benchmarks by gathering data that naturally mixes types. Some focus on videos with sound and visual understanding. Others test models on documents with mixed text and images, like research papers or web pages. Still others create specific tasks that require coordination: "based on this table and this description, what is the answer?" or "watch this video and this happens; describe the next second." The goal is to measure whether models can truly work with mixed information, which is essential as AI moves toward handling real-world tasks.

How the work breaks down

This subtheme splits into five directions: testing whether models understand video and motion correctly, checking if models can work with mixed text and images in structured data, evaluating whether models truly integrate audio with visual information, assessing model behavior in specialized domains like medicine and chemistry, and measuring cross-modal reasoning where models must connect different types of information to reach an answer. These directions diverge because each requires different kinds of coordination and each reveals different failure modes.

Video Understanding and Physics

Videos are hard to test because they involve motion, physics, and time all at once. A model has to watch frames unfold, understand what will happen next, and detect when objects move in ways that violate real-world physics. Simply looking good to the eye is not enough—does the model actually capture how gravity works, how objects collide, how momentum carries through? Building tests that measure this is essential because many real applications depend on physical correctness.

The PhyWorldBench paper tests whether top text-to-video models actually produce videos that obey physics laws. Across 1,050 prompts, it found that the best-performing models frequently violate basic physics—objects fall upward, water defies gravity, people walk through walls. This reveals that models can generate visually plausible video without understanding the underlying physical laws. The Very Big Video Reasoning Suite takes a different angle: it measures whether models can reason about video content and generalize to tasks they have not seen before. When researchers scale up the dataset, they start to see signs that models learn to apply reasoning in new situations, suggesting that bigger and more diverse video benchmarks may push toward real understanding. DrivingGen benchmarks video generation specifically for autonomous driving, finding a tough trade-off: general models produce visually polished video but break physics and driving rules, while driving-specific models capture motion realistically but look less smooth. Together these papers show that video benchmarking must measure physics, reasoning, and task-specific accuracy—not just visual appeal.

Text, Tables, and Structured Data

Tables, databases, and structured data present a unique multimodal challenge. A table is not a paragraph—it has rows and columns, headings, relationships between cells. A model must read both the layout and the semantic meaning. Some tasks require turning a table into SQL, others mean asking questions that blend table data with natural language descriptions. The hard part is that models may parse the symbols correctly without understanding what they mean, or understand words while missing the structure entirely.

The table QA papers approach this from different angles. Same Content, Different Representations compares SQL systems (which excel at querying structured data precisely) against language models (which handle messy questions flexibly but often miss details) and hybrid approaches (which try to get both strengths). The study found consistent trade-offs: SQL is exact but inflexible, LLMs adapt but lose precision, and hybrids work best when the schema is well-defined. The M4PQA paper shows that most current models underperform when tables mix with multi-page documents and questions need cross-page reasoning. It introduces ExTrActor, which helps smaller models use tools like database lookups to match larger models' performance—because intermediate steps give models a way to delegate hard parts, which means smaller models can reach the performance of much larger ones by following better instruction. TopBench focuses on implicit reasoning over tables, revealing that models struggle to recognize what questioners actually want when the intent is hidden. It shows that intent recognition is the real bottleneck, which means even perfect data lookup fails if models misunderstand the goal. These three papers converge on one insight: handling multimodal table-based data requires understanding structure and intent, not just pattern matching.

Vision in Specialized and Complex Scenes

When models see images or video, the challenge deepens if the content is complex, specialized, or involves understanding people. Human scenes have subtle spatial relationships, temporal sequences, facial expressions, and body language. Specialized visual content like circuit boards, medical images, or historical documents require domain knowledge on top of visual perception. Multimodal dialogue where text and images flow back and forth adds another layer. The question becomes: can models truly see and reason about visual complexity, or do they pattern-match and hope?

HumanPCR evaluated 30+ top models on human-centric visual understanding and found major gaps. Models struggle with spatial perception—where is a person in the frame?—and temporal understanding—what happened in sequence? They also fail at mind modeling: understanding what a person might be thinking or feeling. AVTrack adds audio to the human-centric challenge, creating audio-visual tracking in complex scenes. Simply having audio and video separately is not enough; the benchmark exposes that existing methods degrade sharply when the environment is noisy or dynamic. The dialogue paper, multimodal dialogue from text to image, shows that current frameworks cannot replicate human-like back-and-forth interaction with mixed media. The XModBench paper probes whether models consistently understand the same concept across different modalities—when they see a spatial arrangement described in text and then shown in an image, do both perceptions align? Even top models like Gemini show 20+ point gaps between modalities and fail at spatial reasoning below 60% accuracy. These papers collectively show that human-centric scenes, audio-visual coordination, and cross-modal consistency are open frontiers.

Medical, Domain, and Quality Assurance

Medical, scientific, and specialized professional domains demand both general language skill and deep domain knowledge. A model might excel at general reading but miss clinical nuance. It might handle English perfectly but fail on Arabic medical questions. It might sound fluent while giving unsafe advice. Testing in specialized domains reveals whether models truly understand or just mimic patterns they have seen in training data. Quality assurance benchmarks probe whether models are trustworthy in deployment—free from hallucinations, bias, and privacy leaks.

CounselBench evaluated large language models on mental health questions, asking licensed experts to grade the responses. The models scored well on paper but showed recurring problems: feedback was unconstructive, advice was dangerously overgeneralized, and some gave medical guidance without appropriate caution—a major safety issue. MedAraBench expanded evaluation to Arabic medical questions across 19 specialties and five difficulty levels, addressing a gap in non-English benchmarking. This revealed that many models trained primarily on English medical data perform poorly on Arabic medical reasoning. ChemEval tested chemistry understanding across mainstream general models and specialized chemistry models. The finding: general models understand chemical literature but lack deep chemistry intuition, while chemistry-tuned models excelled at domain questions but fumbled at general language. PCB-Bench benchmarked models on printed circuit board design and placement, requiring both visual interpretation of schematics and spatial reasoning about engineering constraints. Models fell short at spatial reasoning and following domain-specific rules. TrustGen evaluated 39 generative models on trustworthiness across multiple dimensions. Even the top-performing models showed significant gaps in resisting hallucinations, ensuring fairness, and protecting privacy. Together, these papers show that domain evaluation and safety assessment must be specific: a model that passes general tests may fail in specialized contexts where errors have real consequences.

Image Generation with Reasoning and Knowledge

Text-to-image generation is a multimodal bridge: a model reads text instructions and produces images. But simple text matching is not enough. A model must reason about spatial layout, understand what objects should look like, apply real-world knowledge about how things are colored or sized, and decide whether generated images actually match the intent. Testing this requires going beyond standard metrics and measuring whether generation truly respects reasoning, maintains consistency across diversity, and embeds correct knowledge.

AtelierEval benchmarked models on how well they prompt text-to-image systems, testing both human prompters and multi-modal language models. It found that successful prompting relies more on imitation (copying successful patterns) than planning. The evaluation achieved strong correlation with expert human judges. CoDA investigated dataset distillation using text-to-image models, showing that without target-specific training, it achieves performance equal to or better than methods built specifically for a target dataset, which means general image models have learned patterns that transfer across domains. GenExam created a multidisciplinary exam that asks models to generate images matching rigorous criteria across science, art, and reasoning. It revealed a large gap between open-source and closed-source models, exposing that integrated understanding and reasoning remain major weaknesses in image generation. GIR-Bench (Generating Images with Reasoning) found that unified models capable of both reasoning and generation consistently underperform on reasoning-driven image tasks compared to reasoning-only systems, highlighting a core tension—trying to do everything sometimes means doing nothing well. WISE (World Knowledge-Informed Semantic Evaluation) tested whether models can ground generated images in real-world knowledge. When asked to generate an image matching real-world facts—like the actual appearance of a species or the layout of a city—20 models (both specialized and general) showed significant limitations. They failed to integrate factual knowledge into generation because models rely on visual patterns they have memorized rather than facts they understand. Across all five papers, the message is consistent: generating images on command requires reasoning about semantics, spatial layout, and world knowledge, not just pattern completion.
