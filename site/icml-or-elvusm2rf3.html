Certain Head, Uncertain Tail: Expert-Sample for Test-Time Scaling

When a model picks experts at test time, high-confidence choices give stable answers while uncertain choices give diversity—so keep one and vary the other.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models (LLMs) often generate better answers when they try multiple times and pick the best one, because hard problems have rare right answers that a single attempt might miss. But each extra attempt costs tokens. A fine-grained mixture-of-experts (a model architecture with hundreds of smaller expert modules) offers a clever way: instead of re-running the full model, just ask different experts to handle the same input, and combine their answers. This paper discovers that some experts are always chosen reliably, while others are picked only sometimes. Expert-Sample keeps the reliable picks fixed but varies the uncertain ones—giving you both stable core reasoning (the certain experts always apply their patterns correctly) and diverse creative attempts (different tail experts explore alternative solutions), without slow temperature tuning.

Zero assumption version

Test-time sampling trades off stability against creativity

When a language model answers a hard question, one try is often not enough. If you ask it to generate multiple candidate answers and pick the best one, you get much better results, because you see more attempts and raise the chance that at least one attempt lands on the rare right answer. But the naive approach—re-running the entire model multiple times with randomness turned up—is slow and wastes tokens.

Models with a mixture-of-experts architecture are different: they contain hundreds of small expert modules, each one trained on different kinds of patterns. On each token, a router (a small decision network) picks which experts should handle this moment. So instead of re-running the whole model, you could ask different experts to activate for the same input, generating different outputs from that single pass. But here is the catch: when you try to get diverse answers by varying which experts activate, the model's reasoning falls apart, because you are forcing the same input through unpredictable expert selections, so the core logic (the sequence of reliable transformations) gets interrupted on each attempt. The core logic breaks because you are mixing in unreliable choices.

The trade-off problem in one sentence. Test-time sampling needs either stable answers (low temperature, few tries) or diverse attempts (high temperature, many tries), but not both—until now.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Read the model's router scores.

For each token, the router outputs a list of confidence scores for each expert.

Rank experts from most to least confident.

Sort the scores in descending order.

Classify into two groups.

Experts with high scores go into the certain head; the rest form the uncertain tail.

Keep certain, resample uncertain.

Activate the top experts deterministically; sample the tail experts multiple times.

Combine outputs into diverse answers.

Each resample gives a different tail selection, generating a new candidate output.

What the paper changes

What changes: which experts to activate at test time

Normally, the router picks one set of experts per token, greedily (always choosing the highest-confidence candidates). Expert-Sample changes this decision: instead of picking all experts uniformly at random or all deterministically, it now makes TWO different decisions. For the certain head (experts with high scores), the choice remains deterministic and fixed across all attempts. For the uncertain tail (experts with low scores), each attempt samples a different expert.

the object it moves  =  which experts to turn on per token
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: router score confidence pattern

measured quantity  =  certainty in expert rankings

Most language models route tokens to experts by scoring each expert and picking the top few. But the distribution of scores is not uniform. This paper discovers an informative pattern: router scores have a clear break—a few experts with very high scores (the certain head), then a sharp drop into many experts with low, noisy scores (the uncertain tail). This pattern is the hidden quantity the paper measures and exploits.

The paper estimates this pattern by collecting router scores from a trained model on a variety of inputs and examining the distribution. The core finding is that the head (high-confidence experts) predicts correct reasoning reliably, while the tail (low-confidence, uncertain experts) contributes to diverse outputs without affecting the core logic.

Why it works

Why keeping certain and varying uncertain works

Here is the core mechanism. In a mixture-of-experts model, each expert learns a different skill. Some experts learn the core reasoning patterns (logic, retrieval, arithmetic)—these are chosen reliably by the router. Other experts learn rare, specialized behaviors—these are chosen inconsistently. When you sample from all experts uniformly, you randomize the core reasoning too, causing the model to forget its logic on each attempt, because interrupting the reliable pattern-matching sequence breaks the chain of reasoning. Instead, Expert-Sample locks in the core (certain head) so the model always reasons the same way (because the same high-confidence experts activate each time, applying their learned patterns consistently), then explores variations only in the specialized (uncertain tail). The certain experts provide the backbone; the uncertain experts add flavors. By keeping the backbone fixed and predictable, the model can still think correctly while the tail explores alternative paths.

Because the router scores already reflect which experts are trustworthy, Expert-Sample exploits this signal directly. No retraining needed. You run the model once, look at the scores, and instantly split the decision. On the first run through the uncertain tail, pick some set of experts; on the second run, pick a different set. Each gives a different output because different experts are activated—they apply different learned patterns to the same input—but the core computation stays the same because the certain head is unchanged—so the model produces stable-yet-varied answers.

Math after the picture

The routing decision formula

First in words:

For each attempt, recount the score of each expert. If the expert is in the certain head (top k highest-scoring experts), always activate it. If the expert is in the uncertain tail, activate it with probability equal to its normalized score.

Then the compact form:

activate_certain_i = 1 for i in top_k_experts; activate_uncertain_i ~ Bernoulli(score_i / sum_tail_scores) for i in tail_experts

Every symbol:

activate_certain_ialways choose expert i if it is in the top k
activate_uncertain_irandomly choose expert i based on its score
top_k_expertsthe k experts with the highest router scores
tail_expertsall remaining experts below the head
score_ithe router confidence score for expert i
sum_tail_scoresthe sum of all scores in the tail
A tiny worked example
Router scores: expert-1 = 8.5, expert-2 = 8.0, expert-3 = 1.2, expert-4 = 0.9, expert-5 = 0.4.
Set k = 2 (top 2 = the certain head). Head = experts 1,2. Tail = experts 3,4,5.
Activation rule: always use experts 1 and 2 (no variation). For tail, use their scores to decide: sum_tail_scores = 1.2 + 0.9 + 0.4 = 2.5.
Attempt 1: expert-3 with prob 1.2/2.5 = 48%, expert-4 with prob 0.9/2.5 = 36%, expert-5 with prob 0.4/2.5 = 16%. Might draw experts 1,2,3.
Attempt 2: same probabilities. Might draw experts 1,2,4.
Result: experts 1,2 are identical across attempts (stable core); experts 3,4,5 vary (diverse tail). Same input, different tails, so different final answers.

The certain head always participates, ensuring the model's core reasoning stays the same across all test-time attempts, while the uncertain tail is resampled to inject controlled diversity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Setting k too small (too few in the certain head). If you lock in too few experts, the core reasoning is starved of specialized knowledge. The model outputs break or become repetitive. The certain head must include all experts that handle core logic.
Setting k too large (too many in the certain head). If all experts are certain, the tail is empty. No diversity is injected, so you get the same output every attempt—defeating the purpose of test-time scaling.
Router scores that do not reflect true expert importance. If the router was poorly trained or the model does not activate experts based on their actual value, the score distribution will not have a clean head-tail pattern. Expert-Sample relies on the router already being well-trained.
Done right: measure the router score distribution and pick k to match the natural gap. Plot a histogram of router scores per token; identify the elbow (the sharp drop-off from high to low scores). Set k to be the number of experts before the elbow. This is training-free and works immediately.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a fine-grained mixture-of-experts model (or reuse a trained one). On a math or reasoning task, generate 32 candidate answers for each question using Expert-Sample: (1) do one forward pass per question, (2) read the router scores for each token, (3) identify the certain head and uncertain tail by finding the natural gap in the score distribution, (4) for each of 32 resamples, activate the certain head deterministically and sample the uncertain tail, (5) collect all 32 outputs, (6) compute pass@32 (percentage of questions where at least one of the 32 attempts is correct) and Best-of-N verification accuracy (pick the highest-confidence answer via a verifier model).

pass@32 = (number of questions with at least one correct answer) / (total questions); verification_accuracy = (number of verified correct answers) / (total questions)

The demo must show that pass@32 improves by at least 10-20 percentage points over greedy single attempts—because sampling different tail experts gives the model multiple ways to approach the problem, raising the chance at least one attempt reaches the rare right answer—AND that the core model outputs stay stable (experts in the certain head are activated on every attempt, so the backbone reasoning chain holds intact).
