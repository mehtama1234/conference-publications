The Label Horizon Paradox: Rethinking Supervision Targets in Financial Forecasting

Train a forecaster on an automatically chosen intermediate time horizon when that horizon carries a cleaner learning signal than the final trading target.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Forecasting papers usually assume the training label should exactly match the number used at deployment. In finance, that means if the final goal is a twenty-day return, training should also regress directly on the twenty-day return. This paper argues that the best training target can sit at a different horizon. A shorter or intermediate label may expose useful price movement before later noise accumulates, so the model learns a cleaner rule because it is trained on a label with more signal tied to today's features and less later contamination.

Zero assumption version

The final horizon is not always the best teacher

A financial forecaster maps today's features into a future outcome, such as the return over a chosen holding period. The common rule is simple: if you want a prediction for horizon H at test time, train on labels measured at horizon H. That sounds natural, but it blends two effects that move in opposite directions over time.

As the horizon gets longer, more of the underlying economic signal has time to play out. At the same time, more unrelated shocks, market microstructure effects, and changing conditions accumulate. The final target can therefore be informative in principle but noisy as a training label. A model trained directly on that label may spend much of its capacity fitting noise rather than persistent structure.

The paradox. The target you care about at prediction time can be a worse teaching signal than a different horizon that reveals the same underlying force with less accumulated noise.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the supervision target. Instead of fixing the training label to the deployment horizon, it treats horizon selection as a learnable choice. The final model is still judged on the desired forecast horizon, but the training signal can come from a different time point if that proxy exposes more useful structure.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: usable signal after noise accumulation

measured quantity  =  usable signal after noise accumulation

The core quantity is not the raw return at a horizon but how much of that label is learnable from today's features. A longer horizon may contain stronger eventual movement, yet be harder to predict because unrelated variation has piled up by the time the label is measured.

The paper frames this as a dynamic trade-off between signal realization and noise accumulation. Early horizons may be too soon, so the relevant economic force has not yet expressed itself. Late horizons may be too late, so the force is buried under additional randomness. The best supervision target lies where realized signal is high enough and accumulated noise is still controlled.

Why it works

Why a proxy horizon can beat the final horizon

Supervised learning does not directly optimize the market. It optimizes the chosen labels. If the labels are noisy, the learned rule is noisy. In finance this matters more than in many static prediction settings because the future label is the end result of many partially independent events that happen after the prediction is made.

Suppose the final target is a long-horizon return. Training on that target asks the model to explain not only the persistent price effect tied to today's features, but also all later variation that enters before the horizon closes. A shorter proxy horizon can be better because it captures the early part of the same effect before much of the later unrelated movement arrives. The learned representation then carries a clearer version of the predictive structure, which later transfers to the final task.

The bi-level optimization matters because the best proxy horizon is data dependent. An outer objective checks which proxy horizon best supports the final forecast, while an inner training loop learns the model under the current proxy choice. Doing both together removes the need to run many separate training jobs for many possible label horizons.

inputMarket features and candidate future labels at many horizons

stepEvaluate how that training helps the final horizon by scoring the real deployment target

Math after the picture

The equation: choose labels that optimize the final target indirectly

First in words:

Find the proxy horizon that gives the trained model the lowest final-horizon loss after the model has been fitted on labels from that proxy horizon.

Then the compact form:

best_proxy_horizon = argmin_over_h final_loss( model_trained_on_labels_at_h )

Every symbol:

ha candidate supervision horizon used to define training labels
model_trained_on_labels_at_hthe forecasting model after inner-loop training with labels measured at horizon h
final_lossthe evaluation error on the deployment horizon you actually care about
best_proxy_horizonthe training horizon that gives the best final-horizon generalization
A tiny worked example
A desk wants predictions for day-20 return.
Training on day-20 labels gives noisy supervision because many extra shocks enter before day 20.
Training on day-8 labels may reveal the same directional effect earlier, so the model learns a cleaner rule.
If that day-8-trained model scores best on day-20 validation, the method selects day 8 as the proxy label horizon.

The paper uses bi-level optimization so this search happens inside one training run rather than by launching a separate model for every candidate horizon.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Proxy horizon learns a different phenomenon. A shorter horizon can be cleaner but irrelevant. If it reflects short-term trading noise instead of the intended longer-horizon effect, the final forecast degrades.
Outer validation overfits the market period. If horizon selection is driven by a narrow historical regime, the chosen proxy may reflect one market episode rather than a stable forecasting principle.
Candidate horizons are too coarse. If the method tests only a few widely spaced horizons, it may miss the real sweet spot where signal has emerged but later noise is still limited.
Done right. The method is most persuasive when the proxy horizon preserves the same economic mechanism as the final target, validation covers multiple market conditions, and the horizon grid is fine enough to capture the best trade-off.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic return process with two components: a slow predictive effect tied to current features and an independent later noise term that grows with horizon. Generate labels for many future horizons. Then train one model directly on the final horizon and another model with a proxy-horizon search that chooses the training label by final-horizon validation.

The demo should plot validation error versus horizon. The curve should first improve because the predictive effect has had time to appear, then worsen because extra unrelated noise has accumulated at later horizons. The selected proxy horizon should lie near the bottom of that curve, and the resulting model should beat direct-label training on the final-horizon test set.

final_horizon_gain = test_score(proxy_horizon_training) - test_score(direct_final_horizon_training)

A convincing result shows that the winning supervision horizon is not necessarily the deployment horizon because prediction quality depends on the label's learnable signal, not only on semantic matching.
