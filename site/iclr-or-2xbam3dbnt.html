EnvSocial-Diff: Crowd Simulation with Environment and Group Interaction

A crowd simulation model that treats pedestrians as drawn by both obstacles and other people, using noise reversal to generate realistic paths.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Crowd simulation is the task of predicting where people will walk in a scene. Most models focus on how people avoid each other, but ignore the walls, doors, and attractions around them. This paper adds two missing pieces: explicit representation of the physical scene (where the walls are, what people are attracted to), and a way to model both one-on-one interactions and the pull of group behavior. The result is more realistic predictions.

Zero assumption version

Pedestrian paths look wrong because models ignore the scene

Real people navigate by two forces: staying away from each other, and moving toward things they want (a door, a window, an attraction). They also follow the crowd when crowds form.

Most existing crowd models focus only on the first force (social avoidance). A naive approach to adding environment would treat obstacles as hard constraints and attractions as fixed points — but this produces stiff, unrealistic paths that ignore how people navigate uncertainty and preference.

The problem: a model trained only on social force misses that some areas have no people because there are walls there, or that a crowd clusters at a doorway because it leads somewhere. The simulated crowds look like they are moving randomly, not purposefully.

The challenge. Include obstacles and attractions in the scene representation so they shape where people actually walk, while also modeling both individual avoidance and group conformity, all in one generative model.

What it actually does

Add scene context

What the paper changes

The thing being changed

The model changes the trajectory predictions (where each pedestrian is expected to go) by incorporating two new signals: a structured encoding of the physical environment (map, obstacles, lighting, points of interest) and a multi-level interaction graph that captures both pairwise avoidance and group conformity.

the object it moves  =  trajectory predictions
The hidden quantity

The noise-reversal schedule

measured quantity  =  Denoising step influence

A diffusion model works by learning to reverse noise: given a noisy version of a trajectory, it predicts a cleaner version one step at a time. At each step, the model must decide how much to move toward the scene constraints (respect the walls) and how much to move toward social constraints (avoid nearby people). The hidden quantity is the learned influence score at each reversal step — how strongly the model relies on environment versus social context to make that particular nudge.

The paper estimates this by examining the model's internal attention weights during the diffusion reversal process — which parts of the scene encoding and which neighbors the model is 'looking at' when it predicts each step. Higher attention on environment means obstacles are guiding that step; higher attention on the group means conformity is dominant at that moment.

Why it works

Why it works: three-layer steering

Old models learned social force as a black box. This paper makes three explicit steering signals: environment (walls block movement), objects (doorways and attractions pull toward them), and groups (people in a cluster adjust their direction to match the cluster's heading).

The diffusion reversal process is the key. Instead of computing a single target trajectory, the model predicts a small nudge at each step. Because each nudge is informed by all three signals (scene, one-on-one avoidance, group direction) at once, the final trajectory naturally balances them. A person approaching a doorway gets pulled forward by the doorway signal, pushed away from nearby pedestrians, and nudged to align with the crowd's direction if one is nearby.

This works because the model learns, during training, when to trust which signal. Early in reversal (noisy state), the broad scene signals dominate because the trajectory is still far from final. Late in reversal (nearly clean), fine-grained social signals take over because the person must make precise movements around others.

Math after the picture

The denoising equation

First in words:

At step t, predict the trajectory by summing three weighted contributions: the previous noisy trajectory, a signal pulled from the scene (obstacles and attractions encoded as tokens), and a signal from neighbors and group identity (computed via a graph).

Then the compact form:

x_t = alpha_t * x_noisy + (1 - alpha_t) * (scene_encoder(S) + social_graph(G_i))

Every symbol:

x_tthe predicted cleaner trajectory at step t
x_noisythe trajectory at the previous (noisier) step
alpha_ta scheduling weight: high at start (trust noise), low at end (trust predictions)
scene_encoder(S)learned features from the map S (walls, doors, lighting)
social_graph(G_i)learned features from neighbors and group of person i (computed via graph neural network)
A tiny worked example
Scenario: person at (50, 50), step 6 of 10 (halfway through denoising).
Noisy trajectory suggests: move to (55, 55).
Scene encoder says: door at (70, 40) is attractive; wall at (40, 50) is repulsive.
Social graph says: nearest neighbors are at (52, 48) and (53, 49); move to (56, 54) to avoid them; group is moving at angle 45 degrees.
Alpha at step 6: 0.5 (equal weight to noise and predictions).
Blend: x_6 = 0.5 * (55, 55) + 0.5 * ((65, 45) from scene + (56, 54) from social) = 0.5 * (55, 55) + 0.5 * (60.5, 49.5) = (57.75, 52.25).
Result: person moves toward the door (65 in x) while avoiding neighbors and respecting the wall.

The scheduling parameter alpha_t shifts control from noise (early steps) to learned predictions (late steps), letting the model balance global scene structure with local social detail.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor scene encoding. If the map representation does not correctly capture the semantic meaning of obstacles versus attractions (e.g., treats a wall the same as a doorway), the denoising process will produce paths that ignore the distinction.
Weak group detection. If the graph layer does not correctly identify which pedestrians form a group, or if it overweights group conformity, solitary walkers will be pulled off their natural path.
Mismatch between training and deployment scenes. If the model is trained on scenes with certain obstacle types (buildings, fences) but deployed on unfamiliar types (scaffolding, dense vegetation), it may not generalize.
Done right. When both scene and social representations are learned from diverse training data and validated on held-out crowd footage, the model produces paths that look natural: they smoothly avoid obstacles, are drawn toward attractions, and follow group norms.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple crowd simulator on a toy scene (a room with two doorways, some obstacles, and a cluster of people). Initialize 50 pedestrians randomly and run EnvSocial-Diff to predict their paths over 10 seconds. Compare the output to three baselines: (1) a model that ignores the scene and just does social avoidance, (2) a model that treats scene as hard constraints (no one walks into walls) but ignores attractions, (3) a model that ignores groups entirely. Measure: what fraction of predicted people correctly move toward the nearest exit? How many collisions occur? How much do people in clusters move together?

Exit-finding accuracy = (pedestrians reaching exit in simulation) / (total pedestrians * number of exits). Collision rate = (predicted position overlaps) / (total pairs * timesteps). Group coherence = average heading angle variance within detected clusters.

A good result shows that explicit scene encoding and group modeling are not just 'nice to have' — they fundamentally change which paths the model learns are realistic.
