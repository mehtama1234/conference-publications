DC-LA: Difference-of-Convex Langevin Algorithm

To sample from a hard probability law with a smooth term and a non-smooth penalty, DC-LA smooths the penalty in two pieces and then runs a Langevin sampler on the reshaped objective.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Many inverse problems ask for samples, not just one best answer. In computed tomography, for example, many reconstructions may fit the measurements nearly equally well, so we want a probability law (the full spread of plausible answers and their probabilities) rather than one point estimate. The difficulty is that the objective can contain a smooth data-fit term plus a penalty built as one convex part minus another convex part. That structure is useful for modeling, but it creates sharp corners and non-convex regions that make standard samplers unstable. DC-LA handles this by smoothing the two penalty pieces separately, moving the concave piece into the smooth side, and then applying a proximal Langevin update to the reshaped problem.

Zero assumption version

Sampling gets hard when the penalty has corners and negative curvature

Suppose we want samples from a target law (the probability rule we want to draw from) whose energy has two parts. The first part rewards agreement with the observed data and is smooth, so small changes in the candidate answer produce predictable changes in score. The second part is a penalty that can encode structure such as sparsity or shape constraints, but it is written as one convex function minus another convex function. That makes the full energy non-convex and often non-smooth.

Standard Langevin methods rely on gradients, which means local slopes, to push samples toward high-probability regions while injecting noise. Sharp corners break those gradients, and strong non-convexity can make the sampler wander or settle in the wrong place. The paper's question is how to keep the useful difference-of-convex structure without giving up convergence guarantees.

The core problem. The model needs a sampler that can handle both non-smooth penalties and non-convex shape at the same time.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Split the penalty

Write the penalty as a convex part minus another convex part.

Smooth each side separately

Apply a Moreau envelope, which means a smooth approximation, to each convex piece instead of to the whole difference at once.

Redistribute curvature

Move the smoothed concave contribution into the smooth data-fit term so its gradient can be handled directly.

Run proximal Langevin

Take a noisy gradient step on the smooth part and a proximal step on the remaining convex part.

Track approximation error

Bound the effect of time discretization and smoothing so the sampler stays close to the original target law.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes where smoothing is applied. Instead of smoothing the whole difficult objective in one block, it smooths the two convex pieces separately and then reassigns the concave piece to the part handled by gradients.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: distance to the true target law

measured quantity  =  How far the sampler's output law is from the desired one

A sampler can look reasonable in plots and still be wrong. The paper therefore tracks a distance between two laws, one produced by the algorithm and one defined by the original model. This distance asks whether the sampler puts mass in the right places, not just whether a few averages look similar.

That distance matters because DC-LA introduces two deliberate approximations. One comes from turning a continuous process into discrete steps. The other comes from smoothing the penalty. Either approximation could distort the target if handled carelessly. The analysis shows how both errors enter the final gap, so the user can shrink them by choosing smaller step sizes or gentler smoothing.

Why it works

Why it works: smooth only what needs smoothing

The key move is to respect the internal structure of the penalty. A difference-of-convex penalty is not arbitrary non-convexity. It already tells us which part is convex and which part creates the concave pull. By smoothing the two convex pieces separately, DC-LA removes sharp corners without erasing that structure.

The redistribution step matters because the sampler handles two kinds of operations well: gradient moves for smooth terms and proximal moves for convex non-smooth terms. After smoothing, the concave contribution can be folded into the smooth side, where its gradient is available. The remaining convex part stays in the proximal side, where exact or stable updates are still possible. That split matches the algorithm to the math instead of forcing one tool to do every job.

The convergence proof matters because non-convex samplers often work only as heuristics. Here the paper shows that if the energy eventually pushes samples back toward a bounded region, then the combination of smoothing and discretization keeps the output law close to the true target law. The result is not exact equality, because smoothing changes the problem slightly, but the error terms are explicit and controllable.

Math after the picture

The equation: split update for one sampler step

First in words:

One step combines a gradient move on the smoothed smooth side, random noise for exploration, and a proximal correction for the remaining convex side.

Then the compact form:

next_sample = proximal_of_smoothed_r1(current_sample - step_size * gradient_of_smoothed_part + noise)

Every symbol:

next_samplethe new draw after one update
current_samplethe current draw before the update
step_sizehow far the algorithm moves each step
gradient_of_smoothed_partthe slope of the smooth data-fit side after the concave piece is added
noiserandom perturbation that keeps the chain exploring
proximal_of_smoothed_r1the correction that handles the remaining convex penalty term
A tiny worked example
Current sample is 2.0.
Gradient of the smoothed side at that point is 0.6, and the step size is 0.5.
The gradient move gives 2.0 - 0.5 times 0.6 = 1.7 before noise.
Suppose the noise draw is 0.1, so the temporary point is 1.8.
The proximal correction for the convex penalty pulls that to 1.6, which becomes the next sample.

The exact formulas in the paper are more detailed, but the structural point is the split between a smooth noisy move and a convex correction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too much smoothing changes the target. If the smoothing level is too large, the sampler may become easy to run but end up tracking a softened problem rather than the intended one.
Step sizes can still be unstable. Even with the split design, a large step size can add discretization bias or numerical drift because the sampler is still a finite-step approximation of a continuous process.
The structure must really be difference-of-convex. The method depends on a useful convex-minus-convex decomposition. If that decomposition is poor, the smooth side and proximal side no longer reflect the real shape of the problem.
Done right. Use a faithful decomposition, choose moderate smoothing, shrink the step size until the approximation gap is controlled, and validate the resulting sample law on known test cases.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a one-dimensional inverse problem with a smooth data-fit term and a penalty written as one convex function minus another. Compare three samplers: plain Langevin, a sampler that smooths everything at once, and DC-LA. Plot sample histograms against a high-resolution reference law and vary the smoothing level and step size. The demo should show that DC-LA stays closer to the reference because it preserves the split structure while removing the corners that break plain updates.
