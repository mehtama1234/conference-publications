Classical Chinese Jailbreak Prompts via Bio-Inspired Search

A classical Chinese prompt optimizer uses fruit fly foraging behavior to automate the discovery of jailbreak attacks that bypass LLM safety guards.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models have safety filters that stop them from giving dangerous advice or generating harmful content. But these filters can be tricked. This paper discovers that classical Chinese—an ancient, compressed language—can partially hide harmful requests from these safety systems. The paper then builds an automated system that finds the best combination of classical Chinese prompt instructions by copying how fruit flies search for food: a population of candidate prompts smells out promising directions, looks around visually, then mutates slightly to explore new areas of the search space.

Zero assumption version

Safety filters fail against obscure language

Modern LLMs come with safety filters—trained detectors that block harmful requests. A naive attempt to jailbreak (simply asking for dangerous content in English) gets caught. But the filters are trained mainly on English text, so they have blind spots in other languages, especially in rare, compressed languages like classical Chinese.

The problem: classical Chinese is concise (a few characters pack complex meaning) and ancient (safety datasets rarely include it). So a harmful request hidden in classical Chinese can slip past the filter, because the filter was not taught to recognize danger in that language. The safety system assumes it is reading normal input, when in fact a human who reads classical Chinese would recognize a request for hacking instructions or malware.

In one sentence. CC-BOS automatically builds classical Chinese jailbreak prompts by exploring millions of combinations of instruction words, tone, context, and role-play scenarios, guided by fruit fly foraging logic.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the prompt into eight dimensions

Role (who you are), Behavior (what you do), Mechanism (how it works), Metaphor (the analogy), Expression (the phrasing), Knowledge (what you claim to know), Trigger pattern (the hook), Context (the frame).

Start with a random population of prompts

Each prompt is a random combination of eight words/phrases from a classical Chinese bank.

Smell search: estimate the quality of the swarm

The best-scoring prompt in the population is detected; its position guides where to search next.

Visual search and Cauchy mutation

Each prompt mutates slightly (one or two dimensions shift), and toxicity scoring measures whether the model outputs harmful text.

Repeat until convergence

After many generations, the swarm has converged on classical Chinese prompts that reliably trigger unsafe model behavior.

What the paper changes

The thing the paper changes

The paper optimizes the jailbreak prompt itself. Instead of hand-crafting one classical Chinese request, it treats the prompt as a vector of eight text choices (role, behavior, mechanism, metaphor, expression, knowledge, trigger pattern, context) and automatically searches over millions of combinations to find the ones that fool the safety filter most reliably.

the object it moves  =  The jailbreak prompt
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Attack Success Rate

measured quantity  =  Attack Success Rate (ASR)

Attack Success Rate measures whether a candidate prompt actually makes the model output harmful content. It is a fraction from 0 to 100%: for a given prompt, submit it to the LLM multiple times, count how many times the model generates a response flagged as harmful, and divide by the total number of attempts. A prompt with 100% ASR always fools the model; one with 0% ASR never does.

The paper also measures the average toxicity score of the outputs—a separate numeric rating of how harmful each response is, not just yes/no. The optimization uses both signals: the paper searches for prompts that maximize ASR and toxicity together.

Why it works

Why the method works: fruit fly foraging in prompt space

The core idea is to use fruit fly optimization, a bio-inspired algorithm that mimics how fruit flies find food. In nature, fruit flies use smell to detect food from far away, then look around visually, then adjust their position slightly. Applied to prompts, the algorithm works like this: a population of candidate prompts explores an eight-dimensional space (the eight instruction dimensions). The prompt with the highest toxicity score acts like the smell signal—it shows the swarm where the "food" (harmful outputs) is located. All other prompts move toward it, mutate slightly (Cauchy mutation adds random noise to one or two dimensions), and then get re-scored.

Why does this work? Because each of the eight dimensions (role, behavior, mechanism, metaphor, expression, knowledge, trigger, context) controls a different aspect of how the model interprets the request. By searching over combinations, the algorithm finds interactions—for example, a certain role paired with a certain trigger pattern might work much better together than either alone, because they reinforce each other. The fruit fly algorithm is population-based (not greedy), so it avoids getting stuck in dead-end prompts; it explores multiple promising areas in parallel.

Classical Chinese itself plays a dual role: it provides the raw material for the prompts (words and phrases in an ancient, compressed language), and its obscurity is what makes the jailbreak work—the safety filter has fewer training examples in classical Chinese, so it misses the harmful intent.

Math after the picture

The search objective and fitness score

First in words:

average_toxicity_score = (sum of toxicity ratings for all responses) / (number of trials). The optimization objective is to maximize both average_toxicity_score and attack_success_rate simultaneously.

Then the compact form:

fitness(prompt) = ASR(prompt) * avg_toxicity(prompt)

Every symbol:

ASR(prompt)Attack Success Rate: fraction of trials where model output is harmful (0 to 1)
avg_toxicity(prompt)Average toxicity score of all responses (1 to 5, where 5 is most harmful)
fitness(prompt)The combined score the fruit fly swarm uses to navigate (higher is better)
A tiny worked example
Candidate prompt A: ASR = 0.8 (80% success), avg_toxicity = 3.5 -> fitness = 0.8 * 3.5 = 2.8
Candidate prompt B: ASR = 0.6 (60% success), avg_toxicity = 4.2 -> fitness = 0.6 * 4.2 = 2.52
Candidate prompt C: ASR = 0.95 (95% success), avg_toxicity = 3.8 -> fitness = 0.95 * 3.8 = 3.61
Prompt C has the highest fitness, so the swarm moves toward it and mutates variants near it.

The fruit fly algorithm searches for the prompt (combination of eight text fields) that maximizes this fitness score.

What can go wrong

Noise in toxicity scoring. The toxicity scorer (used to guide the swarm) is itself an LLM or classifier, so it makes mistakes. If a toxic response is mislabeled as safe, the algorithm will follow a false signal and waste search effort on a dead-end prompt.
Classical Chinese requires translation for evaluation. The responses from the LLM are in classical Chinese, but human evaluators and safety systems work in English. The paper includes a translation module to convert responses back to English, but this introduces a new source of error: mistranslation can hide toxicity or create false positives.
Transfer breaks across model architectures. A jailbreak optimized on GPT-4o might fail on Claude because the models have different internal safety mechanisms. The paper shows the method has cross-model transferability (92% average transfer), but it is not perfect; some optimization is needed per target model.
Defense: safety training in classical Chinese. As this paper makes public, future LLMs will include classical Chinese text in their safety training. Once models learn to reject harmful requests in classical Chinese, the obscurity advantage disappears, and the jailbreak becomes less effective.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

The demo should generate a malicious prompt by running the fruit fly optimization algorithm on a small classical Chinese prompt bank: eight dimensions with three choices per dimension, for a total of about 6500 possible combinations. Start the population at random; run 20 generations of smell search, visual search, and mutation. Measure the attack success rate before and after optimization on a target LLM. The goal is to show that attack success rate rises dramatically—from near zero to above 80 percent on at least one model—because the automated search finds effective jailbreak combinations that a human would not have crafted by hand.
