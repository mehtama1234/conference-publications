Classical Chinese Jailbreak Optimization

The paper uses obscure classical Chinese combined with bio-inspired search to automatically find prompts that make LLMs ignore their safety guardrails.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models are built with safety rules meant to refuse harmful requests. But researchers found that classical Chinese—a terse, old language most models see rarely during training—can partly slip past those rules because the model has weaker learned resistance to it. This paper automates the hunt for such bypass prompts using a search strategy inspired by fruit flies foraging.

Zero assumption version

Safety rules are uneven across languages

Modern large language models have safety filters: when you ask them to explain how to make a bomb or write code to steal credit cards, they say no. These filters work because during training the model learned what kinds of phrases and patterns tend to precede harmful requests—and it learned to recognize and refuse them.

But the model did not see every language equally. English and other common languages appear in the training data billions of times. Classical Chinese, an ancient formal language spoken by almost nobody today, appears only rarely. A beginner might think the model is equally protected in all languages—wrong. Because the model has seen classical Chinese so seldom, it has weaker learned caution about it. The safety signal is thinner.

So a simple classical Chinese rephrase of a harmful request can slip through, even when the English version would be refused. The obscurity that makes the language hard for humans to read is the exact same obscurity that blinds the model's learned defenses.

In one sentence. CC-BOS automatically hunts for classical Chinese prompts that jailbreak a model by testing many candidates and refining them step by step.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode into eight dimensions

Break the prompt into eight changeable parts: the role the user takes, the behavior they want, the reasoning to use, the metaphors to hide the meaning, word choice, background knowledge, the trigger pattern, and surrounding context.

Generate random variants

Create many candidate prompts by varying each of the eight dimensions, producing diverse classical Chinese versions.

Test each candidate

Send each variant to the model and check whether it bypasses the safety guardrail (the model agrees to the harmful request).

Keep the best and mutate

Discard prompts that failed. Make small changes to the successful ones, inspired by how a fruit fly population converges on a food source.

Repeat until strong

Repeat the test-refine loop until finding a prompt so effective it nearly always jailbreaks the model in black-box testing.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the model itself or its weights. Instead, it changes the prompt—the exact wording the user sends. It searches for the specific sequence of classical Chinese words that most reliably makes a language model ignore its safety constraints. The eight-dimensional encoding is the innovation: rather than trying random prompts, it systematically varies eight different aspects of the prompt at once.

the object it moves  =  the input prompt
The hidden quantity

The success rate: what fraction of tries jailbreak

measured quantity  =  jailbreak success rate

The core hidden quantity is how often a prompt actually works—what fraction of times the model agrees to the harmful request when given that prompt. A weak prompt might work 10% of the time, a random guess. A well-optimized prompt might work 80% of the time. The paper measures this by sending each candidate prompt to the black-box model many times and counting successes.

Because the model is black-box (the attacker cannot see inside), they cannot measure the model's internal confidence or decision boundaries directly. They can only observe: did it refuse, or did it comply? The success rate is purely a measurement of observable behavior—how reliably the prompt bypasses the guard.

Why it works

Why the search finds strong jailbreaks

The mechanism has three linked parts. First: classical Chinese is obscure to the model because it saw so little of it during training. The model's learned safety filter was shaped by billions of English examples of harmful patterns—it has no such learned wariness for rare languages. So classical Chinese rephrasings evade the filter not by being clever, but by being outside the model's learned experience.

Second: testing many random variants is slow. The paper uses fruit fly optimization, a bio-inspired search algorithm that works like a population of insects homing in on a smell. Instead of trying prompts one at a time, it maintains a population of candidate prompts, tests them all in parallel, keeps the best-performing ones, and breeds new variants from them by making small mutations. This is much faster than random search because it learns which dimensions matter most.

Third: the eight-dimensional encoding captures the natural knobs a human attacker would twist—the role, the forbidden behavior, the justification, the metaphor, the words, the knowledge frame, the trigger, the context. By encoding the prompt into these eight independent levers, the algorithm can explore a much richer space of classical Chinese variations than if it tried to mutate the raw text. Each dimension can be adjusted independently, so the population converges faster on high-success prompts.

Fitness and refinement

First in words:

success_rate(prompt) = count(jailbreak successes) / total_queries; new_prompt = mutate(best_prompt, random_dimension) with probability proportional to parent_success_rate

Then the compact form:

f(p) = S(p) / Q; p_new ~ sample(best_k, mutate(.), sigma)

Every symbol:

f(p)fitness score of prompt p (how often it jailbreaks)
S(p)number of times prompt p successfully jailbreaked
Qtotal number of queries sent to the model
p_newa new candidate prompt created by mutation
best_kthe k prompts with highest success rate
mutate(.)change one or more of the eight dimensions slightly
sigmastep size controlling how far mutations jump
A tiny worked example
Suppose we test Prompt A (classical Chinese version) on the model 100 times. It jailbreaks 35 times. Success rate = 35 / 100 = 0.35 (35%).
We test Prompt B 100 times. It jailbreaks 72 times. Success rate = 72 / 100 = 0.72 (72%). Prompt B is better.
The algorithm keeps Prompt B and creates Prompt C by changing the role dimension slightly. This new variant is tested again.
If Prompt C succeeds 80 times out of 100, it becomes the new center of the population, and the algorithm continues breeding from it.

The algorithm is not hunting for a single ideal prompt; it is hunting for whatever sequence of classical Chinese words most often bypasses the model's guard.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Model learns the language faster than we optimize. If the model gets updated or retrained with classical Chinese safety examples, the advantage of obscurity disappears. The jailbreak prompt stops working.
Fruit fly search gets stuck in a local best. The algorithm might converge on a prompt that is good but not the globally best one. Tweaking the mutation rate or population size can help, but there is no guarantee.
Model detects the attack style and adds a blanket filter. If a provider realizes that classical Chinese prompts are being weaponized, they can add a special filter for that language or for the signature eight-dimensional encoding pattern.
Done right. The attack works as long as the model remains undertrained in the target language and the search algorithm can explore the prompt space efficiently. Both conditions hold in practice for modern models and rare languages.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A concrete demo should show: (1) Take a harmful request that the model refuses in English. (2) Translate the same request into classical Chinese by hand. (3) Show that the model agrees to the classical Chinese version much more often. (4) Then run the fruit fly optimizer on a set of eight editable classical Chinese prompts, showing that each iteration finds a variant with a higher jailbreak success rate than the last. (5) After 10 iterations, show that the optimized prompt jailbreaks the model at, say, 70% success rate, while the original English request had 0% success rate. The demo measures the improvement in success rate as the algorithm converges.

jailbreak_rate_improved = (success_rate_final - success_rate_baseline) / success_rate_baseline * 100%

The demo must make it impossible to miss that classical Chinese obscurity is real, and that an automated search algorithm can efficiently exploit it to find even stronger jailbreaks.
