SALT: Frozen Teachers for Video Learning

Use a frozen teacher instead of a constantly-updating one to learn video representations more efficiently and transparently.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

Video models need to understand what is happening in videos without being told the answer. The standard trick is to hide some patches (regions) and train the model to guess what is hidden using a teacher model that slowly updates itself. This paper shows you can freeze the teacher instead: train it once to predict pixels, then freeze it, and train the student to predict what the frozen teacher sees. This is simpler because the training loss tells you if the model is working, faster because teacher and student can now be scaled independently, and works better because there are no moving targets confusing the student's learning.

Zero assumption version

Why video models struggle to learn without labels

A video model needs to learn what objects and actions look like without someone telling it the answers (the ground truth). The old way: hide some video patches and have a slowly-updating teacher tell the student what is hidden. The teacher is not frozen; it constantly learns too, which means the target keeps shifting.

This creates two problems. First, picking which model to save is hard: the training loss during learning does not tell you if the model will be good at the real task. Second, the teacher and student are tightly tangled together, so you cannot use a tiny teacher with a big student. This wastes compute.

The stuck point. You train a teacher that keeps changing. The student chases a moving target. Picking the best model is guesswork. Training is wasteful.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Teach the teacher

Train one encoder to predict raw pixel values of hidden video patches using simple reconstruction loss. Train it for N steps, then freeze it permanently.

Stage 2: Student learns the features

Train a student encoder to predict what the frozen teacher sees (in feature space, not pixels), only on hidden patches. A mathematical stop-sign prevents update signals from flowing backward to the teacher.

Loss is now transparent

The student's training loss is a real distance metric that correlates with downstream task accuracy. No complex momentum math. You know if it is working.

Decouple and scale

Teacher and student are fully separate. You can use a tiny teacher (40k steps) with a large student (80k steps) without any coupling penalty.

Freeze at the end

The frozen student becomes your video feature extractor. It works as well as the old momentum method because the two-stage design (pixel reconstruction then feature matching) achieves the same learning goals without the complexity; it is faster because teacher and student are no longer coupled, so you pay zero overhead for training the teacher; it is easier to understand because the loss is a real distance metric that tells you if learning is working.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The teacher mechanism switches from exponential moving average (EMA) update — where the teacher slowly shifts at every training step — to a static freeze — the teacher is trained once and never changes again. This single change ripples through: loss becomes interpretable, model selection becomes reliable, and teacher-student coupling vanishes.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Student loss correlation

measured quantity  =  Loss-to-accuracy correlation (R-squared)

In the old EMA-based methods, the loss the model sees during training does not tell you whether the model will actually work well on the real task. It is noise. This paper discovers a hidden quantity: how strongly the student's training loss predicts downstream task accuracy. With a frozen teacher, this correlation becomes very strong (measured as R-squared, which is 0 when random, 1 when perfect).

The paper measures this by training many student models, recording their loss during training, then testing them on real video tasks and computing the correlation. With SALT, the R-squared is 0.984, meaning the training loss is almost a perfect signal of how good the model will be. This is huge: it means you can pick the best model just by watching the loss.

Why it works

Why freezing the teacher makes everything better

When the teacher updates at every step (EMA), the student is always chasing a moving target. Imagine teaching someone to throw a ball at a hoop, but the hoop keeps sliding. Every correction the student makes might be wrong by next step because the hoop moved. The student's loss does not mean much; the model might be great but just unlucky this step.

When you freeze the teacher, the target becomes stable and honest. The student learns a real skill: predicting a fixed thing. The training loss now measures real progress. Because the student is no longer trying to match a constantly-shifting teacher, the loss is clean. You can look at it and know the model is learning well.

Second, the frozen teacher untangles teacher and student. In the old method, if you want a bigger student, you need a proportionally bigger teacher to keep up. They are coupled. With a frozen teacher, you train it once (even tiny) and then train students of any size on it. A small teacher handles the hard pixel-reconstruction task (a simple job). The large student focuses on learning distinctive patterns in video (the complex job). Each specializes. This is like hiring a small specialist to teach a large team: the small specialist does not get tired and does not scale with team size.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

Student loss equals the average distance between student's prediction and frozen teacher's output on masked regions, with no gradient flowing back to the teacher.

Then the compact form:

Loss = E[||student_prediction - stop_gradient(frozen_teacher_output)||_1]

Every symbol:

student_predictionfeatures the student encoder computed for hidden patches
frozen_teacher_outputfeatures the frozen teacher computed for the same patches
stop_gradienta mathematical wall: backprop stops here, teacher does not update
||...||_1the distance (sum of absolute differences between each feature)
E[...]the average across all video patches and all training batches
A tiny worked example
Teacher was trained to output [0.9, 0.1, 0.8] for a hidden patch.
Student predicts [0.8, 0.2, 0.9] for the same patch.
Distance: |0.9-0.8| + |0.1-0.2| + |0.8-0.9| = 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.3
Frozen teacher: teacher stays [0.9, 0.1, 0.8] forever. Student learns to predict it exactly.
EMA teacher: teacher might drift to [0.88, 0.12, 0.79] on the next step. Student's loss suddenly changes even though it learned nothing new.

The frozen teacher is a stable ground truth. The student learns to match it. No moving targets.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Teacher trained on wrong task. If Stage 1 trains the teacher poorly (e.g., wrong masking pattern, bad data, too few steps), the frozen teacher becomes a bad reference. The student can only learn to mimic a bad teacher.
Teacher trained for too few steps. If the teacher barely trains (say, 5 steps), it has not learned much. The student will predict something useless. But this is easy to catch: the training loss will be huge and the model selection metric will scream. The paper shows that with enough teacher training (40+ steps), even modest teacher quality works.
Frozen teacher does not cover the space. If the teacher only learned to predict patches of one video type, a student trained on different video types will see patches the teacher never modeled. Predictions will be garbage. Stay in the same domain.
Done right. Train the teacher thoroughly on pixel reconstruction (Stage 1). Monitor the training loss in Stage 2. Pick the student when the loss plateaus. The frozen teacher does the job.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny video representation learning pipeline: download a small video dataset (e.g., Kinetics subset or home videos), implement the two-stage process (Stage 1 trains a teacher to reconstruct masked pixels, Stage 2 trains a student to predict the frozen teacher's features on masked patches), and show that the student's training loss tracks downstream accuracy on a small action-recognition task.

Correlation = Pearson(training_loss_over_steps, final_downstream_accuracy)

The demo should make it impossible to miss: as the training loss drops, the student's accuracy on real video tasks climbs in lockstep. The loss is honest. No moving goalposts.
