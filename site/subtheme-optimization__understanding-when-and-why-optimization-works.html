Understanding when and why optimization works

Gradient descent and neural network training have surprising behaviors. What are the mathematical principles and limits? Why does noise sometimes help? When do algorithms get stuck?

What this is

Gradient descent is the workhorse of machine learning: you compute how wrong your model is (the loss), you compute the direction that reduces wrongness (the gradient), and you step in that direction. It has worked for decades, but it should not work because the theory says it would get stuck. The space of possible model settings has billions of dimensions, with countless local minima (dead ends where many directions go uphill) and saddle points (places where the ground slopes both up and down). If you start at a random place and follow a greedy downhill direction, you should get trapped in an early dead end, yet in practice, gradient descent finds settings that work well. This is a mystery. Why does a simple algorithm succeed in a space that theory says should trap it?

A second puzzle is noise. When you compute gradients on a batch of 32 examples (a tiny fraction of a 1-million-example dataset), the gradient is random—it changes every time you pick a new batch. This noise could slow learning because you are taking random directions, but instead it seems to help, which is surprising. Models trained with noisy batch gradients often generalize better than models trained with accurate full-dataset gradients, which happens because noise pushes the algorithm toward flatter regions of the solution space. Adding noise to the update step, or using momentum, or other tricks seem to point the algorithm toward flatter minima, and flat minima generalize well to new data because they are less sensitive to slight changes. But why does this mechanism work? And when does this break?

Papers study these questions from many angles. Some prove that in certain settings, all critical points (places where the gradient is zero) that are not global minima are unstable saddle points, so gradient descent skips over them. Others show that batch noise acts like a regularizer, biasing the algorithm toward solutions that work well on examples it has not seen. Some researchers analyze what properties of the data and model architecture guarantee success. Others study implicit bias: even without explicit penalties for complexity, gradient descent naturally learns simple patterns first. A few papers show failure cases: settings where the theory does not apply and gradient descent gets stuck. The pattern is that optimization works not by magic, but by mathematical principles that researchers are slowly uncovering.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into five directions. First, understanding why flat minima let algorithms generalize better—because flatness makes the model stable when data differs slightly between training and test. Second, analyzing how noise and batch size implicitly push algorithms toward flatter regions by creating hidden complexity penalties. Third, developing theory for adaptive optimizers that adjust step sizes differently for each weight, which accelerates learning by recognizing that different weights have different smoothness properties. Fourth, discovering failure modes where the downhill algorithm spins into chaos at large step sizes, revealing hidden phase transitions. Fifth, applying flatness insights to make models work well when data shifts between training and test, which is possible because flat regions change less when the data changes.

Why flat minima generalize well

A key puzzle is that the downhill search finds solutions that work well on new data the model has never seen. Flatness—how much the loss changes when you move slightly away from the solution—seems related to this generalization mystery. Flat minima insulate against data shift because if the loss barely changes at your solution, then small differences between training and test data won't cause the model to break, so the learned weights remain effective. Papers in this group prove precisely why flatness helps, which means demonstrating the mathematical connection between flatness and generalization, and study optimization methods that hunt for flat minima.

The implicit bias paper on Adam proves that Adam naturally maximizes a margin called L-infinity margin, which means the optimizer steers the algorithm toward solutions where changes to the data cause the least change to the model's output. This is one mechanism explaining why Adam works, because insensitivity to data changes is what enables generalization. The sharpness-aware minimization paper on calibration shows that flatter minima lead to better calibration, meaning the model's confidence matches its actual correctness—a distinct advantage beyond accuracy because miscalibrated confidence breaks downstream decisions. The Bayesian Langevin paper connects flatness to a probability distribution over solutions: sampling from this distribution gives predictions with built-in uncertainty estimates, which links flatness to generalization because averaging multiple nearby solutions reduces overfitting to noise. The paper on feature discovery reveals that in shallow networks, one SAM variant learns in a specific sequence: minor features first, then major features. This sequential structure suggests that sharpness metrics not only pick good solutions but also control the order in which features are learned, which affects generalization because early features often capture signal while late features overfit noise.

Noise and batch size as hidden complexity penalties

When you train on a batch of 32 examples from a million-example dataset, your step direction is noisy because you are averaging loss from a random sample. This noise could harm learning because random directions should slow progress, yet models trained this way often work better than with exact steps—a paradox. These papers explain how noise and batch size act as hidden complexity penalties, pushing the algorithm toward simpler, more general solutions because the noise keeps the algorithm from overfitting to any single batch. The mechanism depends on the learning rate and batch size interacting in surprising ways, which means their combined effect differs from what you would predict from each alone.

The model merging paper proves that noisy steps from small batches create an effective complexity penalty (determined by the learning rate, batch size, and weight decay combined). This unified measure predicts when two separately trained models can be safely merged—a practical application of understanding noise's hidden effects. The conflicting biases paper shows that learning rate actually interpolates between two extremes: with a tiny learning rate, the algorithm minimizes the sum of squared weights (simplicity via weight smallness), but with a large learning rate, it hunts for flatness. These two goals conflict, yet a single learning rate smoothly trades between them. The federated learning paper studies the same phenomenon when training is distributed: noisy updates from different machines create flatness pressure, and the paper corrects a geometric bias to keep this flatness signal consistent across machines. The momentum paper reveals an intricate interaction: small batch sizes amplify momentum's effect on flatness, while large batches recover momentum's classical role in acceleration. This shows the algorithm does not have a fixed behavior—the same update rule works differently depending on batch size.

Adaptive optimizer convergence and problem shape

Adaptive optimizers like Adam and Adagrad adjust the learning rate for each weight independently based on past changes in that weight's direction of descent. They work well in practice, but theory lags behind: the standard tools for analyzing downhill search do not directly apply. These papers prove convergence guarantees by recognizing that how fast an optimizer learns depends on the shape of the loss surface in different directions—a property called smoothness that varies by weight.

The adaptive smoothness paper proves that a measure of surface curvature (how much the loss surface curves in each direction) precisely characterizes when adaptive optimizers converge and enables faster convergence than standard downhill search in certain regimes, which happens because adaptive methods can move faster in directions where the surface is smoother. Smoothness varies by weight direction, so optimizers that adjust per weight should converge faster because they can detect and exploit these per-direction differences. The convergence paper on monotone variational inequalities (a class including nonconvex neural network training) proves Adagrad reaches a specific convergence rate without needing to know how many steps you will run, which is an advantage because you do not need to tune this number in advance and the algorithm automatically stops improving at the right point. The heavy-tailed noise paper analyzes AdaGrad when step-direction estimates have huge outliers—a reality with limited data. It shows AdaGrad adapts to how extreme the noise is by speeding up in directions where the step estimates are reliable, and proves this adaptation may not be achievable by any algorithm in general, meaning AdaGrad is already close to optimal for heavy-tailed noise so trying to beat it would be hard. The non-Euclidean descent paper studies downhill methods using different distance measures—like L-infinity distance instead of ordinary Euclidean distance—to respect the problem's shape (the way coordinates scale relative to each other), which enables faster convergence when the problem has natural coordinates. It shows these methods operate at the edge of stability, meaning they walk a tightrope between convergence and chaos because the different distance measure amplifies small perturbations, and provides diagnostics to detect this edge in practice.

Chaos and instability at large step sizes

Downhill search requires choosing a step size: how far to move in the downhill direction each update. Too large a step and the algorithm can bounce around wildly because it overshoots; too small and it crawls. These papers study the boundary between stable and unstable behavior, revealing surprising phenomena: chaos emerges because nonlinearity creates unstable feedback, boundaries become fractal due to the interplay of different failure modes, and methods can hallucinate solutions that do not exist.

The chaos paper shows that near the critical step size where stability breaks, the algorithm enters a chaotic regime where adding weight penalties (to discourage large weights) actually amplifies sensitivity to starting point and creates fractal boundaries in the space of convergence regions. This happens because the penalty interacts with the chaos to create finer and finer structure. This means on one side of a tiny threshold the algorithm works, on the other it does not, with a jagged fractal boundary between them. The SAM hallucination paper exposes a failure of sharpness-aware minimization: when the radius (how far to look around the current solution) grows too large, SAM optimizes toward solutions that do not actually reduce loss—it hallucinates minima. This occurs because the search radius grows so large that the approximate flatter region no longer contains a real solution. The paper provides a fix that reduces sensitivity to this hyperparameter, showing the method can be made more reliable by avoiding false minima.

Flatness for generalization across data shifts

A practical goal is to train a model on one dataset and have it work well on different data from the same domain—a task called domain generalization. Flatness helps here too because a flat minimum is less sensitive to shifts between training and test data, meaning the model's weights remain effective when the data distribution changes. These papers apply flatness-seeking algorithms to domain generalization and show consistent improvements across many scenarios including noisy labels and small datasets, which proves the flatness principle works in practice.

The Align-SAM paper improves sharpness-aware minimization by seeking solutions that are flat with respect to shifts across different subsets of the training data. This multi-view flatness helps the model treat different data distributions consistently because a flat region remains valid across multiple perspectives. This improves generalization on held-out data because the model is less brittle to unseen variations. The federated learning paper on flatness (FedScar) corrects a bias that arises when training is distributed across many machines: each machine's noisy updates create different flatness pressure, which causes the machines to disagree about which regions are flat, and the paper aligns this to ensure consistent complexity penalties across machines so training converges reliably. The dual adaptive SAM paper for domain generalization adds domain-level guidance, so the algorithm not only seeks flat regions but does so in directions that align across domains, which maximizes the shared flatness that helps all domains. The logits-SAM paper applies flatness directly to preference optimization (training language models from human preferences), showing that penalizing the logits—the raw scores before converting to probabilities—boosts the method's effectiveness on multiple LLM benchmarks, which works because the logit space is where the model's confidence lives.
