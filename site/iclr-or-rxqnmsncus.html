MMDuet2: Knowing When to Speak in Video Conversations

A video conversation model learns to decide on its own when to speak and when to stay quiet, without needing humans to mark every reply moment.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Most video chatbots wait for you to ask a question before they answer. But in a real conversation, sometimes you speak up without being asked. MMDuet2 learns to do this: watching a video and a conversation, it decides all by itself whether now is a good time to jump in with a comment. The trick is that it trains itself on when to speak using a reward signal, not by having humans mark exactly when each reply should happen.

Zero assumption version

Why Chatbots Stay Silent

Most video chatbots only talk when you ask them a question. If you are watching a video together and the model sees something funny or important happening, it says nothing. It waits for your turn.

The naive fix is simple: tell the model a rule, like 'speak up if you see a dog.' But rules break fast. Sometimes a dog should trigger a comment; other times it should not. Teaching a model to decide this requires humans to label thousands of video clips with exact timestamps of when a reply makes sense—a job so tedious that datasets stay small and the model never learns to time its responses well.

The problem in one sentence. Proactive video models need to know when to speak during playback, but labeling exact reply times for training is too expensive.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Watch and listen

At each moment, the model reads the current video frame, the conversation history, and its own past replies.

Make a choice

It asks itself: should I speak now or stay silent? This is a binary decision at each time step.

Generate a reply

If it decides to speak, it generates text that comments on the video and the conversation.

Get rewarded or penalized

Reward depends on timing: early replies lose points, late replies lose points, timely ones gain points.

Learn from many attempts

After many conversations, the model builds an internal sense of what moment types invite speaking.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The core change is the decision itself. Instead of always waiting for the user to speak, the model now owns a timing decision at each frame: speak or stay silent.

the object it moves  =  response-timing decision
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Response Timeliness

measured quantity  =  Response Timeliness Score

Timeliness is a number that measures how well-timed a response is. If the video shows something funny and the model speaks immediately, timeliness is high. If the model waits 5 seconds after the funny moment passes, timeliness is low.

The paper estimates timeliness using a reward function (a scoring rule) that gives points for speaking near the right moment in the video and loses points for speaking too early or too late. The model never sees a human's label of the 'correct' time; instead, it learns to predict timeliness from patterns in the dialogue and video.

Why it works

Why learning without precise timestamps works

The trick is that the model does not need a human to mark the exact frame where a reply should happen. Instead, it trains on many full conversations. Each conversation gets a score based on how natural and well-timed all the replies felt. If the model's responses flow with the video and dialogue, it gets a high score. If they feel random or late, the score is low.

Over time, the model learns that certain patterns (a sudden laugh in the dialogue, a visual shift in the video) often precede moments where jumping in makes sense. It does not memorize a database of 'correct times'; it builds intuition. This is much cheaper to collect than labeling thousands of precise timestamps because one full conversation provides feedback on all replies at once, whereas timestamp annotation requires a human to pause and mark every single moment separately, so the training set can be bigger and more diverse.

The learning method is multi-turn reinforcement learning: the model practices having conversations, gets a reward score at the end of each conversation based on timing quality, and gradually adjusts its decision rules to maximize that score. This teaches timing without the bottleneck of precise human annotations.

The reward equation

First in words:

Total conversation reward equals the sum of individual reply rewards, where each reply gets points for good timing and dialogue fit and loses points for poor timing or context mismatch.

Then the compact form:

R_total = sum_t [w_timing * timing_score_t + w_coherence * dialogue_fit_t - penalty_t]

Every symbol:

R_totalthe total score for the whole conversation
timing_score_thow well-timed the model's reply is at moment t (0 to 1)
dialogue_fit_thow well the reply matches the conversation context (0 to 1)
w_timingweight: how much we care about timing
w_coherenceweight: how much we care about dialogue fit
penalty_tloss for speaking at the wrong time or in the wrong context
A tiny worked example
Imagine a 3-reply conversation. w_timing = 10, w_coherence = 5.
Reply 1: timing_score = 0.8 (good), dialogue_fit = 0.9, penalty = 0.5. Reward = 10*0.8 + 5*0.9 - 0.5 = 8 + 4.5 - 0.5 = 12.0
Reply 2: timing_score = 0.2 (late), dialogue_fit = 0.7, penalty = 2.0. Reward = 10*0.2 + 5*0.7 - 2.0 = 2 + 3.5 - 2.0 = 3.5
Reply 3: timing_score = 0.9 (nearly perfect), dialogue_fit = 0.95, penalty = 0.1. Reward = 10*0.9 + 5*0.95 - 0.1 = 9 + 4.75 - 0.1 = 13.65
Total for conversation = 12.0 + 3.5 + 13.65 = 29.15. The model learns that early and timely replies earn higher scores, so it adjusts to speak more at good moments and less at bad ones.

The model learns to maximize this reward by treating timing as a learnable decision, not a fixed rule.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Training-data bias. If all training videos have people speaking frequently, the model learns to speak too much. If the dataset skews toward certain topics or video types, the model will time replies well on those but fail on others.
Reward hacking. If the reward function does not penalize repetition or off-topic replies, the model might learn to say the same thing over and over at high-reward moments, even if it does not make sense.
Delayed feedback. The model only learns how well a reply timed after the full conversation ends. If the conversation is very long, it takes many cycles to correct timing errors in early replies.
Done right. To succeed, the reward function must score both timing accuracy and content quality because timing alone will teach the model to speak but not what to say, the training dataset must be diverse so the model sees many contexts and generalizes beyond training videos, and the RL algorithm must allow the model to update its timing strategy incrementally as it learns so corrections in early dialogue do not erase what worked before.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small proactive video chatbot on a test video (e.g., a cooking video or sports highlight). Let it watch the video and have a multi-turn conversation with a human. Measure two things: (1) how many times it speaks at moments that feel natural to a human evaluator (timing correctness), and (2) how coherent its replies are (do they match what is on screen). Train one model with a naive fixed threshold for speaking and another with MMDuet2's multi-turn RL. The demo must make it impossible to miss that the RL-trained model speaks at more natural moments.

timing_accuracy = (natural_replies / total_replies) * 100, coherence = (on_topic_replies / total_replies) * 100

The RL model should hit 75%+ timing accuracy while maintaining 80%+ coherence; the baseline should fail on at least one dimension.
