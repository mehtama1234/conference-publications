The First Impression Problem: Internal Bias Triggers Overthinking

A reasoning model's first gut answer gets stuck in its attention and drives pointless repetition.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Reasoning models like o1 spend a lot of compute on thinking, but sometimes they just retrace the same ground over and over — overthinking with no progress. This paper shows that overthinking has a real cause: the moment the model reads a problem, it instantly forms a preliminary guess (an internal bias) that it never fully forgets, even as it tries to reason through step by step. When that first guess and the reasoning later disagree, the model keeps circling back to the original problem statement instead of trusting its own work, wasting the output (printed words) on redundant reflection.

Why reasoning models repeat themselves

Reasoning models are given a hard problem and then allowed to think step by step before answering. You might expect: think step 1, think step 2, think step 3, answer. Direct reasoning is more efficient because each new step builds on the previous one instead of rehashing. But in practice, these models often spiral into overthinking — they generate correct answers early on, then spend hundreds of extra words re-examining the same problem over and over, spinning their wheels while nothing changes.

A beginner would guess that overthinking means the model is just unsure. It finished the problem, but it doubts itself, so it reasons again. If that were the only thing happening, you could cure it with confidence boosting. But the paper reveals something much more specific: the root cause is that the model forms an instant preliminary guess the moment it reads the problem — a first impression — and this guess acts like a persistent hook, constantly pulling the model's focus (where it looks backward through its memory) back to the original problem even after it has done careful reasoning.

The method. Remove the question from the model's view after it generates its first answer, and watch overthinking disappear. This proves the question itself is the culprit.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper removes the input question from the model's view the instant the model generates its first answer, and allows reasoning to continue without access to that original problem statement. This single change — cutting off the question but keeping all the model's own prior reasoning — shrinks the total output because the model no longer re-reads the problem and re-activates its first guess. Removing just this one thing directly confirms that the question itself is the driver of overthinking, not other sources of doubt.

the object it moves  =  Access to the input question
The hidden quantity

The focus-to-question ratio

measured quantity  =  How much the model focuses on the input question during reflection

The model reads the input question early and keeps a representation of it in memory. The question is just data — word-vectors (chunks of numbers representing words) stored earlier in the sequence. When the model reaches a reflection point (a moment where it decides whether to keep reasoning), it can look backward through all its prior words. The paper measures how many of its focus-weights (which prior words it cares about most) point back to the original question versus back to its own previous reasoning steps. A high ratio means the model is stuck on the problem statement; a low ratio means it trusts its own work.

The paper estimates this by computing average focus-scores at reflection moments, grouping all question words together and all reasoning words together, then measuring which group gets more focus. It excludes the focus sink (an artifact where deep neural networks dump extra focus to the first few words) to keep the signal clean. Then it layers this across the middle-to-later reasoning stages, where the model actively decides whether to continue.

Why it works

Why removing the question cures overthinking

When the model forms its first impression (a quick preliminary answer), that answer gets encoded into focus patterns and memory states. The input question stays available in memory, so when the model reaches moments of doubt, it can look back at the question and re-activate that first impression. Re-reading the problem triggers the same fast, shallow reasoning pathway that produced the initial guess. If the model's careful reasoning has moved to a different conclusion, the conflict between the two triggers excessive reflection — the model re-reads, re-reasons, and circles back.

Removing the question blocks that re-trigger loop. The model can still access all of its own prior reasoning, so it can keep working; but it cannot re-read the problem and re-trigger the fast preliminary guess. Without that competing signal, the model's reasoning proceeds in one direction instead of spiraling. The reduction in output is a direct measure of how much of the overthinking was driven by this question-focus loop. The paper also directly manipulates this by injecting false preliminary answers into the model's early reasoning, and shows that bigger, more confident false answers cause more overthinking — further proof that the internal bias, not confusion, is the root cause.

Measuring overthinking reduction

First in words:

Reduction ratio equals (original length minus question-removed length) divided by (original length minus first-answer position). This normalizes by the overthinking budget — the output words spent after the first answer was complete.

Then the compact form:

r = (L_ori - L_rem) / (L_ori - P_first)

Every symbol:

L_oritotal output length in the original setting (with question visible)
L_remtotal output length after question is removed from the prompt
P_firstthe token position where the model first generates a complete answer
rthe reduction ratio: what fraction of post-answer tokens were wasted overthinking
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model's output is 200 tokens total, and it finishes its first correct answer at token 50.
Overthinking budget = 200 - 50 = 150 tokens (all post-answer tokens).
When we remove the question, the same model produces 120 tokens total.
Tokens saved = 200 - 120 = 80 tokens.
Reduction ratio r = 80 / 150 = 0.53, meaning roughly half of the overthinking tokens vanished.
This is r as a decimal; the paper reports similar magnitudes across reasoning tasks.

This ratio tells us directly: of all the extra tokens the model spent after it already had an answer, what fraction were due to the question-loop problem specifically.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Model needs the question for later steps. Some problems may require the model to refer back to the question to check constraints or details. The paper does not fully address whether the model can solve all problems this way, only complex reasoning tasks like counting, multi-step logic puzzles, and competition math. Problems requiring frequent re-reading of the problem statement may not improve, because the model will be forced to rely on only its early understanding of the task without being able to double-check.
Timing of removal matters. The intervention removes the question exactly when the model first generates an answer. If the model's answer generation is ambiguous or happens over many tokens, the method may not cleanly identify the cutoff, leading to partial question removal or mistimed intervention.
Does not cure other overthinking sources. The paper demonstrates that question-attention is a key driver of overthinking, but may not be the only driver. Models may overthink for other reasons — uncertainty from training, reward misalignment, or inherent redundancy in reasoning. Removing the question cuts one loop but does not address the full spectrum.
Bias injection confirms causality. The paper strengthens its causal claim by injecting false preliminary answers and watching overthinking increase proportionally. This rules out mere correlation and supports the idea that the internal bias itself is the trigger, not just a side effect.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple reasoning task: a math problem or a multi-step logic puzzle that a small reasoning model can solve (e.g., Qwen-14B or a smaller o1-distilled model). Run the model twice: once with the problem statement visible throughout, and once where you programmatically remove the problem statement from the prompt after the model generates its first answer (identify the first occurrence of a complete answer, then truncate everything up to and including it, then append any reasoning that came after). Track the total output length, the token count of post-answer reasoning, and whether the model repeats any solutions or reasoning steps. The metric that matters is the overthinking reduction ratio: (L_original - L_removed) / (L_original - P_first), which should be positive (removed setting is shorter). A confident demo shows that the model's accuracy on the first answer stays the same, but the total reasoning is 20-40% shorter, with almost no repeated reflection.

overthinking_reduction = (L_ori - L_rem) / (L_ori - P_first)

Reasoning models overthink not because they are unsure, but because they are glued to their first impression and keep re-reading the problem to confirm it. Removing that re-reading loop cuts the waste without sacrificing accuracy.
