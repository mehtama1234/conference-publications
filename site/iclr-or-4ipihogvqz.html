DoFlow: Forecasting with Causal Interventions

A generative model predicts not just what will happen, but what will happen if you intervene to change the system.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Most forecasts answer: what comes next? But real systems need causal forecasts: what comes next if we stop the factory? When will the patient recover if we try treatment A versus treatment B? DoFlow learns a causal map of the system and predicts under any scenario—normal, intervened, or counterfactual.

Zero assumption version

Why normal forecasts fail under intervention

A standard time-series model learns: given the last few measurements, predict the next measurement. It learns from the past: if temperature keeps rising, keep rising. But the moment you intervene—you flip a switch, you give a drug—the past patterns break. The model has no idea what happens next because the intervention is something new it has never seen.

What a beginner tries: train a bigger model on more data, or add more features. But this does not work because the model learned only correlation, not causation. Correlation means: when A goes up, B goes up. Causation means: A makes B go up. A normal forecaster confuses them. If you turn off the thing that makes A go up, the correlation disappears, but a non-causal model still predicts it will happen—dead wrong.

The core move. Learn the causal structure of the system (which variables cause which) and predict through that structure, so interventions get the right answer.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn causal structure

Infer or assume a directed acyclic graph (DAG) showing which variables cause which.

Train a flow model

Build a reversible neural network that maps random noise to realistic time-series trajectories, respecting causal order.

Encode observations

When you see the first few time steps, compress them into a starting point in the model's latent space.

Apply interventions

Clamp certain variables (the intervened ones) to their new values, then decode the rest.

Decode the future

Sample from the model to generate multiple plausible futures under the intervention or counterfactual scenario.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard generative models predict one random future at a time. DoFlow goes further: it makes those predictions conditional on a causal graph and on interventions. It changes how the model's latent space (the compressed representation of the system) respects causality.

the object it moves  =  Latent representation of time series
The hidden quantity

Causal sufficiency: the true path through the system

measured quantity  =  Causal graph and counterfactual recovery

The hidden quantity is the causal structure itself—the directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each arrow means one variable truly causes another. This graph is invisible in the data; you see only correlations. The paper assumes you either know the DAG or infer it from domain knowledge or statistical tests.

Once the graph is fixed, DoFlow uses it to predict what happens under intervention. It learns a counterfactual recovery function (a learned mathematical operation) that takes an observation and a hypothetical intervention and outputs what the system would have done under that intervention instead.

Why it works

Why changing the latent representation to follow causality fixes the problem

Standard generative models (like diffusion or VAEs) learn to map noise to data, but they do not respect causality. They learn the marginal distribution: what patterns happen in the real data. But causality is about conditional distributions: what happens given an intervention.

DoFlow uses a normalizing flow (a reversible neural network) that factorizes according to the causal DAG. This means the generative process—the path from noise to samples—follows the causal order: first it generates the root causes, then the things they cause, then the effects of those effects. Because the generative process respects causality, when you intervene (clamp a variable to a new value), the rest of the model automatically propagates the consequences correctly.

This works because the flow model learns the true conditional probabilities implicit in the graph. When you say the valve is now OFF (an intervention), the model does not just pattern-match to "valve off" from the training data; instead, it recomputes pressure and temperature via the causal edges that actually depend on the valve. It is not predicting from correlation; it is computing from the causal graph.

Math after the picture

The flow model equation

First in words:

The generative process samples from a prior distribution and transforms it through a series of invertible neural-network layers that are ordered by the causal graph: each layer generates variables conditioned on their causal parents, then passes control to the next layer.

Then the compact form:

x = f(z; theta), where f is invertible and factorizes as f(z) = [f_root(z_root), f_child_1(z_c1; x_parents), f_child_2(z_c2; x_parents), ...], with log p(x) = log p(z) - log |det J_f|

Every symbol:

xthe time-series trajectory we want to generate
zrandom noise (the starting point)
fthe invertible neural network (the flow)
thetathe weights of the network
f_rootthe part of the flow that generates root causes (variables with no parents in the DAG)
f_child_ithe part that generates the ith child variable conditioned on its parents
log p(x)the log probability (how likely the trajectory is under the model)
J_fthe Jacobian (the matrix of how f's outputs change with inputs)
log |det J_f|the change in volume as we transform from noise space to data space
Tiny worked example: two variables with causal link
Causal graph: Valve → Pressure (valve causes pressure).
Sample z_valve = 0.5 (noise for valve), z_pressure = -0.3 (noise for pressure).
f_root computes: valve_value = neural_net_1(0.5) = 0.8 (high, open).
f_child computes: pressure_value = neural_net_2(-0.3, valve=0.8) = 2.4 (high pressure because valve is open).
If we intervene: set valve to 0.1 (closed), then f_child recomputes: pressure = neural_net_2(-0.3, valve=0.1) = 0.6 (low pressure).
Same noise, different valve intervention, different pressure outcome—causality is preserved.

The factorization respects the DAG order, so each variable depends only on its causal parents, and interventions automatically propagate downstream.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong causal graph. If the assumed DAG is incorrect, predictions under intervention will be wrong. For example, if you think pressure causes valve position, but it is the reverse, the model will predict the wrong future when you intervene on the valve.
Hidden confounders. If two variables are both caused by a third variable you did not measure (a confounder), the causal graph will be misspecified. This breaks counterfactual recovery, because interventions will not account for the hidden cause.
Training only on observational data. If you never see interventions in the training data, the model cannot learn how the system behaves under intervention. It will generalize poorly to new interventions, even with the correct causal graph.
Used correctly. If the causal graph is known (from domain experts or from randomized experiments), and the flow model is trained on realistic data, it will forecast accurately under new interventions and counterfactuals.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 3-variable system: a heater (on/off), a room temperature (rises when heater on), and a thermostat reading (tracks temperature, but lags by 1 step). Generate 1000 time steps of data with random on/off decisions. Train a standard LSTM forecaster and DoFlow on the same data. Then: (1) Forecast the next 10 steps in a normal scenario (heater on randomly). (2) Intervene: force the heater off and forecast 10 steps. (3) Ask a counterfactual: if the heater had been on 5 steps ago instead of off, what would the temperature be now? Standard model will fail on steps 2 and 3. DoFlow should succeed because it learned the causal graph (heater causes temperature, temperature causes thermostat).

Accuracy on counterfactual trajectories = (number of time steps where prediction is within 10% of ground truth) / (total time steps in counterfactual query)

The demo proves that causality is learnable and that a causal model generalizes to interventions the training data never saw.
