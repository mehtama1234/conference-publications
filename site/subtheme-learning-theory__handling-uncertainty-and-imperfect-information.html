Handling uncertainty and imperfect information

Methods for models to quantify what they don't know, make robust predictions despite noisy data, and account for missing information in decision-making.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model predicts that a patient has a 92 percent chance of disease, but it offers no information about whether it is 92 percent confident or just guessing. A recommendation system suggests a movie, but does not indicate whether it is recommending because the system is sure you will like it or because it is uncertain and exploring. In the real world, decisions often depend on how confident you should be, so that you can act more cautiously when uncertainty is high. Should you undergo risky surgery if your disease probability is only slightly above 50 percent? Should a self-driving car brake hard if it thinks an obstacle is ahead but is not sure? This subtheme asks: how can a learner quantify its uncertainty so a decision-maker knows when to trust it, and how should it use this uncertainty to make better decisions by recognizing when it needs more information?

The challenge is that most machine learning systems output point predictions — single best guesses — without expressing uncertainty. Deep neural networks can output probabilities, but these often reflect training data frequency rather than true confidence; a well-trained network can be confidently wrong. Some data is inherently noisy (a patient's symptoms do not perfectly determine their disease), so any model must grapple with irreducible uncertainty. Other uncertainty stems from limited training data (the model has not seen enough examples to confidently predict) or distribution shift (new data differs from training data in ways the model did not encounter before). Separating these types of uncertainty and quantifying each is difficult.

Researchers develop Bayesian approaches that treat uncertainty as probability, capturing what the model does not know in addition to what it does. They study conformal prediction — methods that make predictions with explicit confidence bounds, guaranteeing that the true answer falls within the bound a specified percentage of the time. They analyze reliable learning — training models that perform well even when data is noisy or distribution shifts occur. They investigate how much data is needed to reduce uncertainty to acceptable levels. The work includes studying specific scenarios like active learning (choosing which data to collect next to learn most efficiently) and online learning with feedback (updating beliefs as new information arrives). In all cases, the goal is to make learning algorithms aware of their own ignorance and use that awareness to make decisions more carefully.

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme divides into conformal prediction (building prediction sets with guaranteed coverage), Bayesian inference (modeling what we don't know as probability distributions), handling noisy or shifted data (learning when examples are mislabeled or come from new domains), and active decision-making under uncertainty (choosing what to do when you're not sure).

Conformal Prediction Fundamentals and Efficiency

Conformal prediction is a way to wrap predictions with confidence bands—ranges where the true answer is guaranteed to fall, with a specific probability that you set. The challenge is that standard methods produce prediction intervals (ranges) that are often too wide to be useful. Different scenarios make this harder: you might have two-stage models where errors come from different stages, or you're learning online as new data arrives, or you're predicting multiple values at once instead of a single number.

CAOS (“CAOS: Conformal Aggregation of One-Shot Predictors”) makes one-shot prediction sets much smaller than older conformal methods while keeping the coverage guarantee intact—tested on tasks like finding landmarks in faces and sorting text. Decomposition-Based Modular Conformal Prediction (“Decomposition-Based Modular Conformal Prediction for Two-Stage”) improves coverage when your two-stage model has different types of errors at each stage, because it tracks error sources separately so you know which stage is causing bigger uncertainty. Distribution-informed Online Conformal Prediction (“Distribution-informed Online Conformal Prediction”) gives tight prediction intervals even when you're learning constantly from a stream of new data, matching the best coverage with shorter bands because it adapts as you see more examples. JAPAN (“JAPAN: Joint Adaptive Prediction Areas with”) uses normalizing flows—a way to learn flexible probability shapes—to make prediction areas tighter for regression and forecasting, keeping the coverage guarantee because it adapts the shape to the actual distribution. These papers all chase the same goal: smaller, sharper predictions that still maintain the formal coverage promise.

Bayesian Updating and Fast Belief Inference

Bayesian inference means updating your beliefs about unknown quantities based on evidence. The core challenge is computing the posterior—your updated beliefs as a probability distribution. When you have complex models, exact computation is impossible, so you need approximations that work fast and accurately. Sampling is one approach: draw random samples from the posterior and use them to estimate what you need.

Proximal-IMH (“Proximal-IMH: Proximal Posterior Proposals for Independent”) improves a classic sampling method called Independent Metropolis-Hastings by using a better starting proposal—when your first guess about where the posterior is matters for drawing good samples. It works reliably across many model types. Amortised Simulation-Based Inference in Generalized Bayes (“Amortized Simulation-Based Inference in Generalized Bayes”) trains a neural network to quickly estimate posteriors across many different problems at once—like training once, then using that trained network for fast inference on many new tasks. Multi-Task Bayesian In-Context Learning (“Multi-Task Bayesian In-Context Learning”) lets one model work across different problems quickly by learning to adapt; it matches oracle predictions while being thousands of times faster. These three papers span the spectrum from improving classical sampling to completely replacing it with learned neural approximations.

Learning from Imperfect Labels and Noisy Data

Many real datasets have mislabeled examples: a doctor might misdiagnose a patient, an image might be tagged wrong, or a text might be miscoded. Standard learning assumes labels are correct, so bad labels lead to wrong models. The question is: how do you learn well even when labels are noisy, and how much noise can you tolerate?

Language Generation with Feedback (“Language Generation with Feedback: Queries and”) studies generation tasks where human feedback has noise—some answers marked wrong when they're actually right, or vice versa. It characterizes exactly when you can still learn the right model despite that noise, so you know which feedback regimes are salvageable. Enhancing Learning with Noisy Labels via Rockafellian Relaxation (“Enhancing Learning with Noisy Labels via”) tackles noisy labels across vision and language tasks by using a mathematical relaxation technique, improving accuracy no matter the noise type or dataset size because the relaxation smooths out label noise instead of trusting corrupted examples. Just Y-Prediction (“Just Y-Prediction: Enabling Historical Cumulative Inconsistency”) speeds up diffusion-based label smoothing with one-step inference instead of many, converging to the best possible solution on noisy benchmarks because it skips the slow iterative process but keeps the same final quality. All three show you can extract signal from messy data if you use the right technique.

Multivariate Conformal Methods and Distribution Adaptation

Most conformal prediction work predicts a single number (univariate). But many real problems need to predict multiple values at once—predicting the next 10 steps of a robot's motion, or all pixel values in a region of an image. When you predict many values together, the prediction region becomes multidimensional. The challenge is making these regions small (efficient) while keeping coverage valid, and adapting them when the data distribution changes.

Neural Optimal Transport Meets Multivariate Conformal Prediction (“Neural Optimal Transport Meets Multivariate Conformal”) learns the shape of the conditional distribution—what values tend to occur together—then tailors prediction regions to that shape, making them tighter and more informative because generic shapes waste space. Geometric Conformal Prediction with Spatial Ranks and Multivariate Quantiles (“Geometric Conformal Prediction with Spatial Ranks”) uses a different shape idea based on spatial ranks, achieving near-perfect coverage with tighter regions on multi-target regression and multi-class classification because spatial ranks capture the relationship structure directly. SpeedCP (“SpeedCP: Fast Kernel-based Conditional Conformal Prediction”) delivers fast, reliable conditional coverage for black-box models, shortening intervals by 30 percent and running 40 times faster because it uses precomputed distance functions to avoid expensive sampling. These three papers each solve the same core problem—prediction intervals are too wide—but choose different solutions: one learns distributions, one uses geometric statistics, one sacrifices a bit of precision for speed.

Uncertainty and Decision-Making Under Doubt

Uncertainty is only useful when it changes what you do—when it guides you to act differently. When you must act (admitting a patient, applying brakes in a self-driving car, or choosing a treatment), your confidence should change how carefully you proceed, so you act cautiously when unsure and boldly when sure. This requires uncertainty estimates you can actually trust, and methods that tie uncertainty directly to your decision.

A Decision-Theoretic View of Test-Time Training (“A Decision-Theoretic View of Test-Time Training”) explains why adapting a model at test time sometimes fails and gives guidance on when and how far to adapt, grounded in decision theory. On Uniform Error Bounds for Kernel Regression under Non-Gaussian Noise (“On Uniform Error Bounds for Kernel”) derives tighter uncertainty bounds that work under realistic noise (not just Gaussian), applied to safe control—if your bounds are tighter, your controller can be more aggressive without being reckless. Representation Learning for Equivariant Inference with Guarantees (“Representation Learning for Equivariant Inference with”) provides the first formal guarantees on uncertainty from learned representations, tested on robotics tasks. All three tie uncertainty directly to downstream consequences rather than treating uncertainty as an academic metric.
