MindZero: Learning Online Mental Reasoning Without Annotations

Train a visual-language model to guess what a person is thinking from their actions, without hand-labeled thoughts, by rewarding guesses that explain the person's choices.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

AI assistants need to understand what people are thinking so they can help them well. Normally this requires experts to label thousands of examples of human thoughts, which is expensive and slow. MindZero teaches an AI to infer human mental states directly from their behavior using a clever reward system: the AI gets praised for mental state guesses that match what a planner predicts the person will do next. This method learns mental reasoning as a skill, just like learning a language—without needing the labeled examples.

Zero assumption version

The world does not hand you human thoughts

AI helpers like robots or chatbots need to figure out what a person wants or believes in order to assist them well. Today, if you want a model to understand human thoughts, you collect video or text of a person acting, then hire people to write down what they were thinking at each moment—their beliefs, goals, worries. This annotation is slow, expensive, and hard to scale to real-world situations.

A beginner might try running a large language model (a statistical system trained on text that can answer questions) and ask it directly: 'What is this person thinking?' But pure guessing from behavior alone gives wrong answers because the model has no principled way to check if its guess makes sense. It does not know whether a guess actually predicts what the person will do next.

The problem. Humans need AI that infers their thoughts from behavior, but training this today requires expensive hand-written labels of what people are thinking.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

MindZero moves weights inside the language model so it outputs better mental state guesses. Instead of training on hand-labeled examples of what people are thinking, it trains by comparing predicted actions to real actions—a process called self-supervised learning (learning from unlabeled data by creating automatic targets). The paper changes how the model is rewarded during training: it rewards mental state hypotheses that maximize the likelihood a planner assigns to the actions the person actually took.

the object it moves  =  Mental state hypothesis weights
The hidden quantity

The action-prediction match

measured quantity  =  Action likelihood under hypothesis

The hidden quantity is: how likely was the person's actual action, given the mental state the model guessed? If the guess is 'they want the red cup,' and the planner says 'if they want the red cup, there is a 92 percent chance they reach left,' and the person actually reached left, then the guess gets high credit. If the planner predicts 'only 10 percent chance they reach left' but they did, the guess is worse.

The paper estimates this by running a planner (a separate module that models what a rational agent would do) forward from each guessed mental state and computing the probability it assigns to the observed behavior. Higher probability means the mental state guess was good. The language model weights are adjusted to raise the probability of guesses that explain behavior well.

Why it works

Why it works: reward prediction power

Without annotations, the model has no direct feedback on whether its mental state guesses are right. But here is the key: if a guess is right, then a planner trained on rational behavior should predict the person will act the way they actually did. This creates a circular loop of learning: the model generates guesses, the planner checks if those guesses predict real behavior, and the model gets rewarded for guesses that score high on the planner's prediction. Over many iterations, the model learns to generate mental states that accurately predict what the person will do.

Why is this better than asking the language model directly? Because the planner enforces a hard constraint: the mental state must actually explain the behavior, not just sound plausible. A naive language model might generate a guess like 'they are thinking about dinner' which sounds reasonable but has nothing to do with why they reached for a tool. The planner's check prevents this: if that mental state would not predict reaching for a tool, it does not get rewarded. The model therefore learns mental states that predict what the person will do next, not just guesses that happen to sound reasonable.

The reward equation

First in words:

Reward equals the log-likelihood the planner assigns to the observed action, given the guessed mental state. Log-likelihood is the logarithm of probability, a standard way to measure how likely an outcome is. The model trains to maximize this reward.

Then the compact form:

R(h, a) = log P(a | h; planner)

Every symbol:

R(h, a)the reward for mental state guess h and action a
hthe mental state hypothesis the language model guesses
athe action the person actually took
P(a | h; planner)the probability a planner assigns to action a, given mental state h
logthe natural logarithm (turns probability into a score); higher probability gives higher reward
A tiny worked example
Suppose the person reaches left. Model guesses: 'they want the red cup on the left shelf.'
Planner evaluates: if someone wants the red cup, the probability they reach left is 0.85.
Reward = log(0.85) = -0.16 (negative because log of a probability less than 1 is negative, but larger probabilities are closer to zero, so this is good).
Different guess: 'they are thinking about dinner.' Planner: probability 0.02 they reach left if thinking about dinner.
Reward = log(0.02) = -3.9 (much worse; smaller probability = larger penalty).
Model weights shift away from the dinner guess and toward the red-cup guess because the red-cup guess predicted the action better.

The planner is the ground truth: it tells the model which guesses lead to predictions that match reality. The model learns by chasing high reward, which means learning to emit mental states that a planner would say predict the observed behavior.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Planner is inaccurate. If the planner itself does not correctly model how rational agents behave, the reward signal becomes misleading. The model learns to generate mental states that fool a bad planner, not mental states that reflect reality. Testing the planner on held-out behavior is essential before training the language model.
Behavior is ambiguous. If many different mental states could explain the same action, the model cannot tell which is right. For example, reaching for a cup could mean 'I am thirsty,' 'I am clearing clutter,' or 'I am testing my arm strength.' Without richer behavior or context, the learning signal is too noisy. Adding constraints on the mental states (e.g., 'they are always pursuing one clear goal') helps because it narrows the search space, so the planner can distinguish good guesses from bad ones more clearly.
Model mode-collapses. If the language model learns a single mental state that happens to predict many behaviors (e.g., 'they are thinking'), it stops exploring better hypotheses because that one guess is already earning high reward. Adding diversity to the training (sampling multiple guesses per behavior, penalizing repeated guesses) keeps the model searching because it forces the model to try different hypotheses, which helps it find more specific and accurate explanations.
Done right. Pair a well-validated planner with diverse, unambiguous behavior. Run the training on domains where actions are dominated by a clear goal or belief, such as household navigation tasks or tool use. Validate the learned mental states on held-out behavior; if they predict well on new situations, the model has learned generalizable reasoning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that a model trained with MindZero can predict what a person will do next. Set up a simple gridworld or household simulation where an agent performs a sequence of actions driven by a hidden goal (e.g., 'go to the kitchen and fetch a cup'). Hide the goal. Train MindZero on unlabeled action sequences. Then ask the trained model: 'What is the agent thinking at this step?' and 'What will it do next?' Measure accuracy on both guessed mental states and next-action predictions. Compare to a raw language model (which guesses without checking) and a slow planner-only baseline (which checks every guess). MindZero should win on speed (because it learned to guess fast without checking each time) and match or beat the planner on accuracy (because it learned which guesses matter most). The demo proves that MindZero learns coherent mental reasoning without any labeled examples of thoughts.

Accuracy = (correct_mental_states + correct_next_actions) / total_predictions

What matters: the model never sees a labeled thought, yet learns to emit thoughts that a planner says predict real behavior—proof that mental reasoning can be learned as a self-supervised skill.
