WMPO: World Model-based Policy Optimization for Robotic Control

Train robot policies in a pixel-realistic imagined world so they learn from failures without touching a real robot.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) are robot policies trained on human demonstrations. They work well on familiar tasks but freeze when they encounter something unexpected and make a mistake. This paper shows how to teach them to recover from failures by having them practice in a pixel-realistic world model built from robot videos. The policy learns by imagining trajectories and learning which actions lead to success or failure, all without needing a real robot.

Why imitation learning gets stuck

Today's robot policies are trained by watching human experts perform tasks over and over. The policy learns to copy these demonstrations, and it does copy them well. But the moment the robot sees something it has not seen before—a slight shift in object position, a different lighting—the policy has no idea what to do. It makes a mistake, and the mistake compounds. The robot cannot learn what happens after a failure because it was never shown.

Real-world reinforcement learning (RL), where robots learn by trial and error on actual hardware, solves this. But trying thousands of attempts on real robots is slow, expensive, and risky. Each real-world rollout takes time and money. A better path: use an imagined world where the policy can try and fail instantly, because every simulated rollout is nearly free (just a forward pass through neural networks) and you can run thousands in seconds, whereas each real trial could take minutes and risk hardware damage.

The core move. Build a pixel-realistic world model of robot dynamics, fine-tune it to match the current policy's behavior, then run on-policy RL (Group Relative Policy Optimization) in that imagined world. The policy learns from imagined failures without touching hardware.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pretrain a world model

Train a video-generation model on millions of real robot trajectories to predict future pixels.

Align to the policy

Fine-tune the world model on data collected by the current policy so it matches what this policy actually does.

Imagine complete rollouts

Run the policy in the imagined world: the world model generates future frames, the policy picks actions, and we record the full sequence.

Score outcomes

A learned reward model watches the imagined trajectory and predicts: did the task succeed or fail?

Update the policy

Use Group Relative Policy Optimization: compare successes to failures and nudge the policy to choose actions that lead to success.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the policy trains. Instead of learning only from static human demonstrations, the policy learns from dynamic imagined rollouts in a pixel-realistic world. The world model is the bridge: it generates pixel-space trajectories that the Vision-Language model can understand (because the VLA was pretrained on real images), so the policy's pretrained visual reasoning applies directly to the imagined world.

the object it moves  =  training world
The hidden quantity

Policy-world alignment

measured quantity  =  Data divergence between expert and policy

A hidden problem: if you train a world model only on expert demonstrations, it never sees failures. So when the policy (which learns from failures) rolls out in the imagined world, the scenes it encounters are unlike anything the world model has trained on. The world model hallucinates, and the policy learns from a dream that does not match reality.

This paper introduces policy behavior alignment: taking real rollouts the policy collected (which include its mistakes and recoveries), and fine-tuning the world model on them. Now the world model understands both successes and failures, and when it imagines, it sees familiar patterns. The hidden quantity is the divergence between the expert data the world model was trained on and the policy data it sees during training.

Why it works

The chain of cause and effect

Here is the core mechanism. A Vision-Language model (a policy) was trained on real images from the internet and real robot videos. It understands pixels. If you train a world model in latent space (an abstract, compressed space), the world model's predictions do not match pixels, so the policy's pretrained knowledge is wasted.

WMPO does the opposite: the world model outputs pixels, so the policy uses its real pretrained visual understanding. Second, the world model is fine-tuned on the policy's own trajectories (not just expert demos), so it learns what this policy actually does. Third, the policy collects imagined rollouts and a learned reward model scores them (success or failure). Fourth, on-policy RL (Group Relative Policy Optimization) compares successes to failures and pushes the policy to repeat successful actions and avoid failed ones. The policy learns from imagined failures without risking hardware.

Math after the picture

The optimization objective

First in words:

maximize the expected cumulative reward over imagined trajectories, where trajectories are generated by the world model conditioned on the policy's actions

Then the compact form:

max_theta E_tau ~ (pi_theta, p_phi) [R_psi(tau)]

Every symbol:

thetathe policy's weights
taua complete imagined trajectory (sequence of states and actions)
pi_thetathe policy parameterized by theta
p_phithe world model parameterized by phi
R_psithe reward model parameterized by psi
E_tau ~ (pi_theta, p_phi)expected value over trajectories generated by running the policy in the world model
A tiny worked example
One trajectory: pick action A, world predicts 5 frames, reward model scores the outcome as success (reward +1).
Second trajectory: pick action B, world predicts 5 frames, reward model scores as failure (reward 0).
Third trajectory: action variant of A, outcome success (reward +1).
Policy optimization compares: trajectories with actions A and A-variant (both +1) versus action B (0). The policy learns to favor actions like A.
In the real algorithm, you collect dozens of imagined rollouts, group them by reward, and update the policy to increase the probability of high-reward actions relative to low-reward ones.

The objective says: choose policy weights that maximize reward over imagined rollouts. Because the world model is aligned to the policy, the imagined rollouts stay realistic and the learning signal is reliable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

World model hallucinations compound. If the world model is poorly aligned to the policy's actual rollouts, it generates unrealistic frame sequences. The policy learns from hallucinations and transfers poorly to real robots. Solution: Policy Behavior Alignment keeps the world model honest.
Reward model is the weak link. The reward model must correctly predict success or failure from video. If it is wrong, the policy learns the wrong signal. Solution: train the reward model with diverse real trajectories before policy learning, and monitor for reward hacking.
Unfamiliar scenarios at deployment. The real world can always throw surprises not seen even in millions of pretraining videos. The policy trained in imagination may fail on scenarios far outside the pretraining data. Solution: continue to collect real data and update the world model.
Done right. Careful alignment of the world model to the policy, a well-trained reward model, and periodic real-world validation catches these problems early. The on-policy nature of GRPO (learning from rollouts the policy just generated, in a similar state space) is inherently more stable than off-policy methods.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a pixel-space world model on a small set of robotic videos (e.g. 100 trajectories of a reaching or pushing task). Train a simple reward model (binary: task success or failure). Start with a vision-language policy fine-tuned on 10 expert demonstrations. Run WMPO: collect 500 imagined rollouts per iteration for 20 iterations. Show three things: (1) The policy's success rate on the real task improves beyond the expert baseline because imagined rollouts expose the policy to failures and recoveries it never saw in the demos, teaching it how to handle mistakes. (2) Qualitatively, the imagined rollouts show the policy attempting recovery moves (e.g., correcting a mistake mid-trajectory) that do not appear in the expert data. (3) Transfer: test the learned policy on a slightly perturbed task (object 5cm away from training position) and show it succeeds where the expert-only baseline fails.

success_rate = count(reward_model(imagined_trajectory) == success) / total_imagined_trajectories

The demo proves that a policy can learn self-correction and generalization from imagined experience alone, without any real-world trial and error.
