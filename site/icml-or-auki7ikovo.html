MultiLoReFT: Decoupling Shared and Modality-Specific Subspaces

By splitting the output of each pretrained encoder into separate shared and modality-specific channels using low-rank projection layers, the model learns to disentangle what all modalities know in common from what each modality alone contributes.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal learning

When you combine vision and audio, some information is shared between them (like a scene or event) and some is unique (color matters only to vision, pitch only to audio). This paper shows how to take off-the-shelf pretrained encoders and insert small low-rank filter layers on top of them that automatically separate shared from unique information, revealing exactly how each modality contributes to the final prediction.

Zero assumption version

The entanglement problem

Multimodal models must merge signals from different senses—vision, audio, text—into a single prediction. A naive approach is to just concatenate (glue together) all the outputs from each encoder and feed them to a classifier. But this creates a mess: the model learns internal patterns that mix what is shared across modalities with what is unique to each one.

A beginner might try fine-tuning all the encoders end-to-end on paired data, but collecting large labeled datasets with perfect alignment across modalities is expensive. A second beginner might use pretrained encoders and simply concatenate their outputs, losing all interpretability—you cannot tell whether a prediction came from vision, audio, or an invisible interaction between them. The problem bites hardest when you want to understand which modality did what, or when one modality's data is missing at test time.

In one sentence. MultiLoReFT inserts small learnable filter layers that split each encoder's output into a shared channel and a modality-specific channel, so you can see exactly what information each modality contributes and use them flexibly.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Freeze both encoders

Vision and audio encoders stay unchanged; only new projection layers are trained.

Project into separate subspaces

Each encoder output passes through two learnable projection matrices: one to shared space, one to modality-specific space.

Enforce orthogonality

Training penalizes overlap between shared and modality-specific subspaces so they stay independent.

Measure mutual information

The loss function checks that shared features actually appear in both modalities and modality-specific features appear in only one.

Train with structural constraints

No task labels needed; the representation constraints guide learning to automatically disentangle information types.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

MultiLoReFT adds learnable low-rank projection layers on top of frozen pretrained encoders. These layers route the output through two separate subspaces: a shared subspace and a modality-specific subspace. A low-rank projection means the layer is much simpler than the encoder itself—it compresses information into fewer dimensions, so it has far fewer numbers to adjust during training. The paper does not retrain the encoders themselves; it only trains these small filter layers.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: subspace orthogonality

measured quantity  =  Overlap between shared and modality-specific subspaces

When you merge two modalities, the question is: how much does the shared subspace overlap with the modality-specific subspace? Perfect disentanglement means zero overlap; a high overlap means the two subspaces are redundant and you cannot tell what is shared from what is unique. The paper calls this the orthogonality constraint—it measures how independent the subspaces are.

The method estimates this by computing the inner product (a measure of alignment) between the shared projection and each modality-specific projection. High inner products mean the subspaces are aligned and overlapping; low inner products mean they are independent. The training loss penalizes high overlaps, forcing the subspaces apart.

Why it works

Why splitting representations works

When you have two modalities (say vision and audio) and you feed them both to a single classifier without separation, the model learns patterns that are entangled. The hidden neurons in the classifier start treating shared information (what both modalities contribute) the same way they treat modality-specific information (color only from vision, pitch only from audio). This mixing makes the model less interpretable and means it breaks badly when one modality goes missing—the classifier relied on both and has no fallback.

MultiLoReFT solves this by physically splitting each encoder's output into two independent channels before they reach the classifier. The shared channel contains only patterns that appear in both modalities (learned via a mutual-information loss that checks both modalities encode similar information). The modality-specific channel contains patterns unique to that modality. By keeping these channels orthogonal (perpendicular, non-overlapping) during training, you prevent the model from using modality-specific signal to solve the shared part of the task. This forces the shared channel to do the work of finding true commonality.

The result is twofold: first, the classifier receives cleaner, less redundant information because each channel has a distinct role; second, you can read off from the channel weights exactly how much each modality contributed to the prediction. If the shared channel received strong signals from vision and audio, both modalities agree. If the modality-specific channels are large, one modality has unique information. This transparency emerges naturally from the constraint, not from manual interpretation.

The core equation: orthogonality constraint

First in words:

Loss equals the sum of two inner products between the shared subspace and each modality-specific subspace, measuring how much they overlap. Training minimizes this, pushing the subspaces apart.

Then the compact form:

L_orth = ||R_s R_m1^T||_F + ||R_s R_m2^T||_F

Every symbol:

R_smatrix that projects to the shared subspace
R_m1matrix that projects to the vision-specific subspace
R_m2matrix that projects to the audio-specific subspace
||·||_FFrobenius norm, a measure of matrix size (like length of a vector, but for matrices)
R^Ttranspose, which flips rows and columns
A tiny worked example
Suppose R_s is a 4-by-2 matrix and R_m1 is a 4-by-2 matrix (low-rank, much smaller than the encoder output).
Their inner product R_s R_m1^T is a 4-by-4 matrix.
Compute R_s R_m1^T via matrix multiply: rows of R_s dot each column of R_m1^T.
If all entries are close to 0, the subspaces are orthogonal (independent). The Frobenius norm ||R_s R_m1^T||_F sums the squared entries.
Suppose the first calculation gives norm 2.5 and the second gives norm 1.8. Then L_orth = 2.5 + 1.8 = 4.3.
Training nudges the matrices to shrink these norms. If they become 0.8 and 0.6, then L_orth = 1.4, and the subspaces are much more independent.
This descent forces the shared and modality-specific paths to separate.

The loss is unsupervised—it does not use task labels. Instead, it enforces the structure (separation) you believe in, and the representation naturally disentangles.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Enforcing orthogonality too hard. If the penalty weight on the orthogonality loss is too large, the subspaces become so separate they lose all ability to communicate. The shared subspace fails to learn anything useful because the modality-specific subspaces refuse to share any information. The fix: tune the weight of the orthogonality constraint carefully.
Pretrained encoders are weak. MultiLoReFT works only as well as the pretrained encoders it starts with. If the encoders never learned good representations on their unimodal tasks, the projection layers cannot fix that—garbage input will project into garbage output. The fix: use strong, well-trained pretrained models.
Modality misalignment at test time. If the shared subspace assumes both modalities are present and aligned, and you feed only one modality at test time, the shared channel may receive no signal from the missing modality and the prediction degrades. The paper shows accuracy drops but does not collapse, yet unpredictable misalignment can still hurt. The fix: learn separate classifiers for single modalities or use a gating mechanism.
Done right. The method works best when both modalities are present, the encoders are strong, and the data distribution at test time matches the assumptions made during training—that is, that shared information truly appears in both modalities.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy example with two simple encoders (or use small pieces of real ones). Train MultiLoReFT on a small multimodal dataset where you know the ground truth: which features are truly shared (e.g., object class) and which are truly unique (e.g., color from vision, loudness from audio). After training, measure whether the shared subspace captures only shared features and the modality-specific subspaces capture only unique features. Verify this by checking that the learned shared channel outputs are similar for both modalities on the same examples, while the modality-specific channel outputs differ across modalities.

disentanglement score = (fraction of shared-subspace variance explained by both modalities) minus (fraction of modality-specific variance explained by the wrong modality)

The demo proves that unsupervised structural constraints (orthogonality) automatically discover what you believed to be true: that some information is shared and some is unique, and they can be separated without task labels.
