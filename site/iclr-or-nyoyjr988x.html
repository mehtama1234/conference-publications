Depth Promotes Low-Rankness via Coupled Dynamics

Deeper neural networks naturally pull themselves toward simple, low-rank solutions because their training updates couple together in a way shallow networks cannot.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

When a neural network learns to fill in missing numbers in a data table, it could theoretically find millions of different correct answers. But in practice, deeper networks almost always find the simplest answer: one where most of the information is crammed into just a few important directions. This paper reveals why: depth creates a hidden coupling in how different weight changes talk to each other during training, and this coupling is what locks the network into simple solutions.

Zero assumption version

Why simple solutions, and why do networks forget what they learned?

A neural network filling in missing data-table entries is trying to find two smaller tables that multiply back to the big one. Mathematically, infinite solutions exist. A beginner might think: just train the network, and it will find any old solution. But experiments show something strange: deeper networks almost always find the simplest one, a low-rank table (one that can be compressed into a few key directions). Shallow networks do not have this behavior as strongly.

Worse, there is a hidden trap called loss of plasticity. If you pre-train the network on a few observations, then start training again with more data, the network struggles to adapt. It forgets how to learn. Shallow networks are especially bad at this; deep networks mysteriously escape it. The paper asks: what is the hidden mechanism that makes deep networks biased toward simplicity, and why does that protect them from forgetting?

In one sentence. Deeper factorization networks converge to simple, low-rank solutions because their weight updates couple together during training; shallow networks lack this coupling and fail to adapt when learning resumes on more data.

What the paper changes

The single thing this changes

The paper changes how we think about the gradient updates (the nudges that train the network). In shallow networks, each weight moves independently based on its own error signal. In deeper networks, the updates couple: changes to one layer push on the next layer's changes, and they amplify each other. This coupling is the invisible hand steering the network toward simple solutions.

the object it moves  =  gradient update coupling
The hidden quantity

Coupling strength: how synchronized are the updates?

measured quantity  =  update coupling (synchronized weight movements)

Coupling is the degree to which changes to one layer's weights affect changes to the next layer's weights. In a shallow network, layers can move independently. In a deeper network, they are forced to move together, as if connected by invisible springs. The paper measures this by looking at the product of two special matrices (MD and MC) that encode how data and weight changes flow through the network.

When coupling is strong, weight pairs move in lockstep. When it is weak or absent, each weight drifts on its own. The paper proves that low-rank solutions emerge if and only if this coupling is strong—no coupling, no low-rank bias.

Plain reading. Coupling happens when the gradient of weight A pulls on the gradient of weight B. Imagine two pulley weights connected by a rope: pull one down, and the other is yanked up by the tension. That is what coupling does to neural network weights—they cannot move independently.
Paper reading. The paper defines coupling through a mathematical product of three matrices: the observation pattern, and the two factor matrices. When this product keeps a special simple structure throughout training, the updates couple and the network converges to low-rank.
Why it works

Why coupled updates push toward simplicity

Imagine you have two tables to learn, A and B, and multiplying them together should give the true table. Training adjusts both A and B to make their product closer to the true answer. Here is what happens: if the observations you have form a connected pattern (you see row 1 linked to column 1, row 1 linked to column 2, etc.), then the error signal bounces between A and B in a coordinated way. A change in A triggers a change in B that amplifies the original change.

In a two-layer (shallow) network, this coordination can break. The factor matrices can learn to specialize: one handles rows, one handles columns, and they never truly coordinate. But in a three-layer or deeper network, there is an extra layer in the middle that cannot stay decoupled. This middle layer is forced to absorb all the information flow, which means it must concentrate on just a few key directions—the rank-1 structure emerges inevitably.

Once the network is in this low-rank state, it has learned the essential pattern and ignored noise. When you then train on additional data, the network is not stuck in an inflexible memorized state. It can adapt because it never memorized the details—only the core structure matters. This is why deep networks avoid loss of plasticity.

Math after the picture

The equation for loss during training

First in words:

loss equals mean squared error over observed entries plus a penalty that depends on rank and depth coupling

Then the compact form:

phi = (1/|Omega|) * sum over (i,j) in Omega of (A[i]*B[j] - M[i,j])^2

Every symbol:

phithe total training loss (how wrong the predictions are)
|Omega|the count of observed matrix entries
A[i]the i-th row of factor matrix A
B[j]the j-th column of factor matrix B
M[i,j]the true (or observed) value at row i, column j
A tiny worked example
Suppose true matrix entry M[1,1] = 5. Factor A[1] and B[1] predict 3.
Error at this entry: (3 - 5)^2 = 4.
Suppose M[1,2] = 6. Factors predict 3, error: (3 - 6)^2 = 9.
If we have 100 observed entries, sum is ~400 (for illustration). Loss = 400 / 100 = 4.
Gradient descent nudges A and B to shrink this loss. In shallow networks, nudges are independent. In deep networks, nudges couple and amplify, pushing faster toward rank-1.

The loss itself is the same for all depths; the coupling appears in how the gradient descent nudges the factor matrices during each training step.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Diagonal-only initialization breaks coupling If you initialize with only diagonal entries observed (no links between rows and columns), the network never experiences coupled dynamics. It learns decoupled factors, even in deep networks, and loses plasticity. The coupling requires connected observations.
Very small learning rates prevent adaptation Coupling is most effective when gradients flow strongly. If the learning rate is too small, the network converges so slowly that it treats pre-training and post-training as separate, isolated tasks. Coupling requires sufficient gradient magnitude.
Rank-deficient observation patterns limit the benefit If observations form multiple disconnected clusters (some rows only talk to some columns, with gaps), coupling may not propagate through the entire network. Benefit is lost where the data is fragmented.
Done right: use deep networks on well-connected data Coupling works best when observations form a single connected component and depth is at least 3. Under these conditions, the network learns the low-rank structure and remains plastic across continued training.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple demo: create a rank-5 ground-truth matrix (100 by 100). Pre-train a depth-2 and a depth-4 network on 2000 random observations. Measure how many important directions (rank) each network learned. Then add 1000 new observations and resume training. Measure final reconstruction error and effective rank. The demo should make visible that the depth-2 network gets stuck (high loss on new data, stuck rank) while the depth-4 network adapts smoothly (low loss, rank stays low).

plasticity_loss = error_after_pretraining_warmstart - error_from_scratch_on_full_data

If the depth-4 network has plasticity_loss near zero and depth-2 is large, you have confirmed the coupling effect.
