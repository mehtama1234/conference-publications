Much Ado About Noising

Robot policies succeed by iteratively refining predictions with noise during training, not by capturing complex distributions.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

People assumed generative control policies (models that build actions step by step, like diffusion models) won because they could represent tricky, multimodal action distributions. But this paper shows the real reason: iterative refinement—feeding the previous prediction back in for a second step—combined with noise during training. A simple two-step policy that just regresses on actions matches flow-based performance, suggesting distribution-fitting is not the secret ingredient.

Zero assumption version

Why do generative robot policies work so well?

Robot learning trains a model to map observations (camera images, sensor readings) to actions (motor commands). The simplest way is direct regression: for each observation in the training data, predict the exact action taken, and penalize the difference. This works, but people noticed that generative models—which build actions iteratively, starting with noise and refining step by step—performed much better, because the step-by-step refinement seemed to help the model handle cases where multiple actions would work equally well.

Researchers speculated that generative models win because they can handle situations where multiple different actions are equally good (for example, grasping an object from the left or right). They thought the step-by-step refinement was necessary to express these complex possibilities. But no one had carefully tested whether the complexity-fitting was really driving the performance gain.

In one sentence. Iterative refinement supervised at each step, plus noise during training, is what matters—not the ability to fit complex distributions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train with noise

During training, add random noise to the first-step prediction before the second step sees it.

Supervise every step

Give the model a learning signal (a correct answer) for both the first output and the second refined output.

First step: initial guess

From the observation, make a rough prediction of the action, measured against the ground-truth action.

Second step: refine

Take the noisy first prediction, feed it back in along with noise, and refine to a better estimate, because the second pass can correct errors the first pass made.

Test without noise

During testing, remove noise; the learned iterations still work because the model learned the refinement pattern during noisy training.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of learning a single-step direct mapping from observation to action, the model performs an iterative refinement: predict an action in step one, corrupt it with noise, feed that noisy result back in step two along with the original observation, and refine to a better action. Both steps receive their own supervised learning signal during training, because this forces the model to learn how to take noisy intermediate results and improve them rather than memorizing perfect one-shot answers.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: supervision signal timing

measured quantity  =  Intermediate supervision (at each step)

Normally, when you train a multi-step model, you only check if the final output is correct. Here, the paper discovered that providing a correct-answer signal at every intermediate step—not just the final one—makes a large difference in performance. This is called supervised iterative computation (SIC). The model learns that even a rough intermediate prediction is useful when it gets honest feedback, not just a vague signal at the end.

The paper measures the importance of this by ablating it: when they remove intermediate supervision but keep noise, performance drops by a large margin. This shows the model needs that step-by-step guidance to learn the iterative strategy.

Why it works

Why iterative supervised refinement works

When a model trains on direct regression, it must output the action perfectly in one shot, or it is penalized. That is hard, especially when the action space is complex or ambiguous. If the model overshoots or undershoots, there is no recovery.

Iterative supervised computation breaks this into steps. In step one, the model makes a rough guess. It gets a learning signal: how far off that guess is from the true action. That gives the model a clear direction to improve, because knowing exactly how far wrong you are is much more useful for learning than being penalized all at once. Then in step two, the model takes that first-step output (plus noise, which keeps it from memorizing the training data exactly) and refines it. The model gets another learning signal for the second output.

The noise during training is essential because without it, the model could cheat: memorize the exact training data in step one, then pass it unchanged in step two. The noise forces the model to learn a real refinement strategy—how to take a noisy, imperfect intermediate result and improve it, because the model must now discover how to recover from imperfect first steps rather than just copying them. During testing (when the finished model answers new tasks), the noise is turned off, but the learned refinement pattern still works.

This is more efficient than trying to fit a complex distribution, because the model simply learns that making intermediate predictions with supervision, then refining them, is a fast path to a good answer, while distribution-fitting requires learning how to represent many possible outcomes simultaneously. Two steps are enough; more complex distribution-fitting adds overhead without much benefit.

The training objective

First in words:

Minimize the sum of two prediction errors: the error on the first step's raw output compared to the true action, plus the error on the second step's refined output compared to the true action.

Then the compact form:

Loss = mean( ||pi_theta(o, I_0=0, t=0) - a|| + ||pi_theta(o, I_0=pi_theta(o, I_0=0, t=0) + noise, t=t*) - a|| )

Every symbol:

oObservation (sensor input)
aTrue action (ground truth)
pi_thetaThe learned policy (model with weights theta)
I_0=0First step: no prior input, start fresh
I_0=...Second step: input is the noisy first-step output
t=0, t=t*Time/step index in the iterative process
noiseRandom Gaussian perturbation added during training only
meanAverage loss across all training examples
A tiny worked example
Example: grasping an object. True action is (lift 5cm, rotate 30 degrees).
Step 1: Model predicts (lift 3cm, rotate 20 degrees). Error magnitude = 2.4. First loss term = 2.4.
Add noise: prediction becomes (lift 3.1cm, rotate 19.5 degrees).
Step 2: Model takes that noisy prediction, refines to (lift 4.9cm, rotate 29.5 degrees). Error = 0.1. Second loss term = 0.1.
Total loss for this example = 2.4 + 0.1 = 2.5. During training, the model learns to make the second-step refinement work well, reducing the need for perfect first steps.

Both steps incur a penalty for wrong predictions, training the model to refine systematically rather than hoping one shot is enough.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Removing intermediate supervision breaks it. If you keep the iterative structure but delete the learning signal on the first step (supervise only the final output), performance drops sharply. The model has no guidance to refine incrementally.
Wrong noise level causes instability. Too little noise and the model memorizes the training data in step one, negating refinement. Too much noise and the second step cannot reliably recover, since the signal is too corrupted. Tuning the noise level is task-dependent and sensitive.
Testing without noise requires learned refinement. During testing, noise is removed. If the model never learned how to refine under realistic intermediate errors (because it saw only noiseless data in training), the learned refinement pattern fails.
When done right. Simple two-step iterative supervised computation with appropriate training noise and testing without noise achieves performance matching far more complex generative models, while being faster and easier to tune, because the learned refinement strategy is simpler to represent than fitting a full distribution.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a simple two-step robot policy: (1) observe, predict action (step 1), add noise to it. (2) Observe again, take the noisy step-1 output, and predict a refined action (step 2). Supervise both steps with ground-truth actions from a behavior cloning dataset (recorded human demonstrations). At test time, turn off noise. Measure success rate on a robotic manipulation benchmark (e.g., LIBERO object grasping, reaching, or multi-task sequences). Compare against a single-step regression baseline (no iteration) and, if possible, a flow-based generative policy baseline.

Success rate = (number of successful tasks completed) / (total tasks attempted), measured for regression, two-step iterative, and flow models.

The demo succeeds if the two-step iterative policy reaches within 5% of the flow-based policy score, proving that iteration and supervision, not distribution-fitting, are the real drivers of generative control policy performance.
