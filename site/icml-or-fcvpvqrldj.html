Evidential Reasoning for Disease Screening

EviScreen looks up similar past cases to explain what makes a medical image abnormal, rather than guessing from the image alone.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Doctors spot diseases by recognizing patterns in medical images. But most AI screening models act like black boxes—they say yes or no without showing their reasoning. EviScreen changes that: it looks back at similar cases stored in memory, pulls out what made those cases abnormal, and uses that evidence to explain why the current image is concerning. Because the model grounds its reasoning in actual past cases that doctors can inspect, the diagnosis becomes transparent and easier to verify.

Zero assumption version

The black box wall in medical AI

When a doctor uses an AI tool to screen for disease, they need to know not just the answer, but why. A model that says a scan is abnormal without any reason is dangerous—you cannot verify it, challenge it, or learn from it. Most medical screening models train on images and raw measurements, then spit out a prediction. The problem: they have no built-in way to ask, 'Have I seen this pattern before? What did doctors do when they found it?'

A beginner might think: just show a saliency map (a heatmap of which pixels mattered). That sounds reasonable, but it has a deep flaw. A saliency map is computed after the fact, often as a visual afterthought, and it does not anchor to real cases. It does not say, 'This pattern matches the lung nodules in patient X from 2022'—it just highlights pixels. Doctors want cases to compare. They want the model to think like a radiologist: I have seen something similar before, so here is why.

The core flaw. Existing screening models lack any mechanism to reference or learn from historical cases, making their predictions hard to verify or understand.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the current scan

Turn the medical image into a set of region fingerprints (small pieces described by their appearance).

Search dual knowledge banks

Look up which stored cases have regions most similar to the current scan in two databases: one of normal scans, one of abnormal scans.

Retrieve the most relevant evidence

Find the top few cases from each bank where region patterns match, and pull their labels (healthy or diseased).

Apply evidence-aware reasoning

Feed both the current scan and the retrieved evidence cases into a neural reasoner that decides: does the new case look more like the normal or abnormal neighbors?

Localize with contrastive abnormality maps

Instead of a post-hoc saliency map, use the contrastive retrieval itself to highlight which regions drove the abnormality decision.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the model makes predictions: from pure learned patterns to evidence-anchored reasoning. Ordinary screening models learn a single decision boundary from examples. EviScreen adds a retrieval layer—at test time, the model first finds the most similar stored cases, then reasons over both the new case and its retrieved neighbors.

the object it moves  =  prediction basis: from image alone to image plus retrieved cases
The hidden quantity

Region-level evidence alignment

measured quantity  =  Evidence alignment strength

The hidden quantity is how well regions in the current scan align with abnormal regions in stored cases. This is not explicitly output by a radiologist; it is something EviScreen measures internally. A region that matches known disease patterns has high alignment; a region that never appeared in abnormal cases has low alignment.

The paper infers this alignment by comparing the region fingerprints (learned representations of small image patches) between the current scan and retrieved historical cases. Cases with high alignment contribute more to the final diagnosis.

Why it works

Why retrieval-based reasoning works

The core mechanism: a radiologist does not diagnose from scratch every time. They mentally flip through cases they have seen before. If a new scan has a nodule in a spot that usually means cancer, they weight that heavily. EviScreen mimics this by storing region fingerprints of past scans in two bins—known-normal and known-abnormal—then asking, at diagnosis time: 'Which past cases does this current region resemble most closely?' If it resembles abnormal cases far more than normal ones, that region is flagged as evidence of disease.

This works better than a learned-once-and-done model because learned models must guess at all cases they have never seen, which spreads errors. When you ground each new diagnosis in concrete, similar past cases, you gain two concrete things. First, interpretability—a doctor can look at which cases led to the decision and check if they are truly similar. Second, accuracy—because you compare region-to-region (this scan's nodule to that past scan's nodule), you catch patterns a single learned rule would miss. The evidence module weights each retrieved case by how close it matches, so truly similar cases matter more than distant ones.

Math after the picture

Retrieval scoring and evidence weighting

First in words:

For each region R in the current scan, compute its evidence score as a weighted sum of matching regions in retrieved abnormal cases, weighted by how similar they are, minus matching regions in normal cases, so that regions close to abnormal neighbors get high evidence scores.

Then the compact form:

E_R = sum_j w_j * (sim(R, A_j) - sim(R, N_j))

Every symbol:

E_Revidence score for region R
w_jweight (confidence) for retrieved case j
sim(R, X)similarity between current region R and retrieved case region X
A_jabnormal region from retrieved case j
N_jnormal region from retrieved case j
A tiny worked example
Region R in the new scan (a lung nodule).
Case 1 (confirmed abnormal): abnormal region matches R with sim=0.9. Case 1 weight=0.8. Contribution: 0.8 * (0.9 - 0.1)=0.64.
Case 2 (confirmed normal): normal region matches R with sim=0.7. Case 2 weight=0.7. Contribution: 0.7 * (0.2 - 0.7)=-0.35.
Case 3 (confirmed abnormal): abnormal region matches R with sim=0.85. Case 3 weight=0.9. Contribution: 0.9 * (0.85 - 0.15)=0.63.
Final evidence score: 0.64 - 0.35 + 0.63 = 0.92. High score means R likely abnormal.

Cases that match abnormal scans add to the evidence score; cases that match normal scans subtract from it. This pulls the score toward the label of whichever stored cases are most similar, so the diagnosis follows the crowd of neighbors.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

No similar cases in memory. If a new scan has a rare pattern, retrieval will surface only distant neighbors. The evidence scores are weak, and the diagnosis is less grounded.
Biased historical data. If the stored cases are skewed (more male patients, more common disease), the retrieved evidence will echo that bias, leading to wrong calls on underrepresented groups.
Retrieval failure on subtle abnormalities. Some diseases leave faint traces. If the region fingerprints do not capture them well, retrieval will miss relevant past cases, and the evidence signal will be silent.
Done right. Curate diverse case banks. Regularly audit the retrieval quality. Monitor whether retrieved cases truly matched the eventual diagnosis. Use the evidence scores as a confidence signal: low scores trigger human review.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small disease screening task: a few hundred labeled medical images split into memory (training) and test. For each test image, show the retrieval step in action: which past cases are most similar, what were they labeled, what is the model's diagnosis, and which regions in the current image matched abnormal stored regions most closely. Compare two systems side by side: one that retrieves past cases and reasons over them, and one that only looks at the new image. The retrieval version should be more accurate because it anchors its reasoning to concrete similar cases instead of relying on a fixed learned rule.
