Emergent Communication Under Misinformation

When a malicious middle person can intercept and twist messages between agents, they are forced to invent languages with built-in redundancy to survive the tampering.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Two agents develop their own language to complete a task, but a third party intentionally mangles their messages before they reach the other. The question is: what kind of language emerges? This paper finds that the threat of deliberate misrepresentation pushes the agents to build languages where meaning does not collapse if a single word gets changed—a property called compositionality.

Zero assumption version

The real world has middlemen who lie

Imagine two friends texting to plan a dinner. Both invent a secret code to hide from their parents. But a third person—say, a jealous rival—can read both versions, and they deliberately change messages. They flip 'meet at 8' to 'meet at 9' or replace key words with nonsense.

For decades, computer scientists studied how agents invent communication protocols from scratch, assuming the messages travel safely between sender and receiver. In the real world, this is naive: governments intercept and modify intelligence reports, social-media platforms suppress or distort messages, and malicious actors insert fakes into networks.

A beginner might think: just use a longer message. More words means more redundancy, so some words survive intact. But that does not scale: adding more symbols to communicate the same idea is wasteful, and the receiver still cannot know which words were tampered with.

The method: Build a communication game where one agent sends a message, a third party maliciously modifies it, and the receiver must still act correctly. Watch which languages survive this pressure.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the communication game to introduce an adversary: a third party that sits between sender and receiver and changes some words in the message before it arrives. Under this pressure, agents redesign their language from the ground up.

The hidden quantity

Compositionality: meaning in pieces

measured quantity  =  Compositionality score

Compositionality is the property that the meaning of a message is built from the meanings of its individual parts. For example, if the language has separate words for color, size, and material, and you swap one word without changing others, only that one attribute changes in meaning. The opposite is a fused code: the entire message is one blob with no internal structure, so changing any symbol scrambles the whole meaning.

The paper measures compositionality by checking: if two messages differ in only one word, how different are their intended meanings? High compositionality means swapping a single word shifts only one attribute. Low compositionality means a single-word swap scrambles the entire message. The paper quantifies this by testing how much variation in one symbol correlates with variation in one attribute.

Why it works

Why adversarial pressure builds compositionality

Start with a fused language where the entire message is one blob of symbols, and if a single symbol is flipped, the meaning vanishes. When the intermediary tampers with words, the receiver frequently fails because the message is destroyed. Both agents lose reward.

Now suppose a sender tries compositionality: the message has separate words for color, size, and material. A tampered message might lose the size word, but color and material survive. The receiver can infer the target from the incomplete message (at least part of the task is correct). This partial success is better than total failure, because in training, any progress toward the goal pushes senders to continue that strategy—so if partial compositionality already brings 50 percent success instead of 0 percent, both agents get reward and learn to build on it.

Senders that evolve toward compositionality accumulate more reward because incomplete messages still carry partial information. Over time, the population of messages drifts toward languages where each attribute has its own word or symbol. A symbol that is tied to one attribute only becomes valuable: it preserves meaning even under attack.

The core reason: compositionality is not about elegance or compression—it is about information partitioning. Each piece must carry its own independent meaning. That way, losing some pieces still leaves others intact. Under pressure from an adaptive adversary (one that learns what symbols to attack), the evolutionary pressure for compositionality stays strong: the attacker tries to destroy the most informative symbols, so senders keep adding more independent symbols to distribute the information.

Measuring compositionality

First in words:

Compositionality is quantified as the mutual information between each symbol and each attribute. High compositionality means symbols are strongly tied to individual attributes and weakly tied to others.

Then the compact form:

C = sum_s ( max_a I(symbol_s ; attribute_a) - mean_a I(symbol_s ; attribute_a) )

Every symbol:

Ccompositionality score, higher is better
symbol_sthe s-th word or symbol in the message
attribute_athe a-th property (e.g. color, size, material)
I(X;Y)mutual information between X and Y, measuring how much knowing one tells you about the other
sum_sadd up the contributions from all symbols
max_afind the attribute most strongly tied to this symbol
mean_aaverage the mutual information across all attributes
A tiny worked example
Suppose a language has three symbols and three attributes (color, size, material).
Symbol 1 (the color word): I with color = 0.9, I with size = 0.1, I with material = 0.05. Max = 0.9, mean = 0.35. Contribution: 0.9 - 0.35 = 0.55.
Symbol 2 (the size word): I with color = 0.2, I with size = 0.85, I with material = 0.15. Max = 0.85, mean = 0.40. Contribution: 0.85 - 0.40 = 0.45.
Symbol 3 (a noise word): I with color = 0.3, I with size = 0.25, I with material = 0.35. Max = 0.35, mean = 0.30. Contribution: 0.35 - 0.30 = 0.05.
Total compositionality: 0.55 + 0.45 + 0.05 = 1.05. The first two symbols are sharp (each tied to one attribute); the third is scattered and useless.

Compositionality is high when most symbols specialize on one attribute and low when symbols are tangled with many attributes at once.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Weak intermediary does not learn. If the malicious third party does not adapt its attack strategy, it may not discover which symbols carry the most meaning. Senders do not feel sustained pressure and drift back to simpler, less compositional languages. The adversary learning to attack strategically is essential.
Attackers specialize on one symbol. If the intermediary learns to destroy only the most common or most-used symbol, senders will evolve workarounds for that one but may not diversify broadly. Partial attacks (targeting only certain attributes, not all) can still drive compositionality but require the attacker to spread its effort across many different targets.
Compositionality trades off expressivity. Forcing each attribute into its own symbol takes more space and time per message. Senders may resist if the communication budget is tight (e.g., each message limited to very few symbols). The game must allow enough message length for compositionality to flourish, because if senders cannot fit separate words for color, size, and material into one message, they have no way to build an independent code—they are forced back to fusing everything into one blob.
Compositionality emerges when needed. The mechanism works: when the intermediary is adaptive and the message budget is not draconian, agents evolve languages where each piece carries independent meaning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Implement a simple communication game: sender sees a target (a pair of attributes, e.g., color and size), encodes it in a short message using a learned vocabulary. Receiver decodes and guesses the target. Add a fixed attack rule: the intermediary randomly flips 30 percent of the symbols in each message. Train both agents by adjusting their weights (the numbers that control their behavior) repeatedly over 10,000 episodes, tracking the compositionality score and task-success rate each episode. Repeat with three conditions: no attack, fixed attack, and adaptive attack (intermediary learns to prioritize the symbols that convey the most meaning). Plot compositionality over time for each condition.

Compositionality = fraction of symbols that correlate to exactly one attribute; task_success = episodes where receiver guesses target correctly

The demo must show compositionality rising when the attacker adapts, stalling when the attacker is fixed, and collapsing when there is no attacker.
