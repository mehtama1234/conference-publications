VowelPrompt: Hearing Speech Emotions from Text via Vowel-level Prosody

Extract the emotional sounds hidden in vowels—the pitch swings and timing stretches—and write them as plain English so a text-only AI can understand them.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Speech carries emotion not just in the words themselves, but in how they sound: the rise and fall of pitch, the loudness, the timing. When an AI reads a written transcript, it misses all that. VowelPrompt captures these hidden vocal feelings from vowels (the open sounds like 'a' and 'e'), converts them into simple descriptions like 'high pitch, loud, stretched,' and feeds them alongside the text to an AI. Now the AI reasons over both what was said AND how it sounded.

Zero assumption version

Text alone cannot hear emotion

When you transcribe speech to written words, you lose the emotional clues carried in the voice. A person might say 'that's great' as a joke (low, sarcastic pitch) or as genuine praise (high, bright pitch). An AI trained on text sees only the words, not the vocal tone.

A beginner might try: use a separate audio model to extract emotions, then combine its prediction with text predictions. The problem: that audio model is opaque—you cannot see which voice patterns mattered, and it requires special audio infrastructure at deployment time. If your system is text-only, you cannot even use it.

In one sentence. VowelPrompt converts vocal feelings (pitch, loudness, timing) from vowels into written descriptions so a text-only AI can read emotion from the voice.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Find vowels in time

Use forced alignment to find when each vowel (a, e, i, o, u) starts and ends.

2. Measure three vowel traits

For each vowel segment, measure pitch (frequency), loudness (energy), and how long it lasts.

3. Normalize by speaker

Adjust each measurement so that what counts as 'high pitch' accounts for the speaker's natural range.

4. Convert to labels

Bin the numbers into buckets and write plain-English phrases like 'rising pitch' or 'stretched vowel'.

5. Train AI on enriched text

Feed the transcript plus vowel descriptions to an AI, first with labeled examples (SFT), then with reward signals (GRPO).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

VowelPrompt augments the transcript—the raw input to the AI—by appending human-readable descriptions of the vowel's acoustic features. Instead of passing pure text, the AI now sees text plus a prosody gloss.

the object it moves  =  transcript input signal
The hidden quantity

The vowel's acoustic profile

measured quantity  =  per-vowel pitch-loudness-duration triplet

Each vowel in speech carries three acoustic traits: the fundamental frequency (how high or low the pitch is), the energy (how loud the sound is), and the segment duration (how long the vowel is held). These three numbers form the vowel's acoustic fingerprint.

VowelPrompt measures all three for each vowel using standard signal-processing methods (the same algorithms used in audio editing software like Praat), applies speaker-normalization to account for individual vocal ranges, and then converts the numbers into discrete labels ('high,' 'moderate,' 'low') and finally into English phrases. This conversion makes the hidden trait visible to the text AI, because the AI can now reason over 'high pitch' as a linguistic category the same way it reasons over emotional words like 'angry,' instead of trying to make sense of raw acoustic floats.

Why it works

Why capturing vowel traits helps the AI reason better

Speech emotion is NOT encoded uniformly across all sounds. Phonetic research shows vowels are the primary carriers of affective prosody (the emotional tone in pitch and dynamics). Consonants (the hard stops like 't' and 'k') are shorter and carry less continuous pitch information. By focusing measurement on vowels, VowelPrompt isolates the parts of the signal that actually convey emotion, because vowels are where the voice sustains long enough to shift pitch and volume.

The conversion to natural language is critical: it makes the prosodic signal readable by the AI. First, it forces the measurement into discrete bins (not raw numbers), so the AI can reason over clear categories like 'high pitch' rather than cryptic floats; discrete labels are easier for learning algorithms to identify consistent patterns because outliers and noise are absorbed into the binning process. Second, it lets the AI apply its language reasoning directly—pitch and emotion are now both linguistic concepts, so the AI can weave them together the way it naturally handles text.

The two-stage training (SFT then GRPO) further aligns the AI. Supervised fine-tuning on labeled examples teaches it the mapping from vowel descriptors to emotions. Then reward-based training reinforces outputs that are both correct AND structured (formatted consistently), so the AI learns to trust the vowel cues for accurate emotion predictions across new speakers or languages, because the prosodic signal is a speaker-independent marker of emotion underneath the surface variation in accent and pitch range.

Math after the picture

Quantile binning: from raw pitch to readable categories

First in words:

normalized_pitch = (raw_pitch - speaker_mean) / speaker_std; category = quantile_bin(normalized_pitch, K); label = vocab[category]

Then the compact form:

f0_norm_i = (f0_i - mean_speaker) / std_speaker; k = floor(percentile_rank(f0_norm_i) * K); descriptor_i = {very_low, low, moderate, high, very_high}[k]

Every symbol:

f0_ithe pitch (fundamental frequency) of vowel i, in hertz
mean_speakerthe average pitch for this speaker across all vowels
std_speakerhow much the speaker's pitch typically varies
percentile_rank(f0_norm_i)what fraction of normalized pitches fall below this vowel's pitch (0 to 1)
Kthe number of discrete bins (often 5: very low, low, moderate, high, very high)
descriptor_ithe human-readable label for this vowel's pitch
A tiny worked example
Speaker has average pitch 110 Hz, std 25 Hz.
Vowel 1: raw pitch 135 Hz. Normalized = (135 - 110) / 25 = +1.0 std. Percentile rank ≈ 84%, so 84% * 5 = 4.2 -> bin 4 = 'high'. Descriptor: 'high pitch'.
Vowel 2: raw pitch 85 Hz. Normalized = (85 - 110) / 25 = -1.0 std. Percentile rank ≈ 16%, so 16% * 5 = 0.8 -> bin 0 = 'very low'. Descriptor: 'very low pitch'.
Same motion (50 Hz swing), but one vowel is labeled 'high' (emotional engagement) and one 'very low' (emotional flatness). The text AI sees these contrasts.

The pipeline turns raw acoustic numbers into interpretable English so the AI can reason over pitch and emotion as abstract categories, not raw floats.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forced alignment fails on noisy audio. If the audio is heavily corrupted or the speaker's accent is very different from the alignment model's training data, vowel boundaries are mis-identified, and the whole pipeline inherits bad measurements.
Emotion is not purely in vowels. Some emotions leak into consonant timing or voicing quality. VowelPrompt's focus on vowels alone may miss complementary cues, especially in consonant-heavy languages or specific speaker habits.
Speaker normalization can flatten real differences. Normalizing each speaker's pitch range makes 'high' relative to that speaker. If an angry speaker's 'high pitch' is another speaker's 'normal pitch,' normalization may erase the absolute signal—the AI learns speaker-relative patterns rather than absolute emotion cues.
Done right: test on held-out speakers and languages. Test generalization by training on one set of speakers and testing on completely different people. Cross-linguistic tests (train on English, test on Mandarin) confirm the method works across different acoustic styles and vowel inventories.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small emotion-recognition pipeline on a standard dataset (e.g., IEMOCAP: 12 hours of acted speech with emotion labels). Split into train and test sets that include unseen speakers. For each utterance: (1) run forced alignment to find vowels, (2) extract pitch, energy, duration, (3) normalize by speaker, (4) bin into labels and write English descriptions, (5) feed transcript + descriptions to a fine-tuned LLM, (6) compare predictions to gold labels.

accuracy = (correct_emotion_predictions) / (total_test_utterances); cross_speaker_accuracy = (correct_predictions_on_speakers_not_in_training) / (total_held_out)

The demo must show that accuracy with vowel descriptions is substantially higher than with text alone, and that this improvement holds even when testing on speakers the model never saw during training—proving that the vowel cues are a generalizable feature of emotion, not just memorized speaker patterns.
