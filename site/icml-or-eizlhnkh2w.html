Zero assumption version

One curve does not fit all neighborhood shapes

Real-world graphs are not flat. They change over time: people connect and disconnect, transactions rise and fall, road networks widen and close. In each moment, a node's immediate neighbors form a local pattern. Some neighborhoods huddle tight and circular. Others string out in a line. Still others form grids or branching trees. A node in the middle of all of them needs a different math to understand each neighborhood fairly.

Today's methods pick ONE curved surface and embed every neighborhood into it. If you pick a curved surface that works for circles, it stretches lines into sausages and crumples grids. A beginner might try to fix this by making the surface more curvy or flatter, but the same surface cannot honestly represent all three patterns at once. The neighborhood's own shape gets lost in translation.

The fix. Give each neighborhood its own curved surface, picked to match its actual shape.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure the neighborhood's shape

At each time step, look at how each node's neighbors are arranged.

Route to a matching curved surface

A routing module picks which of K expert surfaces best matches this neighborhood's shape.

Embed in the chosen curved space

Compute the node's embedding using the math of that specific curved surface.

Blend results across experts

Combine the embeddings from all experts with learned weights, respecting geometric continuity.

Apply to all layers

Use the same expert-switching in both the attention layer (what the node pays attention to) and the hidden layer (the node's internal transformation).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the embedding surface

The paper does not change which neighbors you look at, or which facts you store. It changes the mathematical surface on which neighborhoods get compressed into a list of numbers. Instead of one global surface, it uses multiple curved surfaces—each expert operates on its own Riemannian manifold (a curved space) with its own curvature value.

the object it moves  =  the embedding surface
The hidden quantity

Curvature: measuring how bent each neighborhood is

measured quantity  =  neighborhood curvature

Curvature is a measure of how much a shape bends. A flat plane has zero curvature. A sphere curves inward at a high positive value. A saddle bends opposite ways, with negative curvature. A neighborhood's graph structure has an intrinsic curvature: a tight cluster bends one way, a chain bends less, and a loop bends a third way. The paper infers which curvature best describes each neighborhood by learning K expert embedders, each tuned to a different curvature value.

The routing mechanism learns which expert to use for which neighborhood. Instead of computing a soft attention weight that adds up to 1.0, the paper uses a ranking constraint: the experts are ordered by curvature, and only one expert is chosen per neighborhood per time step. This ranking keeps the flow smooth—neighborhoods similar in shape use experts close to each other on the curvature spectrum.

Why it works

Why it works: surface alignment amplifies true signals

When a neighborhood's true shape matches the curvature of its embedding surface, the math does not have to twist or stretch the facts. A tight cluster naturally sits nicely on a positively curved sphere. A chain naturally lies flat on a zero-curvature plane. When the surface matches, every step of the embedding calculation stays consistent with the neighborhood instead of fighting it.

This alignment does three things. First, the numbers encoding the neighborhood are more honest—they capture genuine proximity and structure instead of distorted proxy values. Second, the gradients (directions to adjust the weights during training) point more clearly toward the right answer because the calculation is already aligned with the truth. Third, the Riemannian operations (like matrix products in curved space) can be computed more efficiently when the curvature is steady and known in advance. The paper exploits this with a fast attention module—instead of doing complex math to move between different curved spaces, you stay in one space per expert and combine results at the end.

Math after the picture

Embedding in a curved space with routing

First in words:

For each node i and time t, the router assigns it to expert k based on neighborhood shape. The node's final embedding is a weighted sum of expert embeddings, where each expert operates on a Riemannian manifold with curvature κ_k. The ranking constraint orders experts by curvature, so continuity is maintained across the expert space.

Then the compact form:

h_i^t = sum_k w_k * Embed_k(neighbors_i, κ_k) where router(shape_i^t) selects k and w_k decays smoothly away from the chosen expert

Every symbol:

h_i^tthe final embedding of node i at time t
w_kthe weight on expert k's contribution (high for chosen expert, lower for nearby experts)
Embed_k(neighbors_i, κ_k)the embedding computation for node i using expert k's Riemannian geometry with curvature κ_k
shape_i^ta descriptor of node i's neighborhood structure at time t (e.g., clustering coefficient, degree variance)
κ_kthe curvature value for expert k, ordered as κ_1 < κ_2 < ... < κ_K
A tiny worked example
Suppose there are 3 experts with curvatures κ_1=0.2 (nearly flat), κ_2=0.8 (medium curve), κ_3=2.0 (tight curve).
Node i has a neighborhood that is a chain (nodes in a line). Shape measure = 0.3 (low, since chains are flat).
Router assigns node i to expert 1 (κ_1=0.2 fits chains best). Weight w_1 = 0.7, w_2 = 0.25, w_3 = 0.05.
Embedding: h_i^t = 0.7 * Embed_1(...) + 0.25 * Embed_2(...) + 0.05 * Embed_3(...) .
Most of the embedding comes from expert 1 (chain-tuned); nearby experts 2 and 3 add corrections in case the shape estimate was slightly off.

When a neighborhood's true curvature matches an expert's κ, the embedding for that expert is most accurate, so it gets the highest weight and dominates the sum.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Picking the wrong number of experts. If you have only 2 experts, neighborhoods fall into two groups and some remain misfit. If you have too many, routing becomes noisy and experts split the work inconsistently. The paper uses a fixed K, but the right K depends on the graph's diversity.
Neighborhood shape shifts dramatically between time steps. If a node's neighborhood structure changes abruptly (e.g., a cascading disconnect), the routing mechanism may not adapt fast enough, and the embedding may use an out-of-date expert.
Expert collapse due to poor initialization. If all experts initialize to nearly the same curvature, the router has no reason to differentiate them, and all experts converge to the same solution. A good curvature spacing and initialization is required.
Done right. Curvatures are spread evenly across the learned spectrum, because experts only help if they represent different parts of the spectrum. Neighborhood shape is measured consistently across time, so the same structure always routes to the same expert. The routing mechanism is trained to select experts that improve the embedding, because that is the learning signal—if an expert's embedding is wrong, the loss increases and the router learns not to use it.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dynamic graph with three neighborhood types: a tight cluster of 5 nodes all connected to each other, a chain of 5 nodes in a line, and a loop of 5 nodes. Add one central node that connects to all three neighborhoods. Train a graph embedding model with the paper's mixture-of-experts approach (3 experts with curvatures 0, 1, 3) and compare it to a single-curvature baseline. Measure node embeddings: nodes within the same neighborhood should be close in embedding space, and the central node's embedding should reflect all three neighborhoods fairly.

neighborhood_cohesion = average_distance_within_cluster + average_distance_within_chain + average_distance_within_loop ; lower is better. Also report speedup = baseline_time / mixture_time .

The demo must show that the paper's experts automatically assign the cluster to one expert, the chain to another, and the loop to a third—and that this separation yields tighter embeddings than any single global curvature.
