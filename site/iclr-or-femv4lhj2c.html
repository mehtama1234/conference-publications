AdPO: Adversarial Defense via Preference Optimization

A vision-language model learns to prefer normal outputs on real images and reject misleading outputs on attacked images by teaching just the image encoder to compare clean and adversarial examples.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Vision-language models like GPT-4o can understand both images and text, but they fail dangerously when images are subtly attacked with tiny pixel noise. Traditional adversarial training (brute-force exposure to attacks) damages clean performance. AdPO takes a different approach: it frames defense against attacks as a preference problem. The model learns to prefer normal outputs when it sees real images, and reject confused outputs when it sees attacked images — and it does all this by tuning just one part, the image encoder.

Zero assumption version

Vision-language models break under pixel attacks

Vision-language models combine an image encoder (a neural network trained to extract visual features from photos) with a language model (which produces text). They work well on real images, but they are vulnerable: if you add imperceptibly small noise to an image, the model hallucinates or misunderstands it completely.

The obvious fix is adversarial training: show the model attacked images during training so it learns to handle them. But this backfires. When you expose the model to adversarial examples, it learns to be defensive in a general way — and that defensiveness damages its performance on clean, unattacked images. The model becomes pessimistic; it second-guesses itself on good inputs too.

The real problem is that adversarial training treats defense against attacks and clean accuracy as two separate, competing goals. The model cannot pursue both at once.

The method in one sentence. Teach the image encoder to compare pairs: prefer the normal output when it sees a real image, reject the confused output when it sees an attacked image.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take a clean image, feed it into the image encoder.

Extract visual features and let the language model produce a normal description.

Take the same image, add adversarial pixel noise.

The attacked image now looks identical to human eyes but confuses the encoder.

Compare the two outputs side by side.

One is correct, one is nonsense; treat this as a preference pair.

Update the image encoder to raise the score of the correct output.

Increase the probability it assigns to normal understanding.

Simultaneously lower the score of the confused output.

Decrease the probability of the wrong answer on the attacked image.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies only the image encoder (the visual feature extractor at the start of the model), leaving the language model frozen. This is key: by constraining the change to one part, the method is cheap to train and does not accidentally damage the language component that works well on clean inputs.

the object it moves  =  Image encoder weights (the numbers inside the visual feature extractor)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: preference weight

measured quantity  =  Preference margin on the pair

Preference margin is the gap between how confident the model is in the correct answer and how confident it is in the wrong answer. When the margin is large, the model clearly prefers the right output, because a wide gap means the encoder strongly distinguishes the correct visual features from the wrong ones. When the margin is small, it is confused, uncertain, because the encoder barely separates them.

The paper measures this margin by comparing two log probabilities: the likelihood the encoder assigns to correct features from a clean image versus the likelihood it assigns to wrong features from an adversarial image. The bigger the gap, the stronger the preference, because a large difference means the encoder puts much more belief in the correct answer than the wrong one, and during training the model learns to expand this gap by pushing correct outputs higher and wrong outputs lower.

Why it works

Why it works: the alignment of two goals

Preference optimization (also called Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO) is a training technique borrowed from language model alignment. The idea is simple: instead of asking the model to get right answers on all inputs (which leads to conflicting pressure because attacked images create contradictory signals), teach it to prefer one outcome over another — this avoids the conflict by replacing two separate goals with one clear ranking.

Here is the insight: defense against attacks and clean accuracy both need the model to produce correct outputs. The conflict only appears when you force the model to do both at once with equal weight. But if you reframe the problem as pairwise preference — prefer correct output on clean, prefer correct output on adversarial — you transform the two competing goals into a single coherent preference: choose the right answer, always. The language model learns to trust whichever visual features the updated encoder produces. By modifying only the image encoder, you avoid retraining the expensive language model and accidentally breaking the alignment it already has with human values.

The preference pairs come from unlabeled data: take any image, generate a clean version and an attacked version, let the model produce outputs for both, then treat the clean output as preferred and the attacked output as rejected. No human annotation required.

The preference equation

First in words:

The preference margin is the log probability of the correct response minus the log probability of the wrong response; training increases this gap by adjusting the image encoder parameters in the direction that widens it.

Then the compact form:

margin = log p(y_w | x_clean) - log p(y_l | x_adv)

Every symbol:

y_wthe correct output (winner) from the clean image
y_lthe confused output (loser) from the adversarial image
x_cleanan unattacked image from real data
x_advthe same image with adversarial noise added
log pthe log probability the model assigns to that output
A tiny worked example
Start: encoder gives log p(correct on clean) = 2.1 (high confidence).
Start: encoder gives log p(confused on adv) = 2.0 (almost as confident in wrong).
Initial margin = 2.1 - 2.0 = 0.1 (tiny preference).
After training, encoder gives log p(correct on clean) = 2.5 (stronger).
After training, encoder gives log p(confused on adv) = 1.2 (rejects it).
Final margin = 2.5 - 1.2 = 1.3 (strong preference for correct).
The encoder learned to widen the gap: from 0.1 to 1.3.

The margin grows because the encoder learns to extract visual features that the language model trusts on clean images and mistrusts on adversarial images.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Preference pairs are only as good as the adversarial examples. If your attack method is weak (does not actually fool the model), the encoder learns false preferences. Always use strong, diverse attacks.
Training only the encoder does not defend the language model. If an adversarial image happens to produce features the language model already misinterprets, the encoder alone cannot fix it. The language model is frozen.
Transfer may not be perfect. Training on a small model (TinyLLaVA) and transferring to a larger one (GPT-4o) works for efficiency, but some defense gains do not transfer perfectly. Large models have different failure modes.
Done right: use diverse attacks, include both clean and adversarial pairs, and measure both clean and adversarial accuracy to confirm no regression. Validate on unseen datasets and attack types to ensure the learned preference generalizes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small vision-language model on a toy dataset (e.g., shapes or MNIST images). Train one version with standard adversarial fine-tuning and another with AdPO preference pairs. For both, measure accuracy on clean images, accuracy on attacked images, and the preference margin (confidence gap). Plot all three to show that AdPO maintains clean accuracy while boosting both attacked accuracy and margin, whereas adversarial training trades clean accuracy for defense.

Preference margin = log p(correct output on clean) - log p(wrong output on attacked); Clean accuracy = fraction correct on unattacked images; Attacked accuracy = fraction correct on attacked images

The demo makes clear that preference optimization solves the conflict between defense against attacks and clean performance by treating them not as opposing forces but as two aspects of the same preference: always output the right thing.
