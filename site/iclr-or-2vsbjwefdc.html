HoloPart: Completing Occluded 3D Parts with Diffusion

HoloPart uses diffusion models to infer hidden, occluded parts of 3D objects by imagining the positions and shapes of the hidden surfaces.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

When you look at a 3D object from one angle, you see only the surfaces facing you. The back, the underside, the parts hidden inside the chair leg or tucked into the fold—all are invisible. For designing or editing objects, you need to know the complete shape. HoloPart imagines those hidden parts by learning patterns from thousands of 3D shapes, much like how you can imagine what a person looks like from the back even if you only see their front.

Zero assumption version

The invisible half

Today's 3D part segmentation breaks objects into pieces—a chair becomes seat, legs, back, armrests. But it can only identify the parts it sees. If the leg is pressed against a wall, if two parts occlude each other, if the inside of a hollow object is invisible, the algorithm gives up. It marks these regions as unknown.

A beginner's first try: just ignore the occluded parts, segment only what is visible, and call it done. That fails for any real task. A designer cannot edit the hidden shape; an animation system cannot move an occluded part; a physics engine cannot reason about contact forces on surfaces you cannot see. You are flying blind.

The method in one sentence. HoloPart learns to imagine complete 3D parts by training on full shapes, then uses its learned patterns to fill in the hidden regions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a partial shape

A 3D object with some parts visible, some occluded by other parts or the camera angle.

Extract initial segments

Existing segmentation methods label the visible parts. Occluded regions remain unknown.

Feed to diffusion model

The incomplete part segments, represented as 3D point clouds and surfaces, become the starting point for generation.

Denoise step by step

HoloPart applies local attention (for fine detail) and global attention (for shape fit), imagining plausible hidden surfaces that fit the object's overall structure.

Output complete parts

All parts are now labeled and complete, including the occluded regions, ready for downstream tasks.

What the paper changes

The single thing HoloPart changes

HoloPart does not change how you segment. It does not modify the input object. Instead, it changes the initial segment map by filling in the parts that were marked unknown or occluded.

the object it moves  =  incomplete part segments
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: shape-aware completion confidence

measured quantity  =  Geometric plausibility score

HoloPart learns to measure plausibility—how likely a completed part looks given the visible parts around it. It does not have a named score in the paper, but it implicitly learns to rate completions by how well they fit the object's overall shape consistency.

The model estimates this by comparing generated shapes to patterns learned from thousands of complete 3D objects. Shapes that match those learned patterns score high; shapes that clash or break symmetry score low. This invisible judgment guide steers diffusion toward realistic completions.

Paper reading. HoloPart uses local and global attention mechanisms. Local attention scores fine geometric details (edges, curves) of the parts being completed. Why it works

Why completing occluded parts works

The core insight is that 3D objects follow patterns. Chairs have legs below the seat. Tables have undersides parallel to their tops. Symmetry, contact, and gravity shape real shapes. A diffusion model trained on thousands of complete objects learns these patterns deeply.

When you give HoloPart an incomplete part segment, you are handing it a puzzle: 'Here is what I see. What does the hidden part look like?' The model starts with a rough guess (noise). Then, guided by local attention (which learns edge and surface details) and global attention (which learns how parts relate to the whole object), it refines that guess step by step. At each step, it asks: Does this look plausible? Does it match learned patterns? Does it fit the visible parts? Shapes that fail these checks are pushed away; shapes that pass are kept and refined. After many steps, an invisible part crystallizes into existence—one that matches the object's learned style and constraints.

Math after the picture

The completion objective

First in words:

loss equals the distance between the true complete shape and the model's predicted shape, measured across both visible and occluded regions, weighted by a local attention mask and a global consistency term

Then the compact form:

L = E[ ||x_complete - x_predicted||^2 ] + lambda_local * A_local + lambda_global * C_shape

Every symbol:

x_completethe true full 3D shape (geometry of all parts, visible and hidden)
x_predictedthe geometry the diffusion model generated in this denoising step
||...||^2squared distance (how far apart two geometries are)
A_locallocal attention mask (higher weight on edges and fine details)
C_shapeglobal shape consistency (penalty if parts do not align or symmetry is broken)
lambda_local, lambda_globaltuning knobs controlling how much to enforce detail vs. overall consistency
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model is trying to complete a chair leg.
True shape x_complete: leg has 4 vertices, height 40 units, radius 2 units.
Model's guess x_predicted: leg has height 42 units (2 too tall), radius 2 units.
Distance ||x_complete - x_predicted||^2 ≈ (40-42)^2 = 4.
Local attention mask A_local: edge of leg is important, so weight = 3. Penalty A_local = 3.
Global shape consistency C_shape: leg height slightly off but not breaking symmetry, so penalty = 0.5.
Total loss L ≈ 4 + 2.0*3 + 1.5*0.5 = 4 + 6 + 0.75 = 10.75.
The model sees this penalty and nudges the predicted leg height down toward 40, reducing loss on the next iteration.

Each shape mismatch is scored twice: once for local fine details and once for global shape fit. Large, obvious errors (like a leg that is way too tall) are caught by C_shape; subtle edge misalignments are caught by A_local.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Highly unusual or unfamiliar shapes. If a training set lacks examples of a particular shape (say, a twisted artistic sculpture), HoloPart may default to generic shape instead of capturing the true occluded form, because the pattern it learned does not cover this case.
Extreme occlusion. If most of a part is hidden and very few visual cues remain, the model has little to anchor on. It may hallucinate a plausible-looking part that does not match the true shape, especially if multiple completions are equally consistent with the visible evidence.
Local attention overfit. If local attention weights fine shape too heavily, it may over-smooth or over-sharpen edges, especially near occlusion boundaries where ground truth shape transitions abruptly.
Done right. When the object is a common shape (chair, table, car) with typical occlusions (legs behind other legs, underside against floor), HoloPart excels because the training data includes these patterns, and local and global attention work together to ensure both fine detail and overall coherence.

What a runnable demo should prove
