HoloPart: Generative 3D Part Amodal Segmentation

HoloPart uses a diffusion model with two kinds of selective focus to infer hidden parts of 3D objects by reasoning about the whole shape.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

When you see a 3D object from one angle, many parts are hidden from view. HoloPart teaches a model to imagine and complete those hidden parts by reasoning about the complete shape. It does this in two stages: first, segment visible surfaces; second, use a diffusion model (a step-by-step generator that refines random noise into shapes) with two kinds of selective focus (looking closely at local details AND looking at how the part fits in the whole) to fill in the hidden surfaces while keeping the whole object consistent.

Zero assumption version

The Incomplete View Problem

Today's 3D segmentation models (which carve 3D shapes into named parts like handle, bowl, base) only identify what is visible. They see a cup from one side and label the outer walls, but ignore the interior, the bottom, and the hidden rim. This is fine if you only care about the visible surface, but useless for 3D content creation: animators need full parts, modelers need complete surfaces, and material assignment needs hidden areas.

A beginner might try to guess hidden surfaces with simple rules (flip the visible surface, or assume a standard shape). This breaks because objects have wildly different interiors. A car door hides complex hinges; a strawberry hides seeds; a bowl hides an inner curve that does not mirror its outside.

In one sentence. A generative model learns to fill in hidden surfaces of incomplete 3D parts by imagining what lies underneath while respecting how the whole object must fit together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pretrain on complete shapes

Learn a foundation model by training on intact 3D objects, learning to compress them into compressed codes (short lists of numbers).

Encode visible part

Take the incomplete visible surface and convert it into a compressed code using two channels that see both the part and the full shape.

Local focus: study details

Zoom in: look at the part's edges, boundaries, and local curves in isolation.

Global focus: respect whole object

Zoom out: check that the part fits the whole object's size, orientation, and connections.

Iterative refinement

Repeat and refine the compressed code by gradually removing noise, converging to a complete, consistent part.

What the paper changes

What Changes

HoloPart changes the compressed codes (short lists of numbers that represent 3D shapes). These codes are the internal numbers a neural network uses to describe and recreate 3D objects. Instead of accepting incomplete codes derived from visible surfaces only, HoloPart refines and extends them to represent the full, hidden part.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Part Completion Coherence

measured quantity  =  Part completion coherence score

The paper implicitly measures how well the inferred hidden surfaces respect both local detail and global object structure. This is not a single number the authors explicitly name, but rather a balance: locally, does the generated surface follow the part's natural edges and bends? Globally, does it connect properly to neighboring parts and fit the whole object's shape? The model achieves this through two pathways: one pathway examines fine local details, while the other pathway grounds each local decision against the whole shape.

The paper measures this coherence indirectly via Chamfer Distance (how far the generated surface is from the true hidden surface), IoU (what fraction of the complete part the model correctly predicts), F-Score (a mix of precision and recall), and Success Rate (did the model produce a valid, non-degenerate surface?). These metrics all assume that a high-quality completion is one where the generated surface sits close to the true hidden part and connects consistently with the rest of the object.

Why it works

Why Two Kinds of Focus Complete Hidden Parts

The core challenge is that a hidden surface has no ground-truth pixels or sensor data. The model must imagine it. But pure imagination can produce nonsense: an interior curve that contradicts the exterior, or a part size that breaks the whole. HoloPart solves this by reasoning in two channels simultaneously.

Local focus acts like a magnifying glass on the visible patch. It studies the visible edges, their bends, their thickness, their texture, and extrapolates inward. A coffee mug's rim has a specific lip profile; the local pathway learns that profile from visible data and extends it to the hidden interior, because most curved edges continue smoothly underneath.

But local focus alone hallucinates: it might generate an interior that is physically impossible, too thick, or the wrong size. Global focus prevents this by anchoring every local decision to the whole shape. It asks: given the complete object's size, symmetry, and structure, what interior makes sense? This constrains the local guesses to solutions that fit the whole. The result is a hidden surface that is both locally detailed and globally consistent.

The Two-Focus Equations

First in words:

global_output = focus_on_whole_shape(part_code, full_shape_code) + local_output = focus_on_local_details(part_code) + final_code = global_output + local_output

Then the compact form:

z_complete = LocalFocus(z_part) + GlobalFocus(z_part, z_shape) where z_part is the compressed code for the visible part, z_shape is the compressed code for the whole object, and the two focus outputs are summed to produce z_complete, the compressed code for the full completed part.

A tiny worked example
Imagine a coffee mug with the rim and bottom hidden. z_part encodes the visible outer wall (maybe 10 numbers in a list).
z_shape encodes the whole mug learned from training data (maybe 50 numbers in a list).
LocalFocus zooms in: it sees the wall thickness is 0.5 millimeters, the lip profile curves inward at 20 degrees. It produces a 10-number update suggesting how the hidden interior should curve.
GlobalFocus zooms out: it sees the whole mug is 8 centimeters tall, 6 centimeters wide, and should be symmetric. It produces a 10-number update that ensures the interior fits those constraints.
Final z_complete = LocalFocus output (10 numbers) + GlobalFocus output (10 numbers) = 20-number combined code that represents the full rim and bottom, locally detailed and globally consistent.

Both pathways contribute equally in the sum; this ensures fine details do not override global constraints, and vice versa.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hallucinated fine details. If local focus dominates, the model invents plausible-looking but wrong interior details. A table leg might fill with intricate carvings that do not exist, because the local pathways sees a carved outer surface and extrapolates, with no global check.
Global constraint too rigid. If global focus is too strong, the model locks the interior to a generic average, erasing true variation. A bowl might homogenize all interiors to a single smooth curve, because the global pathways over-constrains to match a template.
Training data mismatch. The model trains only on complete objects. If a test object is radically different from all training shapes, both pathways fail: local pathways have no examples of that edge type, and global pathways cannot find a matching template.
Done right: balanced weights. Both pathways carry equal weight and train together on complete shapes. The model learns to trust local cues for small variations and global cues for large structure, because both corrections flow together during training.
