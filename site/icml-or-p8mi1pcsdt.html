HiMAP-Travel: Hierarchical Multi-Agent Planning for Long-Horizon Constrained Travel

Split trip planning into one top-level budget keeper and several day planners so long plans stay within hard rules while the daily work runs in parallel.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Planning a long trip is difficult for one long context window. As the plan grows day by day, the agent can forget the total budget, repeat places, or miss diversity rules. This paper addresses that failure by separating global resource allocation from local day planning, then adding a monitor that can reject local plans when they break shared constraints.

Zero assumption version

Why one long planning chain drifts off the rules

A travel plan is not just a list of places. It must satisfy shared constraints across many days, such as a total budget cap, variety across locations, and limits on duplicate activities. A single sequential planner often loses track of those cross-day facts because each new day adds more context to carry forward.

That drift gets worse on long horizons because a locally good day can still break the whole trip. Spending too much on day two leaves too little for day six. Reusing the same attraction style may satisfy one day but fail the diversity goal for the trip. The paper asks how to keep global rules visible while still planning each day in detail.

The paper's move in one sentence. Use a coordinator to allocate shared resources across days, let separate day executors plan in parallel, and put a transactional monitor in the middle so that invalid local plans are rejected before they damage the full itinerary.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Allocate shared resources

A coordinator sets a budget and other high-level targets for each day because the trip-wide rules must be decided before local planning starts.

Plan each day in parallel

Day executors propose activities for their assigned day using the local budget and goals they received.

Run transactional checks

A monitor checks whether the proposed days still satisfy global constraints such as total cost and uniqueness because local plans can conflict with each other.

Bargain when a day is infeasible

If one day cannot satisfy its allocation, that executor rejects the sub-goal so the coordinator can re-plan with a different split.

Reuse one shared trained rule

All agents use one trained decision rule with different role conditioning so that the system learns common planning behavior while still acting differently by role.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes travel planning from one long serial chain into a hierarchy. The top layer manages trip-wide resources and the lower layer fills in daily plans, with a monitor that can block invalid local outputs before they accumulate.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  how much future room each current day decision leaves for the rest of the trip

The key hidden quantity is future slack. A day planner sees only its local choices, but the real cost of those choices depends on how much budget, variety, and flexibility remain for later days. A meal or hotel that seems acceptable now may make the last days impossible.

HiMAP-Travel exposes that hidden quantity by forcing the coordinator to allocate resources up front and by letting the monitor reject proposals that would overuse shared slack. That helps because each day is planned against a visible budget and visible uniqueness rules, not against vague future hope.

Why it works

Why this works: keep global constraints outside local day details

A long serial planner fails because it mixes two jobs that should be separated. One job is global bookkeeping across the full trip. The other job is detailed day construction. When one agent does both jobs in one running chain, earlier budget and uniqueness facts are easy to lose.

The hierarchy works because the coordinator holds the trip-wide facts in a compact form, and day executors can focus on one local slice. The monitor is the second key piece because it prevents a local plan from being accepted only because it looks good on its own. Parallel day planning also cuts latency because several local plans are produced at the same time instead of one after another.

Math after the picture

Choose day plans under a shared budget and shared rules

First in words:

A simple summary is to maximize the total trip score across all days while forcing the sum of day costs to stay below the total budget and forcing duplicate-sensitive choices to remain within allowed limits.

Then the compact form:

trip_score = sum(day_value_d) subject to sum(day_cost_d) <= total_budget and duplicate_count <= allowed_limit

Every symbol:

A tiny worked example
Suppose a three-day trip has a total budget of 300.
The coordinator allocates 90, 110, and 100 to the three days.
The day planners propose costs of 85, 120, and 96, so the total is 301.
The monitor rejects day two because 301 is above the full-trip budget and asks for a cheaper day-two plan.
A revised day-two plan costs 103, which brings the total to 284, so the trip becomes feasible.

The bargaining step matters because one day planner can say its current budget split is infeasible, which lets the coordinator repair the global allocation before the full itinerary is finalized.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The coordinator can allocate resources poorly. If the initial budget split is unrealistic, many day planners will reject their sub-goals because the assigned limits do not match what that day needs.
Constraint checks may be incomplete. If the monitor forgets one global rule, a locally valid plan can still break the trip because accepted days are only as good as the checks that guard them.
Parallel days can still interact in hidden ways. Two days may compete for the same scarce option or create awkward travel flow, which means the monitor may need richer cross-day checks than simple budget and duplicate counts.
Done right. The design works best when the coordinator carries only global facts, the day executors stay local, and the monitor enforces the full rule set because that separation keeps each part narrow and testable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a trip planner with a fixed total budget, a no-repeat rule for attraction categories, and several days of planning. Compare one sequential planner against a HiMAP-style hierarchy with one coordinator, several day planners, and a monitor.

Track final pass rate on all hard rules, planning time, and the number of rejected day plans that required reallocation. The hierarchy should produce more valid trips because the monitor catches cross-day conflicts, and it should finish sooner because day planning happens in parallel.

rule_pass_rate = valid_itineraries / total_itineraries ; speed_gain = sequential_time - hierarchical_time

The result should show that splitting global bookkeeping from local day construction is useful because the two jobs create different kinds of errors when they are forced into one long planning chain.
