From Seeing to Experiencing: Scaling Navigation with Offline+RL

Combine a foundation model trained on real videos with reinforcement learning in simulation to teach robots to react to obstacles and move safely, without forgetting what they learned from the videos.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A navigation foundation model is a robot controller trained on thousands of hours of real human navigation video. It learns to recognize scenery and predict where to move next. But videos show only what people did; the model never learns what happens if you crash into a wall, or how to dodge a moving person. This paper teaches the model to react to consequences through reinforcement learning in simulation, then runs it on real robots—without erasing its knowledge of the real world.

Zero assumption version

Why videos alone are not enough

A robot controller trained on real navigation video learns to recognize buildings and paths, and can generalize to new cities. But the model only sees what people did; it never experiences crash-and-learn. When given control of a real robot, it may hit obstacles, and it cannot adapt—the next time it faces the same scene, it makes the same mistake.

A beginner might try to train in simulation instead (where crashes are free). But simulation looks different from real video: colors shift, shadows fall differently, walls render cleaner. A model trained only in simulation fails on real cameras. Another beginner might fine-tune the model on real interaction data, but that requires collecting thousands of hours of real robot collisions—extremely expensive and unsafe.

The core stuck point: offline pretraining teaches broad pattern recognition but offers no experience of cause-and-effect. Online reinforcement learning teaches reactivity but quickly forgets visual understanding, because real video and simulation video conflict—updating to fit simulation makes the model worse at real scenes.

In one sentence. Use anchor-guided matching to anchor different behaviors to different trajectories during pretraining; then freeze the pretrained visual layer and train only a small reactive residual layer via reinforcement learning in simulation.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Anchor-guided pretraining

For each frame and ground-truth trajectory, select the anchor (predicted behavior) closest to actual motion, supervise only that path.

Stabilized learning

Instead of training all behaviors equally, focus on the one behavior most similar to ground truth; reduces noise.

Freeze vision encoder

Lock down all pattern-recognition layers that processed real video; they work on real robots.

Residual reaction layer

Attach a small learnable module in parallel; this learns obstacle avoidance from simulation via reinforcement learning.

Real-world deployment

Run on robot with frozen real-world patterns + learned reaction layer; reacts to obstacles while keeping real understanding.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the attention layer (the component that models the relationship between agent and environment). Instead of updating the whole layer when learning from simulation, it freezes the original and trains only a small parallel learnable copy.

the object it moves  =  attention weights
The hidden quantity

Feature shift: how far the encoded visual patterns drift

measured quantity  =  magnitude of feature change

Feature shift is how much the visual encoder's internal patterns change during learning. A small shift means the model still recognizes real buildings; a large shift means it has tuned to simulation and forgotten the real world.

The paper measures this by tracking how much the encoder's numerical outputs change as it trains on simulated data. Full fine-tuning causes feature shift to grow rapidly—the model overfits to sim's clean colors and flat lighting. The residual module keeps shift small because the frozen encoder ignores sim data entirely; only the reaction layer learns.

Plain reading. The pattern detector sees real buildings and learns to recognize them. When you force it to process simulation video (cleaner colors, different shadows), it adapts to the simulation and stops recognizing real buildings. Feature shift measures this drift.
Paper reading. Let ϕV be the vision encoder that processes raw RGB. Full fine-tuning minimizes a reward signal using simulation trajectories, which updates ϕV to match sim statistics. The authors measure the magnitude of changes to intermediate activations in ϕV, and show it grows rapidly—the encoder overfits to simulation domain. The residual module bypasses this by leaving ϕV untouched and training only Θl in parallel.
Why it works

Why it works: separation of concerns

The mechanism rests on a simple separation. The vision encoder learned from thousands of hours of real video to recognize buildings, paths, and obstacles. That pattern-recognition ability works perfectly on real robots—you would ruin it by retraining on simulation. The reinforcement learning step does not need to touch the encoder; it only needs to learn what actions to take given the encoder's output.

The residual module acts as a lightweight reaction layer. It sits in parallel with the frozen cross-attention (the part that combines agent state with observed features). When the robot faces a new situation from simulation, the residual module outputs a small correction to the attention weights—enough to focus on obstacle avoidance, but not enough to overwrite what was learned before. Because the vision encoder is frozen, it still recognizes real buildings the way it was trained. Because the residual layer is tiny, it does not carry domain-shift knowledge back to the encoder.

The training loss: anchor-guided supervision

First in words:

For each frame, identify the behavior (anchor) whose predicted trajectory is closest to ground truth. Compute negative log likelihood on only that behavior. Add a velocity regression loss.

Then the compact form:

L_nll,i = -log N_h(w_x_hat - mu_h_x, sigma_h_x; w_y_hat - mu_h_y, sigma_h_y; rho_h) - log(q_h); L_reg,i = ||v_hat - v||^2_2

Every symbol:

w_x_hat, w_y_hatpredicted waypoint coordinates from behavior h
mu_h_x, mu_h_y, sigma_h_x, sigma_h_ymean and variance of behavior h's distribution
rho_hcorrelation coefficient of behavior h
q_hprobability of behavior h
hthe anchor (behavior) closest to ground-truth trajectory
v_hat, vpredicted and true velocity magnitude
L_nll,inegative log likelihood loss for behavior classification and position
L_reg,iL2 regression loss for velocity scale
A tiny worked example
Robot sees a scene and predicts three possible behaviors: go-straight, turn-left, turn-right.
Ground truth says the person moved slightly left (0.1 to the left, 0.0 forward).
Anchor step: turn-left predicts (-0.15, 0.02); turn-right predicts (0.20, 0.05); go-straight predicts (0.0, 0.1).
Distances to ground truth: turn-left has error 0.06 (smallest), turn-right has error 0.32, go-straight has error 0.10.
Pick turn-left as anchor h. Compute loss only for turn-left: log-likelihood of observed position under turn-left's probability pattern plus L2 penalty for velocity mismatch.
Turn-right and go-straight are ignored this step; no wasted loss on far-away anchors.
Result: training focuses on the behavior that was actually closest, stabilizing learning of diverse motion patterns.

The anchor identifies which predicted behavior was closest to ground truth in each frame, so the loss focuses on refining that one, reducing noise from unrelated behavior predictions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reaction layer too large. If the residual module is not small enough, it can overpower the frozen encoder and still drift toward simulation. The paper's success relies on keeping the learnable copy tiny compared to the frozen weights.
Simulation too different from reality. If the simulation is radically different (e.g., only flat indoor corridors vs. complex outdoor scenes), the reaction layer cannot generalize. The paper assumes simulation and reality share enough visual structure that partial learning transfers.
Pretraining does not encode actionability. If the vision encoder learned only to recognize scenes but not which affordances (passable paths, obstacles) matter, the reaction layer must learn from scratch. Anchor-guided pretraining helps because grouping behaviors by anchor means the encoder sees diverse ways to move through each scene—but if the video data lacks diversity, the encoder still misses key patterns, because it was never shown what those patterns look like.
Done right. Keep the frozen encoder's layer deep enough to capture high-level scene understanding, but the residual layer focused on reactive adjustments to attention (agent-scene relationship). In this design, the two parts do separate jobs and do not fight.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple navigation task in a simulated urban environment with building-like shapes. Load a pretrained navigation model. Split it into frozen vision encoder and trainable residual cross-attention. Collect a short episode of reinforcement learning with a reward for reaching a goal while avoiding obstacles. Measure three metrics: (1) success rate in reaching the goal in the training simulator, (2) success rate when tested on a different simulated environment (transfer), and (3) if possible, a qualitative test on real navigation video (human viewers rate whether the predicted path avoids obstacles without cutting through buildings).

success_real_world = (goals_reached_on_held_out_scenes) / (total_goals); feature_shift = ||encoded_features_after_RL - encoded_features_before_RL|| (should be small)

The robot should reach goals in the training simulator (proving RL worked), generalize to new simulated scenes (proving transfer did not overfit), and still respect real-world building layouts from the pretrained encoder (proving frozen vision survived).
