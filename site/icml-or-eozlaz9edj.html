Influence-Disentangled Federated Training

When someone asks to delete their data from a federated model, a new method lets you do it instantly and correctly without starting over.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Federated learning splits the training work across many devices (phones, hospitals, banks) so the data never leaves the source. But when someone requests deletion, their influence on the model is tangled throughout all previous rounds of training. This paper proposes a way to untangle that influence during training itself, so deletion becomes just subtraction, not retraining from scratch.

Zero assumption version

Deletion is stuck in retraining

Federated learning means each person's device trains on their own data, then sends a small update to a central model instead of sending raw data. The central server mixes these updates together, over many rounds. This is private by design.

But privacy law says people can request deletion: remove my data, forget me. Ideally that means the model acts as if that person was never there. The naive way: throw away the whole model and retrain without that person. For thousands of rounds of training and thousands of devices, this is impossibly slow.

The problem: after round 1, device A sends a small change to the model. Round 2, that change gets mixed with device B's change. By round 100, device A's influence is smeared everywhere — baked into millions of weights (the numbers the model actually uses to make predictions) through chains of mixing. You cannot just subtract device A's original update; the damage is done.

In one sentence. This paper untangles each device's influence during training so deletion is subtraction, not a full retrain.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Log the decomposition

Each round, split the updates into a shared part (what all devices move toward) and a per-device residual (only this device's unique change).

Step 2: Suppress entanglement

Use a math trick (shrinkage) to dial down the components that mix across devices; keep the cleanly separable parts.

Step 3: Build a trace

Over all rounds, accumulate a record of each device's removable influence (the parts that can be cleanly undone).

Step 4: Delete by subtraction

When device A asks for deletion, subtract its entire accumulated trace from the model in one step.

Step 5: Quick repair

Run a short retraining on the remaining devices to smooth out any small inconsistencies and restore model quality.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: per-device influence traces

The paper does not move weights or change training rules. Instead, it changes what is recorded. During standard federated training, each round's updates are decomposed into a shared direction (all devices nudge the model the same way) and a per-device residual (unique to one device). The residual is what matters for deletion: it is the bridge between a device's changes and the final model.

the object it moves  =  per-device influence traces
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: removable influence

measured quantity  =  removable influence per device

After many rounds of federated training, a device's influence is baked into the model in two ways: (1) through shared directions that all devices moved toward together, and (2) through unique residual traces that only this device made. The hidden quantity is the residual trace — the part of the model that would disappear if you removed all of that device's updates.

The paper estimates this by decomposing each update as a shared component (the average direction across all participating devices) plus a leftover (the device-specific residual). By tracking which weight changes are entangled (tied to many devices) versus separable (only this device), the algorithm learns how much of each device's influence can be cleanly removed without touching shared learning.

Why it works

Why it works: decomposition plus repair

The core idea rests on a simple truth: if you keep a record of each device's separate contribution (not mixed in yet), you can undo it instantly. But after mixing for 100 rounds, that record is lost. IDFT recovers it by doing a lightweight decomposition every round, storing the separable parts, and throwing away the tangled parts.

Here is the mechanism. Each round, the algorithm splits the incoming updates into (1) the average of all updates (what all devices agree on, the shared learning) and (2) each device's departure from that average (the residual, only this device's unique signal). The residual is the part we can remove. But residuals from early rounds get mixed into later rounds, so the algorithm uses a shrinkage step: it dampens the components that are most entangled and keeps only the residuals that stay device-specific throughout training. Over time, this builds a clean record of each device's truly removable influence.

When deletion arrives, subtracting the residual trace undoes the device's push on the model in one shot. But because training entangled that device with others, a small inconsistency remains. A short retraining (on the remaining devices only) smooths out that inconsistency, repairing model quality to match what retraining from scratch would give.

Math after the picture

The equation: trace subtraction

First in words:

the model after deletion is the current model minus the accumulated removable influence trace for that device, followed by a short retraining step on the remaining data.

Then the compact form:

theta_delete = theta_current - sum_t (g_residual_i_t * shrinkage_t) + repair_term

Every symbol:

theta_currentthe full trained model before deletion
theta_deletethe model after device i is removed and repaired
g_residual_i_tdevice i's residual update at round t (its unique signal only)
shrinkage_ta factor (0 to 1) that suppresses entangled components; closer to 1 means we trust the residual
sum_tadd up the scaled residuals across all rounds
repair_termthe small change from retraining on remaining devices only
A tiny worked example
Device A's trace over 3 rounds: residual_round1 = 0.02, residual_round2 = 0.015 (shrinkage=0.8), residual_round3 = 0.01 (shrinkage=0.6).
Scaled trace: 0.02 + (0.015 * 0.8) + (0.01 * 0.6) = 0.02 + 0.012 + 0.006 = 0.038.
Delete: theta_new = theta_current - 0.038 (the accumulated influence). Then retrain briefly on devices B, C, D to fix inconsistencies. Result: model acts like device A was never there, with quality nearly matching full retrain.

The shrinkage factor tunes how much we trust each residual; smaller shrinkage means more entanglement, so we remove less. The sum across rounds rebuilds the full influence path.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Device that trained for many rounds. If a device participated in hundreds of rounds, its influence is deeply layered; entanglement is higher, shrinkage is lower, and the residual trace is smaller. Deletion leaves more remnants and requires longer repair.
High heterogeneity (devices far apart in data). If device A's data is very different from device B's, the shared average is less representative of any single device; residuals are larger; entanglement is harder to disentangle. The repair step takes longer.
Deletion of a large coalition of devices. Deleting many devices at once can leave more entanglement than deleting one. The repair becomes more expensive because the model has to relearn more lost shared knowledge.
Done right. IDFT succeeds when repair is brief (a few retraining rounds on remaining data) and gap to full retrain is small. The paper shows this holds on standard benchmarks (CIFAR-10, Shakespeare, Emnist) at typical scales (100-1000 rounds, 10-100 devices).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should run federated training on a small dataset (CIFAR-10 with 20 devices, each holding 250 images) for 50 rounds. Then pick one device and delete it using IDFT: compute the residual trace, subtract it, run a 5-round repair. Compare the final model accuracy to (1) naive deletion (accuracy drops) and (2) full retrain (accuracy matches). Also measure communication cost (IDFT uses one subtraction per device; retrain needs 50 fresh rounds). Show that IDFT deletion uses 100x less communication than retrain while matching retrain's accuracy loss.

retrain_gap = accuracy_after_IDFT_delete - accuracy_after_full_retrain; communication_ratio = rounds_in_IDFT / rounds_in_full_retrain

The demo must make it obvious that deletion can be instant (one subtraction) and nearly perfect (gap < 2% to retrain) without retraining the entire model from scratch.
