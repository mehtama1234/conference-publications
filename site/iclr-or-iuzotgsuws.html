AgentFold: Long-Horizon Web Agents with Proactive Context Folding

Instead of memorizing everything or summarizing everything, a web agent learns to selectively compress its own step-by-step history into the right level of detail, preserving critical facts while hiding busywork.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

LLM-based web agents that search the internet face a trap: if they keep every raw detail of what they did, their memory fills with noise and they forget the main thread; if they summarize everything at once, they lose fine-grained details they later need. AgentFold breaks this trap by learning to actively fold its own history—at each step, deciding what to compress, what to keep crisp, and what to hide entirely.

The memory trap in long web searches

Consider an AI searching for a specific fact across ten web pages. At each step, the agent reads a page, notes what it found, and picks the next link to click. After ten steps, it has a log: page-1 says X, clicked link-3, page-2 says Y, clicked link-7, and so on. Keeping this raw log means the agent is drowning in noise by step 20. But if it compresses the log too aggressively—saying only 'I learned X and Y'—it forgets that link-3 led to a dead end, so it might click link-3 again later.

The standard approach (called ReAct) keeps adding to the history, and the model's memory gets saturated with irrelevant details. The alternative, summarizing the entire history once, is irreversible: once link-3 is erased, the agent can no longer reason about it, even if that detail matters later. A beginner would try building a bigger memory buffer, but that just pushes the problem further—at 50 steps, it still collapses.

In one sentence. AgentFold learns to compress its own history at multiple scales: keeping some details crisp, hiding entire multi-step sequences, trading off what it needs to remember right now versus what can be safely abstracted away.

What it actually does

At each step, the agent faces a choice

Keep the raw record of this action, or fold it (compress it into abstraction).

Granular folding

Compress a single action into a concise summary that preserves the key fact.

Deep folding

Hide an entire sequence of steps (like 'I tried three different links, they all led nowhere') into one sentence.

The model learns which is which

Training teaches it which details matter for future decisions and which are safe to hide.

Long horizons stay navigable

At 20 steps, history is still readable; at 50 steps, still clear; the agent never hits the context wall.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the history itself

The paper does not change the model or add new tools. It changes how the agent manages its own step-by-step record. At each step, before adding the next action to the history, the model learns to compress (fold) some parts of the history and leave others as-is. This folding operation is learned during training, not hand-coded.

the object it moves  =  The step-by-step history (context)
The hidden quantity

The folding decision: what to compress and what to keep

measured quantity  =  The compression choice (what detail level is safe here)

At every step, the agent implicitly decides: is this action important enough to keep in raw detail, or can I abstract it? This is not a hand-coded rule; the model learns it. Some actions are critical (like 'I found the answer: the date is March 15') and must stay precise. Others are exploratory bookkeeping ('I tried clicking this link and it was a dead end') and can become a one-line note.

The paper trains the model to make this choice by measuring how well it predicts the future steps it is about to take. If folding an action would hurt the model's ability to reason about what comes next, that action stays unfolded. If folding has no impact on future reasoning, the model learns to fold it away.

Plain reading. Imagine writing a letter about a trip. You keep the exciting moments in full detail ('we saw the lighthouse at sunset'); you compress the filler ('we drove four hours, stopped for lunch, drove four more'). AgentFold learns what counts as lighthouse and what counts as the road.
Paper reading. The paper trains the model to predict the next action given the current context. An action that is necessary for predicting the next move stays unfolded; an action that is predictable from its fold stays hidden. This is learned by measuring prediction errors and adjusting the model's numbers to minimize those errors.
Why it works

Why it works: retrospective consolidation

Not all information in a sequence is equally important for future decisions. If the agent has taken 20 steps to search a website and tried seven dead ends before finding one promising link, the future steps only need to know the promising link and perhaps which dead ends to avoid. The seven dead ends are themselves similar; bundling them ('tried dead ends 1-7') preserves what matters and removes the noise.

AgentFold applies this idea recursively. At step 5, it might keep the raw record. At step 10, seeing that steps 5-7 were all 'tried similar dead ends,' it folds them into one line. At step 15, seeing that the entire block 'dead-end exploration' is now useless for the current search, it folds that whole block into a single sentence. The model learns which folds are safe (future actions do not depend on the detail) and which are risky (losing this detail would hurt future performance).

Why does this work better than a fixed summary? Because the folding is adaptive. Early in the search, you keep everything; when a branch becomes irrelevant, you hide it. If you make a mistake and fold something important, the next step's prediction task will fail, and the model's error signal will teach it not to fold that detail again. The history becomes a dynamic, self-editing memory.

The folding score: which level of abstraction is best

First in words:

At each step, compute a score for each possible fold of the history. The score is the model's loss (error) if you remove that detail versus keeping it. Keep details with high loss (removing them hurts prediction); fold away details with low loss (removing them does not hurt).

Then the compact form:

fold_loss_i = loss(next_step | history_without_detail_i) - loss(next_step | full_history)

Every symbol:

fold_loss_ihow much the model's error increases if detail i is folded away
loss(next_step | history_without_detail_i)prediction error on the next step if you hide detail i
loss(next_step | full_history)prediction error on the next step with the full unfolded history
detail_ione action, one observation, or one piece of history
A tiny worked example: should we fold the dead-end link?
Full history: 'tried link-3, got page-A, link-3 led nowhere'
Folded history: 'tried link-3, led nowhere'
Prediction task: model predicts next action (which link to try next)
loss(next_step | full) = 0.8 (model is 80% confident in its next guess)
loss(next_step | folded) = 0.82 (removing 'page-A' makes it 82% confident—barely worse)
fold_loss = 0.82 - 0.8 = 0.02 (tiny impact; safe to fold 'page-A' into the abstraction)
Same experiment with the answer: 'found the date: March 15' folded to just 'found answer'
loss(next_step | full) = 0.4 (clear and confident)
loss(next_step | folded) = 0.9 (loses the date, next step fails—90% confused)
fold_loss = 0.9 - 0.4 = 0.5 (huge impact; never fold away the date)

High fold_loss means the detail is critical—keep it raw. Low fold_loss means the detail is busywork—fold it away. The model learns this ranking during training and applies it automatically at each step.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Over-folding early on. If the model is too eager to abstract, it hides a detail that later becomes critical. Example: folding 'link-3 led to page about cars' into just 'dead end,' then later realizing the search is about car prices and that page was actually useful. The training loss will punish this, but in a new situation the model might repeat the mistake.
Fold cascades. A folded detail is abstracted; that abstraction is folded into a larger abstraction. By layer three, the original detail is lost. If the model later needs it, too bad. The paper does not address how deep folding should go before a detail is irretrievable.
Domain-specific blindness. The model learns what is folding-safe in its training domain (say, product searches). When applied to a new domain (say, research paper searches), the old folding rules might hide the wrong details. A retraining per domain is likely needed.
Done right. The model is trained on a mix of search types with different detail needs. The training loss guides it to preserve critical details and fold busywork. At test time, the learned folding strategy adapts to the current search structure on the fly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a web-search agent that runs for 50 steps (e.g., 'find the founding date of company X'). Record the history size at each step. Run it three ways: (1) raw history (every step recorded), (2) fixed summarization (summary made once at step 25), and (3) learned folding (AgentFold, folding decisions made at each step). Measure: at step 50, how much of the history is still readable by a human, and how often does the agent repeat a past mistake (e.g., clicking the same dead-end link twice)?

success_rate = (questions_answered_correctly) / (total_questions); history_clarity = (lines_a_human_can_understand) / (total_history_lines); repeat_mistakes = (dead_ends_clicked_twice) / (dead_ends_encountered)

AgentFold should show higher success_rate and history_clarity at 50 steps compared to raw history, and lower repeat_mistakes than fixed summarization.
