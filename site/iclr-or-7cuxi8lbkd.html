SHAPO: Sharpness-Aware Policy Optimization for Safe Exploration

Teach a robot to explore safely by making policy updates pessimistic in uncertain regions, learned through evaluating actions at slightly perturbed copies of the current policy.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

When a robot learns to move in the real world, it must avoid dangerous mistakes during training. The problem is knowing which states are dangerous because the robot hasn't explored them yet. SHAPO solves this by making the policy update rule itself ask: what would happen if I nudged all the numbers in my decision-making slightly? The answer reveals how uncertain I am about this action, and the robot uses that uncertainty to learn cautiously in unexplored regions.

Why early exploration is dangerous

Reinforcement learning agents learn by trying actions and seeing what happens. In the early stages, they visit regions of the state space they know almost nothing about. A naive update rule treats all actions equally: it nudges the policy based on whether each action worked or not. But in unexplored regions, the agent's estimates of cost and reward are unreliable, so following the direction the numbers should move (to minimize error) can lead to catastrophic mistakes during training itself.

Standard constrained reinforcement learning tries to add a cost penalty to the reward, but this penalty is based on noisy estimates. The update direction points toward a strategy that looked good on today's data, but in regions where the agent has little data, that direction can suddenly become dangerous. The agent confuses luck (it happened to work today) with genuine safety.

The core problem. In regions the agent hasn't explored much, the update direction points toward actions that seem good but are actually fragile because the estimates are unreliable.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect trajectories

Robot runs the current policy and records states, actions, rewards, and costs.

Nudge the policy weights slightly

Create a slightly modified version of the policy by moving all weights a tiny amount in a random direction.

Compute the update direction at nudged weights

Ask: what update direction would this slightly-different policy follow? How much did it change from the original?

Reweight the update direction

Amplify the update component that points toward rare, unsafe actions. Suppress the component from already-safe actions.

Take a smaller, cautious step

Update the policy using the reweighted direction, but with a smaller step size in uncertain regions.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

SHAPO modifies how the policy update rule itself works. Instead of taking a step in the direction the current update rule points, the method first asks: how does this direction change if the policy's numbers shift slightly? Sensitivity to shifts in the numbers reveals uncertainty. The paper reweights the policy update to amplify rare unsafe actions while tempering contributions from already-safe ones.

the object it moves  =  the policy update rule
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: parameter sensitivity

measured quantity  =  sensitivity to perturbations

In regions where the policy is confident, nudging the weights slightly produces nearly the same policy behavior and update direction. In regions of high uncertainty, the same nudge produces a very different policy and a very different update direction. This change in the update direction under perturbation is the core measure the paper uses. It is not measured directly; instead, SHAPO infers it by actually computing update directions at perturbed weights.

The paper treats weight sensitivity as a proxy for uncertainty from lack of data. Actions that come from regions where the update direction is sensitive to small weight changes are rare and unreliable. The reweighting amplifies those rare actions in the update, forcing the learning process to account for their cost.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Standard policy gradient computes one gradient for the current task. SHAPO computes two: one at the current parameters, one at perturbed parameters. The difference reveals how fragile the current gradient is. If the gradient direction changes a lot when parameters shift slightly, it means the policy was learned in an uncertain region. That gradient should not be trusted to point toward actual safety.

The paper shows that reweighting the gradient to amplify rare unsafe actions is mathematically equivalent to making the update pessimistic: assume the worst case in regions you know little about. By suppressing the contributions from actions that are already safe, the algorithm avoids wasting gradient steps on refinement. By amplifying rare unsafe actions, it forces the learning process to account for their cost even when they happen infrequently. This converts uncertainty into caution.

Under-explored regions have unreliable estimates of which way to move the numbers. When nudging the numbers produces a different update direction, it reveals this unreliability. Reweighting based on this fragility forces the policy to learn conservatively exactly where it should, during early training in unknown regions.

Math after the picture

The equation: reweighting under uncertainty

First in words:

Penalty grows with how much the gradient changed when parameters shifted, times how negative the original outcome was.

Then the compact form:

grad_reweighted = grad_original * (1 + alpha * sensitivity_to_perturbation * cost)

Every symbol:

grad_reweightedthe actual gradient step the policy takes
grad_originalthe standard policy gradient from collected data
alphahow pessimistic to be, starts high and decreases as training proceeds
sensitivity_to_perturbationhow much the gradient changed when weights shifted slightly
costthe negative outcome the action produced, or zero if safe
A tiny worked example
Action A was rare (sensitivity 0.8) and unsafe (cost 2.0). Original gradient contribution: 0.5. Reweighted: 0.5 * (1 + 0.3 * 0.8 * 2.0) = 0.5 * 1.48 = 0.74. Amplified 48 percent.
Action B is common (sensitivity 0.1) and safe (cost 0.0). Original gradient contribution: 0.3. Reweighted: 0.3 * (1 + 0.3 * 0.1 * 0.0) = 0.3 * 1.0 = 0.3. Unchanged.
Action A, though rare, now exerts more pull on the policy update. The algorithm learns to be wary of A even though it happened few times. Action B keeps its normal weight because it is already safe.

The reweighting factor is multiplicative: sensitivity times cost, times a confidence budget that shrinks as training progresses and the agent gains data.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Perturbation size wrong. If the perturbation is too small, gradient differences are noise and cannot reveal uncertainty. If too large, the perturbed policy is no longer nearby the original, and the reweighting becomes invalid.
Pessimism schedule breaks. The parameter alpha controls how pessimistic the update is. It should decrease over training as the agent gains data. If it decreases too fast, the method stops being cautious when it should. If too slow, it never commits to learned behaviors.
Rare outcomes still slip through. If an unsafe action happens so rarely that it never appears in a trajectory, its cost is never observed, so reweighting it becomes impossible. SHAPO amplifies rare unsafe actions that were tried; it cannot prevent actions never seen.
Done right. When the perturbation and pessimism schedule are tuned to the task's data collection rate, SHAPO learns safe policies faster and achieves both high reward and low cost, because the method focuses gradient updates on rare dangerous actions rather than wasting steps refining already-safe ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple continuous-control task, for example a 2D reacher where the arm must move to a target but incurs a large cost if it travels through a forbidden region. Run a standard constrained policy algorithm and SHAPO side by side, both for the same number of gradient steps. Track two curves: task return (higher is better, because solving the task faster with fewer steps is the goal) and cumulative cost during training (lower is better, because cost is harm during learning). The demo should show that SHAPO reaches comparable or better task performance while accumulating fewer than half the cost events, because reweighting amplifies rare mistakes so the policy learns to avoid them even when they happen infrequently.

Pareto improvement = (task_return_SHAPO / task_return_baseline) * (cost_baseline / cost_SHAPO)

If Pareto improvement is greater than 1.0, SHAPO achieves a genuine win, because it means the method improved both the task reward and safety at the same time (not trading one off against the other).
