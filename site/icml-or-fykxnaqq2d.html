KITE forecasts future values AND their uncertainty by starting predictions from a smarter place and blocking false correlations between input signals.

Zero assumption version

The forecasting puzzle: the model starts in the wrong place

Time series forecasting means predicting the next values in a sequence. If you want to predict sales or power demand, you know part of the future depends on things you can observe right now (weather, time of year, promotions). The challenge is that you want to predict not just a single best guess, but a full picture of what might happen — every outcome and its likelihood.

The naive way: treat every future scenario as equally likely. Start your model's guess from random noise. The model then refines it step by step, asking 'which values look realistic given the input signals?' But random noise is nothing like the real historical patterns in your data. The model wastes early steps learning what patterns even look like, rather than refining actual plausible futures.

The second problem: input signals often correlate by accident. You might see that rainy days and store promotions both happen during the same season, making the model think rain causes promotion effects (or vice versa). The model locks onto these false links and makes wild, unrealistic predictions.

KITE's move: Start predictions from a smarter guess built from historical patterns, and suppress false correlations between inputs.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Extract patterns from history

Look at past sequences and find the manifold (the lower-dimensional surface of realistic values).

Step 2: Anchor the starting point

Instead of starting from noise, initialize future predictions at a point on this historical manifold.

Step 3: Apply statistical constraints

Use domain knowledge about which input variables should be independent to suppress false correlations.

Step 4: Iteratively refine

Generate future values step by step, guided by the input signals but protected from spurious links.

Step 5: Output full probability picture

Return not just a point forecast but a range of plausible futures and their likelihoods.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spurious correlation score

measured quantity  =  Spurious correlation presence

The hidden quantity is the degree to which the model's conditioning process picks up false associations between input variables. When the model processes exogenous inputs (weather, events, etc.), it normally learns all correlations it sees in the data. Many of these are real (season affects both weather and demand), but some are accidents of the training set (a specific store holds promotions on rainy days by coincidence).

KITE estimates this by comparing what domain knowledge (beliefs about which variables should be independent) would predict versus what the model actually learns. If the learned correlation is much stronger than expected from knowledge, it is likely spurious, because the knowledge encodes true structural independence while the learned extra strength comes from accidental data patterns.

Why it works

Why it works: two fixes cascade

The root problem is that probabilistic forecasting iteratively generates future values, and each step must feel consistent with the input signals you are conditioning on. Starting from pure noise means the model wastes computation moving from 'completely random' to 'plausible given the season and weather,' because early steps focus on basic realism rather than forecasting refinement.

KITE's history-conditional manifold solves this by learning the shape of realistic trajectories from the training history, then seeding new forecasts on that learned surface. A starting point on the manifold is already plausible, so refinement steps focus on personalization (adjusting for this specific weather event or store state), not on basic realism.

The second fix addresses spurious correlations. When the model learns how outputs depend on inputs, it naturally absorbs all correlations in the data, including false ones. By injecting domain beliefs (which variables should be independent), KITE dampens learned interactions that contradict this knowledge. The mechanism is simple: add a penalty term during training whenever the learned dependency pattern strays from what the knowledge predicts.

Together, these two moves reduce both the sample path space (sharper, more focused uncertainty) and the number of step-wise errors (each step stays grounded in realistic dynamics). The result is a forecast that is sharper because predictions stay on the learned surface, and more trustworthy because it avoids false correlations.

The conditioning and constraint equation

First in words:

The conditioning loss combines a baseline conditional likelihood term and a regularization term that penalizes divergence between the learned conditional distribution and a prior distribution.

Then the compact form:

L_cond = E[neg_log_p(y_t | x_t, h_t)] + lambda * KL(p_learned(y_t | x_t) || p_prior(y_t | x_t))

Every symbol:

y_tthe future value at step t
x_tthe exogenous input signal at step t
h_tthe historical context from the manifold
p(y_t | x_t, h_t)the learned conditional probability of y given the signal and history
p_learned(y_t | x_t)what the model actually learns
p_prior(y_t | x_t)what domain knowledge says should be true
KL(...)a divergence measure: how different two distributions are
lambdahow strongly to enforce the prior (higher = trust the prior more)
A tiny worked example
Scenario: predicting power demand (y_t) given weather (x_t) and historical pattern (h_t).
Learned conditional: weather increases demand with strength 0.8.
Prior says: weather and time-of-day should affect demand independently.
If the learned pattern shows weather and day-of-week entangled (spurious), divergence KL = 0.15.
With lambda = 2.0: regularization adds 2.0 * 0.15 = 0.30 penalty.
This pushes the model to learn a cleaner separation, reducing spurious links.

The history-conditional manifold enters in the initialization, anchoring it closer to realistic dynamics, while the knowledge term in the equation suppresses false correlations between input and output.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong domain knowledge If the domain knowledge wrongly declares two variables independent when they are actually related, the model suppresses a real signal. Example: thinking weather and store traffic are independent when they are not.
Manifold mismatch If the historical period is not representative of the forecast period (regime change, structural break), the learned manifold anchors to an outdated surface, and initialized forecasts start in the wrong regime.
Lambda tuning If lambda (prior strength) is too high, real signal is over-suppressed and forecasts become too smooth and miss important patterns. If too low, spurious correlations leak through.
Done right Domain experts provide accurate prior knowledge, the history reflects the forecast scenario, and lambda is tuned on a held-out validation set. Under these conditions, KITE balances realism and protection from spurious patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic time series with known dependencies: demand driven by season and weather, but also a spurious correlation between weather and store promotions (the training data happens to have rainy-day promotions). Implement a naive probabilistic baseline and KITE. Show that KITE's learned conditionals recover the true structure while the naive baseline absorbs the spurious link. Measure this by comparing the learned conditional P(promotion | weather) versus the true independence. Also report forecast error and uncertainty width on a held-out test period.

Spurious_link_absorption = learned_P(promotion | weather) - true_P(promotion | weather). Lower is better.

The demo must make it impossible to miss that KITE recovers true structure while naive methods chase false correlations in the data.
