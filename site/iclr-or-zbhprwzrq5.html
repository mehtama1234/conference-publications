Motion-Aligned Word Embeddings for Text-to-Motion Generation

The paper repairs a weak link in text-to-motion systems by changing word embeddings so motion words carry information that lines up with real body movement segments.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Text-to-motion models generate human pose sequences from language prompts. They often borrow text encoders from large language models trained on generic text, then expect those encoders to understand fine motion detail such as turning direction, speed, limb usage, and temporal order. That expectation is too strong. Generic language training teaches broad semantics, but it does not explicitly bind words such as clockwise, crouch, or quickly to skeletal movement patterns. This paper focuses on that mismatch at the word embedding level. A word embedding is the learned vector, meaning list of numbers, used to represent a word before later layers process it. The authors introduce Motion-Aligned Text Encoding, or MATE, which injects motion-specific supervision into those word representations. It does this with localized text-to-motion alignment and with a disentanglement module that tries to isolate the motion meaning associated with each word instead of mixing many motion factors together.

Zero assumption version

Generic text encoders blur motion semantics

A text-to-motion system must translate a sentence into a sequence of body poses over time. Some parts of the sentence refer to actions, some to direction, some to speed, and some to body parts. If the text encoder represents these words only in generic linguistic terms, downstream motion generation has to infer the detailed kinematic effect later, often from weak supervision.

The mismatch is strongest for words whose meaning depends on movement itself. Directional adverbs, tempo words, and body-specific verbs should map to distinct motion patterns, but pretrained language encoders may place them near other words based on text co-occurrence rather than on physical movement. The paper argues that this misalignment starts early, inside the word representation, so it should be corrected there instead of only in later fusion layers.

The problem: Text-to-motion models inherit word representations that were not trained to distinguish fine-grained movement semantics, so prompts and generated motion drift apart.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the text prompt

Start from a pretrained language model and obtain word-level representations for the prompt.

Localize text to motion spans

Estimate which sub-text aligns with which segment of the motion sequence using soft attention, which means weighted focus rather than a single hard boundary.

Build motion prototypes

Create representative kinematic patterns for different motion words so the model has explicit targets for directional and temporal semantics, because each word can then be compared against a motion pattern that isolates its intended body change.

Disentangle word semantics

Use contrastive training, which means pulling matched word-motion pairs together and pushing mismatched ones apart, to separate the motion meaning tied to each word.

Plug the edited encoder into existing generators

Feed the motion-aligned text representations into standard text-to-motion pipelines with only small integration changes.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the text representation that conditions motion generation. Instead of leaving the language model's word vectors untouched, it adds motion supervision directly to them through localization and contrastive kinematic prototypes.

The hidden quantity

Hidden quantity: word-to-motion specificity

measured quantity  =  word-to-motion specificity

The hidden quantity is how much of a motion sequence can be attributed to a specific word or phrase. In many datasets, several movement factors are active at once: action type, direction, speed, limb emphasis, and timing. If all of those factors stay entangled, a model cannot tell whether quickly should change tempo, whether clockwise should change turn direction, or whether both words should alter the same segment.

MATE tries to increase specificity. Localization narrows where in time a word should matter. The prototype-based contrastive objective narrows what kind of body motion that word should correspond to. Together, those constraints give the generator a cleaner conditioning signal because the representation for a motion word carries a more distinct kinematic meaning.

Why it works

Why editing the word representation helps

The method works because the conditioning error starts before motion generation. If the text encoder already confuses directional, temporal, and action semantics, later layers receive a blended signal and must guess which movement property each word should control. Correcting only the generator leaves that upstream ambiguity in place.

Localization supplies temporal structure. It tells the model where a phrase should apply in the motion sequence, so the representation for bend down is tied to a different span from the representation for stand up. Disentanglement supplies semantic structure. It pushes the representation for clockwise toward motion examples with the matching rotation pattern and away from opposite or unrelated patterns.

These two constraints make the prompt representation more usable for any downstream text-to-motion model because the generator now receives word features whose motion meaning is less mixed and more directly tied to observable body movement.

Contrastive alignment objective

First in words:

Matched word-motion pairs are trained to score higher than mismatched pairs so each motion word acquires a more distinct kinematic identity.

Then the compact form:

alignment loss = positive pair score minus negative pair score, optimized so matched pairs rank above mismatched pairs

Every symbol:

positive paira motion word and a motion segment that belong together
negative paira motion word paired with an unrelated or opposite motion segment
pair scorehow well the text representation matches the kinematic prototype
alignment losstraining objective that separates correct from incorrect matches
A tiny worked example
Suppose the prompt contains the word clockwise.
A localized motion segment shows the body rotating to the right over several frames.
That segment forms the positive pair for clockwise.
A leftward rotation segment forms a negative pair.
Training increases the score of clockwise with the rightward segment and decreases the score with the leftward segment, so the word vector carries direction-specific motion information.

The objective is useful only when localization is reasonably good, because the contrastive step needs plausible candidate segments to know what each word should align with.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Localization can drift. If the model aligns a word to the wrong time span, the prototype objective reinforces the wrong movement evidence and corrupts the word representation.
Motion semantics may stay entangled. Some phrases jointly express action, speed, and direction, so a simple word-level split may not fully separate which factor caused which body change.
Benchmark motion data may be narrow. If the training motions cover limited body types or action styles, the learned prototypes can become too specific to those datasets and generalize poorly.
Done right: Check whether aligned spans are plausible, include negative examples that truly differ in movement meaning, and test the edited encoder across several motion generators instead of only one.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo with prompts that differ by one motion word at a time, such as left versus right, slowly versus quickly, or bend versus stretch. Show the generated pose sequence and highlight the motion spans that the model aligns to each phrase.

The demo should confirm that changing one motion word changes the expected body behavior while leaving unrelated parts of the sequence relatively stable. It should also show that the MATE-enhanced encoder creates sharper differences than a generic text encoder on the same generator.
