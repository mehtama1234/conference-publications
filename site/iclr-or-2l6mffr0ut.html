Multi-agent Coordination via Flow Matching

MAC-Flow learns a flexible joint policy via flow matching, then breaks it into fast individual policies while keeping teams coordinated.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

In a team of robots or agents, you face a hard choice: teach them a complex shared policy that captures all the ways to work together (slow), or train them to act independently and fast (but they clash). MAC-Flow solves this by first building a flexible joint policy that captures all coordinated behaviors from data, then distilling it into individual one-step policies that each agent can run instantly without waiting for the group.

Zero assumption version

The coordination cliff

Multi-agent teams have a scaling problem. To act together in real time, agents need to coordinate (know what teammates will do). But coordination requires thinking about the joint policy (all I agents moving together), not just individual policies (each agent alone).

Diffusion methods solve this: they build a slow, expressive joint policy that captures diverse coordinated behaviors from offline data. They work well but run at 0.1 actions per second. Gaussian methods (traditional neural policies) run at 1-100 actions per second but often fail at coordination because individual policies do not see each other.

A beginner might try to combine them: run diffusion for coordination, accept the delay, or run Gaussian for speed, accept the clashes. Neither works.

The trade-off. Learn a slow, expressive joint policy in two layers: first extract it via flow matching (captures complex coordination), then distill it into fast individual policies (each agent runs alone).

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Stage 1: Learn the joint

Train a flow-based joint policy on offline data via behavior cloning (imitation learning).

Stage 2: Factor into individuals

For each agent, train an individual one-step policy to match its slice of the joint policy.

Alignment objective

Use a loss that keeps individual policies close to the joint (measured via Wasserstein distance).

Value maximization

Also maximize the total reward under the Individual-Global-Max constraint (agents choose actions that align globally).

Deploy decentralized

Each agent runs its one-step policy instantly; no communication needed after training.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

MAC-Flow changes how joint policies are encoded: instead of keeping a single diffusion model that all agents query at runtime, it extracts and freezes individual one-step sampling policies (neural networks with fixed weights) that each agent runs independently.

the object it moves  =  Joint policy representation
The hidden quantity

The distillation alignment error

measured quantity  =  Wasserstein distance between joint and factored policies

The paper measures how well the product of individual policies (agent 1 policy times agent 2 policy times ... times agent I policy) matches the original joint policy learned via flow matching. This is called the Wasserstein distance: it measures the cost to morph one probability distribution (the joint) into another (the product of individuals).

MAC-Flow bounds this distance and minimizes it during training. Smaller distance means the factored policies stay close to the coordinated original, so teams do not fall apart.

Plain reading. The distillation loss makes sure that when you multiply together all the individual agent policies, you get something close to the joint policy. Think of it as a tuning knob: turn it up, and individual policies must align tightly; turn it down, and they drift apart.
Paper reading. Equation 8 upper-bounds the Wasserstein-2 distance W2 between the product of individual policies and the joint policy by the expected squared distance between individual policy outputs and the sliced joint policy outputs (under random noise z).
Why it works

Why breaking apart stays coordinated

Flow matching is a way to learn a policy that generates smooth paths through action space. A diffusion model learns this path slowly (100 steps). But you can train a neural network to go directly from noise to action in one step, imitating the diffusion model. That one-step network is fast.

MAC-Flow does this for multi-agent teams. First, it learns a joint policy (all agents together) via flow matching on offline data. Then, for each agent, it trains an individual neural network to output that agent's slice of coordinated actions. The trick is the distillation loss: it tells each individual network to match its portion of the joint policy, not just any fast approximation.

Why does this preserve coordination? Because the joint policy itself learned from data where teams succeeded. By training individual policies to stay close to the joint, you bake that learned coordination into each agent's weights. When they execute alone in real time, they still pursue the same strategies they learned from watching successful teams.

The distillation and value objective

First in words:

loss = negative total value plus penalty for distance between individual policies and joint slice

Then the compact form:

L_pi(w) = E[o,a,z] [ -Q_tot(o,a) + alpha sum_i ||mu_wi(o_i,z_i) - [mu_phi(o,z)]_i||^2 ]

Every symbol:

L_pi(w)the training loss for individual policies
Q_tot(o,a)the true total team reward for joint action a in state o
mu_wi(o_i,z_i)individual policy for agent i: outputs action given observation o_i and noise z_i
mu_phi(o,z)joint policy learned via flow matching
[mu_phi(o,z)]_ithe i-th agent slice of the joint policy output
alphaweight controlling how strictly individual policies must match the joint
sum_isum over all agents 1 to I
A tiny worked example
Suppose a 2-agent team in a state where the best joint action is (move-left, move-up).
Joint policy mu_phi learned this from data and outputs exactly this.
Individual policy 1 must output move-left; distillation loss measures ||output_1 - move-left||^2.
If output_1 is move-left: loss contribution is 0. If output_1 is move-right: loss is large (say, 1.0).
At the same time, we add -Q_tot: if this action is good for the team, we subtract (say, +10 reward).
Net: loss = -10 + alpha*0 = -10 (good).
If output_1 was move-right: loss = -5 + alpha*1.0 = -5 + alpha. Set alpha=5, then loss is worse than the correct output.
So training pushes toward move-left.

The equation enforces two things at once: maximize team reward (first term) and stay aligned to the joint policy (second term, the distillation loss).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Distillation loss too weak. If alpha is set too low, individual policies drift far from the joint. Coordination breaks. The paper does not fully explain how to set alpha automatically.
Offline data coverage. If the offline data does not cover a coordinated behavior that matters at test time, the flow-based joint policy will not learn it. The factored policies inherit this gap.
IGM assumption. The Individual-Global-Max principle assumes that maximizing each agent's Q-value independently also maximizes the team total. This is not always true in highly interdependent tasks.
Done right. Set alpha via validation, ensure offline data includes diverse coordinated successes, and verify that individual policy outputs sum to team-optimal actions before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small 2-4 agent environment (e.g., a grid world where agents must pass a ball to a goal). Collect offline data with a successful coordinated policy. Train MAC-Flow: first learn the joint policy via flow matching, then distill individual policies. Then, run the factored policies in the environment (each agent alone, no communication) and measure coordination (how often does the ball reach the goal) and speed (inference time per step). Compare to a diffusion baseline and a Gaussian baseline.

speedup = inference_time_diffusion / inference_time_macflow, coordination_quality = goal_reach_rate_macflow / goal_reach_rate_diffusion

The demo must show that MAC-Flow runs at least 5x faster than diffusion because it uses one-step policies instead of iterative diffusion, while achieving at least 85% of the diffusion baseline's coordination quality because the distillation loss keeps individual policies close to the learned joint behavior.
