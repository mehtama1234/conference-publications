Sparse Graph Networks That Still Understand Relationships

When you remove most of the connections in a graph neural network, sparse subnetworks exist that still understand relationships as well as the full network.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graph neural networks are used everywhere — to model molecules, social networks, and knowledge graphs — but they need many weights to work well. This paper proves that even after you throw away most of those weights, there exist sparse networks that understand relationships just as deeply as the original. It shows not only that such sparse networks exist, but calculates the odds that random pruning will find one.

Zero assumption version

Why do graph networks need so many weights?

A graph neural network reads structures like social networks or molecules by passing messages along edges. Each connection in the graph gets its own weights — numbers that are learned from training data. A graph with 1,000 edges might have 100,000 weight numbers that the network learns.

A beginner might think: if the network learns all these weights, then all of them must be important. So to make the network smaller or faster, you could remove random weights and hope you get lucky. But that almost never works because the weights that happen to enable relationship-understanding are scattered through the network in a particular pattern — removing them accidentally breaks that pattern, even if the overall weight count is only slightly smaller.

The puzzle: Does the network really need all those weights, or are most of them just noise that could be thrown away without hurting its ability to understand graph structure?

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper moves weight numbers from 'active' to 'removed' via pruning. But instead of just hoping the network still works, it proves that certain subsets of weights preserve a specific ability: understanding relationship structure in a graph.

the object it moves  =  Weights, removed via pruning
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Relationship-understanding power

measured quantity  =  Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity

Expressivity is a number that measures: can this network tell two different graphs apart? A low-expressivity network might think two very different social networks are the same because it only counts nodes and edges. A high-expressivity network notices patterns like 'triangles of friends' or 'chains of influence'. This is not accuracy on a prediction task — it is a fundamental measure of what the network can possibly understand.

The paper estimates this quantity using a mathematical tool called the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm, which is a test: if you can distinguish two graphs with this algorithm, then a neural network with enough layers and connections can also learn to distinguish them. The paper proves that when weights are pruned carefully, this ability is preserved.

Why it works

Why sparse subnetworks preserve understanding

A graph neural network computes by sending messages along edges. Each message is transformed by weights. If a node has 10 incoming edges, 10 different weights transform those 10 messages, and they get added together to form the node's new state.

The core mechanism works like this: suppose the full network has weights that can compute some pattern of differences between graphs — for example, 'notice if node A has more friends than node B'. This is a particular computation that the network performs. Now, many different weight combinations can do the same computation. If you remove the weights that are NOT part of any of these working combinations, you are left with a subnetwork that still computes the same patterns. The mathematics of Weisfeiler-Leman tells us exactly which computations are possible, which means we can prove that some random prunings will accidentally keep a working combination. The paper derives how many random prunings you need to try before you expect to find one that works.

Math after the picture

The probability formula

First in words:

The probability that a uniformly random pruning to density d preserves Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity is at least d^m, where m is a quantity depending on the network structure and expressivity level.

Then the compact form:

P(expressivity preserved) >= d^m

Every symbol:

P(expressivity preserved)the probability that a random pruning preserves Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity
dthe density, or fraction of weights kept (e.g., d=0.1 means keep 10 percent)
ma lower bound exponent that depends on the network depth and the expressivity level needed
>=at least, meaning the true probability could be higher
A tiny worked example
Suppose a small network needs to keep at least 100 key weights out of 1000 total (density d=0.1). If m=3 (a simple structure), then the probability is at least 0.1^3 = 0.001.
That means roughly 1 in 1000 random prunings preserves expressivity. If you try 1000 random prunings, you expect to find one that works.
In a larger, more complex network, m might be 5, giving probability 0.1^5 = 0.00001 — you would need to try 100,000 random prunings. But the guarantee still holds.

The formula says: as you keep more weights (d closer to 1), the odds of finding a good pruning jump exponentially.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Random pruning is slow. The formula tells you the odds, but finding the right pruning by trial-and-error takes many attempts. For a complex network, you might need thousands of random tries.
The bound is loose. The formula gives a lower bound on the probability — the real odds of success might be much better because the formula only accounts for the expressivity requirement and not other helpful structure in the network. This means the formula is pessimistic, which is safe but not tight.
Temporal networks are harder. When a graph changes over time, you need to track relationships across time steps. The same theory holds, but the probability bound gets tighter (lower odds) because the expressivity test is harder.
Expressivity is not accuracy. A sparse network can understand graph structure (high expressivity) but still make mistakes on a prediction task (low accuracy) if the training procedure is poor or the task is hard. Understanding the structure is necessary but not sufficient for good predictions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a graph neural network on a simple synthetic graph (for example, a grid or a random graph with 50 nodes and 200 edges). Train it fully to understand the structure. Then sample 100 random prunings at different sparsity levels (keeping 10%, 20%, 30% of weights). For each pruning, run the network and measure two things: first, whether it still passes the Weisfeiler-Leman expressivity test (can it still distinguish two different graphs?), and second, its actual prediction accuracy on held-out graphs. Plot the observed success rate against the theoretical lower bound formula. The demo should show that the real probability often exceeds the formula's prediction, confirming that the bound is indeed conservative.

success_rate = (number of prunings that maintain expressivity) / (total prunings tried)

When the observed success rate is above the theoretical lower bound on real graphs, you have found a sparse network that preserves understanding.
