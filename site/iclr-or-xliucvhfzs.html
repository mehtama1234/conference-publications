Post-hoc Probabilistic Vision-Language Models

Vision-language models can estimate their own confidence over predictions without retraining by fitting a probability distribution over their final layers.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Vision-language models like CLIP match images to text by turning them both into lists of numbers and measuring how similar those lists are. But they give point-blank predictions — this image matches this text — with no idea if they are right or wrong. When the image is unlike anything the model saw before, its confidence should drop, but it does not. This paper adds self-doubt: a lightweight Bayesian wrapper that lets the model measure its own uncertainty over predictions, without any retraining.

Zero assumption version

Models that never doubt themselves

A vision-language model takes an image, turns it into a list of numbers (a compressed summary of what the image contains), then measures how similar it is to a text description. The similarity score is a single number: 0.95 is very similar, 0.1 is not. When you use the model on a new task — say, classifying animal photos when it was trained on everyday pictures — the model still outputs a crisp score. But the truth is: this image is far outside what the model trained on, so the score is nearly meaningless.

A beginner would assume the model knows when it is lost. It does not. CLIP and similar models give the same kind of confident answer whether the image matches the description perfectly or happens to be close by accident. For safety-critical work — medical imaging, autonomous vehicles — that lack of self-awareness is dangerous.

The core problem. Vision-language models produce point-estimates with no measure of how much the model doubts its own answer when the image or task is unfamiliar.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take a pre-trained model

Start with CLIP or SigLIP. Do not retrain.

Collect a small reference dataset

Run images through the model and save the internal numbers before the final layer.

Fit a probability model over those numbers

Use Bayesian statistics to build a distribution of plausible final weights, not just one point.

Compute uncertainty for new images

For a new image, sum up the range of possible predictions under that distribution.

Use the band to pick which examples to label

Ask for labels on high-uncertainty predictions first. Training learns faster because examples where the model doubts itself are where retraining helps the most.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper replaces the model's single fixed final layer with a distribution over possible final layers — a cloud of plausible weight vectors instead of one point. This distribution is estimated post-hoc (after training) from the model's own behavior on a reference set.

the object it moves  =  final-layer weights
The hidden quantity

Prediction uncertainty: the honest doubt measure

measured quantity  =  uncertainty over similarity predictions

The hidden quantity is: how much should we doubt the prediction on this particular image? Not the model's training loss or test accuracy, but a measure of how variable the prediction is when you average over the distribution of possible final layers. If the prediction stays nearly the same under all plausible weight settings, the model is sure. If the prediction swings wildly, the model is uncertain.

The paper estimates this by computing how the cosine similarity (the image-text match score) changes as you sample different weight vectors from the fitted Bayesian distribution. A wide range of possible scores means high uncertainty; a narrow range means low uncertainty.

Why it works

Why it works: plausible weight ranges

The core idea is simple: if you have a range of plausible final-layer weights (not just one best guess), you can ask: across all of these plausible weight vectors, what range of predictions do I get for this image? Vision-language models work by converting an image to an internal list of numbers, then multiplying by the final-layer weights to get a similarity score. Different plausible weights give different scores. If all plausible weights agree, the prediction is solid. If they disagree, the model is uncertain — and rightfully so, because it has less evidence to commit.

The paper uses Bayesian posterior approximation: it starts with the trained weights and the covariance of the training errors, then builds a Gaussian distribution (a bell curve) over plausible weight settings. This distribution captures the assumption that weight-combinations close to the learned values are more likely than far-away ones. For a new image, you sample many weight vectors from this distribution, compute the similarity prediction under each one, and measure the spread. That spread is the uncertainty.

Math after the picture

The equation for uncertainty

First in words:

The predicted similarity is a dot product of the image vector and the final-layer weights. Uncertainty is the variance of this dot product when weights are sampled from a Bayesian distribution. The variance comes from two sources: the randomness in which weights are plausible, and the sensitivity of the dot product to weight perturbations.

Then the compact form:

variance(similarity) = z^T * Sigma * z + epsilon^2

Every symbol:

similaritythe image-to-text match score you want to predict
zthe internal image representation (list of numbers from the second-to-last layer)
Sigmathe covariance matrix capturing which weight combinations are plausible
epsilon^2the variance of prediction error from the reference dataset
z^Ttranspose of z (rotate the vector so it can multiply against the matrix)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the image vector has 256 numbers and covariance is small: z^T * Sigma * z = 0.02.
Reference dataset showed prediction errors scattered with variance 0.003, so epsilon^2 = 0.003.
Total variance = 0.02 + 0.003 = 0.023, so uncertainty = sqrt(0.023) ≈ 0.15 (about 15% width).
For a different image with larger z^T * Sigma * z = 0.08, variance = 0.083, uncertainty = 0.29 (29% width — this image is more uncertain).

The variance grows when the image vector is far from the training distribution (when the first term is large) or when reference data was noisy (when the second term is large).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reference dataset mismatch. If the reference set you use to fit the Bayesian distribution is very different from your actual task, the uncertainty estimates will be miscalibrated. The model will be confidently wrong or falsely uncertain.
Out-of-distribution examples fool the model differently. The Bayesian wrapper assumes that uncertainty from weight variation is the right kind of doubt. But if the domain shift causes the image vector itself to land in an unexpected region of the internal number space, the uncertainty may not capture that strangeness.
Computational cost of sampling. Computing uncertainty requires sampling many weight vectors and predicting under each. This is slower than a single point-prediction (because you must do dozens of forward passes), but much cheaper than retraining, because you reuse the pre-trained model.
Works best when reference set is representative. If you pick a reference set that is statistically close to your task, the Bayesian posterior captures real epistemic doubt. The method shines when you have even a small labeled set from the target domain.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple active-learning loop on a held-out test set. (1) Take a vision-language model (CLIP or SigLIP). (2) Fit the Bayesian posterior on a small reference set (e.g., 100 random examples from the training distribution). (3) On a new domain (e.g., sketches, or photos from a different camera), rank predictions by uncertainty. (4) Label the high-uncertainty examples first; train a linear classifier on top of the image embeddings. (5) Measure how many labels you need to reach a target accuracy. Compare to baseline (random labeling, or labeling by model confidence alone).

sample_efficiency = (labels_needed_with_random - labels_needed_with_uncertainty) / labels_needed_with_random * 100%

The demo is not passed until the uncertainty-ranked active learning learns faster than random or confidence-only baselines, because uncertainty correctly identifies the examples the model is most likely wrong on.
