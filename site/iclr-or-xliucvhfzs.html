Post-hoc Probabilistic Vision-Language Models

Bolt uncertainty bands onto pre-trained image-text models without retraining by measuring how confident the final projection layers are.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Pre-trained image-text models like CLIP map images and text to shared spaces, but they give single deterministic answers with no sense of confidence. When the model encounters data from a different domain or a tricky example, it commits to a guess without admitting uncertainty. This paper adds a confidence layer on top of the frozen model by asking: how much wiggle room is there in the final projection layers (the small networks that reshape the outputs before similarity scoring) if we tried to retrain them? The answer is a pair of numbers for each image-text pair: a point estimate and a confidence band around it.

Zero assumption version

Pre-trained models hide their confusion

A standard vision-language model like CLIP takes an image and a text description, encodes each separately, then measures how similar they are via a single cosine similarity score. That score determines if they match. But the score is just a number with no tag that says how confident the model is.

A beginner might try fine-tuning the model on new data to learn when it should be uncertain, but that wastes the pre-training and demands retraining from scratch every time. A safer beginner idea is adding a separate neural net on top to predict uncertainty, but that net also needs training, adding compute cost and slowing down when the model answers new questions.

The real bind: pre-trained models are locked (frozen); adding uncertainty without training new numbers seems impossible.

The core move. Estimate uncertainty by mathematically asking how the final projection layers would shift if lightly retrained, without actually retraining them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Fix the model.

Keep the pre-trained image and text encoders frozen; only the projection layers are movable.

Measure curvature.

Compute the Hessian (a matrix that says how steeply the loss rises in each direction) of the projection layers using the pre-training data or a proxy dataset.

Apply Laplace.

Use the Laplace approximation (a mathematical recipe) to estimate what the range of plausible projection layer weights would be.

Convert to similarity uncertainty.

Transform the projection layer uncertainty into uncertainty over the cosine similarity scores between images and text.

Output scores with bands.

For any new image-text pair, produce both a point similarity score and a confidence interval around it, when answering questions.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies only the projection layers—the small neural networks at the end of the image and text processing models that shape the outputs before similarity scoring. Everything upstream (the main image and text processing models) stays untouched. By placing a probabilistic model over the projection layers (instead of deterministic numbers), the paper converts single similarity scores into pairs of (mean score, confidence band).

the object it moves  =  projection layer weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how steeply the loss rises

measured quantity  =  Hessian of the projection layer loss

The hidden quantity is the Hessian—a matrix that records how sharply the training loss rises or falls in every direction around the current projection layer weights. A high Hessian value means the loss is steep in that direction: if you nudge that weight, the loss shoots up. A low Hessian value means the loss is flat: nudges barely change anything.

The paper estimates this Hessian by scanning through the pre-training data and accumulating how the loss changes with respect to each projection weight. This curvature map is the foundation: high-curvature weights are confident (locked in by the training data); low-curvature weights are uncertain (the training data was indifferent to their exact values).

Why it works

Why it works: from curvature to confidence

When a model's projection layers have high curvature (the loss rises steeply), the pre-training data has tightly constrained those weights: moving them slightly breaks the training loss. This means new examples that rely on those weights get stable, reproducible predictions. Conversely, flat regions indicate that many different weight settings worked equally well on the training data. For a new example, those uncertain weights can swing around without violating training loss, so the final similarity score becomes wobbly.

The Laplace approximation converts this intuition into math: it models the projection weights as a bell-curve pattern (a probability shape) of plausible values centered at the trained point, with width determined by the Hessian. Given this pattern over weights, the paper analytically computes a similar pattern over the cosine similarity scores (via a formula involving the weight pattern and the outputs). The result is a similarity score plus an uncertainty band, derived purely from how steep or flat the loss terrain is, without touching the model.

The mechanism is mechanical: steep terrain near good weights means stable, narrow predictions; flat terrain means wide, uncertain predictions. No new numbers to train, no retraining, just measuring the terrain.

Math after the picture

The equation: from weight uncertainty to score uncertainty

First in words:

The similarity score is the cosine of the projected image embedding and projected text embedding. If the projection layer weights are uncertain (distributed as a Gaussian), then the score is also uncertain. The Laplace approximation gives the covariance of that score uncertainty.

Then the compact form:

sim(x_img, x_txt) = cos(P(phi(x_img)) / ||P(phi(x_img))||, Q(psi(x_txt)) / ||Q(psi(x_txt))||); var[sim] ≈ g^T * H^{-1} * g where g is the gradient of sim w.r.t. weights, H is the Hessian

Every symbol:

sim(x_img, x_txt)the cosine similarity between projected image and text embeddings
P, Qthe projection layer weight matrices for image and text
phi, psithe frozen pre-trained image and text encoders
Hthe Hessian matrix (second derivatives of training loss)
gthe gradient of the similarity score w.r.t. projection weights
var[sim]the variance (squared width) of uncertainty around the similarity score
A tiny worked example
Suppose an image-text pair has similarity score 0.85 with the frozen model.
The Hessian at the projection layers shows: dimension 1 has value 100 (steep), dimension 2 has value 5 (flat).
The rate of change of similarity with respect to each dimension is rate_1 = 0.1, rate_2 = 0.2.
Uncertainty in dimension 1: (0.1) times (0.1) divided by 100 = 0.0001 (small, because steep terrain).
Uncertainty in dimension 2: (0.2) times (0.2) divided by 5 = 0.008 (larger, because flat terrain).
Total variance ≈ 0.0081, so standard deviation ≈ 0.09.
Final answer: similarity = 0.85 plus-or-minus 0.09, a confidence interval built from how steep or flat the loss terrain is.

Steep loss terrain makes the projection weights insensitive to nudges, so similarity predictions are tight; flat terrain makes weights swing freely, so similarity predictions are wide.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Requires access to training data. The Hessian computation must scan pre-training data (or a large proxy dataset), which may not be available for proprietary models. This breaks the pure post-hoc dream on closed models.
Assumes outputs are bell-shaped. The Laplace approximation assumes the projection layer outputs follow a bell curve pattern (a probability shape). Real outputs may have outliers or skew, breaking the assumption and inflating or deflating uncertainty bands.
Only touches projection layers. The uncertainty only accounts for the projection layer weights, not variations in the frozen encoders. Uncertainty in the encoder outputs themselves is ignored, potentially undercounting total uncertainty.
Assumes independence between modalities. The math treats image and text projection layer uncertainties as independent, which is an approximation. In reality, the encoders may be coupled, violating this assumption.
Done right. When the data is in-distribution and the model is well-trained, the uncertainty bands are well-calibrated: scores that the model flags as uncertain are genuinely harder to predict, and confident scores are stable across domains.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small active learning loop: train a classifier on a small labeled subset of a vision-text dataset (e.g., a domain-shifted split of CIFAR or ImageNet captions), then use BayesVLM uncertainty to pick the next images to label. Compare against random selection or uncertainty from a naive fine-tuned model. The demo should show that BayesVLM picks harder, trickier examples (ones near decision boundaries or from rare regions), leading to faster accuracy growth per labeled example because the learner focuses effort on the toughest cases first.

sample efficiency = number of labels needed to reach 90% accuracy; lower is better. Active learning gain = (random labels needed) / (BayesVLM labels needed) = should be > 1.5 for a meaningful result.

The demo must make impossible to miss that the model knows which examples it is uncertain about—and that certainty comes from measuring how steep or flat the loss terrain is, not from retraining.
