Aitchison Embeddings for Compositional Graph Representations

Represent nodes in graphs as mixtures of archetypes, using log-ratios to measure distances so the mixtures stay interpretable during learning.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graph neural networks learn to classify nodes and predict links, but the learned numbers are opaque—nobody can tell what features mean or how they relate to graph structure. This paper proposes a new way to embed nodes using a mathematical structure built for comparing mixtures: each node becomes a list of fractions (adding to one) that say how much it belongs to each underlying archetype (role). Because the numbers directly encode role fractions with a built-in meaning, the node representations are readable by construction, with no need for post-hoc analysis or tricks after training.

Zero assumption version

Node representations are black boxes

In a graph (say, a social network or a citation network), each node (person, paper) needs a numerical description so the model can classify it or predict links to other nodes, because the numbers are the model's internal language for reasoning. Today's methods learn a list of numbers for each node—a vector (just a list of numbers). But these numbers are black boxes. If a node gets a high score for some feature, you have no idea what that feature means, how it relates to the node's actual role in the graph, or which features matter most for a decision.

A beginner might try: just list which archetypal roles (like 'hub node', 'specialist node', 'bridge node') apply to each node. But without proper math, there is no clean way to measure distance between two mixtures, to train representations while keeping mixtures consistent, or to remove a role and ask 'what changes?'. The math breaks down.

In one sentence. Use a mathematical space designed for mixtures so that node representations are readable mixtures of archetypes, with distances that respect mixture structure.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Assume role-mixture structure

Each node is a mixture: so much role A, so much role B, so much role C.

Use the right distance measure

Aitchison space (designed for comparing mixtures) says distance is in log-ratios of roles, not absolute values.

Map to unconstrained space

Isometric log-ratio (ILR) coordinates convert the mixture into free numbers that preserve distance.

Learn in unconstrained space

Optimize node embeddings in regular Euclidean numbers (no mixture constraint); all standard training methods work here.

Read back the roles

Invert the ILR map to recover how much of each role the node holds; remove roles and check if distances stay consistent.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: node representations

Instead of opaque lists of numbers, nodes are now represented as explicit mixtures: a list of fractions (summing to one) describing how much the node embodies each of a fixed set of latent archetypes (underlying roles). The representation still lives in a mathematical space, but that space is built for mixtures, so the values themselves are interpretable.

the object it moves  =  node representations as mixtures
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: archetype weights

measured quantity  =  each node's mixture over archetypes

The paper measures one key hidden quantity: for every node, how much does it belong to each of the learned archetypes (like 'hub', 'specialist', 'bridge')? These fractions sum to one and define the node's role signature. In standard graph embeddings, these weightings are never named or measured; they are buried in the opaque numbers.

The paper estimates this by reversing the ILR (isometric log-ratio) map: starting from the learned embedding coordinates, it solves for the mixture fractions. It then audits the method by removing one or more archetypes, renormalizing the mixture, and checking whether distances and predictions remain coherent—a principled way to test that the roles are real.

Why it works

Why it works: measuring mixture distances with log-ratios

Standard embeddings live in regular Euclidean space, where distances are measured the same way in all directions. But mixtures are different: if a node is 50% role A and 50% role B, moving it to 60% role A and 40% role B is not a 'small' change in standard distance—the roles are competing, so a small shift in one forces a shift in all the others. Aitchison space (the right distance measure for mixtures) captures this by measuring distance via log-ratios: the distance between two nodes depends on the ratios of their role fractions, not the fractions themselves.

The paper uses isometric log-ratio (ILR) coordinates: a mathematical map that converts the mixture-constraint (fractions sum to one) into regular unconstrained numbers. This map preserves distances: two nodes that are close in Aitchison space stay close in ILR space. So the model can learn freely in regular space (all standard training methods work), because it can nudge the numbers without worrying about the mixture constraint, yet the learned representation still respects mixture structure because the map preserves distances between nodes. When you read back the node's role fractions, the structure is still there: removing a role and renormalizing preserves distances in the remaining roles—a signal that the model learned real archetypal roles, not accidents.

Math after the picture

The Aitchison distance in log-ratio space

First in words:

The squared distance between two nodes in Aitchison space is the sum of squared differences of log-ratios of their role fractions.

Then the compact form:

d_Aitchson^2 = sum_i (log(w_i^A / w_i^B))^2

Every symbol:

w_i^Afraction of role i for node A
w_i^Bfraction of role i for node B
sum_isummed over all archetypes
A tiny worked example
Node A: 40% role-1, 60% role-2 (ratio = 40/60 = 0.667, log = -0.176).
Node B: 50% role-1, 50% role-2 (ratio = 50/50 = 1.0, log = 0.0).
Log-ratio difference: 0.0 - (-0.176) = 0.176.
Squared contribution: 0.176^2 = 0.031.
If two roles, total distance: sqrt(0.031) ≈ 0.176. (In Euclidean space, same counts would give distance 0.1, missing the mixture structure.)

Log-ratios ignore absolute sizes and focus on which roles are favored, so distances respect mixture trade-offs.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too many archetypes chosen. If K (the number of roles) is too high, the model can assign each node a unique role mixture; roles blur together and the distance structure becomes noise. The method assumes K is known and interpretable, which requires domain knowledge or careful tuning.
ILR basis is arbitrary. The map from mixtures to ILR coordinates depends on a choice of basis (which roles are 'primary', which are 'secondary'). Different bases preserve distance but change the visual appearance of the embeddings. Picking a bad basis can make the learned coordinates harder to interpret.
Subcompositional coherence can fail on graph structure. Removing a role from a highly interconnected node (one that depends on all roles for prediction) can break the model's ability to handle that node. The method works best when roles partition the graph into loosely-coupled clusters.
Done right: fix the ILR basis via domain logic (roles should reflect graph structure), tune K on a validation set, and audit coherence on held-out nodes. These choices are visible and tunable, making the method more transparent than black-box embeddings.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small citation network (e.g., 100 papers connected by 'cites' edges). Learn representations with three archetypes (e.g., 'foundational', 'applied', 'recent'). For each node, read out the role fractions and verify that they align with paper metadata (foundational papers should score high on 'foundational', etc.). Then remove one role (e.g., 'recent'), renormalize the remaining two, and recompute link predictions. The predictions should remain stable and nearly correct, confirming that the log-ratio distances truly respect the role structure. Finally, compare to a standard graph neural network representation: show that you can extract role fractions for the standard method after training, but the fractions are unstable (small perturbations to training flip which roles dominate), whereas Aitchison fractions are stable.

stability = mean absolute difference in role fractions before-and-after removing one archetype, averaged over all nodes

The demo makes it impossible to miss that Aitchison representations have readable roles that persist under role removal (because the log-ratio distance structure is preserved), while standard neural-network representations do not persist because their learned numbers have no built-in meaning for roles.
