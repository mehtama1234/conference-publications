Bridging different types of data together

Papers connect vision and language, audio and text, and multiple views of the same object by learning representations that make different data types comparable and usable together.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Real-world data often comes in multiple forms: an image with a caption, a video with audio and text, a medical scan with a patient history. Humans understand how these pieces connect because our brains naturally link different types of input — reading the caption enriches what we see in the image, hearing speech narrows down what we notice in a video. But for a machine to learn from this, representations of images, text, and audio need to be in a shared space where they can be compared and aligned. If you train separately, each modality lives in its own world and they never communicate with each other.

The difficulty is that different modalities have different structures. An image is a spatial grid of pixels, text is a sequence of words, audio is a sequence of waveforms. They are not naturally comparable. Researchers address this by learning alignment: train a model so that an image's representation and its caption's representation are close together in a shared number-space, while mismatched pairs are far apart. This is conceptually similar to contrastive learning, but applied across modalities. The challenge is ensuring alignment happens at the right level: a model might trivially solve this by learning that if an image contains a dog and the text says 'dog,' they match, without learning anything deeper about visual or linguistic structure.

Across 63 papers, researchers develop methods for image-text (like CLIP), audio-text, video-text, and even rarer combinations. The payoff is large: models trained to align vision and language have become the foundation for visual question answering, image search, and text-guided image generation. Papers explore different loss functions, different sampling strategies for constructing positive and negative pairs, and how to handle missing modalities or noisy alignment labels. The success of these methods has shown that self-supervised multimodal learning often works better than any single-modality approach because each modality provides complementary information — text grounds the visual content in language meaning, and images constrain the many possible meanings of text.

How the work breaks down

Connecting different types of data—images with text, audio with text, multiple views of the same object—requires learning shared representations. Research splits into five directions: vision-language alignment at scale, handling noisy and unpaired multimodal data, aligning across more than two modalities or unusual pairs, making shared spaces work better by choosing the right mathematical shape, and fixing alignment failures in downstream tasks.

Core vision-language alignment at scale

The foundational problem here is that images and text need to live in the same mathematical space so that matching pairs are close and mismatched pairs are far apart. Simply training separate models for images and text leaves them unable to compare—the image model produces numbers that have no meaning to the text model. Researchers solve this by learning alignment: training so that an image representation and its caption's representation pull toward each other.

The Advancing Analytic Class-Incremental Learning paper (“Advancing Analytic Class-Incremental Learning through Vision-Language”) tackles a specific hard case: a model must keep learning new classes over time without forgetting old ones, and it must use vision and language together to stay accurate. The PHyCLIP paper (“PHyCLIP: $\ell_1$-Product of Hyperbolic Factors Unifies”) goes deeper by arguing that a single shared space isn't enough—objects have hierarchies (a dog is a type of animal) and parts that combine (a red dog is different from a red car). It uses a non-Euclidean space where relationships spread out more the further from the center, which naturally handles hierarchies by giving them more room. The Convergent Representation of Contrastive Vision-Language Models paper (“The Convergent Representation of Contrastive Vision-Language”) proves mathematically why current alignment works: it discovers that vision and language vectors (numbers representing images and text) converge to a shared structure even though images and text look completely different to start. However, CLIP Behaves like a Bag-of-Words Model paper (“CLIP Behaves like a Bag-of-Words Model”) shows a flaw: CLIP treats each word independently and misses how words combine—if the text is 'red dog' and the image is a 'red car,' CLIP still thinks they match because it sees 'red' and 'dog' separately. A simple linear transformation (scaling and rotation) to text vectors solves this.

Handling noisy alignment and missing pairs

Real-world multimodal data is messy. Images on the web have captions that are wrong or only partially describe the image. Sometimes you have images without captions, or captions without matching images—unpaired data. Alignment needs to work anyway, and it needs to correct for the noise. The question is: how can you learn when the pairing signal is unreliable?

Better Together paper (“Better Together: Leveraging Unpaired Multimodal Data”) proves something important: even if you have unpaired image data and unpaired text data (never shown together), you can still learn better representations than if you only used one type of data. The reason is that each modality constrains the other—images show what's visual, text narrows down what's meant. Cross-Modal Knowledge Distillation without Paired Data paper (“Cross-Modal Knowledge Distillation without Paired Data”) goes further by teaching one modality to learn from the other even when pairs don't exist: a model trained on images and text together can teach a text-only model without ever seeing matching pairs. Distributional Vision-Language Alignment by Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence paper (“Distributional Vision-Language Alignment by Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence”) identifies a mathematical problem: standard alignment methods push representations close (alignment) but also try to spread them out (uniformity) at the same time, creating a conflict. It uses a different distance metric that resolves this. Intra-Modal Neighbors Never Lie paper (“Intra-Modal Neighbors Never Lie: Rectifying Inter-Modal”) solves a specific noise problem: when image-caption pairs are noisy, using the structure within each modality helps—if two images are visually similar, their captions probably agree, so the model can trust that signal. Learning with Admissibility paper (“Learning with Admissibility: Robust Fuzzy Hashing”) handles labels that are wrong by learning which label errors are still useful and which will confuse the model. Learning with Dual-level Noisy Correspondence paper (“Learning with Dual-level Noisy Correspondence for”) finds that noisy pairs appear at two levels: instance pairs and modality relationships can both be wrong, and it needs to handle both. SOTAlign paper (“SOTAlign: Semi-Supervised Alignment of Unimodal Vision”) uses optimal transport, a mathematical method for finding the best way to match one distribution to another, to align unpaired vision and language data.

Multi-view and multimodal clustering

When you have multiple views of the same object—like a 3D object photographed from different angles, or a medical image with text description and scan parameters—you need representations where all views of the same thing cluster together. This differs from image-text alignment: here, each view alone should recognize the same object, not just match to a label. Multiple views matter because they provide redundancy: if one view is partial or noisy, others can still identify the object correctly.

Dual-Branch Representations with Dynamic Gated Fusion paper (“Dual-Branch Representations with Dynamic Gated Fusion”) uses two processing branches that interact: one learns what's consistent across views, the other learns what's unique to each view, then they fuse. Dual-stage Contrastive Learning-enhanced Multi-view Variational Clustering paper (“Dual-stage Contrastive Learning-enhanced Multi-view Variational Clustering”) applies contrastive learning—pushing same-object views close, different-object views far—over two stages to improve clustering, because enforcing this structure twice helps the model learn steadier cross-view relationships. GFD-EMVC paper (“GFD-EMVC: Evolutionary Multi-View Classification with Label”) addresses a practical problem: in real multi-view data, some labels are wrong, and this breaks clustering. It learns which views are reliable by checking if they agree. Label-Guided Representation Learning paper (“Label-Guided Representation Learning for Incomplete Multi-View”) uses labels when available—if you know this image shows a dog, use that to guide which views should align. Multimodal Classification via Total Correlation Maximization paper (“Multimodal Classification via Total Correlation Maximization”) measures how much information flows between modalities: if one modality completely predicts another, they're redundant; if they disagree, one is probably wrong. It maximizes the useful cross-modal information. PIRN paper (“PIRN: Prototypical-based Intra-modal Reconstruction with Normality”) handles a hard case: multi-modal anomaly detection, where normal data has all modalities aligned but abnormal data might have misaligned modalities—a person with an unusual voice saying something typical would be anomalous. Supporting Multimodal Intermediate Fusion paper (“Supporting Multimodal Intermediate Fusion with Informatic”) proves that combining modalities in the middle (not at input, not at output) works best, and shows mathematically why: it preserves enough structure from each modality to let the combination capture their unique structure.

Cross-modal flow and few-shot learning

Learning representations from a few examples is hard because there is little data. Adding another modality helps: if you see three images of dogs and three text descriptions of dogs, you have more signal than six images alone because the images show what a dog looks like and the text shows what makes a dog different from other animals, so together they give two independent views of dog-ness. The challenge is aligning cross-modality information while adapting to new classes quickly.

Exploring Cross-Modal Flows for Few-Shot Learning paper (“Exploring Cross-Modal Flows for Few-Shot Learning”) models how information flows between modalities iteratively: first use text to guide image processing, then refine based on the image-guided text, back and forth. This rectification process finds better alignment than a single pass because each iteration reduces misalignment from the previous step, gradually converging to a better shared representation. See, Act, Adapt paper (“See, Act, Adapt: Active Perception for”) uses a vision-language model as a guide: it shows the model a task (e.g., find the dog in this 3D scene) and lets the model act on that scene, observe what happens, then adapt its representations based on what it learned. DVLA-RL paper (“DVLA-RL: Dual-Level Vision–Language Alignment with Reinforcement”) applies reinforcement learning (RL) gating: it learns to decide whether to trust the image or text more for each class, because some classes are visually distinctive and others are named distinctively. Rank-guided Diffusion for Noise Few-Shot Learning paper (“Rank-guided Diffusion for Noise Few-Shot Learning”) assumes the true prototypes (average representations of each class) lie on a lower-dimensional surface, so it finds and uses that surface to reduce the impact of noise. Universal Algorithm-Implicit Learning paper (“Universal Algorithm-Implicit Learning”) learns a meta-algorithm that works across modalities: training only on images, it learns a general way to process data that also works on unseen modalities like text and audio.

Beyond pairs: cross-lingual, multi-sensor, and specialized alignment

Alignment often assumes image-text pairs, but real applications need more. Audio and text together describe a video. Medical sensors produce time-series that need semantic meaning. Models trained in one language need to work in others. Researchers extend alignment beyond the standard two-modality case.

Building spatial world models from sparse transitional episodic memories paper (“Building spatial world models from sparse”) aligns spatial navigation across modalities: it learns where things are by connecting visual observations, movements, and memory. Characterizing Human Semantic Navigation paper (“Characterizing Human Semantic Navigation in Concept”) studies how humans navigate concept space—moving from one idea to a related one—and uses math to track this as trajectories in vector space, enabling cross-language studies. Improving Semantic Proximity in English-Centric Information Retrieval paper (“Improving Semantic Proximity in English-Centric Information”) finds that multilingual vectors are biased: they work better for English than other languages because more English text exists in training. It realigns them by forcing each language to align equally. Beyond Instance-Level Alignment paper (“Beyond Instance-Level Alignment: Dual-Level Optimal Transport”) handles audio-text retrieval: audio and text are both sequences but have different structure (sound happens over time, words are discrete), so it aligns at both the individual level and the distribution level. Giving Sensors a Voice paper (“Giving Sensors a Voice: Multimodal JEPA”) treats time-series sensor data (temperature, pressure, etc.) like it has language: it learns what each sensor reading means by aligning it to text descriptions, so a temperature spike can be matched to 'high fever' or 'heat wave' depending on context. Hyperbolic Multimodal Continual Learning paper (“Hyperbolic Multimodal Continual Learning”) combines two ideas: it learns from multiple modalities (like medical image plus text notes) while adapting to new tasks over time, using a space where relationships spread out more the further from center to preserve hierarchical structure. Multi-Way Representation Alignment paper (“Multi-Way Representation Alignment”) aligns three or more modalities at once: instead of learning image-to-text and text-to-audio separately, it learns a single space where all three live together. Steering Embedding Models with Geometric Rotation paper (“Steering Embedding Models with Geometric Rotation”) shows that meaning shifts consistently across languages: the transformation from one language's vector space to another's is roughly a rotation, so rotating one language's vectors can map them to another.
