Image Restoration via Diffusion Models with Dynamic Resolution

Instead of denoising an image at full resolution all the way through, start small and upscale mid-process to slash runtime while keeping picture quality.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Diffusion models work by taking a noisy picture and gradually denoising it step by step. But when the picture is huge, each denoising step is slow. This paper shows a trick: do the early denoising steps at low resolution, then upscale to full resolution partway through. The model learns to handle this resolution jump because the jump is just extra noise to remove at that point in the timeline, and the result is much faster—with barely any loss in final quality, because early steps just clean up bulk noise which does not need full detail.

Zero assumption version

Why diffusion restoration is slow

A diffusion model restores a broken or blurry image by slowly denoising it. It takes many steps—often hundreds—each one cleaning up the noise a little more. The problem: if you work with the full resolution (a big image with thousands of pixels in each direction), every single step is expensive because the neural network has to process all those pixels.

One natural idea: do the early steps in a compressed space. A variational autoencoder squeezes the image down, the diffusion happens in that tiny space, then you decompress back up. This saves time per step because the neural network works on fewer pixels. But here is the catch: you have to run the compressor and decompressor many times—once for almost every denoising step. All that encoder-decoder work actually ends up slower than just denoising the full-resolution image directly because the overhead of compression and decompression swallows the per-step savings.

The fix: Start denoising at low resolution for the early noisy steps, then upscale once to full resolution for the final refinement steps, where most quality gains happen.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Fine-tune the diffusion model

Start with a trained pixel-space diffusion model; teach it to work with multiple resolution levels during noise removal.

Low-res denoising phase

Shrink the noisy image down, then run many denoising steps at small resolution—fast because the image is tiny.

Detect quality trigger

Watch when the model's best guess (clean image) becomes stable; that is the signal to upscale.

Upscale and inject noise

Expand the small result back to full size; add some noise to match the diffusion timeline at that point.

High-res refinement

Run final denoising steps at full resolution; the structure learned from low-res phase guides detail recovery.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the resolution trajectory during denoising. Instead of staying at one resolution throughout, the diffusion process jumps from a small size partway to full size. The model learns this jump as part of its training and knows how to upsample without losing quality.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: per-step cost

measured quantity  =  Computational overhead per denoising step

Each step in the diffusion process has a cost: the time to run the neural network prediction once on an image of a given size. A huge image costs a lot; a small image costs much less. By spending most steps on a small image and only the last few steps at full resolution, the total cost drops dramatically.

The paper does not just measure wall-clock time—it counts the number of times the neural network runs (called neural function evaluations, or NFEs). Fewer NFEs means less computation overall. Existing latent-space methods require repeated encoder-decoder cycles, which add hidden overhead that negates their per-step savings.

Why it works

Why splitting resolution works

Diffusion models work in two phases: the early steps kill coarse noise and establish overall structure, and the late steps add fine details and polish. Coarse noise is structure-agnostic: it does not matter if you are cleaning a small image or a large image; the learning task is the same thing—remove big grain from an ugly texture. Detail refinement, by contrast, happens in tiny local patterns: it needs full resolution to even detect the edges and features worth refining.

The paper exploits this by denoising coarse structure at low resolution (cheap), then upscaling and refining detail at full resolution (expensive but fast because there are fewer steps left). The model learns to handle the resolution transition by treating the upscale as a fresh injection of noise: when the resolution jumps, add noise to the upscaled image to match the diffusion timeline at that step, then continue denoising from there. The learned structure from the low-res phase guides the detail recovery because the model has already figured out where the shapes and edges should go.

The math: weighted upscale

First in words:

When resolution changes at step t_i, upsample the denoised estimate and mix it with fresh noise using the learned variance schedule to preserve alignment with the diffusion trajectory.

Then the compact form:

x_tilde_upscale = Upsample(x_0_hat) + noise_injection * N(0, sigma_ti^2 * I)

Every symbol:

Upsample(x_0_hat)The low-res estimate expanded back to full size
noise_injectionWeight controlling how much randomness to add
sigma_tiNoise level at step i from the diffusion schedule
IIdentity matrix (adds noise independently to each pixel)
A tiny worked example
At step 80 (low-res phase complete): model outputs clean image guess at 250×250 resolution.
Upsample to 2000×2000: image is now 8× bigger in each dimension.
Diffusion timeline says at step 80 the noise level should be sigma=0.3. Add N(0, 0.3^2) noise to the upscaled image.
Now at step 81 (first high-res refinement step): model denoise the noisy full-res image, guided by the structure learned in low-res phase.
Result: smooth transition; no discontinuity between low-res and full-res phases.

The upscale is not just stretching pixels—it is re-injecting noise to keep the diffusion trajectory valid.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Upscale too early. If you jump to full resolution before coarse structure is stable, the detail-refinement steps have to fix structure, not details. This wastes time and loses quality.
Noise injection mismatch. If you add the wrong amount of noise when upscaling, the model sees a distribution shift—it was trained to denoise from a clean image at that step, but now it faces a different noise level. Quality drops or instability appears.
Starting resolution too low. A tiny starting image loses fine detail information forever; even upscaling cannot recover it. There is a minimum image size below which quality collapses.
Done right. The paper proposes using a learned model to predict the right upscale moment: watch the difference between the model's guess and the best reconstruction; when they align (loss stops shrinking), structure is stable. Inject noise calibrated to the diffusion schedule. Quality is preserved.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple image restoration task: start with a photo, add blur or noise, then run diffusion to restore it. Implement two pipelines: one that denoises at full size for many steps, one that denoises at a small size for most steps, then upscales and denoises at full size for a few steps. Measure runtime and image quality by checking how close the result is to the original photo or by having people rate how realistic it looks.

speedup = full_res_time / dynamic_res_time, quality_loss = baseline_psnr - dynamic_psnr

The demo must show that dynamic resolution finishes in under 60 percent of the time while keeping quality loss under 2 percent (nearly imperceptible to human eyes).
