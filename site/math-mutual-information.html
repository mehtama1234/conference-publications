How much knowing one thing cuts down the surprise about another.

In plain terms

Imagine you're trying to guess what someone had for lunch. Before you know anything, they could have had pasta, a sandwich, salad, or a hundred other things—lots of possibilities, lots of uncertainty. Now someone tells you: they are at the Italian restaurant down the street. That fact doesn't remove all uncertainty (maybe they had pasta, maybe risotto, maybe a salad), but it cuts the possibilities way down. The uncertainty went from huge to manageable. Mutual information measures exactly this: how much a piece of information (which restaurant) actually narrows down what you're trying to figure out (what they ate).

Here's why this matters in machine learning. Models constantly ask: does this feature tell us anything useful about the outcome we care about? Is the weather forecast helping us predict someone's mood? Does knowing the image has red pixels help us recognize if it's a fire truck (because fire trucks are often red)? Mutual information is the measurement tool. It answers: given this new piece of data, how much do we actually learn about the thing we're trying to predict? Not just correlation or association—true reduction in uncertainty. This is why it shows up everywhere when models need to learn which signals matter.

The formula captures this by comparing two uncertainties. First, how uncertain are we about the outcome before we see the information (this is H(X), your starting confusion level about X). Second, how uncertain are we about the outcome even after we see the information (this is H(X|Y), your remaining confusion about X once we've seen Y). The gap between them—H(X) minus H(X|Y)—is exactly how much the information narrowed things down. When they are very different, the information is useful. When they are the same, the information told us nothing.

The equation
I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)
I(X;Y)mutual information between X and Y—how much knowing one reduces surprise about the other
H(X)entropy of X—the total amount of uncertainty or surprise about X before seeing anything else
H(X|Y)conditional entropy—how much uncertainty about X remains even after we observe Y
Xthe thing we are trying to predict or understand
Ythe information or observation we have access to
Worked example
Predicting lunch from restaurant choice
Suppose X = lunch type (5 equally likely options: pasta, salad, sandwich, soup, burger).
Then H(X) = log₂(5) ≈ 2.32 bits. We have 2.32 bits of uncertainty.
Once we observe Y = Italian restaurant, only 2 options remain likely: pasta or risotto.
So H(X|Y) = log₂(2) = 1 bit. Our uncertainty drops to 1 bit.
Therefore I(X;Y) = 2.32 − 1 = 1.32 bits. The restaurant told us 1.32 bits of information.
Why it shows up

Mutual information is the foundation of self-supervised learning and contrastive methods. When a model learns from unlabeled data, it is trying to find representations where mutual information is high between different views of the same data (e.g., the original image and a rotated version should help predict each other because they come from the same thing). The model asks: does my learned representation preserve the information that matters? Mutual information is how you measure 'what matters.'

It also underpins interpretability, reinforcement learning, and alignment. In interpretability, researchers ask: how much information does this neuron actually carry about the final decision? In reinforcement learning, agents use mutual information to figure out which observations are truly predictive of future reward. In safety work, teams measure whether a model's internal reasoning (the mutual information between reasoning steps and outputs) aligns with human judgment. Every one of these questions boils down to: how much does seeing this reduce uncertainty about that? That is mutual information at work.
