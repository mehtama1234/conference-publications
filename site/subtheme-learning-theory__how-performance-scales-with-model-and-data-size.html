How performance scales with model and data size

Understanding the mathematical relationship between performance and scale: how much better do models get as you make them bigger or give them more training data.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When you double the amount of training data, does your model get twice as good? When you double the number of weights in the network, how much better are predictions? These scaling questions matter enormously because they determine how much data and compute you need to reach acceptable performance. Yet scaling is not straightforward. Sometimes doubling data gives almost no improvement because the model has already learned all the variety in the current data, leaving nothing new to find. Sometimes doubling the model size hurts performance because the larger model overfits — it memorizes noise rather than finding patterns. This subtheme investigates: what is the mathematical relationship between model size, data size, and performance?

The difficulty is that scaling depends on many factors: the problem you are solving (some tasks are intrinsically harder than others), the model architecture (some designs scale better than others), the algorithm (training methods matter), and the data itself (are you doubling data diversity or just repetition?), so each factor compounds in unpredictable ways. In the deep learning era, empirically observed scaling laws predict performance from a formula involving model size and data size, yet these are primarily empirical observations without deep theoretical justification, meaning practitioners cannot explain why or when they break. Additionally, scaling is not always monotonic — sometimes a larger model trained on more data performs worse, possibly due to interaction between architectural choices and learning dynamics, which means you cannot assume that more of everything is better. Understanding when scaling helps and when it hurts requires analyzing what happens inside the learning algorithm because this analysis reveals the mechanism, so you can predict whether more scale will improve or degrade outcomes, which guides investment decisions in infrastructure.

Researchers study sample complexity — how many examples must you show a model to achieve a target performance level — for different learning problems and algorithms, because this tells you the minimum data required to trust the model. They analyze convergence rates, predicting how quickly an algorithm improves as it processes more data, because this speed tells you how much training data is worth collecting, so you know when you have collected enough or when diminishing returns set in. They examine overfitting curves, which show performance improving on training data but degrading on test data as models grow too complex, so you can spot when a model is memorizing noise rather than learning patterns. They also study the practical phenomenon of double descent: as you increase model capacity past the point where the model can memorize all training data, performance first gets worse (overfitting), then gets better again (the larger model can express simpler, more generalizable solutions), which contradicts the conventional wisdom that you should minimize model size because it shows complexity does not always hurt, suggesting that the relationship between complexity and generalization is more nuanced than previously believed. This suggests that bigger models, when trained correctly, can generalize better despite the risk of overfitting because the mechanism learned via double descent overcomes overfitting, which explains why modern large models work better than theory predicts and motivates why practitioners now build larger models rather than smaller ones to achieve better real-world performance. The field also investigates Pareto-optimal frontiers, showing tradeoffs between model size (computational cost) and accuracy, helping practitioners choose models that perform well given their compute budget.

How the work breaks down

Understanding scaling requires studying how data, model size, and training algorithms interact. The papers split into four directions: mathematical foundations of scaling laws in linear models and theory (because rigorous theory first appeared here), how training methods like stepping through weighted updates and dropout respond to scale (because these methods change behavior at different sizes), the practical question of data quality and whether more data always helps (because more data can paradoxically hurt), and emergent phenomena like phase transitions and memorization (because they reveal when scaling helps or hurts).

Data size and model repetition

A central puzzle in scaling is understanding when you benefit from having more training data and when you hit diminishing returns. The key challenge is that benefits from more data depend on both the amount of data you have and whether you are cycling through the same examples multiple times during training.

The paper Larger Datasets Can Be Repeated More provides rigorous analysis of this tradeoff in linear regression. It proves that with small datasets, each additional repetition through the data gives nearly linear improvement in performance. But as datasets grow, there is a ceiling—performance plateaus at a point determined by the problem difficulty, not by how many more times you cycle through the data. This means large datasets naturally stop improving once the model has seen enough variety, so the model learns when to stop rather than wasting time on repetition. The paper Why Less is More (Sometimes): A Theory of Data Curation flips this insight: it shows that curated or filtered datasets can outperform the full dataset, because removing noisy or redundant examples lets the model focus on signal rather than noise. The paper uses phase transitions to prove exactly when curation helps, because knowing the transition point lets you predict whether filtering is worth the effort. InfoLaw: Information Scaling Laws for Large Language Models extends this to real large language models trained on mixed data with repetition, showing how to predict performance on new recipes without training them fully. Together these papers show that better scaling comes from matching data to the problem, and matching repetition patterns to dataset size, because these alignments create efficient learning.

Linear regression scaling foundations

Mathematical foundations of scaling laws first emerged from the study of linear regression, because it is simple enough to analyze completely yet complex enough to reveal real insights about how model size and data quantity affect learning. The challenge is proving exactly what the learning curve looks like—how many examples does a model need to reach a target accuracy, and how does this change as you scale the model or reduce numerical precision in computation.

Scaling Laws for Precision in High-Dimensional Linear Regression addresses a practical question: if your hardware can only store numbers with limited precision (like lower-bit weights), how much does that hurt performance? The paper shows that multiplying weights by a scale factor—a soft adjustment—preserves the effective model size, whereas truncating precision directly shrinks what the model can express. This gives practitioners a principled way to choose between different precision levels without blindly losing capacity. Scaling Laws of SignSGD in Linear Regression studies a simplified update rule where you only send the sign of gradients (not their magnitude), which saves communication and computation. It proves that in problems where noise from data variation dominates, sign-based updates converge faster than the standard method because the noise masking the signal is so large that ignoring magnitude helps focus on direction. Fast Escape, Slow Convergence: Learning Dynamics of Phase Retrieval under Power-Law Data extends these insights to nonlinear regression by studying data where some features are much more common than others (a pattern called power-law structure). It characterizes how the algorithm learns at different rates because some feature directions have more data signal than others. Together, these papers provide rigorous mathematics for scaling, and practitioners can use these results to predict scaling benefits because they reveal the underlying mechanism.

Scaling in neural networks: theory and practice

Scaling laws in deep neural networks are harder to analyze than linear regression because the models are nonlinear and non-convex, yet observed scaling laws from real training have become the practical foundation for modern machine learning investment. The puzzle is understanding what these observed laws reveal about how networks actually learn, and how to predict scaling behavior without training every configuration. The challenge is that scaling depends on subtle interactions between model capacity, training algorithm, data's structure or pattern, and architectural choices.

Single-Head Attention in High Dimensions: A Theory of Generalization provides a rare theoretical window into scaling laws for neural network components by analyzing the attention mechanism (the part of transformers—a neural network design—that weights connections between words). It proves that the learned attention matrix has a specific structure—some rows have many important connections and others have only a few key connections—and this structure emerges predictably as you train. The paper shows power-law scaling laws arise from the sequential appearance of these patterns, because each feature gets learned at a predictable rate. Active Budget Allocation for Efficient Scaling Law Estimation tackles the practical problem: to know what configuration to use, practitioners train many models at different sizes and data amounts, but this is very expensive. The paper shows how to estimate scaling laws while training far fewer models by using a smaller model as a guide to predict which full-scale experiments are worth doing. Dropout Universality: Scaling Laws and Optimal Scheduling at the Edge-of-Chaos studies how dropout—randomly removing neurons during training to reduce overfitting—should change as you scale the model and data. It discovers that frontloading dropout (using more early, less late) improves performance 18-35% over constant dropout because early training needs more noise reduction to prevent overfitting to small data, but late training needs less noise since the model is already learning generalizable patterns. Convex Dominance in Deep Learning I: A Scaling Law of Loss and Learning Rate reveals that the learning rate (step size when updating weights through the network) should scale predictably with model size and training duration, enabling extrapolation of optimal rates across 80x differences in training time. Overparametrization bends the loss surface reveals that as you make models dramatically larger, the shape of the loss surface (the surface you optimize over) changes in phases—certain size thresholds unlock new recovery capabilities that were impossible before. Together these papers show that scaling laws in neural networks emerge from understandable structure, not magic, and this structure gives purchase for practical prediction and optimization.

Memorization, generalization, and phase transitions

As models scale, a surprising phenomenon emerges: at some point, models stop learning new patterns and start memorizing training data exactly. This memorization-to-generalization transition is poorly understood and determines whether a large model will overfit or generalize well. The challenge is predicting where this boundary lies and understanding whether the transition is gradual or sharp.

How much can language models memorize? directly measures this by training GPT-style models of different sizes and asking: how much unique information can they store before they run out of capacity? The paper estimates about 3.6 bits per parameter—a fixed ratio that holds across models of different scales. This capacity measure explains why very large models with billions of parameters can memorize enormous datasets, but eventually start forgetting old information to make room for new training examples. LLMs as Noisy Channels: A Shannon Perspective on Model Capacity and Scaling Laws reframes scaling laws using information theory, treating the model as a noisy communication channel. This reveals that models have finite information-carrying capacity, and scaling laws predict when models transition from underfitting (too small to hold the problem structure) to overfitting (so large they memorize noise). Random Scaling of Emergent Capabilities studies why performance sometimes jumps sharply—breakthroughs appear sudden even though the underlying mechanism changes gradually. It shows that sharp leaps in performance (like a model suddenly reasoning correctly) are caused by continuous changes in the learned representations that only appear discrete to outside observers. Risk Phase Transitions in Spiked Regression studies catastrophic overfitting—sometimes adding more data or larger models hurts performance catastrophically, not gradually. The paper identifies alignment between the signal structure and model architecture as the key: when they misalign, increasing scale triggers collapse. t-SNE Exaggerates Clusters, Provably addresses data visualization: the popular visualization algorithm clusters always appear more separated than they actually are, which can mislead practitioners about data quality. This matters for scaling because decisions about data curation often rely on visual inspection of clustering. Together these papers reveal that scaling has critical boundaries where smooth improvement suddenly becomes harmful.

Scaling from language and data structure

Scaling laws for large language models appear to follow simple power laws across a vast range—models with 100 million to 100 billion parameters all improve predictably. Understanding why these laws work so reliably requires studying how data properties and learning algorithms interact, because this reveals why the patterns hold across different sizes.

On the Origin of Neural Scaling Laws: from Random Graphs to Natural Language shows that power-law scaling emerges even without assuming natural language follows power laws—random graphs and simplified languages produce the same scaling behavior. This reveals that scaling laws arise from fundamental learning dynamics, not language statistics. The scaling exponent (how steeply performance improves) depends on data complexity—harder tasks scale differently—but the emergence itself is universal. Deriving Neural Scaling Laws from the Statistics of Natural Language provides the other direction: starting from statistics of actual text, the paper derives theoretical scaling laws and tests them by training GPT-2 and LLaMA models from scratch. The match between theory and what actually happens when training shows that real language structure directly determines the scaling behavior you observe. Scaling Laws and Symmetry, Evidence from Neural Force Fields studies models with built-in symmetry—architectural constraints that respect physical or mathematical structure. It reveals that architectural symmetry helps more at larger scales because the larger model can exploit the structure better, and optimal scaling requires data size and model size to grow together for the architecture to work well. Universal One-third Time Scaling in Learning Peaked Distributions provides mechanistic insight: it proves that learning certain peaked distributions (like multimodal data) follows a predictable one-third power law in training time, which explains why high-level scaling laws have consistent exponents. Together these papers suggest scaling laws are consequences of structure in both data and algorithms, not accidents.
