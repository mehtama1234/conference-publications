CoMem: Compositional Concept-Graph Memory for Vision-Language Adaptation

CoMem stores learned knowledge as a graph of reusable concepts and their relationships, then practises on sampled pieces of that graph to learn without forgetting or storing images.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

A system learning to understand images and text must keep what it has learned before while learning new things, but cannot store all old images due to privacy and memory limits. CoMem solves this by organizing learned concepts into a compact graph of connected building blocks, then practices with practice signals tied to sampled parts of that graph, keeping the model stable yet able to learn new domains without forgetting.

Zero assumption version

Continual learning without storing old data

A model trained on images of dogs learns to recognize dog shapes. Then you show it cats. If you train naively on cats, the model forgets what made a dog a dog — this is catastrophic forgetting. A beginner might say: just train on both dogs and cats together. But in the real world, you cannot store all old images (privacy rules forbid it, and storage is expensive). So you need a way to practice on the old task without the old pictures.

Vision-language models are even trickier — they link images to text descriptions. A model might learn that certain image features go with words like fluffy or stripes. When new data arrives from a different source or in a different style, the model either forgets its old knowledge or the new training breaks what it learned before.

The core problem. How do you practice on old learning without storing the old images or text, keeping both old knowledge and the ability to learn new things?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn and organize

As the model sees new images, CoMem extracts its learned features and builds a graph of concepts, linking related ones together.

Compress to core knowledge

Instead of storing images, CoMem stores only the concept nodes and how they connect, a compact summary of what was learned.

Sample the graph

When practicing, CoMem picks random small pieces of the concept graph — a few connected concepts and their links.

Rehearse in feature space

The model practises predicting those concept relationships directly from learned features, without needing the original images or text.

Filter unreliable updates

CoMem only keeps updates to concepts that are confident and on the learned pattern; uncertain or out-of-pattern changes are skipped to avoid drift.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

CoMem changes what gets stored as memory. Ordinarily, continual-learning systems either store raw images and text (expensive and risky) or use simple summaries like feature averages (inflexible). CoMem stores the compositional structure — a graph of concepts and how they relate to each other.

the object it moves  =  the memory format (from images or averages to concept graphs)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: compositional coherence

measured quantity  =  coherence between part and whole

CoMem measures a quantity it calls compositional coherence: how well the model's prediction of a whole concept (like cat) matches the predictions for its parts (like furry and pointy-ears combined). This is not noise or performance — it is a structure signal. If predictions for the parts agree with the whole, the concept graph is internally consistent and trustworthy for rehearsal.

CoMem estimates this by checking how well a model trained to predict part-concepts can also predict the whole-concept from the same features. A high match means the graph is coherent; a poor match signals that the concept boundaries need adjustment or some parts are drifting.

Why it works

Why compositional graphs prevent forgetting

Ordinary rehearsal replays old images or features to refresh old knowledge. CoMem has no stored images, so it rehearses the relationships in its concept graph instead. When the model trains on new data (say, a new visual style or language), rehearsal feeds in sampled subgraphs and asks the model to predict concept relationships. This forces the model to hold onto the feature patterns for older concepts, because to satisfy the rehearsal task, the old concepts must still be recognizable.

Coherence acts as a guard rail. The model is asked not just to predict concepts in isolation, but to keep part-predictions aligned with whole-predictions. If new training starts to corrupt an old concept (like furry), the coherence loss will spike — the parts no longer agree with the whole — and gradient updates will gently correct the drift. Uncertainty filtering adds a second guard: CoMem only updates concepts that are confident in their prediction; if a concept is ambiguous or on the edge of learned patterns, it does not train on it, avoiding off-pattern noise that would degrade precision.

Math after the picture

The compositional consistency objective

First in words:

total loss equals new-task loss plus lambda times the sum over all part-whole pairs of the squared difference between the part prediction and the whole prediction, weighted by how important each part is

Then the compact form:

L = L_task + lambda * sum_(i in parts) w_i * (pred_part_i - pred_whole)^2

Every symbol:

Ltotal loss to minimize
L_taskerror on the new task (standard classification or retrieval loss)
lambdastrength of the coherence constraint, how much to penalize incoherence
w_ilearned importance weight for part i, how much that part matters to the whole
pred_part_imodel's prediction score for part i from the shared features
pred_wholemodel's prediction score for the whole concept from the shared features
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model is learning a cat concept: furry (part 1) and pointy-ears (part 2), whole is cat.
On a new image, the model predicts: pred_part_furry = 0.85, pred_part_pointy = 0.80, pred_whole_cat = 0.60.
Coherence loss = (0.85 - 0.60)^2 + (0.80 - 0.60)^2 = 0.0625 + 0.04 = 0.1025.
If lambda = 1.0, this adds 0.1025 to the total loss. The gradient nudges the model to raise pred_whole_cat or lower the part predictions until they align.
Meanwhile, new task loss trains the model on new images. The two losses compete: new task pushes forward, coherence loss anchors the old concept graph steady.

The formula keeps part-predictions and whole-predictions aligned; when they drift, the penalty grows, steering the model back to a coherent graph.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The concept graph becomes tangled. If the model updates too aggressively, or if concepts are defined poorly, the graph can develop cycles or contradictory relationships. Rehearsal then becomes noisy because sampled subgraphs do not reflect true structure. Mitigation: use learned importance weights to identify and trust the strongest relationships.
Uncertainty filtering is too strict. If the threshold for what counts as confident is too high, CoMem refuses to update almost anything and the new task cannot learn. If too low, the model learns new data but accepts off-pattern noise. Mitigation: tune the confidence threshold per task, or use adaptive thresholds that tighten when drift is detected.
The new domain is very different. If the new data comes from a radically different visual style or language distribution, the old concept graph may not apply — rehearsing it actually interferes with learning the new domain. When task loss and coherence loss pull in opposite directions, it signals that the old concepts are blocking the new learning. Reducing lambda weakens the coherence penalty, allowing the model to prioritize the new task loss and adapt to the new domain even if it means the old concepts drift, because the new data is genuinely different enough that perfect coherence with the old graph is impossible. Alternatively, allow selective re-definition of concept boundaries — tag certain old concepts as domain-specific and permit them to change on the new domain, keeping only the truly universal concepts locked, because some learned features (like color histogram) are dataset-specific while others (like object shape) transfer across domains.
Done right. Coherence loss is moderate (concepts stay aligned), confidence thresholds filter out-of-distribution noise, new task loss stays low, and rehearsal reinforces old knowledge without storing images. The model learns new domains while retaining old ones.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple continual-learning scenario: train a small vision-language model on images and text from source A (e.g., natural photographs), then show it data from source B (e.g., stylized or synthetic images), then back to a held-out test set from source A. Measure accuracy on source-A test data before and after source-B training. Compare three baselines: naive finetuning (learns B but forgets A), a simple replay buffer (stores old images, so cannot do this experiment), and CoMem (stores only concept graph).

forgetting = accuracy_A_before_training_B - accuracy_A_after_training_B; transfer = accuracy_B_after_training. CoMem should minimize forgetting while achieving reasonable transfer.

The demo must show that CoMem retains accuracy on old data even after training on new data, without storing a single old image.
