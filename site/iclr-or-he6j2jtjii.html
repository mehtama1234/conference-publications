UrbanVerse: Scaling Urban Simulation by Watching City-Tour Videos

UrbanVerse builds large, realistic urban training worlds by automatically extracting 3D layouts from crowd-sourced city videos and populating them with real-world assets.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Urban robots like delivery bots and walking machines need to train on diverse city streets. Building realistic simulations by hand is slow and expensive, and randomly-generated worlds feel fake. UrbanVerse watches thousands of real city-tour videos and automatically extracts the 3D layouts, then fills them with carefully-labeled 3D objects from a library. The result: robots train on hundreds of real-world-inspired scenes and transfer that knowledge to actual streets far more successfully, because they have learned what real urban layouts actually look like.

The scaling wall in urban robot training

Urban robots must handle chaos: pedestrians, traffic, uneven pavement, parked cars, trash cans. A robot trained only in empty simulation-worlds fails when it meets a real street.

Experts can hand-craft realistic scenes in simulators like Isaac Sim, but building 100 different streets by hand takes months. Procedural generation (random roads and trees) is fast but produces fake-looking worlds that robots don't trust when they see the real thing.

What a naive team tries: build 5 hand-crafted scenes, train a robot in them, and hope it works outdoors. It does not. The robot overfits to the specific angles, objects, and lighting of those 5 scenes. When it meets a real street with different building heights, street signs, and shadows, it freezes or crashes into a wall.

The bottleneck. Robots need hundreds of realistic urban scenes to learn a general navigation strategy, but no one can build them fast enough by hand.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Harvest videos

Collect thousands of public city-tour and street-view camera runs.

Reconstruct 3D layout

Run structure-from-motion on video frames to build a precise 3D point cloud of each street.

Semantic segmentation

Identify which pixels are buildings, roads, sidewalks, objects.

Asset retrieval and placement

For each detected object, find the matching item in the 100k-asset library and place it at the right position and angle.

Physics instantiation

Load the assembled scene into Isaac Sim with full physics (gravity, friction, collision).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: realism transfer

measured quantity  =  Real-world semantic preservation

The paper measures something you cannot see from a bar chart: how well a simulation scene keeps the same spatial meaning as the real street it came from. Do the buildings still stand where they were? Are the parked cars in the same spots? Is the street still narrow or wide the way humans perceive it?

UrbanVerse estimates this by: (i) asking humans to rate the visual similarity of real and simulated street views (experts agree the scenes are as realistic as hand-made ones), and (ii) showing that a policy trained in UrbanVerse simulation transfers to the real street with 30% higher success than policies trained in random-world baselines. High transfer means the simulation captured what the real world actually looks like.

Why it works

Why it works: scale unlocks generalization

A robot trained on 5 hand-crafted scenes overfits: it learns 'big white building on the corner means turn left' instead of understanding that buildings are obstacles anywhere. It has seen one specific corner.

A robot trained on 160 scenes from 24 countries learns a different lesson. A white building appears in Tokyo, Boston, and Toronto, each in a different position. A narrow street shows up in Paris, Berlin, and Seoul. By seeing the same object type in dozens of contexts, the robot stops memorizing the exact corner and starts learning what a building or narrow street actually means. When it reaches a new real street in Madrid, it recognizes those features and navigates correctly.

Scaling works because UrbanVerse automated the extraction: you feed it a video (2 hours of work), not hire an artist for a month. The pipeline runs extract-and-fill on 1,000 videos and builds 160 scenes. Each scene trains the robot on a new combination of layout, lighting, season, and object arrangement. The robot sees enough variety that it learns the invariant features (buildings block, narrow streets slow down, parked cars are obstacles) instead of memorizing specific scenes.

The scaling law: why more scenes help

First in words:

success_rate equals baseline plus scaling_factor times log of number_of_scenes

Then the compact form:

success = s0 + alpha * log(N)

Every symbol:

successrobot navigation success rate on a new real street (0 to 100 percent)
s0baseline success with zero training scenes
alphascaling coefficient, how much each scene contributes
Nnumber of training scenes
A tiny worked example
Start: s0 = 30 percent (random navigation). alpha = 10.
With 5 scenes: success = 30 + 10 * log(5) = 30 + 10 * 0.7 = 37 percent.
With 50 scenes: success = 30 + 10 * log(50) = 30 + 10 * 1.7 = 47 percent.
With 160 scenes: success = 30 + 10 * log(160) = 30 + 10 * 2.2 = 52 percent.
Each 10x more scenes adds 2.3 percentage points; doubling scenes adds 0.7 points. Training plateaus but never stops improving.

The log curve means you get the biggest win going from 1 scene to 10, not 100 to 110; UrbanVerse jumps from 5 hand-made scenes (the old ceiling) straight to 160, crossing into the region where the robot learns what the real world looks like.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Video quality or angle ruins reconstruction. If a city-tour video has heavy camera shake, occlusions (a car blocking the view), or unusual angles, the structure-from-motion step fails to build a clean 3D point cloud. Blurry or low-resolution videos lose small details like street signs.
Asset library mismatch. The retrieved asset might be visually similar but functionally wrong (a large statue placed on the sidewalk where pedestrians walk, or a traffic light with incorrect semantics). Wrong physics attributes (a heavy stone trash can as light as a foam cup) break the training.
Seasonal and weather domain gap. A street filmed in summer snow and ice behavior; a night-time video with different lighting and visibility than daytime scenes. The robot trains in summer scenes and fails on snow.
Done right. Curate input videos for consistent quality (daytime, clear weather, diverse geographies). Validate asset semantics and physics before ingestion. Test transfer on held-out real-world routes in the same season and time-of-day as the training videos.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simplified version: take 10 city-tour videos (YouTube or Google Street View clips), run structure-from-motion on frames, segment buildings and cars using a pretrained image model, and retrieve matching low-poly assets from a small 3D library. Instantiate 5 scenes in a lightweight simulator (Pybullet or custom renderer). Train a small navigation policy (a neural network that learns from camera images) on these scenes and one procedurally-generated baseline, then test on held-out video frames and a new real video never seen during training. Measure how well the policy generalizes to new simulation layouts: does it perform better on held-out test simulations than on training simulations? Also test transfer to real data by reusing the visual understanding the network learned, then finetuning only the decision layer on a real trajectory. Compare whether reusing features from UrbanVerse scenes transfers better than reusing from random scenes because real-world layouts teach genuine visual patterns, not noise.

transfer_gain = success_urbanverse - success_random_baseline

The demo must show that even a small set of video-extracted scenes (5-10) outperforms random-world training on both generalization and real-world transfer, because the real-world structure (building positions, street widths, typical obstacles) encodes what the robot actually needs to learn, whereas random-world training teaches it to memorize arbitrary features.
