UrbanVerse: City Videos to Physics Simulation

UrbanVerse builds realistic robot training worlds directly from city-tour videos by parsing layouts with AI, matching real 3D objects, and running physics in simulation.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Training robots to navigate city streets requires thousands of diverse, realistic practice environments. Today, either humans design scenes by hand (slow, expensive, limited) or code generates fake layouts (unrealistic, lifeless). UrbanVerse does something new: it watches real city-tour videos from YouTube, extracts what the city actually looks like, retrieves matching 3D objects from a catalog of 100,000+ real street pieces, and assembles them into physics-aware practice worlds. The result: robots trained in these simulated cities generalize to real streets with 30% higher success.

Where robots learn today

Robots that drive or walk through cities must handle endless variations: street width, car positions, pedestrian flow, building heights, shadow angles. A human designer cannot hand-build all of these. Procedurally generated fake scenes look orderly and artificial: perfect rows, predictable spacing. Robots trained on such orderly fake worlds never learn to handle real chaos (cars parked randomly, sidewalks narrowing, buildings of wildly different heights). When the robot meets a real street, it collides or gets lost because it learned from lies.

A naive solution: hire artists to model 1000 urban scenes. But artists are expensive, slow, and they guess at realism. The result is often plausible but hollow—the city feels set-dressed, not real. Procedurally generated fakes are faster but sterile because they place cars in perfect rows and buildings in grids, which never happens in real cities. Robots trained on such scenes fail when they meet actual streets with unpredictable clutter, uneven terrain, and human chaos, because they learned to navigate tidy, repetitive patterns instead of real disorder.

The core insight. Real cities have consistent patterns (how wide streets are, where trees cluster, how tall buildings sit). These patterns hide in thousands of YouTube city-tour videos. UrbanVerse extracts those patterns and builds simulation worlds that match them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Watch a city-tour video

Feed YouTube footage of streets into the system.

Parse using 2D AI + video motion

Foundation models identify cars, buildings, trees, roads; 3D reconstruction computes their shape and position by tracking how they move across video frames.

Encode a scene graph

Store the layout as objects (with category, location, orientation) plus ground (road/sidewalk extent) plus sky (lighting).

Retrieve matched 3D assets

For each identified object, look up a matching model from the 100K catalog.

Build and enable physics

Instantiate the scene in IsaacSim: turn on physics simulation (gravity, collisions, friction). Assign each object its real-world physical properties (a car is 1200 kg with 0.8 friction; a tree is static and immovable). Now objects behave correctly—the car rolls, the tree stays put, the robot experiences realistic resistance. Plan navigation paths that avoid collisions. Robots train against the same physics they will encounter in reality.

The hidden quantity

Distributional fidelity: how real the simulation looks

measured quantity  =  Distributional fidelity

This is the core quantity the paper measures. Distributional fidelity means: does the simulation capture the real-world distribution of object types, locations, sizes, and spatial relationships? If 30% of real city streets have tall buildings on the left and trees scattered across, the simulated scenes should too.

UrbanVerse estimates it by comparing the video's parsed layout (object positions, categories, heights, distances) against the instantiated scene in simulation. The paper validates against KITTI-360 ground truth: 93% semantic accuracy (did it find the right object type?) and 1.4 m localization error (did it place things at the right distance?).

Why it works

Why this works: bridging video to physics

The breakthrough is a three-layer translation. Layer 1: parse the video. A 2D foundation model (like DINO or DINOv2, trained on billions of images) can recognize objects in 2D even without perfect focus or angle. A structure-from-motion algorithm then lifts these 2D predictions to 3D coordinates by tracking how objects move across frames. Result: a sparse 3D point cloud with labels and rough scale. Layer 2: build a scene graph. This is a simple data structure: a list of objects (each with type, 3D position, orientation, appearance) plus ground surfaces (road, sidewalk) plus sky. This graph is concrete, compact, and independent of any particular simulation engine.

Layer 3: retrieve and instantiate. Instead of procedurally generating a car or building, UrbanVerse looks up a real 3D model from its 100K-asset catalog. Each asset is pre-annotated with physical properties: a car has mass, rolling friction, and collision bounds; a building is static; a tree sways slightly. Because these properties are grounded in real objects (not made up), the simulation behaves realistically. When a robot wheels forward, it does not phase through trees or slide on ice. Robots trained in such worlds see normal physics and normal spatial patterns—exactly what they face in reality.

The scene graph representation

First in words:

V equals the open angle bracket object list comma ground list comma sky node close angle bracket. Objects have category, location as x y z, orientation, appearance texture. Ground has type, spatial bounds. Sky has illumination and background. The fidelity loss is the mean squared error between parsed object positions and ground truth positions from video keyframes.

Then the compact form:

V = <O, G, S> where O = {(cat_i, pos_i, orient_i, tex_i)}, G = {(type_j, bounds_j)}, S = (illum, bg); fidelity_loss = mean_over_i [ (pos_i_parsed - pos_i_truth)^2 ]

Tiny worked example
A video shows a car at 15 m away, 2 m to the left, 0 m height. Parsing predicts car at 15.8 m, 2.1 m, 0.1 m. Error per axis: (15.8-15)^2 = 0.64, (2.1-2)^2 = 0.01, (0.1-0)^2 = 0.01. Sum = 0.66. Average over 100 objects = 0.66. Accuracy: 93% means 93 of 100 objects identified correctly.
Now retrieve a car model from 100K catalog. It has mass 1200 kg, friction coeff 0.8. Place at (15, -2, 0) in IsaacSim. Gravity pulls it down; collision with ground stops it. Friction prevents sliding. Semantically faithful and physically stable.

The scene graph encodes what the video saw; the fidelity loss measures how close the parsing stayed to ground truth; physics assignment ensures the simulation behaves like the real world.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Video quality too low. If the city-tour is dark, blurry, or taken from inside a car with heavy reflections, the 2D foundation model cannot confidently identify objects. Parsing fails silently. The scene graph becomes sparse and inaccurate.
Occlusion hides real layout. If pedestrians, other cameras, or vehicles block the view of key objects (a building's entrance, a crosswalk), the 3D reconstruction cannot see them. The parsed scene misses important features. Robots train on incomplete environments.
No matching asset in catalog. UrbanVerse-100K has 100k+ assets but not all. If the video shows an unusual storefront, a rare traffic sign, or a unique architectural detail, the retrieval fails. The system falls back to a generic placeholder, losing semantic accuracy.
Physics mismatch. If the assigned physical properties (mass, friction) are wrong—e.g., a door marked as static when it swings open, or friction too low so the robot slides unrealistically—the simulation behavior diverges from reality. Robots learn brittle habits that fail on real streets.
Done right. Diverse city-tour videos from multiple seasons and lighting conditions, high-quality outdoor footage, catalog of real 3D city objects with well-measured physics, and validation against ground-truth video sequences (KITTI-360) ensure high fidelity. Robots learn generalizable, stable navigation.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A complete demo would: (1) Download a raw city-tour video from YouTube (10–20 minutes of walking through a real street). (2) Run UrbanVerse parsing to extract objects, positions, and a scene graph. (3) Retrieve assets from a small version of the 100K catalog (e.g., 1000 pre-scanned 3D models of cars, buildings, benches, trees). (4) Instantiate the scene in IsaacSim with physics enabled. (5) Train a simple PPO navigation policy (goal-reaching with obstacle avoidance) on 10 variations of the scene (different start/goal pairs). (6) Deploy the trained policy on a real robotic platform in a city street not seen during training, measuring success rate and number of collisions.

success_rate = (num_goals_reached / num_trials) * 100; sim_to_real_transfer = success_rate_real / success_rate_sim; zero_shot_generalization = (success on unseen_real_streets / success_on_trained_sim)

The demo makes impossible to miss: robots trained in UrbanVerse scenes (built from real-video patterns, not hand-designed or fake-generated) transfer to real, unseen city streets without any retraining, reaching goals that generic simulators would fail.
