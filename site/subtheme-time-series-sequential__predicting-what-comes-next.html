Predicting what comes next

The core problem: given past values in a sequence, predict future ones. Papers explore neural networks, decomposition, frequency analysis, and hybrid approaches to make better forecasts.

What this is

This is the most direct form of sequence work: you have past values, you want to guess future values. For example, a hospital has a patient's blood pressure readings every minute for the last hour, and needs to predict the next hour. Or a warehouse has shipment volumes for every day of the last year, and needs to plan for next week. The naive approach—fit a straight line, or repeat the average—often fails because real sequences have structure that is more complex. Trends curve. Seasonal patterns repeat. Past shocks echo into the future. A good forecaster has to learn these patterns from data.

The challenge is that what matters changes. If you are predicting stock prices, the past hour might matter more than the past year, because markets react to fast-moving news. If you are predicting factory wear, the past week matters less than how the machine has been used over months. A single model that always uses the same look-back window often overshoots or undershoots. Additionally, real forecasts need to be not just point guesses but also ranges: the hospital needs to know not just 'blood pressure will be 120' but 'blood pressure will be between 115 and 125 with high confidence,' because low-confidence predictions are less useful for decisions.

Papers in this cluster explore neural networks that learn which parts of the past matter, including methods that assign higher weights to the time steps that most influence the future. Others decompose the sequence into trend, seasonality, and noise, and forecast each separately. Some use frequency analysis—converting to waves and modeling waves instead of raw values—because waves are often simpler to predict than raw noise. Hybrids combine neural and traditional statistical approaches. The goal is to extract the hidden structure that the raw numbers are masking, so that predictions work better across different scenarios and datasets.

How the work breaks down

Time series prediction splits into five main directions because different aspects of sequences demand different solutions: handling irregular data and missing values when real-world sensors don't report on clean schedules, breaking sequences into components (trend, seasonality, noise) so you can forecast each pattern separately, making predictions work across different datasets when conditions change (a model trained on 2023 might fail in 2024 if the data changed), building efficient models that work on devices with limited memory and compute power, and using retrieval and decomposition to unlock what the data actually contains without overfitting.

Handling irregular and multivariate data

The hardest real-world forecasting problem is that data arrives unevenly. A hospital monitors blood pressure every minute, except when the patient moves, when readings stop for 5 minutes. Financial prices come in tick data with wildly varying gaps—sometimes 100 trades per second, sometimes none for hours. Traditional forecasting assumes equally-spaced measurements; real sensors don't cooperate. These papers handle irregular spacing, multiple variables measured at different rates, and missing data.

Bridging Time and Frequency converts irregular time series into frequency space—the domain of waves and oscillations—where seasonal patterns and trends become obvious, then uses inverse techniques to reconstruct forecasts that respect the original irregular sampling. This works because seasonal patterns are cleaner in frequency space: a yearly pattern shows up as a spike at one frequency, independent of whether readings came every day or every three days. Nested Spatio-Temporal tackles multivariate sequences where you have many related series (e.g., traffic flow on connected roads, or temperature at many locations). It learns that big-picture patterns shared across all variables can improve individual forecasts, so it injects global structure into node-level predictions rather than treating each series in isolation. ASTGI builds a graph to represent relationships between variables, so when predicting warehouse inventory from shipment volumes, it learns that volumes in neighboring regions influence each other because they share suppliers and customers. All three papers reject the false choice between handling irregular data and achieving accuracy: irregular sampling, multiple variables, and missing values are the real world, and models must handle them.

Decomposition for clarity and accuracy

Most sequences hide multiple patterns layered on top of each other. Stock prices trend upward, but also spike daily based on market open/close, and jump on news. Hospital patient vitals have baseline values (their healthy state), drift slowly over time (aging or disease progression), and fluctuate hour-by-hour with circadian rhythm. Predicting the mixture is harder than predicting components separately. These papers decompose sequences intentionally.

KUMA uses Koopman separation—a method from dynamical systems theory—to disentangle the ways a sequence can change. Rather than one black-box forecast, it separates transient patterns (changes that fade, like market shocks) from recurrent patterns (repeating cycles, like daily seasonality) and long-term drift. This separation prevents one type of change from masking another, meaning the model learns each pattern clearly. Numerion achieves similar separation via multi-hypercomplex representations, which naturally split sequences into interpretable components. By forecasting trend, seasonality, and residual noise separately, you get higher accuracy because each component has its own pattern. TimeRecipe goes deeper: it empirically discovers which components of a forecasting model actually matter by benchmarking module-level effectiveness. Most papers stack many components—attention layers, residual connections, decomposition blocks—assuming that adding more architectural pieces will improve performance because more capacity seems safer. However, this assumption often fails because extra components add overhead and complexity without helping the core forecasting task. TimeRecipe shows many components don't actually improve results beyond baseline, and that simple input transformation often matches complex architectures in accuracy, which means the extra pieces are unused capacity. This reveals which architectural pieces pull their weight and which are passengers, so practitioners can build minimal, fast models that don't waste computation on unnecessary pieces.

Handling shifts and noise

A forecasting model trained on 2023 data often fails in early 2024 because the underlying data distribution shifted: the market became more volatile, consumer behavior changed, or a system was upgraded. Additionally, real sensor data is noisy: readings jump randomly, causing models to overfit to noise. These papers tackle distribution shift, noise handling, and cross-variable alignment.

DistDF solves a fundamental problem: different forecasting architectures perform best on different datasets, yet no model consistently wins. Rather than pick one architecture, it uses Wasserstein alignment—a mathematical tool for measuring and correcting distribution mismatch—to align the patterns across diverse models and datasets. This improves accuracy because alignment reduces distribution shift: a model trained on clean data works better on noisy data if you first align the noise. DropoutTS adds sample-adaptive dropout—randomly silencing neurons during training, but more aggressively when data is noisy. During training on a noisy dataset, it drops neurons more often; on clean data, it drops less. This teaches the model to handle noise because noisy inputs are handled by a sparser, more redundant network that can tolerate some failures. Routing Channel-Patch Dependencies uses graph spectral decomposition to route information between channels (different measured variables) and time patches intelligently. Rather than a fixed connection pattern, it learns which connections are reliable and which are noisy, so information flows only between trustworthy pairs. All three papers recognize that real data is messy and distribution shift is inevitable.

Efficient models for deployment

A forecasting model running on a phone, a factory sensor, or an IoT device faces tight constraints: microcontrollers have kilobytes of memory, battery life matters, and forecasting must finish in milliseconds. Standard deep models have billions of weights and take seconds. These papers build efficient forecasters for resource-constrained deployment.

MixLinear achieves high-accuracy forecasting with just 0.1K weights—a hundred numbers—by treating multivariate forecasting as a mixing problem: each output is a learned mix of the most recent inputs, weighted differently per variable. It works because the model implicitly learns which inputs matter most without the overhead of a neural network. A traditional attention-based forecaster has millions of weights; MixLinear is 10 thousand times smaller, enabling deployment on battery-powered devices where power consumption scales with weight count. PMDformer improves this by using patch-mean decoupling: instead of looking at every timestamp, it groups time into patches (like monthly chunks of daily data), computes summaries (means, trends) per patch, then decodes those summaries into forecasts. This reduces computation because it processes patches, not individual timesteps. Patch-based methods are more memory-efficient because they compress input data early, and they outperform standard attention over full sequences because attention over patches is cheaper and captures the right granularity. Both papers show the path to accurate, tiny forecasting models.

Interpretability and specialized applications

Some prediction tasks demand interpretability or domain expertise. Stock traders want to know why a model predicts a price move, so they can validate the reasoning and adjust positions manually. Crime forecasters must explain predictions to police and courts. Medical applications need evidence, not just predictions. Specialized domains like crime hotspots and financial analysis have unique structure that generic models miss. These papers address interpretability, domain knowledge, and super-resolution.

Enhancing Multivariate with Global Temporal Retrieval uses a straightforward idea: instead of predicting purely from weights learned during training, it retrieves similar past sequences from historical data and blends them into the forecast. This makes predictions traceable: you can see which historical periods the model found relevant, enabling interpretation. It outperforms black-box methods consistently across datasets because retrieval grounds predictions in real examples. Reasoning on Time-Series for Financial Technical Analysis generates human-readable reasoning traces—step-by-step explanations of why the model predicts a price move—and validation by industry experts shows these traces capture real financial logic. This is critical for finance because a model that predicts correctly but for wrong reasons will fail on new market regimes. SRT (Super-Resolution) addresses a different need: low-resolution data arrives (e.g., hourly prices, not tick data), but high-resolution forecasts are valuable (predict minute-by-minute for fine-grained trading). Using disentangled rectified flow—a modern generative technique—it upsamples sequences reliably and achieves zero-shot super-resolution, meaning it works without retraining even on data it never saw. ST-HHOL tackles crime prediction via spatio-temporal hierarchical hypergraphs, capturing the structured relationships between locations: neighborhoods influence neighbors through crime spillover and criminal networks. The hypergraph encodes complex multi-way dependencies that linear models or simple graphs miss. All four papers show that generic forecasting is limiting—domain structure and interpretability matter.
