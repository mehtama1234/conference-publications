ParoQuant: Pairwise Rotation Quantization for Reasoning LLMs

Rotate and rescale the model weights before squeezing them into fewer bits, so outlier channels do not waste the available precision.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

LLMs need to be compressed to run faster on hardware, which means squeezing weights into fewer bits. The problem: some channels have wildly different magnitudes, and those outliers grab most of the available precision range, leaving less room for the normal channels. When reasoning tasks require many steps, these precision losses pile up and the model falters. ParoQuant rotates and rescales the weight channels so they all fit neatly into the same magnitude range, then squeezes them all down into low-bit integers with minimal error.

Zero assumption version

Why outliers break compression into fewer bits

When you compress a big neural network into fewer bits, you must map millions of numbers onto a smaller range. For example, storing each weight as a 4-bit integer means only 16 possible values, so you must cram whatever range the original weights occupied into those 16 slots.

The real issue: some channels of weights have values from -100 to 100, while others might range from -0.1 to 0.1. When the compressor (the tool that maps numbers to integers) sees the -100 to 100 channel, it spreads its 16 slots across that huge range. This leaves almost no precision for the tiny channel, which gets rounded to just a handful of different values. In long reasoning tasks where many calculations happen in a row, these small errors add up, and the model gets confused.

Existing methods try to protect these outlier channels by skipping compression on them or using special tricks, but that either defeats the compression goal or adds computational cost at runtime.

The core issue in one sentence. Different channels have wildly different magnitudes, so the compressor cannot fit all channels into the same precision range without destroying the small ones.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Inspect the weight channels

Measure which channels have large values and which are small.

Pick independent rotation pairs

Choose pairs of channels to rotate together in a way that lets GPU do them all in parallel.

Rotate weights to balance magnitudes

Apply a Givens rotation to stretch big channels and shrink small ones so they converge to similar size.

Rescale by channel

Divide each channel by a per-channel scale factor to further even out the dynamic range.

Compress to integers

Squeeze all balanced channels into 4-bit or 8-bit integers with minimal error.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper applies a transformation to the weights (a collection of all the numbers the model learned) before compression into fewer bits. This transformation is a combination of rotations (which change the direction of weight vectors without changing their role) and per-channel scaling (which shrinks or stretches each channel by a fixed factor). This transformation preserves the model's behavior because the network can learn to absorb it, but it changes which weights are big and which are small.

the object it moves  =  the ordering of channel magnitudes
The hidden quantity

The dynamic range per compression group

measured quantity  =  channel magnitude variance

The paper measures how spread-out the magnitudes are within each group of channels that gets compressed together. If one channel is 100 times larger than another in the same group, the dynamic range is huge, and the compressor wastes precision. If all channels in a group have similar magnitude, the dynamic range is tight, and all channels get equal precision.

ParoQuant works by minimizing this variance. After rotation and rescaling, a channel that was once 100 times larger becomes only 1.5 times larger, so the compressor can spread its 16 slots fairly across the whole group instead of hoarding them on the outliers. The paper measures this by looking at the actual output error when weights are compressed—if channels are well-balanced, compression error drops.

Why it works

Why rotation and rescaling shrinks compression error

Imagine you have three weight channels: one ranges from -100 to 100, one from -10 to 10, one from -1 to 1. A 4-bit quantizer can map 16 slots. The naive approach: spread 16 slots across -100 to 100, and the tiny channel gets rounded to almost the same value every time, losing all its variation. The error is huge.

ParoQuant applies a Givens rotation, which is a simple 2D spin. It takes two channels at a time and rotates them in the plane they define. Because rotation preserves length and just changes direction, the total information in those two channels stays the same—but now their magnitudes might be more balanced. For example, after the right rotation, that -100-to-100 channel might become -50-to-50, and the -10-to-10 channel becomes -40-to-40. Much closer in size.

Then channel-wise rescaling divides each channel by its own per-channel factor. The -50-to-50 channel gets divided by 50, becoming -1-to-1. The -40-to-40 channel gets divided by 40, also -1-to-1. Now all channels fit into nearly the same range, so the 16-slot quantizer spreads its slots evenly, and no channel is sacrificed. The model learns an inverse rotation during inference (which is fast because it is designed to be computed in parallel on the GPU), so the squeezed, quantized weights get rotated back to their proper orientation before being used.

The magic is that the rotation does not change what the model computes—it is just a coordinate change. The network can freely learn to absorb rotated and rescaled weights because the math underneath remains unchanged. Quantization error drops because balanced channels mean no precision is wasted.

Math after the picture

The quantization and scaling equation

First in words:

Quantization takes a weight, subtracts its minimum, divides by a scale factor that maps the range to the available bit width, rounds to the nearest integer, and clamps to valid values.

Then the compact form:

Q(X) = clamp( (X - min(X)) / s, 0, 2^b - 1 ), where s = (max(X) - min(X)) / (2^b - 1)

Every symbol:

Xthe weight matrix (or a channel of it)
min(X)the smallest weight value in the range
max(X)the largest weight value in the range
sthe scale factor (how much to shrink the range)
bthe number of bits per weight (e.g., 4 or 8)
2^bthe total number of possible integer values (e.g., 16 for 4-bit)
A tiny worked example
Suppose before ParoQuant, channel A ranges -100 to 100, channel B ranges -1 to 1, both need to fit into 4-bit (16 values).
Without balancing: A gets slot size (100 - (-100)) / 15 = 13.3 per slot. B gets the same 13.3-sized slot, so B's -1 to 1 range only uses 2 out of 16 slots. Huge waste.
After ParoQuant rotation and rescaling: both channels rescaled to -1 to 1 range. Now s = 2 / 15 ≈ 0.133 for both.
Channel A: (100 - (-100)) / 0.133 ≈ 16 slots, but after rescaling to -1 to 1, only uses 1 to 15 of them, all active.
Channel B: (-1 - (-1)) / 0.133 ≈ 0 slots waste, all precision used.
End result: both channels use nearly all 16 slots, no precision squandered on outliers.

The scale factor s divides the available range by the available slots. After balancing, all channels have similar range, so all get similar s, and no channel is sacrificed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Rotation overhead exceeds savings. If the Givens rotations are not carefully designed to run in parallel on the GPU, they add too much latency per forward pass. ParoQuant solves this by constraining rotations to be independent (each pair can be computed in isolation), so GPUs parallelize them freely. Runtime overhead stays under 10%.
Per-layer optimization gets stuck in local optima. When each layer is tuned separately, the rotations chosen for layer 3 might not play nicely with the channels that layer 2 passes forward, leading to poor global accuracy. ParoQuant compensates by using layer-wise output loss (each layer sees the already-squeezed output from its predecessor) so layers learn to recover from earlier compression errors.
Longer reasoning chains accumulate errors. Reasoning models generate many predictions in a chain, each one relying on the previous answer. Even small compression errors per step add up over dozens or hundreds of steps. ParoQuant reduces error per step by balancing channels, which shrinks the error budget enough that reasoning tasks remain accurate.
Done right. When Givens rotations are independent, per-layer optimization tracks output loss (not input loss), and bit-width is chosen high enough to preserve rare channels, balanced quantization succeeds even on long-horizon reasoning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny language model (a simple neural network with 2 layers that learns to make predictions from a small set of words) and train it on a simple reasoning task like arithmetic addition modulo a number. Before ParoQuant, compress the model naively by just mapping each weight range to 4-bit integers and measure accuracy. Then apply the ParoQuant pipeline: compute Givens rotations to balance channel magnitudes, rescale per channel, compress to integers, and measure accuracy again. The demo should show that balanced channels measurably recover accuracy compared to naive compression, especially on longer reasoning chains (e.g., three-digit addition instead of two-digit).

accuracy_recovery = accuracy_ParoQuant - accuracy_naive, measured on chains of reasoning steps. Longer chains should show larger recovery gaps.

The demo proves that rotation and rescaling to balance channel magnitudes is the direct cause of lower compression error, not an incidental side effect. Without balance, channels are squeezed differently; with balance, precision is distributed fairly.
