SEER: Robust Time Series with Smart Patch Replacement

Replace broken time series patches with strong summary markers before forecasting, so corrupted data does not ruin predictions.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting reads past data to predict the future. But real data is messy: measurements go missing, equipment drifts, rare events spike, or noise creeps in. A single bad chunk of measurements can flip the whole forecast. This paper detects broken chunks (called patches) and swaps them for cleaner summary information before making predictions.

Zero assumption version

Why patches with bad data kill forecasts

Forecasting looks at a time series (a list of measurements over time) and predicts what comes next. A common trick: divide the series into small chunks called patches, process each one, and combine them. This works because each patch captures local patterns.

But real data is broken. Sensors fail (missing values), equipment drifts (the baseline shifts), rare events spike (anomalies), or random noise creeps in. When a patch contains corrupted or shifted measurements, the forecaster learns from garbage and makes wild predictions.

A naive solution: clean the patches first, but you cannot know what 'clean' looks like in the middle of a time series, and erasing data leaves gaps. Existing methods just use all patches, even the broken ones.

In one sentence. Detect which patches contain low-quality data, and replace them with a reliable summary of the whole series.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Split and embed

Cut time series into patches; turn each into a vector (a list of numbers).

Route through experts

Multiple specialist neural networks each process patches, combining their opinions.

Detect bad patches

A filter scores each patch for quality; low scores flag corruption or noise.

Create series summary

Compute a single trusted summary vector representing the whole series trend.

Substitute and predict

Replace flagged patches with the summary vector; forecast from the cleaner sequence.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes which patches feed into the forecaster. Bad patches are swapped for a global summary vector (a list of numbers representing the whole series trend) before the model makes predictions.

the object it moves  =  Patches (replaced with a summary vector)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: patch quality score

measured quantity  =  Patch quality score

A patch quality score is a single number for each patch saying how trustworthy that chunk of measurements is. High means the chunk looks clean and normal; low means it contains noise, missing data, or abrupt shifts.

SEER estimates this score by looking at how well the expert networks agree on that patch, and by checking if the patch follows the expected pattern for that point in the series. A patch that all experts struggle with, or that violates the series trend, scores low.

Why it works

Why replacing bad patches makes forecasts honest

The problem: if the forecaster trains on corrupted patches, it learns spurious patterns. A spike from noise teaches it to expect spikes; a gap from missing data teaches it to expect gaps. Then it generalizes those learned artifacts to the future, producing garbage forecasts.

SEER's move: detect patches that do not fit the series pattern, and replace them with a summary vector representing the true trend across the whole series. This summary is hard to fool because it averages over the entire history, so a single corrupted patch has tiny influence. When the forecaster retrains on the edited sequence (broken patches swapped for clean summaries), it learns only real patterns, not artifacts of corruption.

Why it works: The mixture-of-experts sub-networks give you multiple views of each patch. If all experts disagree or struggle (a sign the patch is an outlier), you flag it. The replaced vector is a global summary: much harder to fool than a single patch. The forecaster now sees a cleaner story of the series, so it learns only patterns that repeat across the entire history instead of artifacts tied to one noisy chunk, and generalization to future time steps improves because the learned rules apply more broadly.

Math after the picture

One equation: patch replacement criterion

First in words:

A patch is replaced if its quality score falls below a threshold. The score combines expert disagreement and deviation from the series baseline.

Then the compact form:

replace_patch_i = (max_expert_disagreement_i > theta_d) OR (deviation_from_baseline_i > theta_dev)

Every symbol:

replace_patch_ibinary flag: 1 if patch i is replaced, 0 if kept
max_expert_disagreement_ilargest difference in opinion among expert networks on patch i
theta_dthreshold for expert disagreement (tuning hyperparameter)
deviation_from_baseline_ihow far patch i's statistics sit from the series mean
theta_devthreshold for deviation (tuning hyperparameter)
quality_score_i= 1 / (1 + max_expert_disagreement_i + deviation_from_baseline_i)
A tiny worked example
Patch 2: three experts embed it as [0.4, 0.5, 0.4]. Disagreement = max(0.4,0.5,0.4) - min = 0.1. Series baseline is [0.5, 0.5]. Deviation = sqrt((0.45-0.5)^2 + (0.45-0.5)^2) = 0.07. Total penalty = 0.1 + 0.07 = 0.17. Quality = 1/(1+0.17) = 0.89. Above threshold (0.5)? Yes, keep patch.
Patch 3: three experts encode it as [0.1, 0.8, 0.2]. Disagreement = 0.8 - 0.1 = 0.7. Series baseline [0.5, 0.5]. Deviation = sqrt((0.37-0.5)^2 + (0.37-0.5)^2) = 0.18. Total penalty = 0.7 + 0.18 = 0.88. Quality = 1/(1+0.88) = 0.53. Above 0.5? Borderline, replace.
Patch 3 replaced with global series summary [0.5, 0.5]. Now the sequence is clean.

Patches with high disagreement or large deviation are replaced; the filtering threshold is the tuning knob.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Aggressive filtering kills data. If the quality thresholds are set too tight, you replace too many patches with summaries, losing local detail. The model might smooth out real structure in the time series.
Global summary is also corrupted. If the corruption is systematic (the whole series is shifted, not just one patch), replacing with a global average does not help because the average baseline itself includes the shift. The summary vector carries the same corruption as the patches, so swapping them makes no difference.
Experts do not agree on anomalies. A real anomaly (a one-time spike that is honest) will show high disagreement and might get flagged and replaced, erasing important structure from the series.
Done right. Set thresholds conservatively, so only patches with clear signs of corruption (high disagreement and deviation) are replaced. Monitor which patches are being replaced and retune if real structure vanishes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time series dataset with known corruption: inject missing-value chunks, noise spikes, or changes in the measurement baseline into specific patches. Train SEER and a baseline forecaster on the corrupted series. On test data with and without matching corruption, measure forecast accuracy as the difference between predicted and true future values.

robustness_gain = baseline_accuracy_clean - baseline_accuracy_corrupted) - (SEER_accuracy_clean - SEER_accuracy_corrupted); a positive gain means SEER's forecasts are hurt less by corruption.

The demo should show that SEER's forecast accuracy drops far less than the baseline when the test data is corrupted in the same way as the training corruption (i.e., the replacement mechanism learns to ignore the corruption pattern, not memorize it).
