N2M: Learning Pose Preference for Mobile Manipulation

A robot learns where to position its base before manipulating objects, by training on successful and failed manipulation attempts.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a mobile robot reaches to manipulate an object—like picking up a bottle—where should it place its base? Geometric approaches check if the arm can physically reach the target, but that is not enough. Modern learning-based manipulation policies are sensitive: they only work well from certain positions. This paper teaches the robot to predict which base positions the policy actually prefers by learning from rollouts (trial runs showing what worked and what did not).

Why base position matters, and why reachability alone fails

A mobile manipulation robot has two parts: a mobile base that rolls around, and an arm attached to it that reaches and manipulates. To pick up an object, the robot must first decide where to park its base, then execute a manipulation policy (the learned routine that actually grasps and moves the object). Older approaches treat this as a geometric problem: they check reachability—can the arm reach the target from this base position?—and pick any position that works.

But modern manipulation policies learned from data are finicky. They work well only from positions where the object appears at a certain distance, angle, and size in the arm's view. A position that is geometrically reachable might still fail because the policy never trained on that view, or the arm's perspective makes the grasp awkward. So the best base position is not just any reachable one; it is one where the policy is confident and skilled.

In one sentence. The robot learns which base positions make its manipulation policy work best, by observing which positions led to successful grasps during training.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect rollout data

Run the manipulation policy from many base positions; record which attempt succeeded.

Observe local scene context

For each position and object, capture a view of the workspace around the arm.

Train a preference predictor

Feed (scene view, base position) pairs to a neural network; output: will the policy succeed from here?

Learn viewpoint-invariant patterns

Show the same scene from different camera angles during training. The model sees the policy's success rate stays the same across camera angles, so it stops relying on spurious camera-based patterns and learns only the real scene layout and positions.

Deploy: score and pick

At test time, sample candidate base positions and score each by predicted policy confidence; pick the highest-scoring one.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper shifts base positioning from a pure geometric decision (reachability) to a learned ranking (policy preference). Instead of asking 'Can the arm reach?', it asks 'Will the policy succeed?' and learns the answer from data.

The hidden quantity

Policy pose preference: how much does the policy like each base position?

measured quantity  =  policy pose preference

Every base position has an implicit preference score in the policy's mind: how often does the policy succeed from that spot? The paper calls this preference inherent to the local scene structure. It is a single number for each (object, base position) pair: high means the manipulation policy is well-trained and confident at that position; low means the policy rarely succeeds there.

The paper estimates this preference by observing rollouts: it counts how often the policy succeeded at each base position and trains a neural network to predict this success rate from the scene structure and the base position, even when the scene is shown from a different camera angle (viewpoint augmentation).

Why it works

Why learning from rollouts overcomes geometric reasoning

Reachability-based approaches only check: can the arm reach? They ignore how hard the grasp is from that angle. A policy trained on images learned to manipulate well when it sees objects at certain distances and angles—call these the trained views. From a position outside the trained view space, the policy fails or acts randomly because it has never seen that configuration. Rollout data directly reveals which positions fall in the trained view space and which do not.

The paper's core move is to train a neural network to predict policy success by observing rollouts. The network sees (scene structure, base position) and outputs a preference score. Why does this work? Because successful rollouts cluster in base positions where the policy is skilled; failed rollouts cluster elsewhere. A neural network learns this clustering and can generalize to new objects and scenes, even from different camera angles, because the paper uses viewpoint augmentation—it shows the same scene from many angles during training so the network learns what matters (the scene layout, the object, the arm's reach) and ignores what does not (camera angle).

The preference scoring equation

First in words:

preference score for a base position equals: how many successful rollouts came from near this position, smoothed by a neural network that accounts for camera viewpoint.

Then the compact form:

score(base_pose, scene) = neural_net(augment_view(scene), encode(base_pose))

Every symbol:

score(base_pose, scene)the predicted probability the policy will succeed from this base position and scene
augment_view(scene)a rotated or repositioned view of the same scene, created synthetically for training
encode(base_pose)the base position represented as numbers a neural network can read
neural_neta learned function trained on rollout data to predict which positions succeeded
A tiny worked example
Rollout data: at position A near the bottle, policy succeeded 45 times out of 50 attempts. At position C, succeeded 2 out of 50.
Neural network training: feed (scene, position A) and label success-rate = 0.90; feed (scene, position C) and label success-rate = 0.04.
Viewpoint augmentation: show the same scene from 5 camera angles for each position. Network learns the success rate is the same regardless of angle.
Test time: for a new scene and unknown positions, use the trained network to score each position. Predicted score for position A ≈ 0.90, for position C ≈ 0.04. Pick position A.

The neural network learns to read a scene and a base position and predict: how often does the policy succeed? Viewpoint augmentation ensures the answer does not depend on which camera angle you show the network.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overfitting to training scenes. If rollout data only shows training scenarios, the network learns spurious patterns (e.g. 'position A always works if there is a red background') and fails on new scenes. Fixing this: train on diverse scenes and use viewpoint augmentation so the network ignores irrelevant details.
Policy changes; preferences become stale. If the manipulation policy is updated or retrained, old rollout data no longer reflects policy behavior. The preference scores become wrong. Fixing this: retrain the preference predictor on new rollout data from the updated policy.
Sparse success data. If the policy succeeds from very few positions in the training data, the neural network has few positive examples to learn from and learns poor preferences. Fixing this: collect rollout data from diverse base positions, even failures, so the network sees the full range of success and failure.
Done right. Viewpoint augmentation ensures the learned preferences generalize to new camera angles and scenes. Diverse rollout data ensures the preference model works well across many environments, not just the training scenarios.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a mobile manipulation simulation (for example, a robot arm on a wheeled base in PyBullet or Mujoco). Collect rollout data: run a learned manipulation policy from 20 different base positions around a target object, 5 attempts per position. Record success or failure. Train a neural network on (scene, position) pairs to predict the success rate. To prove it works: generate 10 new test base positions the network never saw. Predict preferences for each. Measure the actual policy success rate at each position. Show that positions the network scores high have high actual success, and low scores predict low success. Report the correlation: if prediction and reality agree above 0.8, the learned preferences are capturing genuine policy behavior.

prediction accuracy = fraction of test positions where (predicted preference > 0.5) matches (actual success rate > 0.5)

The demo proves that rollout data contains a learnable pattern: policy preferences exist and can be predicted from the scene structure and base pose, without needing the policy's internal weights or architecture.
