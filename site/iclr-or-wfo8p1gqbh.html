Adversarial Déjà Vu: Jailbreak Dictionary Learning for Stronger Generalization to Unseen Attacks

Jailbreak attacks unseen during training are not new tricks but remixes of old ones, so training on diverse combinations of attack skills beats training on isolated attacks.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models can be tricked into saying harmful things if you ask in the right (wrong) way—we call this a jailbreak. The old way to defend was to gather jailbreak examples and train the model to resist them. But new jailbreaks that were never seen during training still slip through. This paper's insight: jailbreaks unseen during training are not truly new. They are recombinations of attack techniques from papers published years earlier. By extracting those core techniques—call them adversarial skills—and training on different combinations of them, a model learns to resist attacks it has never encountered.

Why the old method fails on new attacks

A jailbreak is a prompt that tricks a language model into producing harmful content by going around the safety training. Researchers publish new jailbreaks in papers every few months.

The natural defense is adversarial training: show the model examples of jailbreaks and train it to refuse them, just like showing a spam filter examples of spam. But here is the trap: when a brand new jailbreak appears (one the model never saw in training), the model is often still vulnerable. The training did not prepare it. This is the core problem.

Why does adversarial training fail? Because it teaches the model to block specific attacks, not to understand the attack strategy itself. It is like teaching a student the answer to every math problem in last year's test but not teaching them the underlying math—they will fail when the problems change.

In one sentence. This paper extracts the core attack techniques from past jailbreaks, builds a library of them, trains on different combinations, and finds that models trained this way resist unseen attacks much better, because training on combinations teaches the model to recognize and block the underlying attack techniques, not just memorize surface patterns of specific attacks.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Extract adversarial skills

Analyze 32 attack papers; find the core techniques (e.g. indirect requests, roleplay, encoding tricks).

Build a sparse dictionary

Compress those skills into a small set of primitives; generate plain-English descriptions of each.

Verify skills explain unseen attacks

Show that unseen jailbreaks can be written as combinations of old skills—coverage grows, explanatory power grows.

Train on skill combinations

Instead of training on single isolated attacks, train on diverse mixes of skill primitives.

Measure resistance and over-refusal

Test on unseen jailbreaks; measure both success (blocks new attacks) and safety (does not refuse harmless queries).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts what the model trains on: instead of isolated jailbreak examples, it trains on diverse combinations of extracted adversarial skills.

the object it moves  =  training data: from single attacks to skill combinations
The hidden quantity

Adversarial skill coverage

measured quantity  =  adversarial skill coverage

Adversarial skill coverage is the fraction of a new jailbreak's core techniques that the model has already learned. For example, if Attack-E is made of 60% Skill-A and 40% Skill-C, and the model trained on both Skill-A and Skill-C combinations, then coverage is 100%. If the model only trained on Skill-A, coverage is 60%.

The paper measures this by extracting the adversarial skills from attack papers using an automated pipeline, building a dictionary, then asking: how much of an unseen attack can be explained as a combination of known skills? As skill coverage grows (more diverse combinations in training), the ability to predict and block unseen attacks grows monotonically—meaning it never dips, it only gets better—because the model has learned resistances to more of the underlying building blocks, so when a new attack mixes those blocks, the model can stop it.

Why it works

Why recomposing skills works better

The mechanism has three parts. First, adversarial skills are more general than specific attacks—a skill like 'indirect request' or 'roleplay' shows up in many different jailbreak papers. Training on many combinations of skills forces the model to learn what makes each skill dangerous, not just to memorize which attacks trigger refusals.

Second, jailbreaks unseen during training (Attack-E) are typically combinations of older skills (A + C). If the model has trained on different combinations of A and C, it has already learned to resist when A or C appear. It can recognize them in a new mixture, even if the exact mixture is new. This is why skill coverage—the fraction of the new attack's recipe the model knows—predicts whether the attack will succeed.

Third, training on combinations instead of isolated attacks prevents the model from finding narrow, attack-specific defenses that do not generalize. When you train only on Attack-A, the model might learn a shallow shortcut specific to how Attack-A is phrased. When you train on A+B, A+C, and A+D, the model must learn what A really is, at a deeper level, to handle it in context.

The training objective

First in words:

The model minimizes the refusal loss across all skill combinations, weighted so that combinations with higher skill coverage are learned more strongly than combinations with low coverage.

Then the compact form:

total_loss = sum_over_all_skill_combos (coverage * refusal_loss_on_combo)

Every symbol:

coveragefraction of the skill combo's primitives present in training examples
refusal_loss_on_combohow often the model fails to refuse this particular skill combination
total_losswhat the training algorithm minimizes; lower means better refusals
A tiny worked example
Training data has: Skill-A alone (coverage 1.0), Skill-C alone (coverage 1.0), A+C (coverage 1.0).
Unseen Attack-E = A + C (coverage 1.0).
New jailbreak Attack-F = A + Unknown skill (coverage 0.5, only A is known).
The model learned refusals for coverage 1.0 combos strongly. Attack-E (coverage 1.0) is resisted.
Attack-F (coverage 0.5) is only partially defended—the model has learned to resist A but not the unknown part.

Higher coverage of known skills means stronger learned resistances; the model refuses harder when it recognizes the attack recipe, because it has already trained on that skill in multiple contexts and knows how to block it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incomplete skill dictionary. If the extracted skill dictionary misses common attack primitives, new jailbreaks using those unseen skills will have low coverage and still succeed. The pipeline for extracting skills must be thorough.
Over-refusal from redundant training. Training on many combinations can cause the model to refuse harmless queries that coincidentally share words or structure with attack skills. The paper measures this and shows ASCoT keeps over-refusal low, but it remains a real tension.
Adversarial adaptation. An attacker who sees your skill dictionary could deliberately invent attacks that avoid all known skills (zero coverage). This is an arms race, not a solved problem.
Done right. Maintain a live, updated skill dictionary as new attacks appear. Train on broad, realistic combinations. Measure coverage of new attacks before deployment. Treat this as an ongoing process, not a one-time fix.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small jailbreak dataset of 5 known attacks. Extract their core skills (e.g. using an LLM to describe each attack's trick). Build combinations: A+B, A+C, B+C. Train a small language model on refusals to these combinations. Test on a held-out jailbreak (e.g. A+D) that uses known skills but is unseen. Measure: does the model refuse? Compare to a baseline trained only on single attacks. The demo should show that combination training generalizes better, because the model trained on A, B, C separately only learns shallow surface-pattern refusals, while the model trained on A+B, A+C, B+C must learn what each skill is at a deeper level to handle it in context.

refusal_rate_on_unseen = (number_of_unseen_attacks_blocked) / (total_unseen_attacks)

The demo must show that a model trained on 10 skill combinations blocks more unseen attacks than a model trained on 10 isolated examples—because combinations teach the underlying skills, not surface patterns.
