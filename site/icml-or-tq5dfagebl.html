Improving ML Attacks on LWE with Data Repetition and Stepwise Regression

Make learning-based attacks on LWE stronger by increasing effective training signal through repeated examples and by recovering easier secret coordinates first, then using them to simplify the harder remainder.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Learning With Errors (LWE) is a core problem behind many modern cryptographic systems. Each sample hides a secret list of coordinates behind linear equations plus random noise, and security depends on the secret being hard to recover. Earlier machine-learning attacks worked only when the secret was extremely sparse or simplified. This paper shows that two practical changes push those attacks further. First, much larger training sets with repeated examples increase the usable signal seen by the model. Second, a stepwise regression stage recovers easier coordinates first and then uses those recovered coordinates to simplify the remaining task. Together, these changes let the attack recover denser binary and ternary secrets than earlier work.

Zero assumption version

Why earlier learned attacks stall on denser secrets

In LWE, the attacker sees many noisy linear equations of the form a dot s plus error, modulo a large integer. The goal is to recover the secret list s. When the secret has only a few nonzero entries, a learning system can sometimes detect enough signal to guess them. But as the secret gets denser, each coordinate's effect is harder to isolate because many coordinates contribute at once and the noise masks weak evidence.

Earlier learned attacks therefore hit a hard ceiling. They needed very simple secrets or strong preprocessing before they could recover useful coordinates. The paper asks whether the limitation comes only from model design or also from how the training data is organized. Its answer is that more careful data scaling and staged recovery can expose signal that earlier attacks left unused.

The main change. Boost the usable signal in the training data through larger repeated-example datasets, then recover easier secret coordinates first so the remaining regression problem becomes simpler because part of the hidden structure has already been removed.

What it actually does

Generate larger training sets

Create many more labeled LWE samples so the model sees the hidden structure often enough to separate repeated useful patterns from noise, because weak coordinate evidence must appear many times before it becomes reliable.

Reuse examples deliberately during training so weak but useful patterns appear with higher frequency.

Use the repeated data to estimate secret coordinates that are easier to identify.

Subtract the recovered coordinates' contribution from the equations so the remaining task is less entangled.

Attack the residual problem

Continue recovery on the simplified residual system until denser secrets become reachable.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the attack pipeline from one monolithic prediction attempt into a staged estimation process. It also changes the data regime: repeated examples are treated as a way to strengthen the training signal rather than as wasted duplication.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  effective signal-to-noise per secret coordinate

The hidden quantity that governs success is the usable evidence for each secret coordinate after the rest of the coordinates and the noise have blurred it. When that evidence is too weak, the model cannot tell whether a coordinate is zero or nonzero with useful reliability.

Repeated examples help because they raise the influence of informative patterns during training, and stepwise regression helps because removing already recovered coordinates reduces the burden on the next stage. Both changes increase effective signal-to-noise for the unrecovered coordinates.

Why it works

Why repetition and staged recovery help

A learned attack succeeds only if training exposes a stable relationship between observed LWE samples and secret coordinates. For denser secrets that relationship is weak because each observation mixes many coordinates and added noise. Simply changing the model may not be enough if the training signal itself is too faint.

Repeated examples alter that balance. By presenting selected informative samples more often, the learner spends more update effort on patterns that carry real coordinate information. The paper reports a power-law relation between attack success, dataset size, and repetition, which suggests that training signal can be scaled in a systematic way rather than only by ad hoc tuning.

Stepwise regression adds a second gain. Some coordinates are easier to recover than others. Once those easier coordinates are estimated, their contribution can be removed from the equations. The residual problem is then simpler because fewer unknown active coordinates remain. That staged simplification lets later rounds focus on harder coordinates under lower interference.

Math after the picture

The residual update

First in words:

After estimating part of the secret, subtract that part from each equation so the next stage works on a smaller unknown remainder.

Then the compact form:

b' = b - A * s_hat_partial (mod q)

Every symbol:

Athe public coefficient matrix from the LWE instance
bthe observed right-hand side values
s_hat_partialthe currently recovered subset of secret coordinates
b'the residual observation after removing the recovered contribution
qthe modulus used by the LWE system
A tiny worked example
Suppose one stage has already identified two secret coordinates with high confidence.
Their contribution to each observed equation is computed as A times the recovered partial secret.
Subtracting that contribution from b yields a new right-hand side b' that depends mostly on the unrecovered coordinates plus the original noise.
A later regression model now faces fewer active unknowns, so the remaining coordinates are easier to isolate.

The paper's full attack is more elaborate, but this residual equation captures the key staged simplification.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Repeated data can overfit to the wrong pattern. If repeated examples are not actually informative, the model may amplify noise or dataset quirks instead of true secret structure.
Early coordinate mistakes can snowball. Stepwise regression depends on the first recovered coordinates being mostly correct, because subtracting a wrong partial secret corrupts the residual problem.
Success may remain setting sensitive. The attack still depends on dimension, modulus, secret density, and preprocessing, so gains on one setting family do not automatically transfer everywhere.
Done right. The approach works when repeated examples truly raise usable signal and early recovered coordinates are accurate enough because both conditions are needed for the residual task to become easier rather than harder.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build synthetic LWE instances with binary secrets at several Hamming weights. Train one attack model on a standard dataset and a second on a much larger dataset with repeated informative examples. Then add the stepwise residual subtraction stage to the second system.

Measure coordinate recovery accuracy as secret density grows and track how many coordinates are recovered before and after the residual stage. The demo should show that repetition lifts early recovery and that staged subtraction lifts the later rounds because the remaining task contains fewer active unknowns.

recovery_rate = count(correct_secret_coordinates) / count(all_secret_coordinates)

If recovery_rate stays high at larger Hamming weight, the attack pipeline is extracting more structure from the same noisy equations.
