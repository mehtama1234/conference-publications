DropoutTS: Sample-Adaptive Dropout for Robust Time Series

Instead of throwing away noisy data, measure each sample's noise and turn that into a dropout knob so the model learns lightly from noisy samples and carefully from clean ones.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting models learn from historical patterns, but real-world data is riddled with random noise that tricks the model into memorizing nonsense. Existing fixes either discard suspicious data (losing real rare events) or assume the noise follows a known pattern (expensive and wrong). DropoutTS measures how noisy each sample is and uses that to turn down the learning dial: noisier samples get skipped more often, clean samples get studied hard.

Zero assumption version

Time series models memorize noise

A time series is a list of numbers over time: tomorrow's stock price, next hour's power demand, a sensor's readings. Models learn patterns from history. But real data is messy: sensors drift, outliers spike, measurement error creeps in.

A naive model tries to learn from every data point equally. It fits the noise as eagerly as the signal. If a sensor glitches on Tuesday and the model sees that glitch ten times in training, it memorizes Tuesday glitches. When Wednesday comes, it forecasts wrong.

Existing noise-handling tricks fall into two traps: data pruning discards samples that look suspicious, but a rare-but-real event (a sudden storm, a system surge) looks identical to a glitch, so you lose precious information. Prior modeling assumes noise follows a known shape (like a bell curve), but real noise is nonstationary (constantly changing).

The core insight: Measure how noisy each sample is from its own spectral structure, then use that measurement as a dropout rate. Noisier samples get more neurons masked during training because dropping more neurons removes weights that the model would use to fit small noise fluctuations, so the model has less capacity to memorize the garbage and must find patterns that work across many samples.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Detrend the time series

Strip out the long-term trend so only short-term noise remains.

Transform to frequency domain

Use fast Fourier transform to see what frequencies dominate the signal.

Score the flatness

Flat frequency spectrum means random noise; peaky spectrum means real signal.

Map noise to dropout probability

High noise score becomes high dropout rate; low noise score becomes low dropout rate.

Apply adaptive dropout

During training, randomly mask units with the sample-specific probability.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard dropout applies the same probability to every sample. This paper makes that probability adaptive: it becomes a function of the input sample's measured noise level. Instead of using a fixed knob, each sample gets its own knob, turned based on how messy it is.

the object it moves  =  the dropout rate per sample
The hidden quantity

The noise score

measured quantity  =  spectral noise score

The hidden quantity is a single number per sample measuring how much of it is noise versus real signal. The paper calls this the noise score, computed from the frequency spectrum. Here is how: take the time series sample, remove its trend (smooth long-term direction), transform the remaining wiggles into frequencies using fast Fourier transform, measure the flatness of the frequency spectrum. A flat spectrum means all frequencies are equally present, which is the signature of random noise. A spiky spectrum means a few frequencies dominate, which is the signature of true signal.

The paper estimates this noise score in a differentiable way: use a learnable mask in frequency space that learns to separate signal peaks from noise flat spots. The mask is initialized by spectral flatness measure (a physics-based concept), not random guessing, so it starts smart.

Why it works

Why adaptive dropout works

The mechanism is straightforward: noisier samples contain less real information, so we should learn less aggressively from them. Dropout works by randomly erasing activations during training (making the model learn redundant representations). When you apply dropout with probability 0.1, the model drops 10% of its neurons on that sample. If a sample is 90% noise, we want to drop 90% of neurons because with only a few active neurons, the model has too little computing power to fit the noise fluctuations, which forces it to find instead patterns that appear across multiple samples and are sturdier than noise.

The paper estimates the noise fraction from the spectral flatness (how random the frequencies look). A noisy sample gets a high noise score, which maps directly to a high dropout rate. During training, many neurons are masked because the high dropout probability means each forward pass randomly erases weights, so the model loses access to most of its computing power and cannot fit even small noise patterns. A clean sample gets a low noise score and low dropout rate, so the model keeps most weights active because fewer random erasures mean more weights survive, allowing it to learn intricate patterns. This continuous knob, tuned per sample, is much gentler than binary pruning (which would discard the noisy sample entirely and lose any rare-but-real events it contains).

Why does this reduce overfitting? Because the model has less capacity to fit the garbage in noisy samples, it dedicates its learning to real patterns. Capacity is the model's ability to memorize fine details; turning down capacity for noisy data constrains memorization without deleting information.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

dropout rate equals base rate plus sensitivity times noise score

Then the compact form:

p_dropout = p_base + gamma * s_noise

Every symbol:

p_dropoutthe dropout probability for this sample (fraction of neurons to mask)
p_baseminimum dropout rate (e.g., 0.1, always applied)
gammasensitivity knob (how much the noise score amplifies dropout)
s_noisethe measured noise score for this sample (0 clean to 1 noisy)
A tiny worked example
Sample A: clean signal, s_noise = 0.1, p_dropout = 0.1 + 0.4 * 0.1 = 0.14 (14% dropout, light masking)
Sample B: noisy sample, s_noise = 0.8, p_dropout = 0.1 + 0.4 * 0.8 = 0.42 (42% dropout, heavy masking)
Same model, but Sample B loses three times as many neurons per training step, so it cannot fit the noise fluctuations.

The dropout rate directly controls learning capacity: higher dropout means the model discards more information, forcing it to focus on patterns that appear across many samples and are harder to erase.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mislabeling noise as signal. If a true spike (like a one-day market crash) has flat frequency spectrum because it is a transient event, the noise scorer might mark it as noise and apply excessive dropout. The model would underlearn the spike.
Hyperparameter tuning required. The sensitivity gamma needs to be set per domain. Too high and you ignore noisy samples entirely; too low and dropout adds no noise filtering.
Trend removal is assumed separable. The method detrends first. If the true pattern is itself a nonstationary trend, detrending might remove signal.
Done right. When the noise is truly high-frequency and the signal is low-frequency (common in sensor data), the spectral noise scorer correctly identifies noisy samples, because high-frequency noise creates a flat spectrum while real low-frequency signals create peaks. Adaptive dropout then improves accuracy on rare events because the model is not distracted by noise, and keeps clean data performance high because low-noise samples still get full capacity.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create a synthetic time series with a known clean signal plus injected noise of varying intensity per sample. Train a forecasting backbone (like a transformer or LSTM) three ways: standard dropout (uniform rate), data pruning (discard noisy samples), and DropoutTS (adaptive dropout). Measure forecast accuracy on a held-out clean test set and on a test set containing rare events that resemble noise. Plot the trade-off.

accuracy_gap = clean_accuracy_adaptive - clean_accuracy_pruning

The demo should make impossible to miss that adaptive dropout preserves rare events while keeping clean accuracy as high as pruning, using less training time.
