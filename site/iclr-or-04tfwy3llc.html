Reassessing Layer Pruning in LLMs: Simple Often Beats Fancy

When you cut a language model by pruning its final layers and fine-tune only the head and last three layers, you get better results than fancy pruning methods, and it is much cheaper.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Language models are huge and slow. The obvious fix is to delete layers from the bottom of the stack. But the field has gotten complex: many different methods to decide which layers to cut, complex retraining recipes, step-by-step pruning. This paper ran thousands of GPU hours comparing all those approaches. It found something surprising: the simplest approach wins—cut the final 25% of layers, then fine-tune only the model head and the last three surviving layers. That beats all the other methods by 5-17%.

Zero assumption version

The problem: language models are too big and too slow

A modern language model has many layers—Llama-3 has 32 layers. Each layer reads what came before, does some math (attention and feed-forward networks), and passes it to the next layer. Training and running this takes a lot of power.

Naive idea: just delete some layers. You save compute. But which ones? A beginner might try deleting layers evenly (every 5th layer) or at random. That breaks the model catastrophically—accuracy drops 30-50%. So the field invented many layer selection methods: measure how much removing each layer hurts the loss, or measure the size of the weights in each layer, or measure how much each layer changes when you train. Then, after cutting, the field invented LoRA (a trick to add trainable matrices alongside frozen layers), thinking that was the best way to fix the broken model. But does any of this actually work well together?

The setup. Prune layers, then fine-tune to restore accuracy. The challenge: which layers to cut, and how to retrain afterward.

Discover the simple winner

Find the best retraining

After pruning, freezing all but the last three layers plus the output head works better than LoRA, because LoRA adds trainable matrices everywhere and wastes effort retraining layers that already work fine.

Cut once, not step-by-step

One-shot pruning (remove 25% at once) beats iterative pruning (remove 5%, then 5% more, etc.).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces fancy layer selection (using gradient importance scores, activation magnitudes, or loss changes) with a simple rule: always prune from the end. Then it replaces LoRA retraining (which adds trainable matrices to every layer) with partial-layer fine-tuning (freeze most layers, update only the last three and the output head).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: which layers do what

measured quantity  =  Per-layer task importance

Deep inside every layer is a number measuring how much that layer matters for the final answer. The paper calls this the gradient importance (or Taylor score): take the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer's weights, multiply by the weights themselves, and sum across all weights in the layer. Layers with high scores are 'important'; low scores mean 'this layer can be removed without hurting much.'

The paper estimates this quantity by computing gradients on a calibration dataset (a small sample of data used to measure importance without full training). It finds that the layers at the end (the last 25%) have the lowest scores—they are redundant for the general pattern recognition the model is doing. It also finds that a very simple heuristic works almost as well: just measure the magnitude (size) of the weights in each layer.

Why it works

Why it works: information flows front-to-back

Language models are stacked layers, each one reading the output of the previous one. The early layers learn general patterns: 'this is a noun phrase', 'this is a synonym for that'. The middle layers refine and combine those patterns. The final layers specialize: 'given the middle layers' understanding, what word should come next?'

When you cut the final 25% of layers, you remove that specialization step. The pruned model is smaller and still understands the input well because the earlier layers still do most of the work. But now it cannot predict perfectly—the output is 'generic.' To fix it, you fine-tune. Standard LoRA adds trainable matrices everywhere, which is wasteful because the early layers already work fine. Instead, the paper's method freezes everything except the last three layers and the output head. Those layers learn to translate from 'general understanding' to 'task-specific answer.' Since only three layers are trainable, training is fast (fewer parameters to update) and stable (the early layers do not drift).

The equation: gradient-based layer importance

First in words:

Layer importance equals the sum over all weights in the layer of (gradient with respect to that weight) times (the weight value itself). High importance means the layer has a big effect on the loss.

Then the compact form:

Importance_n = sum_k |dL/dW_n_k * W_n_k|

Every symbol:

Importance_nimportance score for layer n
Lthe loss (how wrong the model's prediction is)
W_n_kthe k-th weight in layer n
dL/dW_n_khow much the loss changes if this weight changes by 1
Tiny worked example: two layers
Layer 1 has 4 weights with gradients and values: |0.3 * 0.8| + |0.2 * 0.5| + |0.1 * 0.3| + |0.4 * 0.6| = 0.24 + 0.10 + 0.03 + 0.24 = 0.61.
Layer 32 has 4 weights: |0.05 * 0.2| + |0.02 * 0.1| + |0.03 * 0.15| + |0.01 * 0.1| = 0.01 + 0.002 + 0.0045 + 0.001 = 0.0175.
Layer 32's importance is 30x smaller than Layer 1's. Layer 32 is safe to remove; removing Layer 1 would hurt a lot.

Layers at the end have low importance scores, so they can be removed safely. The paper prunes the final layers (lowest scores) and fine-tunes only the top and output.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pruning the wrong layers. If you prune early layers (which hold core knowledge), the model breaks badly. Pruning the final layers works because they are redundant. Intuition fails if you do not measure first.
Freezing too many layers during retraining. If you freeze all but the output head (only 1 layer trainable), you cannot recover performance. The last three layers plus the head is a sweet spot—enough to adapt to the new structure, few enough to train quickly.
Iterative pruning (removing layers in multiple rounds). Removing 8 layers once is cheaper and works better than removing 2 layers, retraining, removing 2 more, retraining again, because each intermediate retraining is expensive and the model has to readjust multiple times, wasting GPU hours with no gain in the final accuracy.
Done right: measure importance, prune from the end, fine-tune only the top and head. The paper's recipe is simple and works across many models and datasets, because each piece addresses a real cost: pruning from the end removes truly redundant layers, freezing most weights reduces training parameters by 90%, and one-shot avoids wasted retraining cycles.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a demo that loads a small language model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B or Gemma-2-2B), removes the final 25% of layers, then fine-tunes only the output head and the last three layers on a small task (e.g., answering 100 QA pairs). Measure accuracy before pruning, after pruning (broken model), and after fine-tuning. Also run the same task using LoRA on the pruned model (trainable low-rank matrices on every remaining layer) and compare time and accuracy. Show that simple partial-layer fine-tuning recovers much faster and reaches higher accuracy because you have far fewer parameters to update and no overhead from LoRA's low-rank matrices.

Accuracy before pruning - accuracy after fine-tuning (should be small, maybe 2-5%). Training time (partial-layer should be 10x faster than LoRA).

The demo must make it impossible to miss: the simplest approach—cut the end, fine-tune the top three layers—beats all the fancier methods the field invented.
