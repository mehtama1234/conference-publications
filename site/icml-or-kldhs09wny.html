Equivariant Covariance Tensors for Geometric Learning

When a geometric model predicts a tensor (like stress on a material), you need to know not just the guess but also its confidence — and that confidence must respect the symmetries of rotation.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Geometric deep learning models can predict physical quantities shaped like matrices (tensors), such as stress on an object or the dielectric response of a material. But confidence measures for these predictions are missing: existing models give point guesses with no uncertainty. When the underlying physical laws are the same no matter how you rotate your coordinate system, the confidence measure must also be rotation-invariant. This paper builds confidence matrices (covariances) for tensor predictions while preserving that symmetry exactly.

Zero assumption version

No confidence for tensor predictions

A geometric deep learning model predicts a tensor (a matrix-like quantity) from a molecular or material structure. It outputs a single best guess: the inertia tensor, the stress field, the dielectric response. But what if the training data was sparse or noisy in some direction? The model might be wrong by a tiny amount or by a lot, and there is no way to tell.

A naive approach would compute a confidence matrix (a covariance) directly from the numbers, but this breaks under rotation. Rotate your coordinate system (a symmetry of physical law), and suddenly the confidence matrix says you are confident in different directions — even though the physics did not change. This is nonsense: if the laws are rotationally symmetric, your confidence should be too.

In one sentence. Build a confidence matrix for tensor predictions that stays mathematically valid and rotationally symmetric.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decompose the covariance

Split the full 6x6 confidence matrix into three rotationally-aware pieces (scalar + rank-2 + rank-4).

Predict each piece

Train the model to output those pieces separately, each one respecting rotation symmetry.

Exponentiate to validate

Measure uncertainty

Use the confidence matrix to compute risk (how much does the guess vary) and out-of-distribution sensitivity (does this structure look unlike training data).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: decomposition into irreducible pieces

measured quantity  =  irreducible representation structure

Physical systems are built on symmetry. When a tensor transforms under rotation, it does not transform all at once: it breaks into pieces that transform predictably. A rank-2 symmetric tensor (like stress) splits into one scalar part (a trace, which stays the same under any rotation) plus two rank-2 parts (which rotate together) plus one rank-4 part. The paper explicitly measures how much of the total uncertainty lives in each piece.

This decomposition is not just mathematical beauty: it is the key to building a covariance that is guaranteed to respect rotational symmetry. By predicting each irreducible piece separately using networks that are built to be rotationally aware, the paper ensures the full reassembled covariance has the same symmetries as the underlying physics.

Why it works

Why it works: from pieces to the curved space

The core problem: a confidence matrix is not a flat grid of numbers you can tweak freely. It must be positive-definite (all its eigenvalues must be positive), or it describes nonsense — say, an 'uncertainty direction' with negative variance, which makes no physical sense. Rotating your coordinate system should rotate the confidence the same way; otherwise it says the model's uncertainty depends on how you label your axes, which is false.

The paper solves this in two steps. First, it uses irreducible representations to enforce rotational symmetry. Instead of predicting a raw 6x6 matrix, the model predicts three separately-rotationally-symmetric pieces (a scalar, two rank-2 blocks, and a rank-4 block). Because each piece is built to transform predictably under rotation, their combination is guaranteed rotationally symmetric. Second, it uses matrix exponentiation to guarantee positive-definiteness: the predicted pieces live in a flat space (the Lie algebra of symmetric matrices), but the paper exponentiates them onto a curved space (the manifold of positive-definite matrices). On that curved space, every point is valid. The rotation-respecting structure is preserved through exponentiation because exponentiation is itself a rotationally-symmetric operation.

Math after the picture

Decomposing the covariance into rotationally-aware pieces

First in words:

The full covariance is reconstructed from its irreducible representation blocks. A symmetric rank-2 tensor can be decomposed into irreducible SO(3) representations as a scalar (l=0) plus two rank-2 pieces (l=2) plus one rank-4 piece (l=4). The paper predicts parameters for each piece, exponentiates them to the valid manifold, and combines them back into the full six-by-six covariance.

Then the compact form:

C = exp( lambda_0 * P_0 + sum_{i,j in rank-2} lambda_2ij * P_2ij + sum_{i,j,k,l in rank-4} lambda_4ijkl * P_4ijkl )

Every symbol:

Cthe final 6x6 covariance matrix
lambda_0, lambda_2ij, lambda_4ijkllearnable parameters predicted by the network (one value per irreducible block)
P_0, P_2ij, P_4ijklbasis matrices (fixed) that correspond to each irreducible representation
exp(...)matrix exponential: map from the flat tangent space to the curved manifold of positive-definite matrices
A tiny worked example
Suppose a scalar irreducible block predicts lambda_0 = 0.5, a rank-2 block predicts one learnable value 0.3, and a rank-4 block predicts one learnable value 0.1.
We form the sum: 0.5 * (6x6 basis matrix for scalar) + 0.3 * (6x6 basis matrix for rank-2) + 0.1 * (6x6 basis matrix for rank-4) = a 6x6 matrix M.
We exponentiate: C = exp(M). Every eigenvalue of C is positive (exp of any real number is positive), so C is guaranteed valid.
If we rotate the coordinate system, each basis matrix P_j rotates by the same orthogonal matrix. The exponentiated result C rotates the same way, preserving exact symmetry.

The exponentiation bends the flat learned numbers onto the curved surface where all matrices are positive-definite; the irreducible representations ensure the final covariance respects rotations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Heavy-tailed errors in training data. If the training data has outliers (occasional very wrong labels), the learned covariance underestimates risk. The paper addresses this with a loss function based on the Laplace distribution, which down-weights extreme errors and prevents the model from learning to ignore them, maintaining stable learning even when some training examples are way off.
Insufficient decomposition. If you predict only the scalar piece and ignore rank-2 and rank-4 pieces, you miss directional uncertainty. The full decomposition is necessary to capture that a prediction might be uncertain in one tensor direction but confident in another.
Exponentiation cost. Computing matrix exponential is slower than a raw prediction. For real-time systems, the overhead may matter; trade-off between accuracy and speed.
Done right: physical consistency. The method is validated on two real tasks: inertia tensors of 3D shapes and dielectric tensors from materials science. The model's uncertainty aligns with how wrong it actually is, and out-of-distribution detection works (the model grows more uncertain on structures unlike the training set).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train the model on a subset of inertia tensors from ModelNet40 (a dataset of 3D shapes) with added noise in the labels. Withhold a second subset with different rotations (same shapes in rotated coordinate systems). On the holdout set, rotate both the predictions and their confidence matrices by the same rotation. Check that the model's predicted uncertainty aligns with its prediction error (mean squared error of the tensor estimate), and that uncertainty is the same after rotation (no spurious dependence on coordinate choice).

alignment = correlation(predicted_confidence_magnitude, prediction_error) ; symmetry_error = |alignment(original) - alignment(rotated)|

The demo must show that rotating a structure and its prediction does not change the uncertainty magnitude — the model's confidence is a property of the structure itself, not the arbitrary choice of axes.
