TetraGT: Tetrahedral Geometry-Driven Explicit Token Interactions with Graph Transformer for Molecular Representation Learning

Instead of ignoring how atoms are angled to each other, the paper makes those angles themselves into learnable pieces the model can reason about.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

When a model learns about molecules, it usually knows atoms and bonds, but forgets the angles between atoms. This matters: a molecule bent one way works completely differently than the same atoms bent another way. This paper makes a model that treats those angles as first-class things to learn from, the way it learns atoms and bonds.

Zero assumption version

Angles matter but nobody measures them directly

In a molecule, atoms sit in 3D space at certain angles and twists to each other. A water molecule bent at 104 degrees, not 180 degrees, changes everything about how it behaves. When you predict how a drug binds to a protein or how fast an enzyme works, the exact shape — the angles — is what matters.

Current models read a molecule as a graph: nodes are atoms, edges are bonds. They learn atom identity and bond type, but not the angles between bonds or the twist around them. A naive approach tries to patch this by encoding the angle into each bond, but that buries the angle inside a number and never lets the model think about it directly. The angle stays hidden.

The paper's move in one sentence: Make bond angles and torsion angles into their own learnable pieces (numbers the model updates during training), because when the model sees them directly instead of hidden in bonds, it can learn how they constrain each other and predict molecular behavior.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds angle pieces to the input. Normally a graph transformer sees only atoms and bonds. Now it sees atoms, bonds, bond angles (the angle between three atoms), and torsion angles (the twist around a bond). Each angle becomes a learnable list of numbers, updated during training the same way atom numbers are.

the object it moves  =  angle pieces
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: structural importance

measured quantity  =  angle importance score

When the model learns, some angles matter more for predicting the molecular property (like drug potency) and some barely matter at all. Structural importance is the measure of how much changing an angle would change the prediction. An angle critical to the 3D shape gets a high importance score; an angle in a floppy region gets a low one.

The paper does not measure importance directly as a number. Instead, it lets the transformer's attention weights reveal which angles the model finds important — angles that many other angles attend to are implicitly more important. The tetrahedral attention mechanism (attention between angles in the same local 3D neighborhood) creates a pattern where geometrically critical angles accumulate high attention.

Why it works

Why explicit angle pieces work

A molecule is a 3D object. Its shape — the angles and twists — determines its function. When angles are buried inside bond numbers, the model has to infer they matter by trial and error. With explicit angle pieces, the model sees the shape directly.

Second, angles interact. The twist of one bond constrains the angles on neighboring bonds. A transformer with angle pieces can learn these constraints. It focuses from one angle to nearby angles, discovering that when angle A bends, angle B must adjust. This is much harder to learn when angles are implicit.

Third, tetrahedral attention (attention only between angles in the same local 3D neighborhood) keeps computation tractable and forces the model to focus on the spatial relationships that matter — angles that are close in space usually constrain each other.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

Attention score between angle tokens is nonzero only if the angles share a common atom within a certain distance in the tetrahedral neighborhood.

Then the compact form:

attention_score(angle_i, angle_j) = (query_i · key_j) / sqrt(d) if share_atom(i, j) else 0

Every symbol:

angle_ithe learned vector representation of angle i
angle_jthe learned vector representation of angle j
query_ithe query vector derived from angle_i, learned by the model
key_jthe key vector derived from angle_j, learned by the model
dthe number in each vector (usually 64 or 128)
share_atom(i, j)true if angles i and j share an atom in the tetrahedral neighborhood
A tiny worked example
Angle 1 (bond 1–2–3 at 109°): list of numbers (query and key numbers) computed.
Angle 2 (bond 2–3–4 at 115°): shares atom 2 and 3 with angle 1. Focus score = (109 query · 115 key) / sqrt(64) ≈ 12500 / 8 = 1562.5; they focus on each other.
Angle 5 (torsion at atom 10, far away): does not share atoms. Focus score = 0; they do not interact.
Result: nearby angles learn to coordinate; distant angles are independent.

The tetrahedral constraint (share_atom) grounds the model in the actual 3D distances; only angles touching the same atom in 3D space can attend to each other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Angle computation error. If bond angles or torsion angles are computed from atom coordinates with numerical errors, the angle pieces feed garbage to the model. The model learns from noise, not the real 3D shape.
Over-attention to irrelevant angles. If tetrahedral filtering is too loose (includes angles not actually close in space), the model wastes focus learning spurious angle interactions that do not match the real 3D distances.
Overfitting on small molecules. On tiny training molecules, the model might memorize angle patterns instead of learning general principles. Transfer to larger molecules fails.
Done right. Angle pieces are computed with numerical care, tetrahedral filtering matches actual 3D distances, and the model is regularized (e.g., dropout) to learn general patterns in how angles change, not memorize specific molecules.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a graph transformer with and without angle pieces on a small set of molecules where you have the 3D structure and a simple property (e.g., total energy from quantum chemistry, or how strongly a drug binds a protein). Compute the error (prediction minus true value) on a held-out test set of molecules the model has never seen. The angle-piece model should have lower error on molecules where 3D shape matters critically (e.g., chiral drugs, where mirror-image molecules have opposite potencies), and should not hurt performance on simpler molecules.

error_no_angles - error_with_angles = how much angle tokens improve

The demo makes impossible to miss: when you hide the angles, you lose signal about molecular shape; when you show the angles, the model gets the predictions right.
