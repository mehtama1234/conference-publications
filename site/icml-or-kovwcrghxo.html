One Humanoid Controller, Many Robots

Train one neural network controller once, and it works on dozens of different humanoid robot body shapes without retraining.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Humanoid robots come in many sizes and proportions — some are stocky, some are tall, some have different motor strengths. Today, you must retrain a control policy for each robot. This paper shows you can train ONE policy that learns to walk on a wide range of randomly randomized humanoid bodies during training, and then that single policy works on brand-new robot designs it has never seen before.

Zero assumption version

Why each robot needs its own training

Today's humanoid robot controllers are trained on a specific body. If the robot is 180cm tall and weighs 60kg, the controller learns to move that exact body. If you take that same trained controller and run it on a different robot — say, one that is 150cm tall or has different motor strength — the policy crashes immediately, because the neural network was tuned to swing legs that take 1-meter strides and use a specific amount of motor power — if the new robot has shorter legs, those learned stride patterns are too wide and the robot topples; if it has weaker motors, the learned commands demand more power than the motors can deliver.

This happens because the neural network's behavior is tightly wound up with the specific body it learned on. The leg length, motor strengths, and moment of inertia (the resistance to spinning) are baked into every learned behavior. A small robot needs faster, twitchier motions (shorter legs cover distance slower, so quicker beats are needed to maintain balance), while a tall robot needs slower, more languid movements (longer legs move more distance per swing, so slower beats stay steady). A controller trained on one cannot transfer to the other because the timing and magnitude of learned motions do not fit a different body.

The problem. Each new humanoid robot design demands a fresh training run from scratch.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Randomize the body shape

During training, vary robot dimensions (leg length, torso width), mass, and motor strength within realistic bounds.

Semantically align observation space

Normalize sensors (e.g., joint angles, velocities) by leg length and body size so they mean the same thing across morphologies.

Semantically align action space

Scale motor commands by actual motor strength so a magnitude-5 command means the same thing on weak and strong robots.

Train one policy on all morphologies

Use reinforcement learning to discover motions that work on every randomized body in the distribution.

Test zero-shot on new robots

Feed a previously unseen robot to the policy. It walks without retraining.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: morphological invariance

measured quantity  =  how much the policy works across different body shapes

Morphological invariance is a measure of whether the learned policy 'understands' walking in a general sense, independent of specific leg length or motor strength. High invariance means the policy has learned motion principles that transfer across bodies. Low invariance means it has memorized tricks specific to one body.

The paper estimates this by training on a distribution of randomized morphologies and measuring whether the policy succeeds on new morphologies it has never encountered — including real robots it never trained on. If it walks smoothly on all of them, the invariance is high.

Why it works

Why training on many shapes creates a universal controller

When you train on a fixed robot, the policy finds movements tuned to that robot's physics: if the legs are 1 meter long, move them in 1-meter-wide sweeps; if the motors are weak, use gentle torques. That policy encodes the specific body into its learned behavior.

Training on many randomized bodies forces the policy to find principles that work across all of them. A short-legged robot cannot use wide sweeps; a weak-motored robot cannot use large torques. By mixing all these constraints in one training run, the policy learns to do the same task with different motion strategies — adapting to what the body can do. This is not explicit adaptation (the policy does not say 'measure my leg length and adjust'); instead, the policy has internalized many different body shapes and learned a control strategy that does not break when the body changes shape.

The semantic alignment (normalizing sensors and actions) amplifies this. By expressing 'bend the knee by 20 degrees' in normalized units that mean the same across bodies, and by expressing 'apply 50% motor strength' in units that scale to actual motor power, the policy receives signals in a common language. It then learns that language — so when a new robot speaks the same language, the policy understands it.

Math after the picture

How the policy learns a shared control law

First in words:

success = average_reward(policy, sample from randomized_morphology_distribution). Maximize this over all morphologies in the training set.

Then the compact form:

max_policy sum_morphology P(morphology) * E[return | policy, morphology]

Every symbol:

policythe learned neural network controller
morphologya specific body configuration: height, mass, motor strength, etc.
P(morphology)the probability distribution over different body shapes during training
E[return | policy, morphology]the expected cumulative reward when running the policy on that morphology
A tiny worked example
Suppose we train on three morphologies: tall (P=0.33), medium (P=0.33), stocky (P=0.33).
For one policy candidate, tall gets reward 90, medium gets 85, stocky gets 70. Average = 0.33*90 + 0.33*85 + 0.33*70 = 81.5.
For a better policy, tall gets 88, medium gets 87, stocky gets 85. Average = 0.33*88 + 0.33*87 + 0.33*85 = 86.7.
The second policy is better because it maintains performance across all morphologies, not just the tall one.

The policy that wins is the one that keeps all morphologies happy, not just the average.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Morphology distribution does not match the test robot. If all training bodies are tall but the test robot is short, or all training bodies have weak motors but the test robot has strong ones, the policy has not seen that regime and will fail.
Semantic alignment is poor. If you normalize observations or actions incorrectly, the policy receives garbled signals. A tall robot and a short robot will not speak the same language, and transfer fails.
The task is physics-specific. Humanoid walking relies on balance and momentum. A policy trained on Earth gravity will not transfer to the Moon or to humanoids with entirely different mass distributions.
When done right. If the training shows many realistic morphologies, and sensors and actions are aligned by body scale and motor strength, a universal policy will walk new robots without falling on the first try.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulation with two humanoid morphologies: one tall (height 1.8m, leg mass 4kg) and one stocky (height 1.2m, leg mass 6kg). Train a policy for 100,000 steps on a randomized distribution of bodies that interpolates between these two extremes and a few other variants. Then evaluate the trained policy on each of the original two morphologies and on a held-out intermediate morphology (e.g., height 1.5m, leg mass 5kg) that was never seen during training. Measure walking success rate (does it fall?) and forward velocity (how fast does it move?).

generalization_score = (accuracy_on_tall + accuracy_on_stocky + accuracy_on_unseen) / 3

The demo must show the same policy walking smoothly on all three morphologies, including the unseen intermediate one. This proves the policy has learned a morphology-invariant strategy, not memorized specific bodies.
