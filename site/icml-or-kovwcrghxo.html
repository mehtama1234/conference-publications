Cross-Humanoid Whole-Body Control

One trained policy controls many different humanoid robots by learning from randomized robot bodies during training and mapping all robots into a shared observation-action language.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Humanoid robots come in wildly different shapes: some have thick legs, others are spindly; some have arms, others don't; some weigh 50 kg, others 150 kg. Today, each robot needs its own separately trained controller. This paper shows you can train ONE policy that works on all of them, by making the policy learn from random variations of the robot's body during training. The trick is teaching the policy to read its own body's shape and adapt on the fly.

Zero assumption version

The robot-specific wall

Right now, training a humanoid controller is expensive: you build a controller for one robot shape by trial and error, feeding the simulator thousands of examples and tuning thousands of weight values. When a new robot arrives with different leg lengths or arm masses, you throw away the old controller and start over, because the old weight values no longer apply. This makes scaling hard because adding each new robot to your fleet costs weeks of development, so every new robot is a new project.

A beginner might think: just train on the target robot and adapt. That fails because each robot is unique; a weight value that works for a short leg doesn't work for a long leg, and you can't know in advance which values to reuse. When a new robot arrives with different proportions, you must spend weeks retraining from scratch, so each new robot is a new project and growing a fleet of robots becomes expensive and slow.

In one sentence. This paper trains one policy on many random robot variations, so it learns to read the current robot's shape and adapt.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build a generic robot blueprint.

Define a template with slots for 32 joints, masses, link lengths — parameters that vary between real robots.

Randomize the blueprint during training.

Randomly tweak the masses, link lengths, joint limits using a method that preserves real physics.

Map every robot into the same 32-joint language.

A robot with 20 joints gets padded; a robot with 25 joints gets reordered — all speak the same vocabulary.

Policy reads the robot's numbers and computes actions.

Uses a graph neural network (or Transformer) that sees the robot skeleton, then outputs the movement command for each joint.

Deploy: the policy sees the new robot's shape and walks.

On a real robot with new body proportions, the trained policy reads those proportions and produces correct motions immediately.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of training one policy per robot, the paper changes the TRAINING PROCEDURE: it teaches the policy from hundreds of random robot variations. The policy learns to watch the robot's shape (measured as joint angles, link masses, and kinematic structure) and adjust its moves accordingly.

The hidden quantity

The morphology representation: what the policy must read to adapt

measured quantity  =  Robot morphology encoding

The hidden quantity is the robot's structural properties — the things that make one humanoid different from another: leg lengths, mass distribution, joint limits, how joints connect (a tree of limbs). The policy must encode these into a compact representation to know how to move each unique robot.

The paper estimates this using three tools: (1) explicit parameters (link lengths, masses stored in a template), (2) joint angles the policy observes directly (the current pose tells you limb configuration), and (3) a graph structure (a connection diagram of which joints link together). The policy passes these through a Graph Convolutional Network (which is a model that learns from tree-like structures) or a Transformer (a model that compares all joints against each other to find patterns).

Why it works

Why one policy can walk all robots

The core trick is this: if you train on many random robot shapes, the policy learns the UNDERLYING PATTERNS of how to walk, not the specifics of one robot. For example, when training sees a tall skinny robot, it learns the policy must take longer strides; when it sees a short heavy robot, it learns to use shorter, more cautious steps. By the time training ends, the policy has internalized a broad rule: read the robot's dimensions, then scale your motions accordingly.

Specifically, the paper does three things. First, it randomizes the robot's inertial properties (masses and mass distribution) using a mathematically sound method that keeps physics realistic — you can't make a link have a negative mass or infinite density, so the paper parameterizes masses in a way that always respects the laws of physics. Second, it forces all robots into a shared 32-joint language: a robot with 20 joints gets the unused slots filled with zero; a robot with unusual joint orderings gets remapped to a canonical order. This way, the policy sees 'joint 5' and knows it's always the left knee, on every robot. Third, it gives the policy a model that explicitly reads the robot's graph structure — using a Graph Convolutional Network that sees which joints are neighbors in the skeleton tree, so it learns to coordinate nearby joints together rather than treating all joints as isolated.

Math after the picture

The core equation: randomized inertia parameterization

First in words:

The inertia matrix for each link must stay physically valid. The paper factorizes any valid inertia matrix as the product of an upper-triangular matrix times its transpose, then randomizes the triangular matrix instead of the inertia directly.

Then the compact form:

If J (is positive definite), then J = L * L^T where L is upper-triangular with positive diagonal.

Every symbol:

JThe inertia matrix: a 3x3 table describing how hard it is to spin the link
LAn upper-triangular matrix (zeros below the diagonal) that encodes J safely
positive definiteAll eigenvalues are > 0; the matrix describes a real, rotatable object
L^TL transposed: swap rows and columns
A tiny worked example
Real robot leg: inertia J = [[8, 0, 0], [0, 6, 0], [0, 0, 4]]. Factor it: L = [[2.83, 0, 0], [0, 2.45, 0], [0, 0, 2.0]].
During training, randomize L by adding noise to each element: L becomes [[2.83, 0.1, 0], [0.05, 2.45, 0.2], [0, 0.1, 2.0]].
Reconstruct inertia: J_new = L_new * L_new^T. Result is guaranteed physically valid (still positive definite) even with the noise.
Old way: randomize inertia directly → sometimes J becomes invalid (negative eigenvalue → unphysical). This way: always valid.

By parameterizing inertia through its Cholesky factor, random tweaks never break physics, so training sees only realistic robot variations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Randomization breaks physics. If you perturb masses, link lengths, or joint angles without care, training sees physically impossible robots (negative mass, infinite stiffness) that teach the policy nonsense rules. This paper fixes it by parameterizing through Cholesky factorization — randomness never violates physics.
Robots don't share a common vocabulary. Robot A has joints [ankle, knee, hip, ...], Robot B has joints [hip, knee, ankle, ...] in different order. The policy gets confused about which joint is which. This paper fixes it by mapping all robots to a canonical 32-joint global space.
Policy ignores robot structure; treats each joint independently. Humanoid robots are tree-structured (each limb branches from a main trunk). If the policy doesn't know this and learns each joint in isolation, it can't coordinate (one arm pulls while the other pushes). This paper fixes it by using a Graph Convolutional Network that sees the skeleton tree.
Done right. Train on randomized valid robots in shared notation with graph-aware encoders. Deploy to new robots in the same family and watch them walk correctly on day one.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulator with three humanoid designs: one short and stocky (legs short, heavy), one tall and thin (legs long, light), one medium. Create a fourth design never used in training. Train a single policy on randomized versions of designs 1–3 (vary leg lengths and link masses 200 times each). Deploy the trained policy to design 4 without retraining. Measure walk speed, stability, and whether the robot falls.

Generalization success rate = (number of unseen robots the policy walks stably) / (total unseen robots). Compare to a baseline policy trained only on one of the designs.

The core finding must be unavoidable: the generalist policy walks unseen robot designs on day one, while a specialist policy trained on one design fails completely on the others.
