R2PS: Robust Real-Time Pursuit under Imperfect Information

When a pursuer does not know exactly where the evader is, this paper learns strategies that still catch them by reasoning about all possible positions and planning ahead.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

In pursuit-evasion games, one agent (the pursuer) tries to catch another (the evader) on a graph-shaped environment. Real strategies face two hard problems: you do not know the evader's exact position, and the evader can see your moves and respond. This paper shows how to learn policies (strategies) that handle both unknowns by reasoning about possible positions and planning against worst-case evasion, all at real-time speeds on any graph structure.

Zero assumption version

Chasing someone you cannot fully see

Pursuit-evasion games are classic problems: one player (the pursuer) must catch another (the evader) in a graph—a network of connected locations. The pursuer knows the graph layout but not the evader's exact position; it only sees hints (nearby observations). Meanwhile, the evader watches the pursuer move and can dodge accordingly.

Existing solutions assume either you know where the evader is (perfect information) or the evader moves randomly. But in reality, you have incomplete position data AND the evader is intelligent and anticipatory. No real-time algorithm does both.

Without a strategy built for uncertainty and intelligence, the pursuer chases blindly, the evader slips away, and many unsafe or inefficient applications (like security patrols, target tracking, or autonomous interception) fail.

The move. Learn a pursuer policy by combining belief reasoning (track possible evader positions) with reinforcement learning against worst-case adaptive evasion.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a belief state (a tracked set of possible evader positions) to the standard dynamic programming pursuit algorithm. This single addition lets the algorithm work when information is incomplete.

the object it moves  =  belief state (tracked positions)
The hidden quantity

The belief coverage gap

measured quantity  =  Reachable belief size

The paper measures one key quantity: the reachable belief size—how many locations the evader might occupy after the pursuer moves. This shrinks as the pursuer moves closer, grows when observation is poor, and stays bounded because the graph is finite.

The learning process tunes the pursuer's moves to keep the belief small and shrinking. A good strategy quickly narrows down where the evader can hide; a bad one lets the belief stay large or even grow, giving the evader escape room.

Why it works

Why it works: three-part chain

The method rests on three insights. First, dynamic programming for pursuit-evasion still finds optimal strategies even if the evader moves asynchronously (not perfectly synchronized with the pursuer). This is proved mathematically and is the foundation.

Second, you can embed belief reasoning into that DP framework: instead of tracking exact positions, track sets of possible positions. When the pursuer moves, the belief shrinks to only positions the evader can lawfully reach from its previous possible locations. This keeps the algorithm correct.

Third, the paper wraps this into a reinforcement learning training loop. The RL agent (the pursuer) learns by playing against a solver that computes the evader's worst-case counter-move. Because it trains against worst-case evasion, the learned policy works against intelligent, adaptive evaders—not just predictable ones. And because the training uses graph neural networks (learned models that treat all nodes the same way), the policy generalizes to new graphs with different sizes and structures without needing to retrain.

Math after the picture

The belief update rule

First in words:

After the pursuer moves to a new position, the belief shrinks to include only evader locations reachable from the previous belief positions within one step.

Then the compact form:

B_new = {v in V : exists u in B_old, (u,v) in E}

Every symbol:

B_newthe new belief set (possible evader positions after update)
B_oldthe old belief set (possible positions before the move)
vany location (vertex) in the graph
Vall locations in the graph
uany previous possible location
Eall connections (edges) in the graph
(u,v) in Ethere is a direct path from location u to location v
Tiny worked example
Graph: nodes A, B, C, D. Edges: A-B, B-C, C-D, A-D.
Before move: belief = {A, D} (evader might be at A or D).
Evader can reach: from A to A or B; from D to C or D.
After update: B_new = {A, B, C, D} (all reachable).
If pursuer moves close and observation says evader is not at B: belief shrinks to {A, C, D}.

This update keeps the belief consistent with the graph structure and the evader's physical movement limits.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Observation is too sparse. If the pursuer gets almost no clues about the evader's position, the belief grows large and the strategy weakens. The method still works, but the pursuit takes longer and may not catch the evader in time.
Graph is very large. A huge graph means the belief set can be huge, and updating it becomes slow. The paper assumes the graph is bounded, so this is a practical limit.
Evader has a completely unexpected strategy. The method trains against the worst-case rational evader. If the evader uses a bizarre irrational move (not just optimal play), the learned policy may not handle it.
Done right. The policy is trained against a solver that assumes the evader knows the pursuer's strategy and will find the best counter-move. Training against this worst-case assumption ensures the learned policy works even against intelligent, adaptive adversaries.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small grid graph (e.g., 5x5 or a small network), place an evader at a random location, and give the pursuer only partial observations (e.g., "evader is within 2 steps of position X"). Run the learned policy against both a random evader and an optimal adversarial evader. Measure how often the pursuer catches the evader and how many steps it takes.

catch_rate = num_caught / num_trials, steps_to_catch = sum_caught_trials steps / num_caught

The demo must show that the belief-based pursuer catches the evader more reliably and faster than a baseline that ignores uncertainty (because it reasons about possible positions instead of guessing), and that the policy generalizes to graphs it has never seen (because graph neural networks treat all structures the same way).
