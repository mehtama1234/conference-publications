DEEPSYNTH: A Benchmark for Deep Information Synthesis

A benchmark measuring whether AI agents can synthesize information from multiple real-world sources, not just look up facts.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Today's AI language models (LLMs—large systems trained to predict and generate text) can browse the web and use tools. But existing test sets only measure whether they can retrieve single facts. DEEPSYNTH is a benchmark (a standardized test) of 120 real tasks across seven domains that demands something harder: agents must gather information from many sources, reason over it, and discover insights—the kind of work a human research analyst would do. When tested, even the best agents score only 8.97 out of 100.

Why current benchmarks miss the real job

Today, researchers test language models on benchmarks—standard sets of questions with known answers—to see if they work. Most existing benchmarks ask one thing: can the model find a fact in the training data or by searching the web? Does it know that Paris is the capital of France? Can it look up current stock prices?

But the real world is messier. A human analyst solving a real business or research question must do much more: browse multiple websites, extract numbers from tables and text, combine information from places that don't directly answer the question, reason over the combined facts, and synthesize an insight. For example, determining whether rising air quality in a region correlates with lower pneumonia deaths requires gathering official statistics from multiple countries, computing trends, and reasoning about causation. That is synthesis—putting pieces together. Current benchmarks do not measure whether agents can do synthesis. Instead, they measure whether agents can retrieve a fact that exists somewhere. A naive agent that only memorizes or does web lookups will succeed on easy benchmarks but fail when the answer is hidden behind reasoning.

The gap. Current benchmarks test fact retrieval; the real world demands information synthesis: gathering from multiple sources, combining them, and reasoning to new insights.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Expert curates real data sources

16 domain experts propose diverse topics and official data sources from their fields.

Hypotheses are formulated

For each source, experts write plausible insights that could be inferred (not trivial facts).

Hypotheses are validated

Experts analyze the data to confirm each hypothesis is true and insightful.

Questions and reasoning chains are written

Experts formulate a question, the correct answer, and the full step-by-step reasoning path (average 7.54 steps).

Agents are tested and scored

LLM agents solve tasks using web search, tools, and reasoning; answers are scored with semantic matching (not exact-match only).

What the paper changes

What the paper builds

DEEPSYNTH creates a new benchmark—a new test set with a different kind of question. Instead of asking agents to retrieve facts, it asks agents to reason over scattered information. The benchmark itself is the object: 120 carefully designed task instances where the correct answer is hidden behind multi-step reasoning, not in any single source.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Synthesis capability

measured quantity  =  Synthesis capability

The paper measures something that existing benchmarks ignore: the agent's ability to synthesize—to gather pieces of information from multiple sources, hold them in mind, reason over relationships, and produce an insight that is not explicitly written anywhere. This is a form of understanding that goes beyond pattern matching or fact lookup.

The paper estimates this by designing tasks where (a) the answer requires information from multiple sources, (b) the answer cannot be found by searching for it verbatim, and (c) intermediate reasoning steps are essential. By building 120 such tasks and testing agents on them, the paper exposes how far each agent falls short. The score gap (humans ~92%, agents ~9%) is itself the measure.

Why it works

Why benchmarking synthesis matters

Here is the mechanism. Today's benchmarks are gamed by simple strategies: memorize from training data or search the web verbatim. An agent trained only to recognize and retrieve facts will ace those benchmarks but will fail when the goal is synthesis. By building a benchmark where verbatim lookup fails—where the answer is hidden across multiple sources and requires reasoning—you expose whether the agent can actually reason.

The paper's design methodology enforces synthesis: experts start with real data sources (not fake questions), formulate hypotheses they want to validate, then write questions whose answers they had to reason through to find. This reverses the typical flow (start with a fact, hide it) and ensures the task actually reflects real analytical work. The multi-step reasoning chain (average 7.54 steps) and multi-source requirement (average 4.2 web pages) force agents to plan, execute tool calls, and combine results. When agents are tested on this benchmark, the low scores reveal that synthesis—not mere retrieval—is where they fail.

Measuring synthesis with a score

First in words:

The score rewards semantic equivalence and numeric tolerance, not just exact matches. For strings, it accepts small semantic variations; for numbers, it allows 1% to 5.5% error margin. This grades the reasoning, not the phrasing.

Then the compact form:

score = sum_i w_i * match(answer_i, gold_i) / N

Every symbol:

answer_ithe agent's output on task i
gold_ithe correct answer on task i (from human experts)
match(answer_i, gold_i)1 if answer is correct (within semantic or numeric tolerance); 0 if wrong
w_iweight for task i (usually 1, or higher for harder tasks)
Ntotal number of tasks (120 in DEEPSYNTH)
A tiny worked example
Task 1: 'Is the gap widening?' Agent says yes (correct). Task is straightforward. match = 1.
Task 2: 'What is the 2024 figure?' Agent says 47.3%, gold is 47.8%. Difference 0.5% (within tolerance). match = 1.
Task 3: 'What caused the trend?' Agent says supply shock (incomplete reasoning). Gold answer explains policy + demographics. match = 0.
Score = (1 + 1 + 0) / 3 = 0.67 (on this 3-task mini-benchmark). The benchmark's 8.97% average means agents are correct on only ~9 out of 120 tasks.

The scoring forgives minor variations but fails agents that do not reason through all steps, exposing shallow synthesis.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hallucination on multi-source tasks. When an agent cannot find a fact across multiple sources, it may invent a plausible-sounding answer instead of saying 'I do not know.' Multi-source synthesis tasks expose this because agents must combine incomplete information without guessing.
Reasoning over large information spaces fails. When a task requires holding and reasoning over 7+ intermediate facts from different domains, agents lose track, mix up relationships, or compute incorrectly. The longer the reasoning chain, the more likely an error accumulates.
Tool use breaks under complexity. Agents struggle to plan a sequence of tool calls (web search, then extract, then compute). They may fetch a source but fail to extract the right data, or extract correctly but misinterpret the result.
Done right. Tasks are built by experts who actually solved them, with verified reasoning chains, so DEEPSYNTH captures genuine synthesis work, not artificial difficulty.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a mini-benchmark: pick 2 tasks that require synthesizing information from at least 3 sources (e.g., 'Correlate school funding policy in Australia with STEM enrollment trends,' requiring budget reports, enrollment data, and policy timelines). Test an off-the-shelf LLM agent on the tasks. Log every tool call and reasoning step. At the end, measure whether the agent's answer is correct (not just whether it retrieved the right source).

synthesis_accuracy = tasks_with_correct_reasoning / total_tasks_attempted

The demo must show that even a capable agent fails to complete multi-step reasoning when information is scattered, confirming that synthesis is a distinct skill from fact retrieval.
