Harmonized Cone for Physics-Informed Neural Networks

When a neural network tries to solve physics equations but multiple goals pull its updates in different directions, pick the update that satisfies all goals at once.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (neural networks trained to solve differential equations) must balance three goals: match the equation, match starting conditions, and match boundary conditions. When you nudge the network to fix one goal, you often break another. This paper finds the set of safe updates that fix all three at once by mapping out where these conflicting directions overlap.

Zero assumption version

Three goals, one network, conflicting pulls

Physics-Informed Neural Networks solve differential equations (the rules that describe how things change over time and space, like heat flowing through metal or fluid motion). To train one, you need three separate error signals: one that measures how well the network respects the differential equation itself, one that checks the starting condition (what the system looks like at time zero), and one that checks the boundary condition (what happens at the edges of the space). Each pulls the network's weights in a different direction.

A beginner would train on all three errors at once, combining them into a single score. But here is the trap: a weight adjustment that reduces the equation error might increase the boundary condition error. The network gets torn in three directions. Researchers have tried scaling the three errors differently, but scaling is a blunt tool—it either shifts focus between goals or creates updates that mathematically cannot work for any of the three at once.

The problem in one sentence. Training requires satisfying three conflicting goals, and existing scaling methods either ignore some goals or propose updates that mathematically break at least one of them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compute nudge directions for each error

For each of the three error sources, calculate which way to nudge each weight to reduce that error.

Find the safe overlap zone

Find where all three cone-shaped safe regions overlap, creating an update space that respects all three goals' constraints.

Pick extreme directions

Identify the boundary edges of this safe zone (the extreme rays that span it).

Combine them into one update

Blend the extreme directions into a single update that balances all three goals.

Step and repeat

Apply the update to the network weights and loop back to recompute the nudge directions.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the network's weights are nudged during training. Instead of combining three error signals into one weighted sum, it searches for an update direction that simultaneously reduces all three errors (or at least does not make any worse). This is the update direction.

the object it moves  =  update direction
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: feasible directions in conflict space

measured quantity  =  feasible and non-conflicting update direction

The paper measures a hidden geometric object: the set of all weight nudges that satisfy every goal at once. Mathematically, each error signal defines a cone (a region of allowed directions), and the paper finds their intersection—the harmonized cone. Directions inside this region are feasible (they reduce error) and non-conflicting (they do not break any other goal).

The paper estimates this quantity by analyzing the nudge directions from each of the three errors. It uses an algorithm called the Double Description method to enumerate the extreme rays that bound this safe zone, then combines them into a single update.

Why it works

Why it works: geometric steering

The core insight is that the three error signals define three cones in the space of weight changes. A cone is like a cone of light: any direction inside the cone is allowed, and any outside is forbidden. Each error signal says: 'to improve me, nudge the weights in directions inside my cone.' When the three cones overlap, directions in the overlap satisfy all three goals because they sit where the allowed regions coincide. When they do not overlap completely, the paper finds the directions that satisfy the most demanding constraints simultaneously.

The paper uses an algorithm called Double Description to find the extreme rays (the edges and corners) that define the boundary of the safe zone. These extreme rays are the purest versions of conflicting-free updates. By blending them (taking a weighted sum), the algorithm creates a single update that balances all three goals. This is better than picking one error signal and scaling the others, because it respects the hard constraints that say some directions literally cannot help all three at once.

Why does this fix the training problem? Because now every weight nudge moves in a direction that genuinely improves the network's ability to solve the differential equation, satisfy the boundary, and match the starting state—all three. No longer does one step forward mean two steps back elsewhere. Training is faster because every step is coherent across all three goals.

Math after the picture

The equation: balancing three constraints

First in words:

The new weight equals the old weight plus a step size times a blended update, where the blended update is a weighted combination of the extreme rays that bound the feasible region.

Then the compact form:

w_new = w_old + alpha * sum_i lambda_i * r_i

Every symbol:

w_newthe updated weight value
w_oldthe weight before the step
alphathe step size (how far to move)
lambda_ithe blend weight for the i-th extreme ray (non-negative, sum to 1)
r_ithe i-th extreme ray direction bounding the feasible zone
sum_iadd up contributions from all extreme rays
A tiny worked example
Suppose two extreme rays are identified: ray 1 is 'nudge weight w_a by +2, keep w_b flat' and ray 2 is 'keep w_a flat, nudge w_b by +3'.
The algorithm chooses lambda_1 = 0.6 and lambda_2 = 0.4 to blend them (sum to 1).
The blended update is: 0.6 * (+2, 0) + 0.4 * (0, +3) = (+1.2, +1.2).
With alpha = 0.1, the weight step is 0.1 * (+1.2, +1.2) = (+0.12, +0.12).
Both w_a and w_b move, but in a direction that respects all three constraints.

The blend weights are chosen so that every extreme ray contributes fairly, and their weighted sum stays inside the feasible cone.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Harmonized cone is empty. If the three error signals are so misaligned that their cones do not overlap, there is no feasible direction. The paper proves this cannot happen in theory, but in practice it means the problem is over-constrained.
Computational cost of finding extreme rays. The Double Description algorithm is expensive for high-dimensional weight spaces (millions of individual adjustable numbers). The paper shows it is tractable for standard PDEs, but it may slow training on very large networks.
Blend weights are non-obvious. Once the extreme rays are found, the paper must choose how to blend them. A poor choice of blend weights can favor one goal over others, defeating the point of the harmonized cone.
Done right. When all three error signals are genuinely present and the network is flexible enough to improve on all of them, the harmonized cone finds the best balanced direction because the overlap region automatically encodes which adjustments help all three at once, and training accelerates.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small neural network that solves a simple differential equation (like the one-dimensional heat equation: how temperature spreads along a rod). Set up three losses: one for the differential equation, one for the boundary condition (ends held at specific temperatures), and one for the initial condition (starting temperature profile). Compare training curves: naive scaling method (weighted sum of three losses) versus the harmonized cone method (using the geometric cone-intersection approach). Measure final error on each of the three components separately, not combined.

total convergence time = (steps until equation error + boundary error + start condition error all drop below threshold) / 100

The demo must make it impossible to miss that the harmonized cone method reduces all three errors together while the naive method always leaves one high.
