Embed heat-transfer physics directly into 3D Gaussian Splatting to reconstruct scenes that change over time as temperature evolves.

Zero assumption version

Reconstructing a scene that only changes its temperature

The world contains thermal events: cooling, heating, heat transfer between objects. A camera that sees both regular light (RGB) and infrared heat (IR) captures these changes. The naive approach: treat each photo as a separate snapshot and stitch them all together. That breaks because it does not understand that temperature evolves by a smooth physical law — it treats yesterday's temperature and tomorrow's as unrelated guesses, wasting the signal that heat moves predictably.

Existing 3D reconstruction methods assume the shape stays frozen and only the appearance (color, lighting) shifts. But thermal reconstruction is different: the shape is fixed, and temperature is the appearance that changes. The naive learner has no way to say 'temperature tomorrow will be lower because of heat loss' — it just memorizes each snapshot independently.

In one sentence. The paper gives each 3D point a thermal model — a formula describing how its temperature changes over time — instead of just guessing each temperature independently.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a 3D point cloud

Each point in the scene gets two things: a color (like RGB Gaussian Splatting) and a thermal number that describes how it cools.

Equip each point with a heat-transfer formula

Instead of storing a fixed temperature, store the rate at which it cools (or heats). This is a simple equation like initial_temp minus cooling_rate times elapsed_time.

Render both RGB and thermal images

At any timestamp, evaluate the thermal formula for each point. Blend all visible points to create a thermal image, just like blending colors in normal Gaussian Splatting.

Train on the real data

Compare rendered thermal images to real IR photos. Use backpropagation (gradient descent) to adjust the cooling rates and initial temperatures until the formulas predict the data accurately.

Render at any time, including between observations

Because the formula is analytical (closed-form), you can ask for the temperature at any moment without numerical solving — 10ms, 500ms, or any time you want.

What the paper changes

The thing the paper changes: thermal formulas baked into the model

Each 3D point in the reconstruction now carries not just a color but a thermal evolution equation. Instead of training the model to guess independent temperatures for each moment, the paper trains it to learn the coefficients of a heat-transfer formula. The object is the thermal formula per point — specifically, the starting temperature and cooling rate (the two numbers that define the formula).

the object it moves  =  Thermal coefficients (initial temperature, cooling rate)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how fast does heat leave each point?

measured quantity  =  Cooling rate (or thermal time-constant)

The paper measures a quantity usually invisible in thermal images: the rate at which each 3D point loses (or gains) heat over time. In the real world, this depends on the material, the surface area, and the surrounding environment. The paper does not model all that; instead, it lets the model learn one number per point: the effective cooling rate, a hidden quantity that summarizes 'how fast does this point approach ambient temperature?'

The paper infers this rate by fitting the heat-transfer formula to the observed temperatures across all the captured moments. If a region cools slowly, the learned rate is low; if it cools fast, the learned rate is high. This learned rate then becomes the mechanism for rendering accurate thermal images at any time, including moments between observations.

Why it works

Why it works: physics as a learning constraint

The trick is using an analytical solution to the heat-transfer equation. Instead of numerically solving a differential equation at render time (which is slow and noisy), the paper uses a closed-form formula: temperature at time t equals initial_temperature minus an exponential decay. This formula has only two unknowns per point: the starting temperature and the cooling rate. Training then becomes: adjust these two numbers until the formula predicts all the observed temperatures.

Why does this beat naive memorization? Because the formula forces the model to learn a smooth, physically plausible thermal trajectory. A Gaussian point cannot 'jump' in temperature or cool backwards; it must follow the exponential law. This constraint is a feature: it is a strong inductive bias that says 'temperature evolves like heat in the real world.' The model learns the rate faster and with less data because it is not wasting its leeway (degrees of freedom, just two numbers per point) on thermal wobble that violates physics.

Irregular or out-of-order observations (camera 1 sees 10ms, camera 2 sees 20ms, then camera 1 again at 25ms) become easy to handle. The formula evaluates at any timestamp, so the learning algorithm simply pulls each observation's expected temperature from the formula and compares. No need to interpolate or reorder the data.

Math after the picture

The heat-transfer formula

First in words:

Temperature at time t equals the starting temperature minus the difference (starting minus ambient) times an exponential decay factor.

Then the compact form:

T(t) = T_ambient + (T_0 - T_ambient) * exp(-t / tau)

Every symbol:

T(t)temperature at time t (what we want to predict)
T_ambientroom or background temperature (fixed, known)
T_0initial temperature at time zero (learned per point)
telapsed time since the start of the sequence
tauthermal time-constant (how fast cooling happens, learned per point)
A tiny worked example
Say room temperature is 20 degrees. A hot object starts at 100 degrees (T_0 = 100). Its time-constant is tau = 2 seconds.
At t=0: T(0) = 20 + (100 - 20) * exp(0) = 20 + 80 * 1 = 100 degrees. Correct — it starts hot.
At t=2: T(2) = 20 + 80 * exp(-2/2) = 20 + 80 * exp(-1) = 20 + 80 * 0.368 = 20 + 29.4 = 49.4 degrees. Half the excess heat is gone.
At t=6: T(6) = 20 + 80 * exp(-6/2) = 20 + 80 * exp(-3) = 20 + 80 * 0.050 = 20 + 4 = 24 degrees. Nearly room temperature.
The same point's temperature drops fast at first (100 to 49 in 2 seconds) and slows as it approaches room temp. This exponential shape is what the paper lets the model learn.

The model learns the starting temperature and cooling rate for each point so that the formula predicts all observed thermal images. No numerical solving needed at render time — just plug in the elapsed time and compute.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Assumption of exponential decay breaks if an external heat source arrives. If a heater turns on halfway through the sequence, the temperature will start rising instead of falling. The exponential model cannot capture this change of regime. The paper assumes the thermal process stays stationary (no new heat sources or sinks entering the scene).
The model is sensitive to the assumed ambient temperature. If you set the room temperature wrong, the learned cooling rates will be garbage. The paper does not discuss how to estimate this in practice — it must be supplied or calibrated.
Irregular or very sparse observations can lead to poor calibration. If thermal images come in large time gaps, the exponential formula may fit many different (starting temperature, cooling rate) pairs equally well. The model might converge to a wrong solution. Dense temporal sampling helps because tightly-spaced observations lock down the cooling rate unambiguously.
Done right. When observations are dense and fairly regular, the ambient temperature is known, and the thermal process is genuinely passive cooling or heating (no external interventions), the method works well. The learned rates become interpretable and accurate.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic scene: a 3D object (like a teapot) with an initial temperature. Simulate the physics — each point cools exponentially toward room temperature, with different rates for different materials. Generate RGB-IR image pairs at 10 millisecond intervals for 5 seconds. Train ETGS on these pairs. At test time, render the scene at timestamps it has never seen (e.g., 2.3ms, 3.7ms) and measure how close the rendered thermal image is to the ground-truth simulated image.

Thermal MAE = mean absolute error of predicted vs. ground-truth temperature, summed over all pixels and test times

The key observation: ETGS's thermal prediction at unseen times is accurate (error close to the simulated noise level), whereas a naive static model or a frame-independent baseline will be visibly wrong — ghosting, blurring, or temperature inversions at new times.
