TopoFormer: Topology Meets Attention for Graph Learning

Turn a graph's topological structure into an ordered sequence of tokens that a Transformer can understand, capturing patterns from local cliques to global organization.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graphs are networks of connected nodes and edges. Many real graphs hide important patterns at different scales: a cluster of tightly-connected nodes (a local pattern), or the way those clusters connect together (a global pattern). Traditional graph learning methods struggle to see both scales at once. TopoFormer encodes graph topology—the actual shape and structure of the network—into a sequence that looks like language, then processes it with a Transformer (a neural network that learns relationships between items in a sequence by looking at which items should focus on each other). By converting topology into a sequence, it lets one model capture local details and global organization without expensive mathematical machinery.

Why graphs are hard to read with standard deep learning

A graph is a collection of nodes (points) connected by edges (links). Real-world graphs—molecule structures, social networks, protein interactions—have patterns at multiple scales. A molecule might have a central ring (local structure) surrounded by branches (global shape). Traditional graph neural networks (models that learn from graphs by passing information between connected nodes) excel at extracting local structure but often miss the big picture. They also cannot easily combine local and global patterns in one representation.

A beginner might try encoding a graph as a flat list of all nodes and edges and feeding it to a Transformer (a general-purpose neural network that learns relationships between any sequence of items). But this approach discards the graph's actual structure and treats nodes as if they were words in a random order. The Transformer has to rediscover the structure from scratch, wasting compute and missing multi-scale patterns that should be obvious.

In one sentence. TopoFormer extracts the graph's topological shape, converts it into an ordered sequence that highlights structure at multiple scales, and feeds that sequence to a Transformer.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Choose a filtration direction

Decide how to peel the graph into layers: by node degree (how many neighbors each node has) or by edge properties.

2. Slice the graph into a sequence

Topo-Scan removes nodes or edges layer by layer, recording which topology appears at each step, creating an ordered sequence.

3. Encode each layer as features

Convert each topological snapshot into a feature vector (a list of numbers) that represents the structure at that scale.

4. Process sequence with Transformer

Feed the snapshot sequence to a Transformer, which learns relationships between layers and builds a unified graph representation.

5. Output final graph representation

The Transformer produces one list of numbers per graph that captures multi-scale patterns for classification or prediction.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

TopoFormer changes what gets fed into a standard Transformer. Instead of raw node features or an arbitrary node ordering, it transforms the graph into a sequence of topological snapshots. Each snapshot is a view of the graph at a particular scale (captured by removing nodes or edges in a structured order called a filtration). This ordered sequence is the input—a list of items that encode multi-scale structure. A Transformer then processes this sequence using learned relationships (where each part of the sequence decides which other parts to focus on), letting different layers of the network learn which scales matter and how they relate.

the object it moves  =  Graph representation: raw graph to ordered sequence of topological snapshots
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: topological stability

measured quantity  =  Topological stability across scale layers

Topological stability is a measure of how much a graph's shape persists as you peel away its layers. To understand it, remove the least-important nodes or edges first, then watch what connected components (groups of closely linked nodes) survive or merge as you strip further. Some structures vanish quickly (fragile, present only at one scale); others endure (present across many scales, fundamental). A stable structure shows up in multiple snapshots across the filtration, indicating a genuine feature of the graph. An unstable one flickers in and out, likely a coincidence or noise.

TopoFormer estimates stability by computing a node or edge filtration (an ordering of nodes or edges by some criterion like degree or edge weight), generating a sequence of graph states, and encoding how long each topological feature persists. The Transformer then learns to weight stable features more heavily when building the final graph representation. This approach is inspired by persistent homology, a rigorous mathematical framework that measures which topological structures survive across scales, but TopoFormer avoids expensive homology computations—it works directly with the sequence.

Why it works

Why it works: layers reveal structure at each scale

A graph's topology is not one static thing—it has depth. Take a real molecule: at the finest scale (single atoms and bonds), it has local chemical structure; at a broader scale, atoms form functional groups; at the broadest scale, functional groups assemble into a 3D shape. Traditional graph neural networks operate at one scale, either by design or by accident. TopoFormer sidesteps this by decomposing the graph into layers, each representing a different scale.

The decomposition works by ordering nodes or edges (called a filtration) and removing them one by one. At each step, a new topological snapshot appears: some nodes drop away, clusters merge or split, the graph's connected components shift. This sequence of snapshots encodes multi-scale structure in a form that a Transformer can process. The Transformer's attention mechanism is ideal for this task: it learns which snapshots are important, how they relate to one another, and how to synthesize them into one unified representation. Because the sequence explicitly records scale-wise changes, the Transformer never has to guess whether a pattern is local or global—the sequence already separates them by definition.

Math after the picture

The topological encoding equation

First in words:

Graph embedding is computed from attention over a sequence of topological snapshots, where each snapshot is encoded and weighted by how stable it is across the filtration.

Then the compact form:

Graph_embedding = mean( Transformer( [Enc(Topo_0), Enc(Topo_1), ..., Enc(Topo_L)] ) )

Every symbol:

Topo_iTopological snapshot at filtration step i (connectivity state after removing i nodes or edges)
Enc(Topo_i)Encoded feature vector for snapshot i (graph statistics like edge count, component count, clustering)
TransformerAttention-based model that learns relationships between snapshots across scales
LNumber of filtration steps (depth of the decomposition)
meanAverage the Transformer output across all steps to get one graph-level representation
A tiny worked example
Suppose a graph has 4 nodes and 5 edges. Filtration by degree removes nodes in order of lowest degree first.
Step 0: all 4 nodes, 5 edges. Enc(Topo_0) = [4, 5, 1, 0.8] (node count, edge count, largest cluster size, clustering coefficient).
Step 1: node 1 removed (degree 1). Now 3 nodes, 3 edges. Enc(Topo_1) = [3, 3, 1, 0.5].
Step 2: node 2 removed (degree 2). Now 2 nodes, 2 edges. Enc(Topo_2) = [2, 2, 1, 1.0].
Transformer processes sequence [[4,5,1,0.8], [3,3,1,0.5], [2,2,1,1.0]], learns which scales matter (e.g., full graph has diverse clusters, but removing low-degree nodes preserves dense cores), and outputs a combined representation. Mean of learned outputs = final graph representation.

The sequence of encoded topologies lets the Transformer see how structure changes with scale; attention weights reveal which scales are most informative for the prediction task.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Filtration order is arbitrary. If the chosen ordering (by degree, by weight, by some heuristic) does not reflect the graph's true multi-scale structure, the snapshots may not separate scales into distinct types. A bad filtration creates a sequence that looks random rather than progressive, and the Transformer cannot learn consistent patterns.
Very large or very dense graphs. Computing a full filtration and encoding every snapshot is expensive on massive graphs. If the graph has millions of nodes, the token sequence becomes very long, and Transformer attention, which compares every token to every other token, can become slow. A truncated filtration loses scale information.
Noisy node or edge weights. If the weights used to order the filtration are noisy (e.g., corrupted node degree data or unreliable edge weights), the snapshots do not reflect real structural changes, just noise. The Transformer then learns spurious patterns that do not generalize to clean test data.
Done right. When the filtration is chosen to match the graph's true multi-scale organization (e.g., by criteria informed by persistent homology theory), and the graph is large enough that scale-wise decomposition reveals distinct layers, TopoFormer captures both local and global structure efficiently, often matching or exceeding specialized graph networks while remaining interpretable and fast to compute.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo takes a standard graph classification benchmark (a dataset with labeled graphs, like molecular graphs with known properties), applies TopoFormer, and compares accuracy to a baseline graph neural network. The demo should run two experiments: one where the filtration is chosen intelligently (e.g., by degree or by a measure related to the task), and one where it is random. It should display the topological sequence for one example graph side-by-side with the baseline's feature extraction, showing visually how Topo-Scan decomposes the graph into snapshots. A second test should measure compute time and memory: confirm that the filtration and encoding are fast (linear or near-linear in graph size) and avoid the expensive homology computations of traditional topological methods. The final metric should show that accuracy on graphs requiring multi-scale reasoning (e.g., molecule classification where both local chemistry and global shape matter) improves with TopoFormer, because the explicit multi-scale decomposition lets the Transformer learn which scales predict the target property, whereas a single-scale baseline must guess whether to focus on local cliques or global connectivity and often chooses poorly. This improves accuracy especially when the single-scale baseline stops improving, because at that point the baseline has extracted all the information it can from one scale and cannot do better, while TopoFormer gains new information from the multi-scale view.

Accuracy_improvement = Acc_TopoFormer - Acc_baseline, also measure compute_time_TopoFormer vs compute_time_traditional_topology
