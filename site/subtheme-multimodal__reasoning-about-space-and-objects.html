Reasoning about space and objects

Teaching models to understand where things are located, how objects relate to each other, and what the physical layout looks like in images.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When you look at an image, you instantly understand the spatial layout. You know that the person is standing in front of the building, or that the cup is to the left of the plate, or that one object is farther away than another. You understand occlusion — when one thing is hidden behind another, you still know both exist. You can predict what is out of frame — if you see the left half of a car, you know roughly where the right half would be. Models need to build this kind of spatial and object reasoning too. This is not just about recognizing what objects are present; it is about understanding their relationships: where they are, how far they are from each other, what they can touch, what is hidden, and what the three-dimensional structure of the scene is.

The challenge is that a camera flattens the three-dimensional world into a two-dimensional image. The model has to learn to invert this projection — to recover the three-dimensional structure from a flat picture. This is inherently ambiguous: a small object close to the camera and a large object far away might look the same size. A model needs to use context, knowledge of typical object sizes, shadows, perspective cues, and other hints to reason about depth. Understanding object relationships is also tricky: a model needs to track multiple objects at once, understand which are in front and which are behind, and reason about interactions. If a person is reaching toward a cup, the model should reason that the person intends to grab the cup. If two objects are far apart, they probably cannot interact. These kinds of spatial and relational conclusions are not explicit in the image; they have to be figured out from context and what the model already knows.

Research in this space builds models with geometric and relational reasoning. Some approaches use scene graphs — a representation that explicitly lists objects and their relationships: 'person standing on floor,' 'cup on table,' 'book to the right of cup.' The model learns to construct these scene graphs from images, and then reasoning about spatial relationships becomes easier. Others use 3D representations internally, learning to estimate depth and reconstruct a three-dimensional model of the scene from the image, even though the input is flat. Some work focuses on visual relationship detection, training models to identify relationships like 'holding,' 'wearing,' 'in front of,' between pairs of objects. Some use knowledge of physics and everyday cause-and-effect to reason about what is likely — a ball on a slope should roll downward. Others combine spatial reasoning with question answering, so that questions like 'how many objects are to the left of the person?' can be answered by reasoning about the layout. The goal is to build models that understand not just what objects are in an image but where they are, how they relate, and what the three-dimensional reality behind the flat picture looks like.

How the work breaks down

Spatial reasoning splits into two main directions: understanding where objects sit in three-dimensional space given a flat image, and recognizing how objects relate to each other across both space and modalities. One direction tackles depth recovery and 3D scene understanding by learning geometric constraints. Another focuses on multi-image reasoning, where a model must connect spatial information across multiple camera views or images to answer questions about position and layout. A third centers on cross-modal alignment, including sound localization from audio paired with visual information. Finally, some work diagnoses what fails when models attempt spatial reasoning, revealing gaps between perception and reasoning capability.

Building 3D structure from flat images

The fundamental challenge here is that a camera compresses three-dimensional space into a flat two-dimensional image. To recover what is really there, a model needs to learn constraints—visual cues like perspective, shading, and known object sizes that hint at true 3D structure. But these constraints alone are ambiguous (a small object close to the camera can look like a large object far away), so adding external guidance helps reduce the ambiguity by providing extra information like semantic meaning or context that the visual cues alone cannot provide.

Let Language Constrain Space (“Let Language Constrain Geometry: Vision–Language Models”) uses language and vision together to fix 3D generation: when a model creates a 3D object or scene, language provides semantic guidance, and the vision system checks whether the result makes spatial sense. The method steers the generation process itself, catching spatial errors early, which prevents generating physically implausible layouts. Mitigating the Modality Gap in Vision-Language Models (“Mitigating the Modality Gap in Vision–Language”) approaches the problem differently, aligning the feature spaces of images and language by representing data as lying on curved mathematical surfaces rather than flat spaces. This helps models keep spatial structure consistent when they combine information from different modalities, because the curved space naturally preserves how objects relate to each other.

Audio source localization and direction

Sound sources emit waves that arrive at different microphones at different times and with different strengths. Localizing where sound comes from requires reasoning about how the microphone array is arranged and how sound waves travel through air. This is a spatial reasoning task in the audio domain: placing sound sources in three-dimensional space relative to the listener.

OWL (“OWL : Geometry-Aware Spatial Reasoning for”) teaches an audio language model to reason about sound direction in terms of compass directions and elevation. The model learns to estimate the direction to a sound source accurately, supporting complex reasoning like comparing sounds coming from different directions. PhaseCoder (“PhaseCoder: Microphone Geometry-Agnostic Spatial Audio Understanding”) handles a practical problem: microphones come in different arrangements, and a model trained on one type might fail on another. It learns to adapt to microphone array shape so that the same physical sound source gets localized correctly regardless of how the microphones are positioned, because the model explicitly accounts for how array shape affects sound arrival times. Physics-Informed Audio Representation Learning (“Physics-Informed Audio-Geometry-Grid Representation Learning for Universal”) builds an internal model using physics: it encodes how sound propagates directly in its representations, which helps the system generalize to conditions it has never encountered before because it has learned the underlying physical rules rather than just memorizing patterns. All three papers ground audio spatial reasoning in the actual microphone setup and physics of sound.

Multi-image and camera-centric spatial reasoning

A single image is inherently ambiguous about 3D space, but when you have multiple images from different camera positions, the overlaps and differences reveal the true three-dimensional layout. Reasoning spatially across images means connecting objects and places across views, understanding how perspective shifts when the camera moves, and drawing conclusions about relative position and distance.

From Correspondence to Actions (“From Correspondence to Actions: Human-Like Multi-Image”) uses visual correspondence—matching pixels or regions across images—to reason about spatial layout and predict interactions. A human looking at two images of the same scene from different angles immediately infers depth and object relationships; this paper teaches models to do the same. Thinking with Camera (“Thinking with Camera: A Unified Multimodal”) keeps the camera in focus: it trains on a dataset of vision-language-camera triplets, learning to understand not just what is in the scene but how the camera sits relative to objects. This enables reasoning about what lies outside the frame and how scenes look from different viewpoints. Thinking with Space (“Thinking with Geometry: Active Geometry Integration”) actively builds spatial representations during reasoning: it constructs explicit models of where things are, so that conclusions about position rest on actual spatial math rather than just visual pattern matching. Together, these three papers show that connecting across multiple viewpoints—whether through matching regions, camera position, or explicit math—unlocks richer spatial understanding.

Cross-modal alignment and spatial awareness

When a model combines vision and language, or vision and audio, the representations from each modality live in different feature spaces. Aligning them correctly matters for spatial reasoning: language can describe left-right relationships, vision can show depth cues, and the model must understand that they refer to the same spatial structure. This requires both understanding what each modality says and learning how they map to each other.

Modality Alignment across Trees on Heterogeneous Hyperbolic Manifolds (“Modality Alignment across Trees on Heterogeneous”) aligns modalities by learning on curved mathematical spaces that naturally encode hierarchical relationships—useful for taxonomies and categorical structures. GeoSense (“GeoSense: Internalizing Geometric Necessity Perception for”) builds spatial awareness into a model by training it to judge when a spatial interpretation is necessary based on visual evidence. On the Generalization Capacities of MLLMs for Spatial Intelligence (“On the Generalization Capacities of MLLMs”) tests whether spatial reasoning transfers across different cameras: it shows that models need explicit camera awareness to apply spatial reasoning learned in one viewpoint to another. The insight is that basic multimodal models fail because they do not track how perspective changes—they need mathematical grounding in how space works when the viewpoint shifts.

Diagnosing spatial reasoning failures

Even models trained on spatial tasks sometimes fail mysteriously. Understanding why spatial reasoning breaks matters for building better systems, because each failure type points to a different solution. Some failures are perceptual—the model misses what is actually in the image. Others are reasoning failures—the model sees the right things but draws the wrong conclusion. And some are simulation failures—the model cannot predict how the spatial scene evolves over time.

Seeing Without Understanding (“Seeing Without Understanding: Disentangling Perception, Reasoning”) dissects three distinct failure modes in vision-language models playing games that require spatial reasoning: the model's spatial perception drifts over time at rates far higher than chance (meaning the system is not stable), reasoning about spatial relationships caps out well below full capability (so the model reaches a ceiling), and the model's predictions about future positions diverge from reality (which means it cannot forecast motion). These are separate problems requiring different fixes. Unlocking Zero-Shot Geospatial Reasoning via Indirect Rewards (“Unlocking Zero-Shot Geospatial Reasoning via Indirect”) takes a different approach, showing that models can learn to reason about geographic and spatial layouts using only weak signal—indirect proxy rewards rather than exact supervision. The finding suggests that spatial reasoning does not require dense ground-truth labels because models can use looser forms of signal to bootstrap understanding. Both papers reveal that spatial reasoning is harder to learn and transfer than models typically assume, and that understanding failure modes can guide solutions.
