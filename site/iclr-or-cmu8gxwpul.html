AutoDrive-P3: Chain of Thought for Autonomous Driving

Train a self-driving AI to reason step-by-step through what it sees, what will happen next, and what to do—by giving it separate reward signals for each step, not just the final decision.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Self-driving cars need to make safe decisions in complex situations. Current AI models either skip over important reasoning steps or train each step separately, losing the ability to coordinate. This paper trains a vision-language model to reason through three linked stages—perception, prediction, and planning—using a single unified training method that rewards each stage appropriately.

Zero assumption version

Why does end-to-end driving fail?

Self-driving requires three things: seeing what is around you (perception), guessing what will happen next (prediction), and deciding what to do (planning). Modern vision-language models (large neural networks trained on images and text) are very good at this. But current systems have two breaking problems.

Some models skip straight from camera input to driving commands, bypassing perception and prediction. This works sometimes but misses the actual reasoning a human driver uses—the model cannot explain its decisions and makes careless mistakes in tricky situations.

Other models do all three stages but train them separately. Perception gets one signal, prediction gets another, planning gets a third. Each stage learns alone without knowing what the others learned, so the stages do not help each other, because the model only updates each stage's weights based on that stage's reward, not on how well it serves the next stage. This lack of coordination causes bigger errors at the end.

The naive approach. Train each stage separately, or skip stages and train only the final planning output.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of training the three stages separately or skipping some stages, this paper trains all three together using reward signals that encode their causal order. The change is in the training algorithm: it gives each stage explicit feedback on how well it does its own job.

The hidden quantity

Reward flow: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  reward signal for each stage

The paper measures how much feedback reaches each stage during training. In old methods, planning got all the reward signal; perception and prediction got little or none. This paper explicitly computes a reward for perception (did you identify the pedestrian?), a reward for prediction (did you forecast the car's path?), and a reward for planning (did you choose the safe action?). Each reward is measured independently using metrics that match what that stage is supposed to do.

The algorithm tracks these rewards and uses them to update the model weights. The key innovation is that these rewards are coupled: the planning reward depends on having good perception and prediction first. So the model learns that investing in accurate perception pays off in planning performance, because poor perception makes the downstream prediction harder, which makes the final plan less safe and earns lower reward.

Why it works

Why giving each stage its own reward makes it work

Consider a car approaching an intersection. Old training would only care: did you choose the safe action? It would not care if you misidentified the pedestrian, as long as you braked. So the perception stage never learned to be accurate—it only learned whatever trick made the final answer lucky.

This paper assigns a separate score to perception: did you correctly identify objects in the scene? This score has no connection to planning safety. It is honest feedback on the perceptual job alone. The model now has a direct incentive to see clearly. When perception is accurate, prediction becomes easier—the model learns to forecast the pedestrian's motion from a clearer picture. When prediction is accurate, planning becomes easier—the model can make safer, more confident decisions.

The second innovation is encoding dependencies: the algorithm does not treat the three stages as independent. Instead, it builds the reward structure so that planning reward is harder to earn if perception failed first. This mirrors the real causal order—you cannot plan safely without seeing and predicting. The model learns this structure during training, so it naturally invests in early stages when early stages are unreliable.

Math after the picture

The training formula

First in words:

Loss equals the negative log-likelihood of the correct outputs, minus the normalized advantage function summed over all three stages, plus a KL penalty to keep the model from drifting too far from its starting point.

Then the compact form:

Loss = -log P(targets) + sum_stage (alpha_stage * A_stage) + beta * KL(new_policy || old_policy)

Every symbol:

log P(targets)the log probability the model assigns to the correct answers for all three stages
A_stagethe advantage: how much better this trajectory is than the average for that stage
alpha_stagethe weight for how much to care about that stage's reward
betathe strength of the penalty for changing the model too fast
KLa measure of how different the new model is from the old one
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model outputs perception, prediction, planning and gets stage rewards: perception +2, prediction +1, planning +1.
The average rewards historically are: perception +1, prediction +0, planning +0.
Advantage for each: perception = 2 - 1 = +1, prediction = 1 - 0 = +1, planning = 1 - 0 = +1.
If we weight all three equally (alpha = 0.33 each) and lambda = 0.1, total advantage bonus = 0.33 * (1 + 1 + 1) = 1.0.
The loss is reduced by 1.0, so the model weights move toward this trajectory.
If perception had been -1 (bad), its advantage would be -2, pulling the whole loss down and discouraging the update.

The algorithm trains all three stages together by rewarding what each stage actually does, and penalizes changes that stray too far from a safe starting point.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward misalignment. If the perception reward does not match what matters for planning, the model learns accurate but useless perceptions. The rewards must align: accurate object detection must actually help the final decision, because otherwise the coupled training learns to optimize something the real task does not value.
Stage ordering mistakes. If the model is penalized for bad planning even when perception is impossible, it learns to output high confidence even when unsure. The reward coupling must respect the real causal order.
Expensive to annotate. Building the training dataset requires humans to label perception (what is in the scene), prediction (what moves next), and planning (what is the safe action) for every scenario. This is slower and more costly than labeling just the final planning output.
Done right. The rewards are ground-truthed against real measurements; the stage dependencies match the causal order; the training dataset is built once and reused across many model sizes.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple driving simulator or use a recorded dataset. Train two vision-language models on the same task: one using the old approach (reward only the planning output) and one using P3-GRPO (reward perception, prediction, and planning separately with causal coupling). Record the perception accuracy, prediction error, and planning safety for both. The demo should show that the hierarchical reward model has higher accuracy at all three stages and crashes less often, because the explicit supervision at earlier stages removes ambiguity about what the model should learn.

planning_safety = (correct_plans - incorrect_plans) / total_plans; perception_accuracy = (correctly_identified_objects) / total_objects; improvement = P3_GRPO_safety - baseline_safety

The demo makes it impossible to miss: rewarding earlier stages explicitly, with causal coupling, makes all three stages better together, because the model cannot earn high planning reward without first fixing perception and prediction.
