Benchmarking Open-ended Segmentation

When a model describes what it sees in an image, simple similarity scores disagree with humans about whether it got it right.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Open-ended segmentation asks a model to look at a region of an image and write a free-form description of what is there. The catch: how do you judge if the description is correct? Current methods measure similarity between the model's words and the expected answer using simple embedding distances (turning text into lists of numbers and measuring how close they are). But this breaks — models say something like fluffy four-legged animal when the test expects dog, and the embedding distance stays the same whether the mistake is trivial or catastrophic. This paper shows the real problem: those distance scores diverge sharply from human judgment, and proposes a fix using the deeper relationships between words.

Zero assumption version

Why simple text similarity breaks

When a segmentation model writes a free-form description — like a fluffy mammal with four legs for an image region — how do you score it? The obvious approach: convert the model's words and the ground-truth label into embeddings (lists of numbers that capture meaning), then measure the distance between them using cosine similarity or similar metrics. A small distance means the model said something close to correct.

But embeddings are not calibrated for grading. A model that writes a dog when the answer is a canine gets perfect embeddings score even though canine is formal. A model that writes a cat also gets the same embeddings score, but a human knows that saying cat is dead wrong, not a synonym. The embedding distance cannot tell the difference between near-miss and categorical error.

Worse: when the paper tests this on real benchmarks, embedding similarity scores and human annotations disagree often. Some descriptions that humans mark wrong get high similarity scores. Others that humans mark right get low scores. The evaluation is measuring something, but it is not measuring what the benchmark intends.

In one sentence. Current evaluation metrics for free-form image descriptions use raw text similarity, which disagrees with human judgment about whether descriptions are actually correct.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the evaluation metric (the scoring function that judges whether a description is correct) with a new one that considers lexical relationships between words instead of just raw embedding distance.

the object it moves  =  evaluation metric
The hidden quantity

The human agreement signal

measured quantity  =  lexical relationship score

The hidden quantity is a score that captures how a model's words relate to the ground-truth label. Simple embedding distance misses these relationships. Is fluffy four-legged animal a synonym for dog? No. Is it more general (a hypernym, like animal is more general than dog)? Yes. Is it more specific (a hyponym)? No. Is it related by loose association? Maybe.

The paper estimates this by checking multiple lexical dictionaries and word relationships. For each pair of words, it computes: are they synonyms (WordNet, GloVe cosine, BERT embeddings)? Is one more general? Is one more specific? Is one a common paraphrase of the other? Then it combines these signals with weights learned from human annotations. The resulting score aligns much better with what humans actually judge than the simple embedding distance, because these discrete yes/no checks capture the semantic structure that humans perceive, whereas a single continuous distance treats all mismatches alike.

Why it works

Why the mapping function works

Embedding distance measures whether the overall statistical pattern of word usage is similar. But language has structure: dog and canine have nearly identical patterns (they refer to the same animal), while dog and cat have similar patterns too (both are four-legged pets). Embedding distance cannot distinguish these cases because both pairs have been seen together in similar contexts online.

The lexical mapping instead asks a different set of questions: Does one word specifically mean a more-general version of the other? Does one word appear in dictionaries as a known alias? Are the two words listed as synonyms? By combining these discrete, semantic-level checks (yes/no questions about word relationships) instead of relying on a single continuous distance score, the mapping recovers the fine structure that humans see. A human knows dog and canine are interchangeable, dog and puppy are close but off-by-specificity, and dog and cat are entirely wrong. The mapping function, trained on human annotations, learns to weight these lexical relationships and reproduce that judgment.

Finally, the paper uses this improved scoring to train a new multimodal model (vision + language) with a contrastive objective. Contrastive learning means: when the model sees a real image-description pair, it learns to make their embeddings close; when it sees a mismatched pair, it pushes them apart. By training with the new mapping-informed metric, the model learns to produce descriptions that pass the human-aligned evaluation, not just the faulty embedding similarity.

The scoring equation

First in words:

mapping_score equals w1 times synonymy_signal plus w2 times hypernymy_signal plus w3 times hyponymy_signal plus w4 times embedding_cosine_distance, where the weights w1 w2 w3 w4 are learned from human annotations to minimize the difference between the score and human judgment.

Then the compact form:

mapping_score = w1 * syn(u,v) + w2 * hyper(u,v) + w3 * hypo(u,v) + w4 * cosine(emb(u), emb(v))

Every symbol:

umodel's description (one or more words)
vground-truth label
syn(u,v)1 if u and v are synonyms, else 0
hyper(u,v)1 if u is more general than v, else 0
hypo(u,v)1 if u is more specific than v, else 0
cosine(emb(u), emb(v))embedding similarity, a number between 0 and 1
w1, w2, w3, w4weights learned from human annotations
A tiny worked example
Ground truth: dog. Model says: puppy.
syn(puppy, dog) = 0 (not exact synonyms)
hyper(puppy, dog) = 0 (puppy is not more general)
hypo(puppy, dog) = 1 (puppy is more specific)
cosine(emb(puppy), emb(dog)) = 0.92 (very similar embeddings)
Learned weights: w1=0.4, w2=0.3, w3=0.6, w4=0.2
Score = 0.4*0 + 0.3*0 + 0.6*1 + 0.2*0.92 = 0.6 + 0.184 = 0.784
Ground truth model says: table. Cosine = 0.15, all lexical signals 0.
Score = 0.4*0 + 0.3*0 + 0.6*0 + 0.2*0.15 = 0.03
The second case (wrong answer) scores much lower because lexical signals matter.

The mapping function combines discrete yes/no checks about word relationships with a continuous embedding distance, weighted by how much each signal predicts human agreement.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lexical resources are incomplete. WordNet and other dictionaries do not cover all specialized terms or slang. A description of husky dog breed might not find husky in the lexical database, even though a human would recognize it. The mapping function then relies only on embedding distance, losing precision.
Multiword expressions confuse the signal. A model might say white four-legged animal and the ground truth is dog. Checking each word separately (white/dog, four-legged/dog, animal/dog) gives different signals, and combining them naively can produce the wrong score.
Embedding distance still matters for unfamiliar pairs. If neither the model's words nor the label appear in any lexical resource, the mapping function has no lexical signals to use, and falls back entirely to embedding distance (which may still be unreliable).
Done right. The paper learns the weights w1, w2, w3, w4 from real human annotations, so the combination adapts to whichever signals are most predictive in practice. This makes the metric data-driven and less sensitive to dictionary gaps for common concepts, because if synonymy is unreliable (due to gaps), the learned weights will lower w1 and rely more on the other signals.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take a trained open-ended segmentation model and a test set with ground-truth labels and human judgments. Compute the mapping function score for each model prediction. Then compute the Spearman rank correlation (a measure of agreement) between the mapping scores and human judgments. Compare this correlation to the Spearman correlation between embedding-distance scores and human judgments. The new metric should have a correlation 10-20% higher, because the lexical signals directly encode the types of word relationships humans care about (synonymy, specificity level), whereas embedding distance conflates these distinctions.

improvement = correlation(mapping_scores, human_judgments) - correlation(embedding_scores, human_judgments)

If the correlation does not improve, the lexical signals are not capturing the kinds of mistakes humans actually care about, because either the test set is unusual (e.g., mostly formal technical terms that dictionaries do not cover) or the lexical resources are too sparse, revealing where to strengthen the mapping function's foundation.
