Fixing Attention Blur to Stop Hallucinations

When a multimodal model looks at an image with divided attention across many heads, it sees a blurry picture and invents objects; this paper straightens the sight lines.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Multimodal models (systems that read both text and images together) often describe objects that are not really there. The paper traces this to a human-like problem: when your eyes jump around instead of staying focused, you see a blur and guess wrong about what is there. In these models, the same blur shows up as different processing pathways staring at different places in the image, and then as the model keeps talking, it forgets to look at the image at all. The fix is simple: nudge all the pathways to look at the same place, and remind the model to keep looking back at what it already saw.

Zero assumption version

Why models see things that are not there

Imagine you are reading a book while also watching a tennis match out of the corner of your eye. Your focus bounces between the two. When you try to describe the room, you might invent details because your gaze was divided. Multimodal models work the same way: they have multiple processing pathways (like multiple eyes, each watching for different patterns), and when these pathways disagree about where to look, the model gets a blurry picture of the image.

A beginner might try ignoring this disagreement and just averaging what each head sees. But that does not work: if head 1 stares hard at the dog and head 2 stares hard at the bush, averaging their views gives a fuzzy ghost image that belongs to neither. The model sees something in the blur that was never there, so it invents a name for it. As the model keeps generating text, it also gradually forgets to look at the image at all and just guesses based on what it said before.

The core fix. Make all processing pathways look at the same place by boosting the focus of pathways that disagree, and remind the model to stay grounded by pulling in what earlier words learned about the image.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure head disagreement

Compute how much each attention head differs from the average of all heads.

Nudge heads toward alignment

Add a small bonus to the attention scores of heads that are far from the average, pulling them closer.

Measure temporal fading

Track whether the model is still looking at the image or has drifted to text-only mode.

Re-anchor with history

When fading is detected, bring back the strongest focus pattern from earlier words that successfully grounded the image.

Apply while the model answers

Do all of this while the model is generating its response, with no retraining.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the weights of the model. Instead, it changes the attention scores (the final soft weights that determine how much each head looks at each part of the image) while the model is answering, by adding two corrections: one that pulls disagreeing heads together, and one that remembers what the model learned from the image earlier in its answer.

the object it moves  =  attention scores during decoding
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: head inconsistency

measured quantity  =  Head Inconsistency

Head inconsistency is a single number that measures how much the attention heads disagree. Imagine lining up twelve heads, each staring at a spot in the image, and asking how far apart they are. If all heads point to the same spot, inconsistency is zero (perfect focus). If they point all over the place, inconsistency is high (blurry blur).

The paper estimates this by comparing each head's attention distribution (where it is looking) to the average attention distribution across all heads. Heads that match the average contribute low inconsistency; heads that diverge contribute high inconsistency. The paper shows that high inconsistency is a strong predictor of hallucinations: models with inconsistent heads are much more likely to invent objects.

Why it works

Why fixing attention heads stops hallucinations

The mechanism has two parts, mirroring the two symptoms the paper identified. First, spatial inconsistency: when processing pathways disagree about where to look, the model cannot tell whether it is seeing a dog or a plant or a blur. The fix is to measure each pathway's divergence from the group average and nudge pathways that are far off back toward the center. This does not require retraining; it is a surgical post-processing step applied to the attention scores while the model is generating its answer. Pathways pull into alignment because the nudge is small but consistent, and aligned pathways produce a clear picture instead of a ghost.

Second, temporal fading: as the model generates more text, it gradually forgets to look at the image and relies only on what it has already said, leading to hallucinations because there is no visual ground truth to push back. The fix is to spot when the model stops looking at the image and dig back into the focus patterns from earlier in the generation when the model was still looking. The paper identifies the word from the past that was most helpful (the one that had the strongest visual alignment when it was generated) and pulls that historical visual focus pattern back into the current step. This is like reminding the model of what it saw before.

Together, these two moves keep attention focused and grounded throughout generation: heads stay aligned (spatial clarity) and the model keeps looking back at the image (temporal continuity).

Math after the picture

The attention adjustment equation

First in words:

New attention score = old attention score + nudge based on head disagreement + gate multiplied by historical attention

Then the compact form:

A_corrected(l,h,t) = A_orig(l,h,t) + lambda * [A_avg(l,t) - A(l,h,t)] * I(disagreement > tau) + G(l,h,t) * A_historical(l,h,t_past)

Every symbol:

A_corrected(l,h,t)the final attention score after correction, at layer l, head h, time step t
A_orig(l,h,t)the original attention score before correction
lambdathe strength of the alignment nudge (how hard to pull heads together)
A_avg(l,t)the average attention across all heads at this layer and time step
A(l,h,t)the original attention of this head
I(disagreement > tau)1 if this head disagrees more than threshold tau, else 0
G(l,h,t)a gate: 1 if visual attention is fading, else 0
A_historical(l,h,t_past)the attention pattern from an earlier, well-grounded step in generation
A tiny worked example
Suppose layer 3, head 5 at time step 10 has original attention A_orig = 0.15.
The average attention across all heads is A_avg = 0.25 (heads are spread out).
The difference is 0.25 - 0.15 = 0.10, which exceeds the disagreement threshold.
Set lambda = 0.1 (gentle nudge). The alignment correction adds 0.1 * 0.10 = 0.01 to the score.
Visual attention ratio has dropped, so gate G = 1. Historical attention from step 5 was A_hist = 0.12.
We add G * A_hist = 1 * 0.12 = 0.12 to restore grounding.
Final corrected score: 0.15 + 0.01 + 0.12 = 0.28 (both aligned with the group and anchored to image memory).

The correction adds a small multiple of the disagreement vector (to pull heads together) plus a gated copy of historical attention (to restore focus), all without training.

What can go wrong

Threshold too loose. If the disagreement threshold tau is set too high, most heads are left alone and spatial inconsistency persists. Hallucinations continue.
Threshold too tight. If tau is too low, even heads that are correctly specialized get nudged. This can erase useful diversity and make the model worse at some tasks.
No historical data early on. At the start of generation, there is no strong past word to anchor to. The paper uses a smaller gate or skips the enhancement step until enough history exists.
Done right. The paper tunes tau and lambda on a small validation set, and uses a data-driven gating function that only applies historical enhancement when image attention is genuinely fading. This avoids both over-correction and under-correction.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small demo that takes a multimodal model and an image with a simple scene (three objects: a dog, a plant, and a plate on a table, clearly separated). Prompt the model to describe the image in a single sentence. Run it three times: once with no correction, once with only spatial correction, and once with both spatial and temporal corrections. Measure hallucination rate (how many objects in the output that are not actually in the image).

hallucination_rate = (hallucinated_objects) / (total_objects_mentioned)

The demo must show that the model with no corrections invents at least two objects that are not in the image, spatial correction alone cuts this in half, and the full AFIP correction nearly eliminates it.
