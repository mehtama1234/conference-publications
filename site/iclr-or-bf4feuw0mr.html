DemoGrasp: Universal Dexterous Grasping from a Single Demonstration

Instead of learning from scratch with complex rewards, start with one video of a successful grasp and teach the robot to edit that grasp trajectory for any object.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Dexterous grasping (teaching a robot hand with many fingers to pick up different objects) normally requires training with trial-and-error across thousands of attempts and carefully tuned reward signals. DemoGrasp takes the opposite approach: show the robot one video of a successful grasp, then teach it to adjust that grasp for new objects by editing two key parts of the trajectory—where the wrist moves and how the fingers curl.

Zero assumption version

Why teaching a robot to grasp is hard

A robot with many fingers can hold objects in countless ways. To teach it which way works for each object, researchers usually run thousands of trial grasps in a computer simulation, rewarding the ones where the fingers close around the object without crushing it or dropping it. This trial-and-error is slow because the robot must explore billions of possible finger positions and wrist locations.

Teachers try to speed this up by adding hints to the reward signal—extra bonuses for certain hand shapes, for keeping fingers close to the object, for lifting smoothly. But adding more hints often backfires: they pull the learning in conflicting directions, and the robot ends up learning moves that work only on the training objects, not on new ones.

The naive approach breaks down here: Trial-and-error learning requires complex reward design and explores blindly in a massive space of finger positions, making it slow and brittle across different objects.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Record one grasp

Collect one video of the hand successfully grasping a single object, with the hand moving toward the object and then curling closed.

Describe the trajectory as two sequences

The grasp is two independent sequences: the wrist position over time and the finger joint angles over time.

Define editable axes

Learning problem: for a new object, apply a global wrist offset and a global hand-curl offset to the recorded trajectory.

Use single-step RL

Train a neural network that sees the object at the start and predicts optimal wrist offset and hand offset, then executes the edited grasp.

Transfer to real robot

Train a second network to predict the same edits from camera images, then test on real objects.

What the paper changes

The one thing being changed

The paper changes the exploration space itself. Instead of the robot searching over all possible finger positions and wrist locations (a space with thousands of dimensions), it learns to make just two edits to a pre-recorded grasp: a wrist offset (move the hand left, right, up, or down) and a hand-curl offset (open or close the fingers slightly from the recorded shape).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: demo replay translation invariance

measured quantity  =  translation invariance of the demo

The paper measures something that existing methods miss: how much a successful grasp strategy depends on the exact location where you start. The recorded demonstration grasp has a property called translation invariance—if you replay the same hand movements on the same object placed 10 centimeters to the left, the hand still grasps it successfully. This happens because the hand follows a path relative to the object, not to the table. So the robot does not need to re-learn the grasp for every possible starting location.

The paper exploits this by training with random object placements but using the same learned wrist offset and hand curl edits. Because the demo respects object-relative coordinates, spatial randomization does not confuse the learning—unlike baseline methods that must memorize the optimal grasp at each table position.

Why it works

Why it works: leveraging structure in the demonstration

The reason this method works is that a successful grasp already encodes most of the hard-won knowledge about hand shape and motion strategy. The demonstration shows: start at a certain distance from the object, move toward it, then curl the fingers in a specific sequence to close around it without colliding. When you replay this motion, you only need to adjust two small things—where to start (wrist offset) and how much to curve (hand curl offset)—rather than inventing a grasp from scratch.

Because these two edits capture the key degrees of freedom, the learning problem becomes tractable: the robot trains a single-step policy that takes one observation (the object at the start) and outputs just two numbers (offset amounts), then executes the edited trajectory. This is far faster than learning every joint angle over a long sequence of timesteps. The binary reward (binary success term and a robot-table collision penalty) is enough because the demonstration structure handles the hard part—finding a working hand shape and motion sequence.

For transfer to new objects or new hand embodiments, the same principle holds: the demonstration grasp strategy (approach-then-curl) generalizes across objects and different numbers of fingers because it respects object-relative motion, not specific finger count or object size.

Math after the picture

The learning objective

First in words:

Reward equals one point if the grasp succeeds, minus a penalty for any collision, minus a penalty for wrist offset magnitudes.

Then the compact form:

reward = success_binary - collision_penalty - lambda * (||wrist_offset||^2 + ||hand_offset||^2)

Every symbol:

success_binary1 if object is lifted, 0 otherwise
collision_penaltycost incurred when hand hits table
wrist_offsetthe (x, y, z) displacement applied to hand approach
hand_offsetthe scalar adjustment to finger curl timing
lambdaweight controlling how conservative the edits are
A tiny worked example
Trial 1: wrist_offset = (0.02, 0.01, 0.03) meters, hand_offset = 0.15 seconds. Grasp succeeds. collision_penalty = 0. Offset magnitude squared: 0.02^2 + 0.01^2 + 0.03^2 + 0.15^2 = 0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0009 + 0.0225 = 0.0239. With lambda=0.5, penalty = 0.0239 * 0.5 = 0.012. Reward = 1.0 - 0.0 - 0.012 = 0.988.
Trial 2: wrist_offset = (0.10, 0.08, 0.12) meters, hand_offset = 0.50 seconds. Grasp fails but avoids collision. Offset magnitude squared: 0.10^2 + 0.08^2 + 0.12^2 + 0.50^2 = 0.0100 + 0.0064 + 0.0144 + 0.2500 = 0.2808. Penalty = 0.2808 * 0.5 = 0.140. Reward = 0.0 - 0.0 - 0.140 = -0.140.
The policy learns that small, conservative edits are safer, but only rewarded if they succeed—pushing it to find small offsets that work.

The equation rewards successful grasps while penalizing large edits to the demonstration, keeping the learned adjustments close to the original working strategy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Demonstration is unrepresentative. If the single demo grasp relies on a lucky hand pose or a brittle approach, the robot inherits that brittleness. Editing a flawed demo does not fix the underlying flaw—it only shifts it.
Object shape contradicts demo assumptions. If the demo shows grasping a mug by the handle, but the test object is a sphere with no handle, large wrist offsets may not save the grasp—the demo strategy itself is wrong for that shape class.
Offset magnitude limits are too tight. If the penalty on edits (lambda) is set too high, the robot cannot adapt far enough. If lambda is too low, the robot forgets the demo entirely and falls back to random exploration.
Done right: demo choice and offset tuning. Choosing a representative demo (one that works across a range of object sizes and shapes) and tuning lambda solves most failure modes, because a demo that generalizes well to diverse shapes provides a strong foundation that edits can build on, and lambda balances learning speed against safety—too low and the robot forgets the demo, too high and it cannot adapt. In practice, the paper trains on 175 diverse objects, so the demo strategy works across variation.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A concrete demo would build a simulator with a robotic hand and record one successful grasp. Then, train a neural network to predict wrist offset and hand-curl offset given observations of new objects. Finally, test the learned policy on objects the robot has never seen, measuring success as whether the object is lifted without collision.

The key experiment: show that with only binary reward (success or failure) and no hand-engineered bonus terms, the robot reaches over 85% success on unseen objects. Compare against a baseline trained with the same objects but from scratch, which requires complex reward terms and achieves lower success. Plot success rate as a function of how far the object is displaced from its training positions, showing the spatial generalization curve.

A successful demo must show that starting from one video of a grasp and learning only two numbers to adjust the trajectory outperforms learning from scratch with complex rewards, especially on objects and positions never seen during training, because the demo embeds a working approach and hand motion, so learning only edits is far simpler than learning everything—smaller search space, fewer trials needed, and simpler reward signal suffices.
