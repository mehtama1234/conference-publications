Robust and fair predictions

Papers on making models work reliably across different groups and conditions, handling noisy data, and making predictions that don't discriminate.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model that works perfectly on clean, typical data might fail catastrophically when things are different. A self-driving car trained on clear roads fails in rain. A face recognition system trained on faces of one skin tone fails on others. A loan-approval system trained on historical data might perpetuate past discrimination. You need models that work reliably even when conditions change, that treat different groups fairly, and that do not hide their failures under the assumption that the world will always be as clean as the training data.

The hard part is that reliability and fairness require balancing many competing demands. A model that works reliably across different conditions often becomes less accurate on typical data. A model that is perfectly fair in one sense (equal accuracy for all groups) might be unfair in another sense (equal outcomes across groups, even if it means treating people differently). And you cannot always measure fairness directly — you might not even have labels for the groups you care about, or the groups might be defined in complex ways. You also have to decide whether to aim for reliability when conditions change naturally or when someone is actively trying to fool the model.

Researchers work on reliability by training on diverse data that includes edge cases, by using techniques that make the model's predictions stable (small changes in input should lead to small changes in output), and by designing models that can detect when they are out of their depth. They work on fairness by auditing models for bias, by explicitly optimizing for fairness metrics, by debiasing training data, and by building in transparency so people understand what the model is doing and can contest its decisions. Some focus on worst-case reliability (making sure the model never fails dramatically) while others focus on average reliability (making sure it works well across different conditions on average).

How the work breaks down

Making predictions that work reliably and fairly splits into four natural directions: keeping models fair when matching or ranking people (like job recommendations); defending against adversaries who actively try to break the model; handling messy, noisy, or shifted data that looks different from training; and knowing when to trust predictions by building in uncertainty estimates.

Fairness in matching and ranking

When a model makes decisions about people—whether to hire them, match them to jobs, or rank them for opportunities—fairness and accuracy pull in different directions. A matching algorithm might maximize the number of good pairs but ignore that some groups get worse matches. A ranking system might predict accurately but perpetuate past discrimination. The challenge is building a model that does not just follow historical patterns but actively improves outcomes for everyone, because historical data often reflects past injustices that will repeat if you simply learn from it.

The retention-optimized matching paper tackles this by shifting the goal from pure accuracy to long-term retention: instead of asking which matches look best on paper, it asks which matches actually make people stay. This reframes fairness as a practical outcome measure. The fairness-aware evidential learning paper solves a different piece: it ensures predictions come with honest uncertainty estimates that do not hide doubt behind false confidence. By allocating evidence fairly across groups, this paper makes the model's confidence calibrated for everyone, not just the majority. Both papers recognize that fairness is not an add-on; it is baked into how you define success.

Surviving attacks and adversarial noise

A model trained on clean images might crumble when someone intentionally adds small perturbations designed to trick it. A neural network tuned for accuracy on typical data can fail spectacularly when an adversary actively tries to break it. Staying accurate means the model's predictions stay sensible even when someone or something is pushing it off the rails. The hard part is doing this without sacrificing performance on the normal data the model will see most of the time.

The FERD (fairness-enhanced distillation) paper faces this head-on: it trains models to resist adversarial attacks (small, crafted changes to input) while keeping predictions fair across groups. Here is what matters: training only for resistance to attacks can actually make unfairness worse, so this paper balances both by adjusting how data is distributed during distillation. The spiking neural networks paper for adversarial attacks tackles the same problem from a different angle—using a different kind of neural network (spiking neurons that mimic biological brains) and hardening it against adversarial examples. It works by making the network less sensitive to tiny input changes and reducing a weakness called state-flipping where neurons flip between states unpredictably. Both papers show that adversarial toughness and fairness must be designed together.

Handling noisy and shifted data

Real-world data is messy: some labels are wrong, the data itself looks different from what the model trained on (when customers have different patterns than the historical dataset), and it is hard to know which part of the mismatch is noise versus a fundamental change. A model confident on clean training data can fail when it encounters mislabeled examples or when the world changes. The models need to distinguish between random label errors and systematic shifts in what the data looks like.

The noise-aware generalization paper does exactly this: it separates label noise (random mistakes in the ground truth) from domain shift (when data comes from a different source or time), then uses cross-domain information to make that distinction sharper. This means the model does not just ignore noise; it actually learns which examples are mislabeled and adjusts. The causal-aware anomaly detection paper takes a different path: instead of assuming all anomalies follow the same pattern, it learns which features actually cause an instance to be anomalous, then looks for breaks in those causal relationships. Both papers recognize that reliability in messy data comes from understanding the structure of what is going wrong, not just smoothing over it.

Reliability in distributed settings

When a model lives on millions of phones or edge devices, coordinating learning and prediction becomes fragile. One compromised device, one person sending corrupted data, or one server that is out of sync can break the whole system. Federated inference—where pieces of a model run across different machines that do not fully trust each other—needs to work reliably even when some participants are adversarial or careless.

The federated inference paper for resisting attacks solves this by combining two ideas: first, train the model with adversarial examples so it is already tough to trick, and second, at test time, aggregate predictions carefully so one bad device cannot hijack the final answer. By pairing adversarial training on the model side with defensive voting on the aggregation side, the system stays accurate even when attackers try to poison it. This shows that distributed reliability is not just about making each piece better; it is about designing the connections between pieces to be defensive, because one weak link can break the entire system if the pieces trust each other blindly.
