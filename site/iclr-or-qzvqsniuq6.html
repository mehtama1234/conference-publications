Interior Nodes for A/B Testing under Network Interference

When you run an A/B test on a network, ignore the edges and just measure the middle nodes, then fix the bias with a predictor.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

A/B testing on a social network faces a problem: your outcome depends not just on your treatment but also on what your neighbors got. Existing solutions trim the data to keep only special nodes, but throw away so much that their estimates are wildly uncertain. This paper shows that if you measure only the interior nodes (whose neighbors all got the same thing as they did), you can compute a much more precise answer—and then use a trained predictor to fix the remaining bias.

Zero assumption version

Network interference breaks naive A/B tests

Imagine you run an A/B test on a social network: you give some people a feature (treatment) and others nothing (control). The naive approach is to measure how many of each group liked it. But here is the snag: because people are connected, a person in the treated group can influence their friends in the control group. So the control group's outcome is not pure—it is poisoned by spillover from their treated friends.

Existing solutions use cluster randomization: you group users into communities, give the entire cluster one treatment, and measure only the nodes on the interior—nodes whose neighbors are all within the same cluster. This cancels the spillover problem. But now you have thrown away most of the data. The remaining interior nodes are rare, so your estimates bounce around wildly. Your answer is precise in direction but uncertain in magnitude: high variance.

The problem. Existing network-aware A/B tests trim data so heavily that their estimates have enormous uncertainty.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Cluster the network

Run a graph clustering algorithm to group users into communities.

Mark interior nodes

Flag users whose neighbors are all within the same cluster.

Average interior outcomes

Compute the mean for treated interior nodes, mean for control interior nodes, take the difference.

Train a predictor

Feed the entire network (not just interior) to a model; learn to forecast outcomes from treatment and network structure.

Adjust for bias

Use predictor to estimate what interior node outcomes miss, add back the gap.

What the paper changes

The object being changed

The paper changes which units get counted and how. Specifically, it stops using reweighting (where rare interior nodes get enormous importance weights) and instead computes a simple average of interior node outcomes, then corrects for the fact that interior nodes are not representative of the whole population.

the object it moves  =  reweighting scheme
The hidden quantity

Bias due to unrepresentativeness

measured quantity  =  how far interior nodes differ from the whole population

Interior nodes are not a random sample of the population. They tend to cluster in certain parts of the network and have different network properties (more tightly connected neighborhoods, certain age/location patterns). This creates a shift: the interior nodes' average outcome differs from what the whole population would see, even before any treatment effect. This shift is the hidden quantity.

The paper estimates it by training a predictor on the full network, then asking: what would the interior nodes' outcomes have been under both treatments? The difference between what the interior nodes actually show and what the predictor says they should show is the correction term.

Why it works

Why simple averaging works better

Old estimators reweight every data point by the inverse of its selection probability. If interior nodes are 1% of the data, each interior outcome gets weight 100. This balloons the variance: a single noisy interior outcome is multiplied 100 times. The paper's insight is that you do not need to invert the rarity—just average the interior nodes equally. Yes, they are biased (not representative), but the bias is small and predictable. The variance drops dramatically.

The predictor layer fixes the remaining bias. You train a model on all units to predict outcomes from treatment and network structure. Then you measure the gap between what interior nodes actually show and what the predictor forecasts they should show (accounting for their network position). That gap is the adjustment. The model learns what is typical for the full population, then you correct the biased interior sample by asking: how far off are interior nodes compared to what the model expects?

The estimator formula

First in words:

treat interior average minus control interior average, then add a correction term from the predictor

Then the compact form:

tau_hat = (sum of treated interior outcomes / count treated interior) - (sum of control interior outcomes / count control interior) + (predictor forecast gap)

Every symbol:

tau_hatthe estimated treatment effect
interiornodes whose neighbors are all in the same cluster
predictor forecast gapactual interior outcomes minus the predictor's forecast of what they should be
sum of treated interior outcomesall outcomes from interior nodes assigned to treatment
count treated interiorhow many interior nodes got the treatment
A tiny worked example
Suppose 100 users in a cluster: 50 treated (interior), 50 control (interior).
Treated interior outcomes: 10 users score 8, 40 users score 6. Average = (10*8 + 40*6) / 50 = 6.4.
Control interior outcomes: 25 users score 4, 25 users score 5. Average = (25*4 + 25*5) / 50 = 4.5.
Naive difference = 6.4 - 4.5 = 1.9 units of lift.
Predictor trained on all 10,000 users in the network forecasts: interior nodes should average 6.2 under treatment, 4.2 under control.
Residual = (6.4 - 6.2) + (4.5 - 4.2) = 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.5 units of bias to add back.
Final estimate = 1.9 + 0.5 = 2.4 units. Interior node bias corrected.

The formula says: take the clean interior average, then layer on a learned correction so interior bias (bias from being unrepresentative) does not mislead the result.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad clustering makes interior nodes even less representative. If the clustering algorithm produces clusters that span very different neighborhoods, the interior nodes inside will be atypical. The predictor correction can only adjust so much. A coarse clustering algorithm amplifies the problem.
Predictor trained on biased data generalizes poorly. If the network has strong homophily (treated users friend other treated users), the predictor sees a distorted version of how treatment spreads. It then mis-corrects interior nodes. The fix depends on the model's accuracy.
Very small interior population kills precision. In sparse networks or tightly clustered data, interior nodes might be rare (less than 10% of the sample). Even simple averaging is still noisy. The method trades extreme variance for moderate variance, not zero.
Done right: coarse clustering, large interior sample, predictor trained on held-out data. Use a fast, deliberate clustering algorithm that balances cluster size. Ensure interior nodes are plentiful. Train the predictor on data separate from the test to avoid overfitting the bias correction.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Simulate a social network (100–500 users, 2–10 communities via random block model). Assign treatment at the cluster level (Bernoulli). Simulate outcomes: base outcome plus spillover term (outcome goes up if neighbors are treated). Run three estimators: (1) naive difference-in-means (ignores interference, high bias), (2) old reweighting (high variance), (3) this paper's interior mean plus predictor (target low bias and low variance). Measure the variance and bias of each. The demo must show that (3) beats both (1) and (2) in the root-mean-square error (bias squared plus variance).

root-mean-square error = sqrt(bias^2 + variance). Naive is high bias. Reweighting is high variance. This paper is low both.

The demo must make visible that when you stop over-weighting rare data and instead predict to adjust bias, you win on both uncertainty and accuracy.
