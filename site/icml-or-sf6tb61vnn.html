Thompson Sampling's Hidden Optimization

Thompson Sampling works because it is secretly solving an online optimization problem where you greedily pick the best choice but only pull back from greed by your honest uncertainty.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Thompson Sampling is a famous strategy for making smart bets when the payoffs are unknown. You keep a running guess (your best current theory, updated as you learn) about each option and randomly sample a possible value from each theory. Then you pick the option with the highest sample. Decades of math proves it works great, but nobody knew why — what was the invisible thing it was optimizing? This paper shows Thompson Sampling is really just solving a moment-by-moment optimization problem where you choose the best option but only disagree with yourself by the size of your own doubt.

Zero assumption version

Why is Thompson Sampling mysterious?

Imagine you walk into a casino with 10 slot machines. Each one pays out a different amount on average, but you do not know which. You pull each arm a few times, learn a bit, then must decide: pull the best-looking one for guaranteed reward now, or keep testing uncertain machines to find a better one later? Each pull costs time and money, so this choice matters — this is the exploration versus exploitation tradeoff, because testing new machines explores the unknown but wastes pulls when you could be exploiting (cashing in on) the best one you have found so far.

Thompson Sampling solves this beautifully: maintain a running guess (your evolving best theory of what each machine pays, based on all pulls so far) about what each machine pays. At each step, sample one guess from each theory and pull the machine with the highest sample. Over time you learn, your guesses get more certain, and you pull the good machines most. It works amazingly in practice and the math proves low regret.

But here is the mystery: what is Thompson Sampling actually trying to maximize? When you sample from a guess rather than picking the machine your guess says is best, what hidden goal are you serving? Nobody had a clean answer. A beginner might think you are trying to maximize your reward, since sampling should give you better results on average than ignoring some information — but that does not explain why sampling a guess is better than just acting on your best guess directly, because your best guess should logically give you the highest expected payoff, so acting on it alone should be just as good.

In one sentence. Thompson Sampling balances greed (picking the best option) against doubt (pulling back only as much as your uncertainty says you should) by solving a per-step optimization problem.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Thompson Sampling does not change which machine you pull; it changes which metric it is secretly optimizing at each moment. Instead of asking what is the machine that gives me the most cumulative reward over a lifetime, the paper shows it is really asking: what machine balances my greedy choice (pick the best I know) against my honest uncertainty (doubt might mean I am wrong)?

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Regret per Decision

measured quantity  =  Moment-by-moment regret against an oracle

The paper introduces a new idea called time-invariant regret. Instead of asking how much money you left on the table by not always playing the best machine, ask: at this one moment, if you knew the truth, what would the oracle pick? Compare to what you pick. That per-step gap is your moment-by-moment regret.

Thompson Sampling tracks this without knowing the oracle. It keeps your uncertainty (the width of your belief) as a regret penalty: the less sure you are, the larger the regret gap you allow yourself to tolerate — because sampling from a wide belief introduces randomness, and that randomness will sometimes pick a machine that is not actually the best, so you must allow yourself a bigger gap. The math shows Thompson Sampling matches what an oracle would do if it had to pay a cost equal to the size of your doubt — the wider the belief, the higher the cost of being uncertain, so the algorithm pulls back from greed and explores more.

Why it works

Why it works: greedy choice minus doubt

The paper shows Thompson Sampling solves this: at each moment, pick the machine that maximizes your expected immediate reward, but only if it also beats a baseline that includes a penalty for your doubt. The penalty is not arbitrary; it equals the width of your belief.

Here is why this is genius. Early on, you are very unsure (wide belief, big penalty). You cannot afford to be purely greedy because the exploration cost is huge. So you sample, explore, and learn. As you learn, your belief narrows (doubt shrinks), the penalty falls, and greed dominates. By the end, you are playing the best machine nearly always because you barely doubt anymore. The regret falls naturally as a side effect of learning.

The Bellman-optimal policy is the oracle that knows the truth and plays greedily. Thompson Sampling mimics that oracle by setting its exploration penalty equal to its uncertainty. Result: you do exactly what the oracle would do if it were charged a price for its uncertainty.

The core equation

First in words:

At each step, Thompson Sampling acts as if it is solving: pick the machine that maximizes your expected payoff from playing it this round, minus a penalty that equals your uncertainty about its payoff.

Then the compact form:

a_star = argmax_a [ E[r_a] - lambda * U_a ]

Every symbol:

a_starthe machine you pick at this step
aa candidate machine
E[r_a]your best guess of the average payoff from machine a
U_athe width of your uncertainty about machine a (how spread out your belief is)
lambdathe cost per unit of uncertainty; Thompson Sampling sets this automatically from the problem structure
A tiny worked example
Machine 1: your guess is 5.0, your doubt (belief width) is 2.0
Machine 2: your guess is 4.5, your doubt is 0.5
Machine 3: your guess is 3.0, your doubt is 3.0
Set lambda = 1.0 (a simple choice for this example)
Machine 1 score: 5.0 - 1.0 * 2.0 = 3.0
Machine 2 score: 4.5 - 1.0 * 0.5 = 4.0
Machine 3 score: 3.0 - 1.0 * 3.0 = 0.0
You pick Machine 2 (score 4.0 is highest).
Even though Machine 1 has the highest guess, Machine 2 wins because your doubt about Machine 1 is large and costly.

The equation formalizes the idea in the mechanism: trade off between grabbing what looks best and holding back because you might be wrong.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatched belief shape. If your belief about each machine is badly calibrated (you think you are unsure but are actually certain, or vice versa), the doubt penalty misfires. Thompson Sampling will either explore too much or too little.
Non-stationary payoffs. If the machines change their payoffs over time, Thompson Sampling learns the old payoffs and keeps playing machines that used to be good. It does not naturally adapt to a shifting world; the belief narrows and doubt shrinks, so it stops exploring.
Delayed feedback. If you do not learn the payoff until much later, your beliefs do not update in time, and you cannot adjust your exploration penalty. The algorithm acts on stale information.
Done right. When your beliefs are well-calibrated, the world is stationary (or changes slowly), and you get immediate feedback, Thompson Sampling finds the best machine with minimal wasted pulls, and regret grows logarithmically.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple bandit problem: 5 slot machines with fixed but unknown payoffs. Write Thompson Sampling and a naive greedy baseline. Run both for 1000 pulls. Plot the cumulative regret (total money left on the table) over time.

The demo must show: Thompson Sampling starts exploring (pulling sub-optimal machines), then converges to the best machine, and ends with cumulative regret growing much slower (logarithmically) than greedy, which gets stuck with constant regret once it commits to the wrong machine.

Cumulative regret = sum over all pulls of (best_payoff_available - actual_payoff)

The inflection point where Thompson Sampling discovers the best machine and stops wasting pulls is where the doubt penalty falls below the gain from switching.
