Forecasting Motion from a Single Image

Instead of generating pixels like a video model, generate the paths that objects will trace—and do it better.

Why video models fail at forecasting

When you see a photo of a ball suspended in air, you can picture it falling. The direction and speed depend on forces you cannot see—gravity, air, spin. A naive forecaster might try to guess: feed the image to a model and ask it to predict the next frame as pixels. That is what modern video generators do.

But here is the trap: video generators are optimized to paint pixels that look realistic to a human eye, not to trace the true paths of objects. A tumbling block might appear blurred in the next frame, smeared across ten pixels by motion blur. The model learns to paint blur because frames are judged on visual realism, not on whether the blur honestly tracks the block's real trajectory. So when tasked with forecasting motion, video generators guess wrong. They generate plausible-looking pixels, not plausible physics.

The root problem. Video generators optimize for pixel realism, not trajectory truth. They trade one for the other.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the output modality. Instead of asking the model to produce the next video frame (a grid of RGB colors), it asks the model to produce a trajectory grid (the 2D paths traced by every object in the scene). Same architecture, different target. This tiny switch—trajectories instead of pixels—cascades into a massive improvement in accuracy.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: trajectory realism

measured quantity  =  trajectory realism

Trajectory realism is how faithfully the predicted paths match the true motion of objects. It is measured by comparing dense grids of predicted point paths to ground-truth paths from simulation or video. When a model predicts a block will fall at a 45-degree angle but it actually falls at 60 degrees, the paths diverge; realism drops.

The paper measures this by computing the mean distance between predicted trajectory points and true trajectory points across the entire grid. It also measures the diversity of sampled predictions—whether the model can produce many plausible futures (since physics is inherently uncertain without knowing forces). Here is why this matters: pixel-based models mix two things together: visual plausibility (blur, soft edges) and motion plausibility (where the object truly goes). Trajectory models pull them apart. Trajectories force the model to answer the true question: where will the object actually go?

Why it works

Why it works: direct motion over pixel painting

Video generators fail because they optimize for the wrong target. When you ask a model to paint frame 2 given frame 1, it learns that blur is fine—blur looks cinematic. A block tumbling across five pixels gets painted as a smooth smear (a transition from one color to another), and that smear looks correct to a human eye. But the smear is not a trajectory; it is a hedging bet that conceals a wrong prediction.

This paper's model sidesteps the problem by asking for trajectories directly. For every point in the input image grid, output where that point will move. Now the model cannot hide behind blur. It must commit to a specific path. Because the training loss compares predicted paths to true paths (not pixels), the model learns to predict physics, not appearances. If a block will fall straight down, the model learns to output a straight-down path; if it will roll, it learns to curve the path. The architecture remains a decoder like video generators use, but the training signal points it at motion instead of color.

The second breakthrough is that trajectories scale naturally to diverse futures. Physics is random—without knowing the exact forces, there are many plausible futures. The model encodes this uncertainty into a latent code (a compressed summary of the motion), and sampling different codes produces different trajectory samples, all plausible. Video models do this too, but they must trade off plausibility against visual realism; trajectory models are free to propose bold, varied motions.

The training objective

First in words:

minimize reconstruction loss (Huber loss) between predicted trajectories and true trajectories, plus a regularization term that keeps the learned motion codes close to a standard Gaussian distribution

Then the compact form:

L = L_huber(trajectories_predicted, trajectories_true) + beta * KL(N(mu, sigma), N(0, I))

Every symbol:

L_huberreconstruction loss measuring how far predicted paths drift from true paths
trajectories_predictedpaths output by the model for each grid point
trajectories_trueground-truth paths from simulation or video
betaweight controlling how much to penalize codes straying from normal distribution
KLKullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of distance between two distributions
N(mu, sigma)learned Gaussian distribution of motion codes (mu=mean, sigma=variance)
N(0, I)standard normal distribution (mean 0, identity covariance)
A tiny worked example
Say a falling block's true path at pixel (x, y) is a curve ending 20 pixels down. The model predicts 18 pixels down.
Huber loss for that point: difference is 2 pixels, so loss is small, roughly 2.
Across a 128×128 grid, sum these point losses: 10,000 is plausible if most predictions are close.
KL regularization term: if the learned motion codes drift far from a standard Gaussian, add penalty (e.g., 5). Total: L = 10,005.
In the next batch, the model learns to predict paths closer to true (lower Huber) and keep its codes closer to Gaussian (lower KL). Both push the same direction: plausible trajectories.

The loss directly measures prediction error in trajectory space, not pixel space. The model cannot fool itself with blur.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Unobserved forces. A ball rolling off a table could bounce high or stick low depending on friction you cannot see in the image. The model captures this uncertainty by sampling multiple futures, but no forecast is perfect when hidden variables matter. The model learns which scenarios are plausible given the scene, but real outcomes will sometimes fall outside its samples.
Rare or unusual events. If the training data mostly shows blocks falling straight, the model will predict straight falls. An unusual tilt in a test image might produce a prediction the model never trained on. Transfer from synthetic scenes (computer simulations) to real ones helps, but the model struggles when it encounters scenarios outside its training range, because it has never learned to extrapolate beyond the physics it memorized.
Grid resolution trade-off. The model outputs a dense grid of trajectories (one per pixel or every few pixels). Very fine grids mean more computation; coarser grids miss small objects. The paper fixes this by training separate models at different resolutions, but there is no one-size-fits-all sweet spot.
Done right. When the scene involves simple physics (gravity, rolling, stacking) and the model has seen diverse training scenarios, trajectory prediction is highly accurate. The model resolves the motion ambiguity by sampling many plausible futures and picking the best ones, not by committing to a single wrong guess.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

The demo should show three stacked blocks in a photo, then let the model forecast the motion if they fall. Run the model on a single input image (no video), sample three different future trajectories from it, and overlay all three on the original image as colored curves. Then show a real video of the blocks falling. The demo wins if: (1) the predicted paths stay close to the real paths, and (2) the three samples explore the space of plausible futures (some curves diverge more than others, capturing the inherent randomness in where each block lands).

trajectory_error = mean absolute distance between predicted trajectory points and real trajectory points (pixels)
