Agentic Confidence Calibration

To predict when an AI agent will fail, watch how confident it stays across every step, not just at the end.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Modern AI agents tackle multi-step tasks like reasoning through questions or planning tool use. But they often overestimate their own confidence, claiming a solid answer when they are actually wrong. Old methods checked only the final answer's confidence. This paper shows that the real signal lives in the entire path — how the agent's confidence jumps or dips at each intermediate step reveals hidden failure modes that the ending never shows.

Zero assumption version

The agent's last word is not the whole story

Today's AI agents run a long chain of thinking steps to answer hard questions. Each step produces a confidence score — a number saying how sure the model is. A naive approach: check only the final score. If it is high, trust the answer.

This breaks because a critical mistake often happens in the middle, not at the end. The agent may recover and sound confident anyway. Imagine an agent working through a math problem: it misunderstands the setup in step 2, then by step 7 it convinces itself the wrong answer is right. The final confidence score is high, but it is confidently wrong.

In one sentence. Watch the entire path of confidence scores, not just the final one.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect confidence everywhere

As the agent generates each token (word-piece) in each step, record its numerical confidence.

Extract signals from within each step

Measure the spread, peaks, and dips of confidence as the model produces words in that single step.

Extract signals across steps

Measure how confidence rises or falls from step to step, and whether the first or last step stands out.

Combine into one scorecard

Feed all these measurements into a lightweight model that learns which patterns predict failure.

Predict pass or fail

The calibrator outputs a new confidence: how likely the agent actually got it right.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the agent itself. It changes where we look for the danger signal: from just the final confidence score to the entire sequence of confidence changes across the full trajectory.

the object it moves  =  the confidence signal: from end-only to trajectory-wide
The hidden quantity

The failure pattern buried in the path

measured quantity  =  trajectory instability pattern

Every agent trajectory — the sequence of steps it takes — leaves behind a signature. Some steps produce steady, high confidence; others wobble. Some confidence values seem out of place. Some steps stand apart from the rest. These traces form a failure pattern: a mathematical fingerprint of whether the agent went wrong.

HTC finds this pattern by measuring eight kinds of statistics: the average and spread of confidence within each step, the highest and lowest values, the entropy (disorder) of the confidence curve, how skewed (lopsided) it is, and how much it changes from step to step. Together, these measurements form a 'trajectory feature vector' — a list of numbers describing the entire path. A lightweight model (a simple linear classifier or small neural network) learns which combinations of these statistics reliably predict failure, without ever seeing the actual task details.

Plain reading. The failure pattern is the shape of the confidence curve: when and where it dips, how much it wobbles, and whether the beginning or end stands out.
Paper reading. The paper computes process-level diagnostic features across two axes: within-step (mean, variance, min, max, entropy, skewness via finite differences) and across-step (step-to-step differences, positional indicators for first and last step). These form the input to the calibrator.
Why it works

Why watching the path spots failure

When an agent makes a critical mistake early on but does not realize it, the error leaves a trace in the confidence path that the final score hides. The agent might recover and sound confident, but the trajectory tells the truth: confidence dropped at step 2, then climbed back up later. A calibrator trained on real agent failures learns that this pattern — a dip followed by recovery — often means the answer is wrong despite sounding confident.

The mechanism works because different task types produce different failure signatures. In multi-step math, an early misunderstanding (low confidence at step 2) is the killer signal. In knowledge-intensive questions, the combination of first-step and last-step confidence matters most. By measuring both within-step wobbles and cross-step dynamics, HTC captures this diversity without hardcoding task-specific rules. The lightweight model simply learns to recognize these patterns and downgrade confidence when it sees them.

Critically, this approach avoids looking at the task itself — it needs no labeled data per task to work. Train once on a variety of agent trajectories from different tasks, and the calibrator can be dropped into a new domain without retraining, because the failure signatures are universal: an overconfident agent leaves a distinctive trajectory shape, regardless of whether the task is math, reasoning, or retrieval.

Math after the picture

The calibration equation

First in words:

calibrated confidence equals a weighted sum of trajectory measurements, passed through a squashing function to output a probability.

Then the compact form:

calibrated_conf = sigmoid( w1 * mean_conf_step1 + w2 * var_conf_step1 + w3 * entropy_allsteps + w4 * firstlast_diff + w5 * max_dip + bias )

Every symbol:

mean_conf_step_iaverage confidence across all tokens in step i
var_conf_step_ihow much confidence wobbles within step i
entropy_allstepsdisorder of the entire confidence curve
firstlast_diffdifference between first-step and last-step confidence
max_diplargest drop in confidence between any two steps
w1, w2, ..., w5learned weights; bigger weight means that signal matters more
biasa constant offset tuned during training
sigmoidsquashing function that converts any number into a probability between 0 and 1
A tiny worked example
Suppose mean_conf_step1 = 0.75, var_conf_step1 = 0.08, entropy = 0.6, firstlast_diff = 0.10, max_dip = 0.35.
Learned weights from training: w1=0.5, w2=0.3, w3=0.4, w4=-0.2, w5=-0.6, bias=0.1.
Raw sum = 0.5*0.75 + 0.3*0.08 + 0.4*0.6 + (-0.2)*0.10 + (-0.6)*0.35 + 0.1 = 0.375 + 0.024 + 0.24 - 0.02 - 0.21 + 0.1 = 0.509.
Apply sigmoid(0.509) = 1 / (1 + e^(-0.509)) ≈ 0.625.
Recalibrated confidence: 62.5%. The large max_dip (negative weight) dragged it down from the original (hypothetical) 75%, because a big confidence dip predicts failure.

The weights learn which trajectory shapes predict failure; negative weights on dips mean dips lower the recalibrated score.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The agent hides all failures uniformly. If the agent is overconfident everywhere (high confidence on both right and wrong answers), no trajectory measurement will separate them. HTC cannot rescue a fundamentally uncalibrated agent.
The task is unlike training data. If the new task produces a failure mode not seen during calibrator training, the learned weights may not apply. The paper shows this by testing transfer across very different domains; some transfers work, others degrade.
The agent framework changes. If the agent uses a different language model or reasoning framework, the trajectory statistics may change shape. The paper tests multiple LLMs and agent architectures; HTC generalizes across them, but edge cases are possible.
Done right. Train the calibrator on a diverse dataset of trajectories from many tasks and agent types. Apply it as a plug-and-play layer on any new agent. Retrain only if the new domain shows systematic miscalibration.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple agent that answers multiple-choice math questions by reasoning step by step. Run it on 100 questions, collecting the confidence at each token and step. Compute HTC's trajectory features (mean, variance, entropy, dips, first vs last). Train a logistic regression model on 50 of the trajectories to predict success. Test on the other 50. Measure how well HTC predicts failure compared to the last-step-only baseline.

ECE (Expected Calibration Error) = average of |predicted_confidence - actual_accuracy| over bins; lower is better. Also AUROC: area under the curve of true-positive rate vs false-positive rate when using the calibrated score to rank answers.

The demo must show that trajectories with a large mid-step dip predict failure even when the final step looks confident, and that HTC's recalibrated scores are closer to true accuracy than the original model scores.
