Understanding knowledge and relationships at scale

Papers that handle complex networks like knowledge graphs and databases, extracting meaning from relationships between entities and reasoning over connected information.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Knowledge bases are enormous graphs of facts: 'Einstein' is connected to 'Physics' via 'field of study', and 'Physics' is connected to 'Relativity' via 'related to'. Real knowledge graphs have millions of entities and billions of relationships. The tasks are unique: complete missing facts (infer likely connections), reason over chains of relationships (if A causes B and B affects C, then A affects C), and extract semantic meaning—the idea that certain relationships are more important or carry more weight than others.

The hard part is scale and incompleteness. Most large graphs are sparse—most possible connections don't exist—and incomplete—missing connections you'll never see the data for but could infer. Also, relationships have different meanings and strengths. A 'parent of' relationship is very different from a 'similar to' relationship, and a 'voted for' relationship in politics is different from a 'voted for' in a jury. A simple averaged model loses these distinctions. And at millions of entities, even reading through all neighbors of a node becomes expensive.

Researchers build models that embed entities and relationships together, learning vector representations where reasoning becomes simple vector math: if relation R takes entity A to entity B, then A plus the direction R should point toward B. They study temporal reasoning (relationships change over time) and multi-hop reasoning (paths through multiple relationships). They handle the incompleteness by learning to score how likely a missing connection is based on the patterns around it. Some adapt transformer-style attention to work with enormous graphs by sampling or aggregating smartly instead of considering all neighbors.

How the work breaks down

Work on knowledge graphs at scale splits into four main directions: handling time and causality as relationships change, building models that work across millions of entities without slowing down, combining language models with structured graph facts for smarter reasoning, and adapting graph models across different databases and domains.

Reasoning over time and causality

Real knowledge graphs change over time. When Einstein discovers relativity, new 'discovered by' relationships appear. When a gene is linked to a disease through a chain of biological steps, understanding the causal path matters as much as knowing the link exists. These papers tackle the hard problem that events have heterogeneous causes—some relationships directly cause others, while some just correlate or co-occur.

The temporal knowledge graph paper (Beyond Entity Correlations) explicitly separates different types of causal connections at the event level rather than just marking entities and links. By disentangling which events cause which, it lets the model reason about why something happened, not just that it happened. The emerging entities paper (Inductive Reasoning for Temporal Knowledge Graphs) solves a practical problem: when a new person, place, or company appears in the graph that you've never seen before, you still need to predict what relationships it has based on its semantic properties and local context. This matters in news graphs, company databases, and medical records where new entities arrive constantly. Together these papers show that temporal reasoning requires both causal structure and the ability to handle completely new entities.

Scalable models across massive graphs

Knowledge graphs like Freebase, Wikidata, and enterprise knowledge bases contain millions of entities and billions of relationships. A naive model that compares every possible pair becomes too slow. These papers ask: how do you build models that are mathematically complete—they can learn any relationship-based reasoning—but also run fast even on graph datasets with 10 million entities?

The Flock paper (Knowledge Graph Foundation Model via Learning on Random Walks) proves that a model trained on random paths through the graph becomes a universal approximator—it can express any function that treats equivalent graph structures the same way. This matters because it means the model won't arbitrarily change its answer if you relabel entities, as long as their neighborhood patterns stay the same. Tested on 54 knowledge graphs, it predicts both missing entities and missing relationship types. The Ramba paper (Selective State-Space Models for Relational Deep Learning) takes a different angle: instead of sampling, it uses a linear-time state-space model—a technique from signal processing—to aggregate information from all neighbors without examining each one explicitly. This achieves similar accuracy to dense methods but scales linearly with graph size, making it practical for real deployment. Both solve the core tension between expressiveness and speed.

Hybrid reasoning: language models and knowledge graphs

Language models like Llama and GPT are trained on text and understand meaning, but they hallucinate facts—they will confidently state false relationships. Knowledge graphs are structured and precise, but they are incomplete and don't handle natural language well. The question these papers address is: how do you combine a language model's semantic understanding with a knowledge graph's factual reliability?

The Chain-of-Decomposition paper (Enhancing LLMs for Knowledge Base Question Answering) breaks down complex questions step by step. When asked 'What are the children of people who worked at Apple?', it first extracts the sub-questions ('Who worked at Apple?' and 'What are their children?'), answers each using graph lookups, then reasons across the answers. A Llama model with 7 billion numbers (parameters—the model's weights) that is fine-tuned with this method beats GPT-4 on benchmarks because the decomposition forces the model to use the graph instead of relying on its training data. The Knowledge Reasoning Language Model paper (Unifying Knowledge and Language for Inductive Knowledge Graph Reasoning) goes deeper: it co-trains the language model and graph together so that the language model learns to generate reasoning paths that stay within domains where the graph is trustworthy. If the graph says 'Einstein is in the Physics domain', the language model learns to only reason about physics-related properties for Einstein. Both papers reject the false choice between language models and graphs and show they work best as partners.

Logical reasoning and finding explanations

Abductive reasoning means inferring the best explanation for observations. If you observe 'the plant is wilting' and 'the soil is dry', the explanation 'it wasn't watered' is much more likely than 'it has a rare fungus'. Knowledge graphs let you represent these chains of causality, but generating reasonable hypotheses at scale is hard—there are too many possible explanations to enumerate them all.

The Controllable Logical Hypothesis Generation paper (CtrlHGen) uses control signals—like 'explain this using medical concepts' or 'focus on environmental factors'—to steer the model toward relevant hypotheses. This matters when you have a specific context or domain constraint. Instead of generating all possible causes, it generates plausible ones that match what you actually need to explain.

Transferring and adapting across different graphs

In practice, you often have a model trained on one knowledge graph (say, a company's internal database) and need to apply it to a different one (a partner's database with different entities and relationship styles). The challenge is that the semantic meaning changes—a 'parent-child' relationship in one domain might correspond to a 'manager-direct report' relationship in another, and the graph structure itself is different. These papers tackle transfer learning for graphs and relational databases.

The Learning Structure-Semantic Evolution Trajectories paper (Graph Domain Adaptation) models graphs as they change over time and learns how their structural and semantic properties evolve. By watching patterns shift from source to target domain, it figures out the right transformation so that a model trained on one graph works on another. The Rel-MOSS paper (Relational Deep Learning on Imbalanced Relational Databases) solves a practical problem in enterprise settings: when you query a relational database, some classes are rare—a fraud detection task might have 0.1% fraudulent transactions. Standard models ignore the rare cases. Rel-MOSS learns weighted representations that focus harder on rare but important relationships, improving performance on both balanced accuracy and sensitivity to rare cases. The Relatron paper (Automating Relational Machine Learning) goes one step further: it meta-learns which algorithm to use for a given task. When data has many relationships but they are simple, hand-crafted features work fast; when relationships are complex and nested, deep learning on the graph structure extracts them automatically. Relatron learns numeric codes (called task representations—vectors that summarize a task's properties) and selects the right approach automatically based on what it observes in the data, avoiding the need for an expert to choose.
