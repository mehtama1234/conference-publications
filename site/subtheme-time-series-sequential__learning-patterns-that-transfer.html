Learning patterns that transfer

Building foundation models and using transfer learning so that knowledge learned on one time series task or dataset generalizes to new ones without retraining from scratch.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A trained model on one dataset often fails on a new dataset. A forecasting model trained on 10 years of New York weather will give bad predictions for Boston. A model trained on one patient's heart rate patterns will not generalize to another patient because bodies are different. This is the transfer learning or generalization problem. Ideally, you could train once on a large, diverse dataset, then quickly adapt that knowledge to new sequences without retraining from scratch. This saves compute because you do not redo the expensive large-scale training, reduces data requirements because you reuse knowledge learned elsewhere, and enables models to be deployed faster because adaptation is quicker than full training.

Why is transfer so hard for sequences? Sequences carry many hidden factors. Weather patterns depend on latitude, time of year, and local geography. Heart rates depend on age, fitness, current stress, and medications. Factory sensor readings depend on equipment model, operating conditions, and maintenance history. A model trained on one distribution often overfits to spurious correlations in that distribution. For example, a weather model might learn that 'when the forecast says rain, the next day is cooler'—but that is because rain and cooler temperatures are correlated in the training location, not a causal rule. When transferred to a new location where the correlation differs, the model fails. Transfer learning requires learning the invariant patterns that actually generalize.

Papers build foundation models—large models trained on many diverse sequences from different domains and sources. The idea is that if a model sees sequences from factories, hospitals, finance, and climate science simultaneously, it learns general principles about how sequences work rather than domain-specific quirks. Then, for a new sequence, you fine-tune just a small part of the model or add a small adapter. Some papers also investigate what features transfer: do models that understand seasonal patterns transfer well, even if the specific seasons differ? Others use domain adversarial training, where you force the model to be unable to tell which domain a sequence came from, so it must learn domain-invariant representations.

How the work breaks down

Work splits into four natural directions. First, building foundation models on many diverse sequences together so they learn general principles rather than dataset-specific quirks. Second, understanding which parts of a trained model transfer well to new data and which parts need retraining. Third, adapting pre-trained models to new sequences quickly with minimal retraining and new labeled data. Fourth, deploying foundation models that work well on new data without extensive tuning or retraining.

Building diverse foundation models

Building a foundation model means training once on many different time series from different domains and companies, then reusing that training on new sequences. The hard part is: how do you combine data from hospitals, factories, and weather stations without one domain drowning out the others? If you train on 90% factory data and 10% hospital data, the model learns mostly factory patterns and performs poorly on hospitals.

FeDaL tackles this by using federated learning, which means many hospitals and factories each train a small local model on their own data, then share only the learned patterns with each other instead of sharing raw data. This protects privacy and lets each organization keep control, while the shared patterns help everyone. CauKer approaches the problem differently by generating synthetic sequences instead of collecting real ones. The insight is: if you can create fake time series that look realistic, you can make as much training data as you want. When CauKer tests scaling from 10,000 samples to 10 million, synthetic data scales smoothly, while real datasets plateau. This suggests that real datasets may hide biases that prevent larger-scale learning.

Understanding what transfers and what does not

Once a foundation model is trained, it contains thousands of pieces—embeddings, attention heads, layers—many of which may not actually help when transferred to new data, because some pieces learned patterns specific to the original training data and do not generalize. The hard question is: which pieces matter? Do we need all of them, or can we remove some without hurting performance on new data? If we remove unnecessary pieces, training on new datasets becomes faster because you have fewer pieces to update, and requires fewer numbers to tune because fewer moving parts means fewer decisions to make during adaptation.

The design-choices paper investigates how small decisions early in building a foundation model ripple through its behavior. For example, whether you normalize data before feeding it to the model, or how you handle extreme outliers, affects whether the model learns stable patterns that generalize or learns spurious correlations. The redundancies paper directly removes layers and attention heads from trained foundation models and measures what breaks. It finds most models tolerate removing large portions and still perform well, but a few specific heads are responsible for problems like motif parroting (just copying a pattern from earlier in the sequence) and seasonality bias (assuming a pattern that exists in the training set will repeat). Once these problem heads are identified, they can be retrained or handled separately.

Quick adaptation to new sequences

Real-world deployment requires speed. If a hospital gets a new patient's heart-rate data, you cannot spend weeks fine-tuning a model. Ideally, you take a pre-trained foundation model and adapt it in minutes with a small amount of the hospital's own data. This saves compute and data compared to retraining from scratch because the model already learned from thousands of sequences elsewhere, so you do not need as many examples or as much GPU time. But how do you adapt without overfitting to just one hospital's quirks, losing the general knowledge the model learned?

The medical foundation-model paper shows that lightweight label-query training—asking the model to predict labels on a few examples while only updating 0.1% of its parameters (the 'adapter')—achieves 35% improvement in accuracy over specialized models that trained on just one hospital's data, because the foundation model has already learned general patterns and the adapter fine-tunes those patterns to the new hospital. The UniCA paper extends this to multimodal covariates (sequences that have multiple related signals, like heart rate and temperature together). Instead of forcing all sequences into one fixed format, UniCA learns how to adapt the model's inputs when a new sequence has different types of covariates, which means the model can handle new combinations of signals without breaking. The Adapt Data to Model paper flips the approach: instead of adapting the model to the data, adapt the data to the model. It automatically finds small transformations of the input sequences—normalizing by a different mean, rescaling in a different way—that make the pre-trained model perform better because it aligns the new data with the model's assumptions about what sequences look like. Testing on multiple datasets, this sometimes reduces prediction error by 68%. The Channel Adapter paper handles a specific adaptation problem: multivariate forecasting where a new dataset has a different number of signals (channels) than the training data. It introduces lightweight adapters that map the new channels into the model's expected input space, so the pre-trained model can run on data with different shapes.

Deployment without tuning

Some domains cannot tolerate slow adaptation or require immediate predictions without any retraining. Scientific simulations (modeling turbulence or chaotic systems) must produce predictions in real time with no access to labels for fine-tuning. In these cases, you want a foundation model that works on unseen sequences straight out of the box. The question is: can a pre-trained model predict well on totally new data without any adjustment?

Context parroting is a surprisingly simple baseline: when predicting the next time step, just look at the context (the recent history) and output the average of what you see. This requires no machine learning at all. When tested on chaos, turbulence, oscillators, and ECG data, context parroting matches or beats many complex foundation models while being computationally trivial, which reveals a limitation in how foundation models are built: they may be learning spurious patterns from the data they were trained on instead of underlying physics. Mantis improves classical foundation models through test-time prediction: instead of a single prediction, it generates multiple predictions by taking different subsets of the model's intermediate layers and combining them through voting and averaging. This requires no retraining but improves accuracy across diverse time-series datasets because combining diverse predictions reduces errors that any single prediction would make. Zeus pursues a different angle: tuning-free means the model should work well on multiple kinds of tasks (classification, forecasting, anomaly detection) without adjusting hyperparameters per task. It achieves competitive results on five tasks and performs 8% better than expected on data it was never trained on, which suggests it learned genuinely generalizable patterns rather than overfitting to the original training data, because a model that overfit would perform worse on new data, not better.
