SwiftTS: Picking the Right Pre-trained Model for Time Series Tasks

Instead of testing each pre-trained model one at a time, learn to predict which one will work best by comparing the shape of your data to patterns the model has seen before.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you have a time series problem (predicting stock prices, detecting anomalies in sensor data, forecasting demand), pre-trained models exist that work well on related data. But with dozens in the model hub, testing each one by fine-tuning is slow. SwiftTS learns from past data-to-model matches to instantly predict the best model for your new dataset, skipping the expensive trial-and-error.

Zero assumption version

The Bottleneck: Model Shopping Is Slow

Real forecasting problems need the right pre-trained model (a finished model trained on related data). A researcher or engineer has 8 good candidates, each trained on different time series data.

A beginner tests each one: take your dataset, fine-tune model #1 (run training for hours), measure accuracy, then repeat for models #2 through #8. After a day of compute, you pick the winner. But what if you have 50 datasets and 100 models? The grid explodes.

The problem: picking a model by brute-force fine-tuning is expensive and repeated for every new task. There is no fast way to guess which model fits your specific data before training.

In one sentence. SwiftTS looks at your dataset's shape and structure, then instantly predicts which pre-trained model will work best without expensive testing.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Embed the data

Convert your time series into a list of numbers (an embedding) that captures its characteristics (trend, seasonality, volatility, scale).

Embed the models

For each pre-trained model, convert its learned parameters into a list of numbers representing what kinds of time series patterns it specializes in.

Score compatibility

Compare the data embedding to each model embedding by breaking both into small chunks (patches) and scoring how well they match piece by piece.

Adjust for your horizon

Different time series tasks have different prediction lengths (forecast 1 day ahead vs. 30 days ahead). Dynamically reweight expert scores for your specific horizon.

Pick the winner

Return the model with the highest compatibility score. Use it directly without fine-tuning, or fine-tune briefly if needed.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

SwiftTS changes how model selection is done: from brute-force testing of all candidates to instant scoring based on data characteristics and model fitness. The central insight is that models trained on similar time series patterns will perform similarly — so by learning to read your data and match it to pre-trained models, you avoid testing each one.

The hidden quantity

The Hidden Quantity: Data-Model Compatibility

measured quantity  =  patchwise compatibility score

The paper measures one core quantity: how well your dataset matches each pre-trained model. This is not accuracy yet — it is a score that predicts which model will do well after fine-tuning.

Compatibility is computed by breaking your time series into small overlapping windows (patches), doing the same to model parameters, and scoring how aligned they are. A high score means the model has already learned patterns that exist in your data; a low score means it specializes in different patterns. The paper learns this scoring function by studying historical dataset-model pairs and how well they performed.

Why it works

Why the method works

Pre-trained models encode patterns from their training data into their learned weights. A model trained on stock prices has different parameter values than one trained on sensor streams. By learning to read both your dataset and the model parameters as lists of numbers in the same space, SwiftTS can measure alignment: if your data contains the same recurring patterns (seasonal cycles, sharp jumps, smooth trends) that a model has seen, the embeddings will match. If your data is completely different, the embeddings stay far apart.

The paper makes this work by training on historical pairs: for past datasets and models, record both the compatibility score and the actual fine-tuned accuracy. The model learns the connection: high compatibility leads to high accuracy, because when a pre-trained model has already learned the patterns in your data, it needs less additional training to specialize to your task. Then on a new dataset, compute compatibility scores for all candidates instantly — no training required. Horizon-adaptation handles the fact that forecasting 1 step ahead versus 30 steps requires different model skills; the paper learns to reweight expert contributions based on your specific forecast length.

The payoff: instead of waiting hours for brute-force fine-tuning, you get a ranked list of models in seconds. You can fine-tune the top candidate (much less trial-and-error) or use it directly.

The scoring equation

First in words:

Total compatibility = sum of patch-pair similarities, weighted by horizon importance

Then the compact form:

score = sum_j (w_j * sum_i sim(patch_data_i, patch_model_i)) / num_patches

Every symbol:

scorecompatibility score (higher is better fit)
sum_jsum over all expert groups or horizons
w_jimportance weight for expert j (reweighted by horizon)
patch_data_ithe ith small chunk of your dataset
patch_model_ithe ith small chunk of the model's parameters
sim(...)similarity (e.g., cosine distance between chunks)
num_patchestotal number of patches compared
A tiny worked example
Suppose your 100-day dataset breaks into 5 patches, model has 5 parameter chunks.
Patch 1 data has smooth trend; model chunk 1 specializes in trends: sim = 0.9.
Patch 2 data has noise; model chunk 2 is good at noise: sim = 0.8.
Patches 3-5: sim = 0.7, 0.6, 0.5 (decreasing fit).
Mean similarity = (0.9 + 0.8 + 0.7 + 0.6 + 0.5) / 5 = 3.5 / 5 = 0.7.
With horizon weight w_1 = 1.2 (for 7-day forecast): score = 1.2 * 0.7 = 0.84.
Do the same for 7 other models; highest score wins (no training needed).

The score uses patchwise similarities because time series have local structure — a model may excel at handling trends early in a sequence but not seasonality late; breaking both data and model into patches captures this fine-grained alignment.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Data unlike anything seen in training. If your dataset has patterns the pre-trained models have never seen (e.g., a completely new measurement instrument, a rare economic shock), the compatibility scores will be uniformly low or misleading. SwiftTS has learned to recognize familiar patterns; unfamiliar data breaks this assumption.
Horizon mismatch. If you trained SwiftTS on models fine-tuned for 1-step forecasts but your real task requires 30-step forecasts, the horizon-adaptive weights may not generalize. The paper shows this works across 8 horizons in training, but extreme extrapolation (forecasting 100 steps when training only saw 7) can fail.
Model architecture shift. SwiftTS learns from historical model-dataset pairs. If a new pre-trained model with a fundamentally different architecture appears, compatibility scores may not transfer; the paper only tested 8 existing models.
Done right. SwiftTS works well when your data resembles what the pre-trained models saw during training, your forecast length is within the training range, and you can afford a small accuracy dip (2–3 percentage points) for speed. In these cases, instant selection beats brute force, and you can optionally fine-tune the top candidate if accuracy is critical.
