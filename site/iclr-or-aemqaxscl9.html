Stage-Aware Reward Modeling for Long Horizon Robot Manipulation

The paper fixes robot learning from messy real-world video by training a reward model that predicts task stage and progress, then uses those scores to filter out bad demonstrations before training the policy.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot to fold a T-shirt is hard because different people fold differently—some are fast, some are slow, some mess up mid-way. Vanilla imitation learning (behavior cloning) learns equally from all these videos, which makes the robot confused. This paper solves it by first training a reward model that watches a video and predicts both what stage of the task is happening and how close to done it is, using natural-language task descriptions to label the training videos. Then it uses those reward predictions to upweight good, consistent demonstrations and downweight the messy ones before training the robot policy.

Zero assumption version

Why robot learning fails on messy real-world data

Teaching a robot via imitation (behavior cloning) means collecting videos of humans folding shirts, then training the robot to copy the actions. But human demonstrations are messy: some videos are fast and clean, some are slow, some show mistakes mid-way. All of this goes into the same training pot.

A naive approach gives equal weight to every demonstration. So the robot learns an average of good and bad examples—ending up confused about what the right move is at each moment. On a task like T-shirt folding, this fails badly: 0% success rate on hard variants, even 8% on easier ones.

The core problem. Demonstrations vary wildly in quality and speed, and learning equally from all of them makes the robot imitate the average—a strategy that works nowhere.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Label video frames

Given videos and natural-language subtask names, compute how far each frame is through the task, from start to completion.

Train dual-head reward model

A video encoder (a frozen image feature extractor plus a transformer, which reads a sequence of images as chunks) predicts both task stage and fine-grained progress.

Score each demonstration

Run the reward model on each video to get progress estimates across all frames.

Compute sample weights

For each demonstration, the reward model predicts progress. High smooth progress gets high weight; jumpy or inconsistent progress gets low weight.

Train weighted behavior cloning

Minimize weighted imitation loss, upweighting clean demonstrations and suppressing noisy or slow ones.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the way demonstrations are weighted during behavior cloning training. Instead of giving every video the same weight, it computes a per-demonstration weight based on how clean and consistent the reward model thinks it is.

the object it moves  =  demonstration weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Progress consistency

measured quantity  =  frame-level progress estimate

The paper measures progress as a single number between 0 and 1 at each video frame, predicted by the reward model. This is not what any human labels—it emerges from training the model jointly on stage prediction and progress supervision. Good demonstrations show smooth, monotonic progress: always moving forward through the task. Bad or slow ones show jumpy, inconsistent progress: backtracking, plateaus, or sudden jumps that do not match the actual task state.

The paper uses this frame-level progress to assign an overall weight to each demonstration: videos where the reward model predicts consistent forward progress get high weight and influence the policy strongly, while videos with noisy or backtracking progress get low weight and contribute less to what the robot learns.

Why it works

Why this fixes the problem

The core issue is that vanilla behavior cloning treats all demonstrations equally, so it learns an average strategy that works nowhere. The paper fixes this by introducing a middleman: the reward model. The reward model is trained on the labeled video frames to predict progress, so it learns what good progress looks like—smooth, forward-moving, no backtracking. Once trained, the reward model watches each raw demonstration and assigns a score: if it sees smooth, consistent forward progress, the demonstration is marked as reliable (high weight); if it sees jumpy, inconsistent, or backtracking progress, the demonstration is marked as noisy (low weight). Behavior cloning then trains on a weighted average of demonstrations—focusing on the clean ones and ignoring the messy ones. The net effect is that the policy learns from mostly-good examples, so it learns a clear, consistent strategy instead of a confused average.

Why does this work? Because the reward model learns an inductive bias about what real progress looks like, using the natural-language task descriptions as an anchor. Task descriptions keep the reward model grounded—it does not learn arbitrary patterns, but learns to recognize genuine task progress from the video.

Math after the picture

The weighted imitation loss

First in words:

For each demonstration, assign a weight based on how consistent the reward model thinks the progress is. Minimize the weighted imitation loss: sum of (weight times error on that demonstration), divided by sum of weights.

Then the compact form:

L_RA-BC(theta) = sum_i w_i * loss(policy(o_i), a_i) / (sum_i w_i + epsilon)

Every symbol:

thetathe policy's parameters (the weights of the robot's neural network)
w_ithe weight for demonstration i (1.0 if clean, 0.0 if noisy)
loss(policy(o_i), a_i)how wrong the policy's action is compared to the human action
o_ithe observation (image and state) at step i
a_ithe human action at step i
epsilona tiny number to avoid dividing by zero
A tiny worked example
Demo 1 (clean): reward model score 0.9 → weight w_1 = 0.9, loss = 0.5 → contribution 0.9 * 0.5 = 0.45
Demo 2 (messy): reward model score 0.2 → weight w_2 = 0.2, loss = 0.6 → contribution 0.2 * 0.6 = 0.12
Demo 3 (clean): reward model score 0.85 → weight w_3 = 0.85, loss = 0.4 → contribution 0.85 * 0.4 = 0.34
Total loss = (0.45 + 0.12 + 0.34) / (0.9 + 0.2 + 0.85) = 0.91 / 1.95 = 0.47
Same mistakes matter less when they come from downweighted noisy demos.

The weight for each demo comes from the reward model's confidence in the progress estimate, acting as a filter: clean demos dominate the loss, messy ones barely contribute.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward model is poorly trained. If the initial labels (task progress annotations) are wrong or inconsistent, the reward model will learn to recognize bad patterns. It will then upweight bad demonstrations, which means the policy imitates more of the mistakes that the reward model mistakenly flagged as good, making the policy worse than vanilla imitation.
Task descriptions are vague or missing. The reward model uses natural-language subtask names (flattened, folded, placed) to ground its progress estimates. If the names do not match the actual demonstrations, the model will not learn what genuine task progress looks like, and will output random or flat scores.
Reward model fails on very different tasks. If the test task is very different from training data, the reward model's weights may not transfer. The paper shows some generalization to similar tasks, but the method offers no guarantee when the task is radically different from what the reward model was trained on.
Done right. When task descriptions are clear and annotations careful, the reward model learns to recognize genuine progress, and reweighting demonstrations gives the policy a clear signal to imitate.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple T-shirt folding simulator or use a subset of real data. Collect 20–30 human demonstrations, some clean (no mistakes) and some messy (backtracking, slow). Train a reward model on labeled progress (0.0 to 1.0 for each frame). Then run two experiments: one where you train a behavior-cloning policy on all demonstrations equally, another where you train it with weights from the reward model. Compare success rate on a held-out test set. The weighted version should win by a large margin, showing that the reward model successfully identified which demonstrations were clean.

success_rate = (number of rollouts that reach goal) / (total rollouts), measured separately for vanilla BC and RA-BC.

The demo should make it impossible to miss that the reward model's weights matter—filtering out noisy demonstrations should yield a policy that works reliably where vanilla cloning fails.
