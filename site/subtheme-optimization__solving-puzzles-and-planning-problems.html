Solving puzzles and planning problems

Combinatorial puzzles—routing, scheduling, assignment—are computationally hard. Can learning and neural methods find good solutions faster than traditional algorithms?

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Imagine you run a delivery company and need to find the shortest route visiting 100 stops. Or you schedule hospital staff shifts so everyone gets fair hours. Or you assign students to dorm rooms so everyone is happy. These are combinatorial optimization problems—problems where the answer is a discrete choice (this route, not that one; this person in this dorm, not that building). The challenge is that the number of possibilities explodes: with 100 stops, there are more routes than atoms in the universe. No computer can check them all.

Classical algorithms (like branch-and-bound or dynamic programming) can find good answers by being clever—they prune impossible branches, use symmetry, and solve subproblems first. But they are slow on hard problems. Modern neural networks are fast at making decisions, but they have no guarantee their answer is actually good. The research question is: can you train a neural network to learn heuristics that beat classical methods, or can you combine neural prediction with classical search to get speed and quality both?

Papers use several approaches. Some train a neural network to predict which stops to visit next in a traveling-salesman-style problem, then use classical branch-and-bound to refine its guess. Others treat the problem as a graph and use message-passing networks to learn patterns in the graph structure—if you see this shape, this assignment usually works. Some papers learn to warm-start a solver: instead of beginning from scratch, the neural network guesses a decent starting point that is already pretty good, and the solver improves it faster than it would from a random start, which saves computation because fewer refinement steps are needed. A few use reinforcement learning, where the network makes choices one-at-a-time and gets rewarded for finding short routes. The common thread is: combine the speed of neural prediction with the reliability of classical methods.

How the work breaks down

Research in this subtheme splits into several directions based on the core challenge: how to trade off speed for solution quality. Some work teaches neural networks to guess good starting points or to recognize patterns in graph structure. Others combine learning with classical branch-and-bound search, getting the speed of neural prediction plus the guarantees of traditional methods. A few papers focus on continuous relaxations or multi-objective tradeoffs, where you need to satisfy several goals at once. Finally, some explore how to make solvers handle new kinds of problems or adapt to different constraints.

Neural networks and classical search together

The hard problem: classical solvers are slow but correct; neural networks are fast but can make poor decisions. Can you train a network to recognize which decisions lead to good solutions, then feed its predictions into a classical solver to refine the answer?

ViTSP tackles this by using a vision-language model (treating a traveling-salesman problem as something you can see on a graph) to generate a sequence of promising stops in order, then lets classical branch-and-bound refine it. It achieves nearly optimal solutions on tours with up to 88,000 stops—far larger than traditional solvers handle alone. Dynamic Stratified Contrastive Learning improves the branching decisions inside a mixed-integer linear solver by learning which variable to split on next; it trains on the structure of partial solutions and learns patterns that help early in the search tree, where decisions cascade into huge speedups downstream. Vision-Zero takes the idea further: instead of just warmstarting with a neural prediction, it treats solving itself as a game, using self-play to iteratively improve the solver's ability to make branching decisions—by playing against itself, the solver gets feedback on which decisions actually led to better solutions, which accelerates learning beyond what supervised training alone can achieve. These three papers share a single insight: the classical solver's strengths (correctness, logical guarantees) plus the neural network's speed (fast pattern recognition) beat either alone.

Message-passing networks for graph structure

The hard problem: discrete optimization problems live on graphs—cities connected by roads, sudoku cells related by constraints, assignments where one choice limits others. How do you teach a neural network to learn patterns in graph structure so it predicts good assignments?

HONet (Hypergraph Optimization Network) learns to solve exact-cover puzzles like Sudoku by encoding the constraints as a hypergraph (a graph where edges can connect more than two nodes) and training a message-passing network to learn where conflicts hide. It reaches 100% accuracy using far fewer training examples than before because it naturally exploits the constraint structure. LoRe (Adaptive Interaction-Evaluation Routing) does the same idea for larger problems like maximum independent set and traveling-salesman by routing neural messages through the graph in adaptive patterns—it learns to spend computation where it matters and skip irrelevant parts, achieving orders of magnitude larger instances with major speedups compared to fixed message-passing. Optimal Transport Guided Stochastic Control adds a different flavor: it uses optimal transport (a mathematical tool for matching two sets of points so that total distance is minimized) to guide the neural network's choices in graph combinatorial optimization, making sure the network's predicted solutions move smoothly toward feasible ones. All three learn the graph's hidden structure instead of treating it as noise.

Learning to warmstart and guide solvers

The hard problem: solvers need good starting points or good heuristics to make decisions fast. A poor warmstart wastes many iterations; a good one cuts solving time dramatically. Can learning predict where to start or what direction to search?

BoGrape (Bayesian optimization over graphs with shortest-path encoded) learns to warmstart a solver for graph optimization by predicting good initial designs; it works on molecular design problems where you need to find a molecule that satisfies many constraints at once. Optimal Design for Multinomial Logit tackles a different flavor: selecting the best product assortment from thousands of choices. It learns what samples to ask for (which assortments to test) to pinpoint the best one with fewer experiments—it cuts the required number of trials from random guessing. RAPNet (Accelerating Algebraic Multigrid with Learned Sparse Corrections) warmstarts an iterative linear solver for PDE problems by having a neural network predict the first approximation; classical multigrid then refines it. The speedup comes because the first guess is already close to the answer, so fewer refinement sweeps are needed. All three show: a good learned warmstart beats starting from zero.

Multi-objective and constraint-aware solving

The hard problem: real-world problems rarely have a single objective. You need to minimize cost and travel time at once, or balance fairness with efficiency. How do you train a solver that keeps multiple goals in view?

Preference-Modulated Structural Attention (attention is a neural mechanism for deciding which parts of the input matter most) solves multi-objective combinatorial problems by changing how the neural network focuses on different parts of the graph depending on preference weights—the network learns to perceive the graph structure differently depending on which objective matters most. It beats both single-objective methods and general multi-objective solvers on benchmark routing and assignment problems. Plug-and-Play Benchmarking of RL Algorithms for Flow Control takes the multi-objective idea to physical simulation: controlling fluid flow involves balancing drag reduction, stability, and energy efficiency. It builds a benchmark (FluidGym) where reinforcement-learning methods learn to find control policies that balance these competing goals in large-scale flow fields. These papers recognize that real optimization is rarely about one number—it is about making tradeoffs between competing goals.
