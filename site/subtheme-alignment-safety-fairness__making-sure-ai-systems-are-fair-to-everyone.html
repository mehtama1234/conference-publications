Making sure AI systems are fair to everyone

These papers address bias in AI—ensuring models don't unfairly discriminate against groups of people and that decisions are explainable and legitimate to everyone affected.

What this is

A credit scoring model is trained on historical loan data. Historically, banks discriminated against certain groups, so the data is biased: those groups had fewer approvals and higher default rates (partly because they were steered into worse terms, partly because of economic inequality). A model trained on this data learns those patterns and replicates the discrimination. A hiring model trained on resumes and hiring decisions will learn the historical biases of the hiring committee. A language model trained on internet text will absorb stereotypes and slurs. This is algorithmic bias: the model doesn't intend to discriminate, but it learned discrimination from data. And because the model is faster and cheaper than human review, it can scale the harm to millions of people. Worse, it can hide behind a veneer of objectivity: 'the model said so' sounds more authoritative than 'I think so.'

Why is this hard? Because bias in data is often invisible at first. You might not notice that your training set has fewer examples of women in engineering, or that it has stereotypical descriptions of certain ethnic groups. And even if you notice, it's not obvious how to fix it. If you remove features (like race or gender), the model can often recreate the discrimination using correlated features—zip code, education level, name. And fairness itself is contested: there are multiple incompatible definitions of fairness, and satisfying one often breaks others. For example, if you define fairness as equal accuracy across groups, but one group's data is less representative, then you might have to degrade the model's overall accuracy. If you define fairness as equal approval rates, you might approve people who will default. There is no free lunch.

The papers in this area work on detection, measurement, and mitigation. Detection includes audits and tests that reveal whether a model discriminates. Measurement means defining fairness precisely—there are dozens of definitions, each capturing something different. Mitigation includes re-weighting training data to balance it across groups, using fairness constraints during training, applying post-hoc thresholding to balance outcomes, or using causal reasoning to understand which discrimination is justified (someone's credit score correlates with income, which is a real predictor) and which is not (same score but different approval based on race). Some papers also study explainability: if a model makes a decision about you, you should be able to understand why. A decision that is fair in aggregate might still harm specific individuals, so some work focuses on fairness for individuals. And some papers study the human side: how do people perceive and trust algorithmic decisions, and what does fairness mean to them?

How the work breaks down

Fairness research splits into five main directions. First, auditing and measurement: detecting when models discriminate and defining what fairness means mathematically. Second, debiasing large language models: stopping them from learning stereotypes and harmful patterns from training data. Third, fair decision-making under constraints: balancing accuracy with fairness guarantees in real applications. Fourth, understanding data bias at its source: tracing discrimination back to the datasets used to train models. Fifth, individual and subgroup fairness: moving beyond aggregate fairness to ensure everyone—especially underrepresented groups—gets treated justly.

Auditing and Measuring Bias in Models

Bias in machine learning is often invisible until you explicitly measure it. This group tackles the detection problem: how do you know a model is discriminating? The papers here develop audits and benchmarks to surface bias, especially in large language models and vision systems. The hard part is that bias can hide in multiple ways—sometimes a model refuses to answer certain questions, sometimes it gives different answers to the same question when you change demographic details, and sometimes it learns harmful stereotypes from its training data.

The Bias Similarity Measurement paper audits fairness across different versions of large language models (like Gemma, LLaMA, and GPT-4) by checking how each model handles demographically sensitive questions. The study reveals that instruction tuning (the process of fine-tuning a model to follow instructions well) often works by teaching models to refuse certain questions rather than genuinely fixing the underlying bias. Different model families respond differently—some models lean heavily toward abstention while others actually approach real neutrality, and the paper shows that smaller models like Gemma 3 can match larger, more expensive models' fairness. The BiasFreeBench paper is different: it builds the first standardized benchmark for testing bias mitigation strategies. Rather than hand-picking a few test cases, it systematically compares whether prompting techniques (asking the model carefully) or training-based fixes work better, tests models of different sizes, and checks whether fixes that work on one kind of bias actually work on other kinds. This matters because a debiasing method that only works for gender bias but fails for racial bias is not useful. The Person-Centric Annotations paper goes upstream to the data itself: it carefully reviewed images from a large public dataset (LAION-400M) and labeled them for demographic imbalances and harmful associations. The findings show that 60-70% of the gender bias in vision models like CLIP and Stable Diffusion actually traces back to co-occurrences in the data—for example, if the dataset has more images of women in makeup than women in engineering roles, the model learns that association.

Debiasing Language and Vision Models

Once you know a model is biased, how do you fix it? This group focuses on practical debiasing methods that work within or after a model's training. The challenge is that reducing bias often comes with a cost: you might degrade the model's accuracy, or create new problems like excessive refusal to answer questions. A good debiasing method should fix real discrimination without breaking what the model does well.

The first paper targets the internal machinery of language models by focusing on attention—the parts of a model that decide what to focus on when processing text. When a model processes a sentence, attention mechanisms (simple decision circuits that learn which words matter) can encode biases. This method simplifies the continuous precision of attention patterns into simpler buckets across multiple features, which reduces bias because simpler patterns are less likely to capture subtle stereotypes. Tests on five different models (BERT, T5, GPT-Neo, Mixtral, LLaMA) show it consistently reduces bias across multiple demographic categories while keeping downstream task accuracy within 0.4% of the original, so you get fairness without sacrificing accuracy. Bi-directional Bias Attribution takes a different angle by working directly on the hidden representations inside a model without modifying the prompts users give it. The method is model-agnostic—it works across different architectures—and shows measurable bias reduction while preserving overall performance, which means users don't need to change how they interact with the model. For vision models, two papers tackle the problem of biased attention heads and multimodal bias. Locate then Correct identifies the specific attention heads in CLIP (a vision-language model) that encode biased associations and then corrects them surgically, removing the bias directly from where it lives in the model. The results show over 50% improvement in worst-group accuracy on fairness benchmarks compared to simpler post-processing methods, because fixing the source of bias works better than trying to patch outputs afterward. Test-Time Debiasing with Probabilistic Prompts uses a probabilistic approach for vision-language models by sampling multiple prompts and combining them because different prompts can trigger different biases, so averaging over many prompts smooths out individual biases. This method improves both fairness and accuracy together in multi-class debiasing scenarios where the model needs to fairly handle many different categories at once, because the averaging reduces the extreme biases without overshooting into poor results.

Fairness Constraints in Optimization and Decision-Making

In many real applications, you need to make fast decisions—approve a loan, rank a search result, select candidates for a job. Fairness here means ensuring that your decision rule treats groups equitably even when data itself is biased. This group develops methods to build fairness constraints directly into the optimization process, so a model learns to satisfy fairness guarantees automatically rather than post-processing its outputs.

Doubly-Regressing Approach for Subgroup Fairness addresses a scalability problem: fairness is hardest to achieve when there are many protected attributes (many groups you care about) because fairness constraints multiply in complexity. The paper shows a two-stage regression technique that outperforms baselines by working with manageable subsets of the constraints at a time, especially as the number of sensitive attributes grows, while using much less computation than naive methods that try to handle everything at once. Fair Policy Aggregation takes a reinforcement learning angle: when you train a policy (a decision rule) to maximize fairness instead of just accuracy, it gets expensive because you need to run many iterations checking fairness across all groups. This paper shows how to extract fairness guarantees from standard policy optimization methods without orders of magnitude more computation, making fair RL practical and affordable. Fair Classification by Direct Intervention operates on the decision thresholds of a trained model. Even after a model is trained, you can adjust which predictions map to which decisions, because many models output a score but a human decides where to cut to make a yes-or-no decision. This paper shows how to do that adjustment to simultaneously satisfy three different fairness definitions (demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity) on real datasets like COMPAS criminal risk assessment. The trick is doing it with minimal accuracy loss and few interventions, so the model still works well while being fair. Differentiable Optimization Layers takes another approach: it builds a fairness constraint into a neural network as a differentiable layer, so fairness becomes part of the training process rather than a post-processing step, which means the model learns fairness from the start. This guarantees aggregate fairness properties and lets you send error signals backward through fairness constraints during training to update the model, because once fairness is a goal during training, the model can internalize it.

Fairness in Multimodal and Generative Models

Multimodal models (those that work with both text and images) and generative models (those that create new content) introduce new fairness challenges. When a model generates images or text, bias doesn't just affect decision-making—it creates new biased content at scale. Understanding and fixing fairness in these domains requires different tools than classification fairness.

Fair in Mind, Fair in Action? studies multimodal models that both understand images and generate descriptions (or answer visual questions). The paper uncovers a new phenomenon called the generation gap: a model might understand fair concepts correctly in text but generate biased content when creating descriptions, because understanding and generating use different model components. It also finds personality splits, where the model's behavior toward different groups differs systematically, so the model treats some groups more favorably than others. The paper maps these failure modes by identifying where unfairness appears and offers diagnostics so you can spot the specific kinds of bias you need to fix. Equalized Generative Treatment tackles fairness in generative models directly. When you generate new content (images or text), you want the quality and characteristics of what you generate to be fair across groups because bias in generated content reaches millions of users. This paper uses a min-max approach: it finds the training procedure that makes the worst-off group as well-off as possible, which guarantees no group gets treated unfairly. The experiments on both image and text generation show this consistently produces fairer outcomes than competing methods while keeping overall quality competitive, so you don't sacrifice quality for fairness. HoloFair: Unified T2I Fairness builds a comprehensive fairness evaluation toolkit for text-to-image generation and then uses it to develop Fair-GRPO, a debiasing method based on reinforcement learning from human feedback. The key problem it solves is detecting and preventing reward hacking—when an optimization method technically improves the fairness metric but does so in a way that makes the content worse (like generating blank images to avoid stereotypes)—because you need to actually improve fairness without breaking the model. The method measurably improves fairness across multiple dimensions (gender, race, occupation) while preserving high image quality because it balances the competing goals: debiasing during training, but with enough penalty on quality loss so the optimizer doesn't just degrade content to be fair.

Causal and Data-Driven Approaches to Fairness

Not all bias is equally bad. If a model approves more loans for people with higher income, that's using a real predictor. But if it approves more loans for people of a certain race, that's discrimination. The challenge is distinguishing justified associations from unfair discrimination. This group uses causal reasoning and information-theoretic tools to understand fairness at a deeper level.

Fair Conformal Classification develops a method that gives prediction sets with coverage guarantees separately for each protected group, including groups that are underrepresented in training data. It works by learning representation-based groups—representations that capture fairness properties—and achieving equalized coverage across those groups. The key contribution is making prediction sets small enough to be useful while guaranteeing they're fair. Fairness via Independence frames fairness using information theory: specifically, CS divergence, a measure of how correlated a model's output is with a protected attribute. The paper proves that CS divergence gives tighter theoretical bounds than alternative fairness measures and connects many previous fairness approaches under one consistent principle. From Gradient Volume to Shapley Fairness solves a different problem: multi-task learning, where one model does multiple things. The hard question is allocating model capacity fairly when tasks have conflicting demands. This paper uses Shapley values (a concept from game theory for fairly distributing value) to solve this, achieving top results on both supervised and reinforcement learning benchmarks where tasks demand different amounts of model resources.
