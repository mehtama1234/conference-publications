Quadratic Direct Forecast for Multi-Step Time-Series Training

Teach time-series forecasts by weighting future steps based on how tangled they are with each other and how hard they are to predict, not treating all steps equally.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When teaching a model to predict future values in a time series (like tomorrow's stock price or next week's temperature), most trainers treat every future step the same way — same weight, same importance. But real future steps are not independent: step 2 depends on step 1, step 3 leans on step 2, and so on. QDF fixes this by using a two-part weight matrix: one part accounts for how much future steps lean on each other, and the other part gives harder-to-predict steps more practice time. The model learns faster because it avoids wasting effort on steps it has already learned well, and more accurately because it spends training time where the gap between predicted and real is widest.

Zero assumption version

Treating all future steps the same loses critical structure

When you train a model to forecast multiple steps ahead, you give it examples and measure how wrong it is on each step. The most common way is mean squared error — measure the gap between predicted and real on step 1, step 2, step 3, and so on, then average all those gaps and call that the error to fix.

But this approach has two hidden problems: first, future values in a time series are not independent. What happens at step 2 is tied to step 1 — they are correlated. If you predict step 1 wrong, step 2's error is partly baked in before the model even tries. Treating them as separate mistakes misses this tangle.

Second, some steps are intrinsically harder to forecast than others. Step 1 (one day ahead) is usually easier because the immediate future is more predictable; step 10 (ten days ahead) is harder because the signal has scattered. But standard training treats them equally, wasting the model's effort on easy steps and under-training on hard ones.

In one sentence. The model weights all future steps equally, ignoring that they are tangled together and have different difficulty.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure the correlations

Look at the training data and compute how much each future step depends on the others.

Estimate step difficulty

For each step ahead, measure or estimate how hard that step is to forecast.

Build a two-layer weight matrix

One layer captures correlations between steps; the other assigns higher weight to harder steps.

Train using the matrix

When training, penalize mistakes on hard steps more; account for correlations so you do not double-count mistakes that flow from step 1 to step 2.

Update weights during training

As the model improves, re-measure difficulty and update the weights to keep focusing on weak spots, because a step that was hard at epoch 1 may become easier by epoch 10, freeing up weight for remaining hard steps.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes the training weight matrix — the list of multipliers you apply to each step's error before summing. Instead of uniform weights (all ones), it uses a matrix that accounts for correlations and difficulty.

the object it moves  =  Training weight matrix
The hidden quantity

Label autocorrelation: how tangled future steps are

measured quantity  =  Autocorrelation among future steps

In a time series, consecutive values are not independent — they feed into each other. This dependence is called autocorrelation. If you know step 1's true value, step 2 is partly determined already. If you wrongly predict step 1, your error spills into step 2.

QDF measures this tangle using the sample covariance of future values in the training data. High off-diagonal entries in this matrix mean strong dependence — steps are tightly coupled. The paper uses these off-diagonal elements as part of its weighting matrix, so mistakes on early, high-correlation steps are weighted more carefully (to avoid double-penalizing dependent steps) while errors on independent, high-difficulty steps get full weight.

Plain reading. The autocorrelation is simply: if you measure the year's daily temperatures and notice that each day is similar to the day before, that similarity is autocorrelation. When you train a model on this data, ignoring this sameness means treating each day's error as totally separate, even though they are not.
Paper reading. The paper estimates autocorrelation from the training data's covariance matrix (the pattern of how pairs of values move together) of future values and uses its off-diagonal entries to scale the weighting matrix, preventing double-penalty on correlated forecast errors.
Why it works

Why weighting by correlation and difficulty fixes the training

The mechanism has three parts. First, when you weight steps equally and some of them are highly correlated, you penalize the model twice — once for step 1's mistake and again for step 2's mistake, which was partly caused by step 1. By shrinking the off-diagonal weights where correlation is high, you reduce this double-penalty.

Second, some steps are inherently harder to forecast. For instance, predicting five steps ahead is noisier and less accurate than predicting one step ahead. By measuring this difficulty (the paper uses task-specific variances or task-specific training metrics) and boosting the weight on hard steps, you force the model to spend more training focus on weak spots. The model does not waste effort on steps it already learned well.

Third, because the paper updates these weights adaptively during training as the model improves, you get a form of curriculum: early on, hard steps get very high weight; as the model learns them, the relative difficulty may shift, and weights adjust. This keeps the training targeted on the bottleneck.

Math after the picture

The quadratic weighting equation

First in words:

Loss equals the forecast error transposed, multiplied by a weight matrix, multiplied by the error itself, where the weight matrix is built from observed step correlations and step-specific difficulties.

Then the compact form:

Loss = e^T * W * e, where W is a matrix with correlation adjustments on off-diagonals and difficulty-based weights on the main diagonal.

Every symbol:

eerror vector: [predicted_step1 - true_step1, predicted_step2 - true_step2, ..., predicted_stepN - true_stepN]
Wweight matrix: accounts for correlations between steps and the relative difficulty of each step
W_ii (diagonal)weight for step i, boosted by how hard step i is to forecast
W_ij (off-diagonal)weight for the pair (i,j), scaled down if steps i and j are highly correlated
e^T * W * equadratic form: sum of all pairwise weighted error products
Tiny worked example: three time steps, increasing difficulty
Errors: step 1 = 0.5, step 2 = 0.6, step 3 = 0.8
Correlation between steps 1 and 2: high (0.7), between 2 and 3: medium (0.4)
Difficulty (variance): step 1 = 0.1 (easy), step 2 = 0.2 (medium), step 3 = 0.4 (hard)
Weight matrix W (after correlation and difficulty adjustment):
W_11 = 0.8 (low difficulty weight, but reduced for dependence) = 0.8
W_22 = 1.6 (medium difficulty, reduced for correlation) = 1.6
W_33 = 3.2 (high difficulty, less reduced) = 3.2
W_12 = W_21 = -0.25 (negative weight to reduce double-penalty from high correlation)
Loss = [0.5, 0.6, 0.8] * W * [0.5, 0.6, 0.8]^T
= 0.5*0.8*0.5 + 0.6*1.6*0.6 + 0.8*3.2*0.8 + 2*(-0.25)*(0.5*0.6 + 0.6*0.8)
= 0.2 + 0.576 + 2.048 - 0.3 - 0.24
= 2.284
Compare to equal weights [1,1,1]: Loss = 0.25 + 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.25 (undercounts hard step 3)
QDF focuses training on hard step 3 and avoids double-penalizing correlated steps 1 and 2.

The weight matrix makes errors on hard steps cost more, while negative off-diagonal entries prevent the model from being over-penalized for mistakes that are caused by an earlier, correlated step.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Miscalculating correlations with little data. If your training dataset is small, the estimated correlations between steps will be noisy. Wrong correlations lead to wrong off-diagonal weights, which can hurt instead of help because you are shrinking or amplifying weights based on a false picture of how steps depend on each other. The paper's adaptive update helps here, because as training progresses, the actual error pattern will show whether your correlation estimate was wrong and weights will drift toward the true structure; but with tiny data, that correction takes longer or may not fully work.
Overweighting hard steps when they are hard for a bad reason. If step 5 is hard only because the data is chaotic (random noise, not a real pattern), boosting its weight does not help the model learn; it just makes the model chase noise. You need a way to distinguish inherent difficulty from noise.
The weight matrix becoming ill-conditioned. If correlations are very strong or difficulty spans a huge range, the matrix can become numerically unstable (hard to invert or use during training). This causes training to diverge or become slow.
Done right. The paper's adaptive update helps: as the model trains, if your weight estimate was wrong, the new error pattern will reveal it (because you are measuring the actual per-step losses and re-fitting the weights to the current data), and the weights shift toward what the data shows. Also, standard numerical regularization (adding a small constant to diagonal elements) prevents ill-conditioning by ensuring the weight matrix can be inverted smoothly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic time series (e.g., random walk with a weak trend) over 100 steps. Split into train and test. Train two models: (1) standard equal-weight MSE, (2) QDF with adaptive weights. Measure accuracy on each step ahead separately, especially on the hardest (farthest) steps. The demo should show QDF improving most on steps 5+ where equal-weight training fails.

improvement = (qdf_accuracy_on_hard_steps - equal_weight_accuracy_on_hard_steps) / equal_weight_accuracy_on_hard_steps

The demo makes clear: when you stop treating all future steps as equal tasks and instead account for how tangled they are and how hard each is, the model learns the real pattern instead of spreading effort evenly.
