ATOM: A Pretrained Neural Operator for Multitask Molecular Dynamics

ATOM learns to predict how molecules move by removing strict symmetry locks, using attention to decode multiple future timesteps in parallel, and training on many molecules at once instead of one at a time.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Predicting how molecules move — the positions and velocities of atoms over time — is the foundation of drug discovery and materials science. Today's best ML models enforce strict symmetry rules (to encode physics) but this slows them down, limits how they consider atoms far apart, and locks them into predicting one future step at a time. ATOM removes those locks: it uses a looser symmetry design, lets a neural network weigh all atoms and future times together, and trains on 80 different molecules at once. The result: one pretrained model that predicts atomic motion for any new molecule without retraining.

Why old MD models are stuck

Molecular dynamics (MD) means simulating how atoms move: given their starting positions and velocities, compute where they'll be 0.1 femtoseconds later, then 0.2 fs, then 0.3 fs, building a long trajectory. Classical physics (quantum mechanics) can compute these moves exactly, but it's slow — hours per femtosecond. ML models can predict the next state 1000 times faster: they skip the quantum math and use learned patterns instead, so a computer gives an answer in milliseconds instead of hours of calculation. This speed makes it practical to design new drugs or materials by testing thousands of virtual molecules.

Today's best MD models (called equivariant networks) work by baking in a symmetry rule: 'if I rotate all atoms in space, the predicted forces rotate the same way.' This is physically sound. But the cost is steep. Enforcing exact symmetry at every layer balloons the model size and slows it down. The model also can only decode one future timestep at a time, feeding each prediction back into the next step (called autoregressive rollout), which means longer predictions eat up compute.

Even worse, these models train on a single molecule with a fixed time interval (e.g., 'predict the next 0.1 fs for benzene'). To use that model on a new molecule or a longer time interval, you have to retrain from scratch. There's no way to learn a general 'rules of atomic motion' once and reuse it.

The old way locks you into one molecule, one timestep, one slow step-by-step prediction at a time. Quasi-equivariant attention: give atoms a symmetric 'head start' but then let a flexible neural network attend to any atom, decode many future states in parallel, and train on many molecules together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Lift positions and velocities

E(3)-equivariant layers embed atom positions and velocities into a feature space where symmetries are baked in by construction.

Augment with atom types

Append atomic number to each lifted position-velocity pair, creating a phase-space feature for each atom.

Heterogeneous temporal attention

A neural network processes features across all atoms (spatial) and all future timesteps (temporal), using time-aware rotation encodings.

Parallel multi-step output

Instead of predicting one step ahead and looping, decode 8 future timesteps in parallel from a single forward pass.

Pretrain on diverse molecules

Train on 80 compounds and random time intervals, so the learned rules generalize to new molecules without retraining.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

ATOM changes how atoms are represented internally and how predictions are decoded. The paper relaxes strict symmetry enforcement: instead of enforcing symmetry at every layer (expensive, inflexible), ATOM lifts positions and velocities symmetrically once, then uses a standard neural network that can attend globally without symmetry handcuffs. It also flips from sequential prediction (decode t+1, feed back in, decode t+2...) to parallel prediction: decode multiple future timesteps together in a single forward pass.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: which atoms matter most at which times

measured quantity  =  Learned attention weights across space and time

The paper does not explicitly name this, but the core hidden quantity is which atom pairs and which time intervals the model attends to most. Classical equivariant models treat all atom pairs equally (enforced by symmetry). ATOM's neural network breaks this: it learns attention weights that assign importance to different atoms at different future timesteps.

How the paper uses it: temporal attention computes these weights on the fly during forward pass. For each future timestep, the model queries 'which atoms' responses and which past states are most relevant, and weights them accordingly. The weights are never stored or explicitly tuned; they emerge from training.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

The core idea is to decouple two jobs that classical equivariant networks jam together. Job 1: respect physical symmetries (if I rotate the input, rotate the output). Job 2: flexibly attend to any atom and any timestep. Old methods enforce both at once, which balloons the model. ATOM does Job 1 once, at the input, then unleashes Job 2 everywhere else.

Here's the mechanism. Positions and velocities are inherently rotationally symmetric (they point in space). ATOM lifts them via E(3)-equivariant linear layers, which means the lifted features are symmetry-respecting by construction — like rotating a 3D object by rotating each coordinate. This happens once. Then ATOM appends atomic number (which is a single number, rotationally invariant), and passes the result to a neural network.

The neural network is not constrained by symmetry; it can learn to weight any atom pair at any time. But why does this work? Because the input features are already symmetry-aware. The model does not need to re-enforce symmetry; it can focus on learning which atoms interact strongly. This is like having a map already oriented correctly, so your navigation system can just look up interesting locations.

Parallel decoding matters too. Classical models predict t+1, feed it back in to predict t+2. Small errors compound. ATOM predicts t+1, t+2, ..., t+8 all at once via temporal attention. The neural network sees all timesteps and can route information forward more cleanly. This also parallelizes: eight predictions in one GPU pass, not eight separate passes.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

For each atom and each future timestep, apply heterogeneous temporal attention: mix learned queries over that atom with keys from all atoms' phase features, weight by scaled dot products and time-aware rotations, and sum values. Repeat for position, velocity, and combined features.

Then the compact form:

output_F = softmax( score_matrix / scaling_factor ) times value_matrix

Every symbol:

output_Fattention result for feature type F (position, velocity, or combined)
score_matrixquery dot product with keys, measuring how similar each atom is to the query
value_matrixlearned value projections of all atoms' phase features
scaling_factora constant that keeps scores in a stable range during training
softmax(·)turn raw scores into fractions that sum to 1, so weights add up
Tiny worked example: predicting nitrogen motion at t+0.2fs
Suppose at current time we have 4 atoms: N, C, O, H. We want to predict N's position at t+0.2 fs.
Query: encode N's phase features (position, velocity, type) into a list of 64 numbers (a query).
Keys: encode all 4 atoms' phase features into lists of numbers (keys), scale by time-aware rotation for lag 0.2 fs.
Scores: query · keys = [-0.8 (N self), 2.4 (N←C), 1.1 (N←O), 0.3 (N←H)] raw attention scores.
Softmax: [exp(-0.8), exp(2.4), exp(1.1), exp(0.3)] / (sum) ≈ [0.01, 0.88, 0.09, 0.02] normalized weights.
Result: the model learns N's next position is 88% influenced by C (close, bonded), 9% by O, 1% by itself, 2% by H.
Parallel: the same network computes this for t+0.1fs, t+0.3fs, ..., t+0.8fs all in one pass.

Temporal attention is the key. By rotating query and key by the time lag, the model learns to encode 'what changes over 0.1 fs vs 0.5 fs', which it could not do with fixed time encodings.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Training on noisy MD trajectories without noise injection. If the input trajectories (from simulators or experiments) contain measurement errors, the model will memorize noise instead of learning physics. ATOM injects random perturbations during training (position and velocity noise) to make it less sensitive to noise. Skip this and it overfits to junk.
Ignoring time intervals during pretraining. ATOM trains on random time lags (Δt from 0.01 to 3000 fs). If you only pretrain on one fixed interval (e.g. 0.1 fs steps), the model learns that specific interval only and fails on others. Must randomize during pretraining.
Decoding too many timesteps at once. The model predicts 8 steps (0.8 fs) in one shot. Beyond that, errors compound because later predictions depend on earlier predicted states, not true states. For 10+ fs trajectories, roll out the 8-step predictions sequentially.
Pretraining on chemically diverse data. The paper curates TG80 with 80 compounds spanning different element types, bonding patterns, and phases. Training on diverse data teaches general rules. Single-molecule pretraining teaches brittle patterns and fails to transfer.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny simulation: take a pretrained ATOM model on the TG80 dataset. Load a new molecule that was NOT in the training set (e.g., aspirin or a material compound). Start from a random initial state (atom positions and velocities). Use ATOM to predict the next 0.8 femtoseconds (8 parallel steps). Compare against ground-truth MD from a slow but accurate simulator (or a held-out test trajectory).

The demo must show two things: (1) the predicted atomic trajectories (e.g., a 3D animation of atoms moving) closely match the true physics, and (2) the speed: ATOM's prediction on a GPU takes milliseconds; the true simulator would take minutes or hours.

mean squared error in atom positions = (1/N) * sum of (predicted_position_i - true_position_i)^2 averaged over all atoms i and timesteps

The demo must show two things to be convincing. First: a new molecule unseen during training, and ATOM predicts its motion with high accuracy. Second: the wall-clock speed — GPU inference in milliseconds versus quantum simulation in hours (100-fold difference). Together these demonstrate the core idea: one trained model works across molecules and timescales without retraining.
