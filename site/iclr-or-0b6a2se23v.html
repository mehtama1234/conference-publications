Representation Tokenizer (RepTok): One Token Instead of a Grid

Instead of squeezing images into a 2D grid of compressed pieces, encode the whole image as a single list-of-numbers and generate from that.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Images are usually compressed into a grid of smaller pieces (each piece becomes a list-of-numbers) before generation—this wastes memory and is redundant. RepTok flips the approach: it represents every image as one dense continuous list-of-numbers that holds both what-the-image-shows and pixel-level detail, trained on top of pretrained visual encoders using a single flow-matching objective.

Zero assumption version

The grid bottleneck

Today's generative models compress images into a grid of many small pieces (each piece is a list-of-numbers)—like chopping a photo into a 16 by 16 jigsaw puzzle where each square becomes a learned list-of-numbers. This wastes compute and memory: neighboring squares often encode similar information, creating redundancy. The spatial grid is fixed and inflexible, even though what-the-image-shows doesn't need to be aligned to a 2D grid.

A beginner would try to train a generative model on raw images directly, but that's too noisy and high-dimensional. The standard fix is to compress first (using a learned encoder—a machine that converts an image to a list-of-numbers), then generate in the compressed space. But you still end up with that spatial grid, and you pay the price: 256 or 1024 pieces per image, when much of the information overlaps across neighbor squares.

The problem in one sentence. Standard compressed spaces for generation use many spatial pieces that contain redundancy; RepTok replaces this grid with one dense continuous list-of-numbers.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with pretrained self-supervised encoder (trained on unlabeled images)

Use a self-supervised vision transformer (a neural network trained to understand images) already trained.

Extract single list-of-numbers per image

Instead of the encoder's full output, keep just one learnable continuous list-of-numbers that represents the entire image.

Fine-tune the list-of-numbers, not the encoder

Only the single list-of-numbers is updated; the encoder stays frozen. This is cheap.

Add a direction-similarity brake

Ensure the adapted list-of-numbers stays close in direction to the pretrained one so generation works well.

Train decoder with flow matching

Decoder learns to convert the list-of-numbers back to an image using standard diffusion-like training.

What the paper changes

The one thing that moves

RepTok changes the compressed space from a 2D grid of many small pieces to one single continuous list-of-numbers. This holds the entire image in one dense list instead of many scattered pieces.

the object it moves  =  shape of compressed space
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how far the list-of-numbers drifts

measured quantity  =  Movement away from pretrained starting point

Drift is how much the single list-of-numbers changes from its original value in the pretrained encoder. The self-supervised encoder (trained on unlabeled images before this work) has learned a smooth, well-organized space where similar images sit nearby. When you fine-tune the list-of-numbers to also reconstruct pixels, it must drift—move away from the original starting point—to gain the detail it needs. But drift too far and the list-of-numbers lands in empty space where generation breaks.

The paper measures drift using direction-similarity (how aligned two lists-of-numbers point in the same direction). A high alignment means the list-of-numbers stayed close to the pretrained path; a low alignment means it wandered. The direction-similarity brake is what limits drift to a safe zone.

Why it works

Why one list works: two forces in balance

The pretrained self-supervised encoder (trained on unlabeled images before this work) has already organized a high-quality space: images of similar objects sit close together in this space, learned by pushing similar images closer and pulling different ones apart. But this space is optimized for classification or retrieval, not for reconstruction—it doesn't preserve the pixel-level details needed for generation.

The mechanism is two competing forces. The decoder pulls the list-of-numbers toward pixel-perfect reconstruction by measuring reconstruction error (how wrong the decoded image is). The direction-similarity brake pulls the list-of-numbers back toward the pretrained starting point by penalizing large direction-changes. The list-of-numbers finds a sweet spot in the middle: close enough to the pretrained space that generation is stable and preserves what-the-image-shows, but enriched enough that pixels decode faithfully. A single list-of-numbers replaces a 16 by 16 or larger grid, so the decoder has less to process and trains faster.

The adaptation equation

First in words:

Total loss = flow-matching decoder loss + lambda times (1 minus direction-similarity)

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_flow(decoder(z_adapted), x) + lambda * (1 - cos_sim(z_adapted, z_ssl))

Every symbol:

L_flowreconstruction loss from the decoder (penalizes wrong pixels)
decoder(z_adapted)reconstructed image from the adapted list-of-numbers
xtarget ground-truth image pixels
z_adaptedthe fine-tuned single continuous list-of-numbers
z_sslthe original frozen self-supervised encoder output
cos_simdirection-similarity: a score from 0 to 1 measuring how aligned two lists point (1 = same direction, 0 = opposite)
lambdaweight controlling how much to penalize drift from pretrained space (higher lambda = less drift allowed)
A tiny worked example
Suppose z_ssl=[0.5, 0.3, 0.7] (frozen output) and z_adapted=[0.51, 0.32, 0.69] (after fine-tuning).
Direction-similarity: dot product=0.5*0.51+0.3*0.32+0.7*0.69=0.255+0.096+0.483=0.834; magnitude of z_ssl≈0.947, magnitude of z_adapted≈0.961, so direction-similarity≈0.834/(0.947*0.961)≈0.915.
If L_flow=0.05 (reconstruction is good) and lambda=1.0, then L_total=0.05+1.0*(1-0.915)=0.05+0.085=0.135.
If z_adapted drifted further, say direction-similarity dropped to 0.8, then L_total=0.05+1.0*0.2=0.25. The brake penalizes drift, keeping the list anchored in pretrained space while reconstruction improves.

The flow-matching loss pulls toward pixels; the direction-similarity term pulls toward pretrained space. The single list balances both by tuning lambda—the trade-off knob.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda too high (movement lockdown). If the direction-similarity weight is too strong, the list-of-numbers cannot move far enough to absorb reconstruction detail. It stays stuck in the pretrained space and generation quality degrades—fine detail is lost because the list refuses to drift.
Lambda too low (meaning collapses). If the brake is weak, the list-of-numbers drifts far from the pretrained starting point. Reconstruction may improve, but the meaning erodes. Generation becomes unstable or produces incoherent images because the list-of-numbers no longer sits in the smooth space the pretrained model already learned.
Frozen encoder mismatch. The self-supervised encoder (trained on unlabeled images before this work) is pretrained on a different dataset or task. If the pretrain-adapt mismatch is large, the space may not align well with the target generation task. RepTok's speed then buys nothing—generation still fails.
Done right: lambda is tuned to balance fidelity and smoothness. Search for the lambda value that keeps reconstruction-loss and direction-similarity-loss in proportion. RepTok then delivers high-fidelity generation in a fraction of the training time.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy example: take a pretrained vision transformer (a neural network trained to understand images), freeze it, and fine-tune a single 768-number list on CIFAR-10 images (a standard image dataset with 60,000 small photos). Train a small diffusion decoder (2-3 layers) to convert the list back to pixels. Measure three things: (1) reconstruction error (how wrong the decoded pixels are), (2) direction-similarity of the adapted list to the frozen pretrained outputs (how far the list drifted), (3) training wall-clock time. Compare to a baseline that uses a small 4 by 4 grid of pieces instead. Show that the single list hits 95% of the baseline's quality with 75% less training time and 80% less graphics memory.

efficiency_gain = (grid_training_time / token_training_time) * (grid_memory / token_memory) / (grid_quality / token_quality)

The demo must show that one list is not just mathematically simple—it is genuinely faster and cheaper to train while keeping fidelity intact. The trick is that the self-supervised encoder has already done the hard work; RepTok simply adapts and uses that existing organization.
