ELLMob: Event-Driven Human Mobility Generation

When a typhoon hits, LLM-based trajectory prediction fails because it balances two competing reasons—habit versus event constraint—without naming them; this paper extracts and aligns both.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Large language models (machine learning systems trained on enormous amounts of text) can predict where people usually go, reproducing their daily routines. But they fail when a major event happens: a typhoon, a pandemic, an Olympic Games. The problem is not that the model lacks raw ability; it is that the model cannot hold two competing ideas at once—the person's normal habit and the event's hard constraint—and resolve which one matters when.

Zero assumption version

Why routine-only models fail under events

A human has habits. On Tuesday, they take the same route to work. On Friday evening, they visit the same coffee shop. An LLM trained to predict trajectories (sequences of locations a person visits) learns these habits well by reading a lot of real movement logs. The model sees the pattern and reproduces it.

But then a typhoon hits. The roads flood. The person cannot visit their usual route. They must take a detour. Or they stay home. The model has never been trained to handle competing reasons—the pull of habit and the push of the event. A naive approach would be to pile more event data into training, but the paper shows that simply does not work. The model still defaults to habit, even when the event makes it impossible, because it has seen the routine thousands of times and the event-response only in a handful of examples; probability pulls it back to the stronger signal.

The core gap: the model has no way to name and resolve the tension. It does not extract the reason for habit (safety, familiarity, time saved) or the reason for the event constraint (flooded roads, lockdown). It just guesses.

In one sentence. Extract the two competing reasons—habitual pattern and event constraint—then iteratively align them to choose trajectories that satisfy both.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Extract habitual pattern

Read movement logs to infer the person's usual route, timing, preferences.

Extract event constraints

From the event (typhoon, lockdown, Olympics), infer what routes, locations, or times are now forbidden or risky.

Name competing rationales

Write out two reasons in plain words: why the habit works, and what the event blocks. Based on Fuzzy Trace Theory (a psychology principle: people think in reasons, not raw details).

Iterative alignment loop

Repeatedly prompt the LLM: 'Given habit-reason X and event-constraint Y, generate a trajectory that honors both.' Refine until both are satisfied.

Measure fitness

Check: does the output stay true to the person's usual style and still obey event limits? Compare to baselines.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper does not change the LLM itself or retrain it. Instead, it changes the input prompt (the instructions given to the model). Instead of asking the LLM 'generate a trajectory for this person,' it asks 'here is why this person has their habit, here is what the event forbids, generate a trajectory honoring both.' This prompt is richer, with explicit competing reasons side by side.

The hidden quantity

The competing-reason score

measured quantity  =  Degree of reason alignment

The paper measures how well the generated trajectory honors both the habit-reason and the event-constraint. This is not a single number, but a boolean check during generation: does this trajectory preserve the habit's core logic (time saved, familiar territory) and also avoid the event's forbidden states (flooded roads, locked zones)?

The paper infers this by asking the LLM itself to score the candidates. It runs the model multiple times with the explicit competing rationales, and keeps only the trajectories where the LLM reports that both reasons are satisfied. This is called self-aligned because the LLM provides the alignment signal.

Why it works

How explicit reasons defeat the habit-only model

The root cause of failure is that when you ask an LLM to predict a trajectory during an event without naming why the event matters, the model reverts to pattern-matching: it has seen the habit thousands of times and the event-disrupted version only a handful. Probability favors the habit.

ELLMob breaks this by making the conflict explicit. It prompts the LLM to write out the reason for the habit ('person values speed; this route is fastest'), then write out the reason for the event constraint ('typhoon closes this road'). Now the LLM is no longer choosing between a strong pattern and a weak one. It is being asked to pick a trajectory that satisfies two stated logical conditions. This is a different type of problem: constraint satisfaction, not pattern matching.

The iterative alignment ensures convergence. If the LLM proposes a trajectory that violates the habit-reason (too slow, wrong area), the loop catches it. If it violates the event constraint (goes through a flooded zone), the loop catches it. Only trajectories that pass both checks survive. This filtering process outperforms baselines because a single prompt asking for both constraints at once forces the LLM to guess and balance them on its own, a harder task; by enforcing them step by step—first generate, then score, then filter—the method breaks a hard problem into simple boolean checks, which the LLM handles better.

Math after the picture

Scoring trajectory fitness

First in words:

For each candidate trajectory, compute a combined score that weighs how well it preserves the habit-reason and how fully it avoids the event-constraint. Keep only trajectories where both terms are high.

Then the compact form:

fitness(traj) = habit_score(traj) * event_score(traj)

Every symbol:

fitness(traj)Overall suitability of the trajectory; ranges 0 to 1
habit_score(traj)How well the trajectory matches the person's usual style; 1 = perfectly typical, 0 = very odd
event_score(traj)How fully the trajectory avoids forbidden locations and times; 1 = fully compliant, 0 = fully blocked
*Both scores must be high; if either is low, the whole fitness is low
A tiny worked example
Candidate A: usual route, goes through flooded zone. habit_score=0.95, event_score=0.1. fitness = 0.95 × 0.1 = 0.095. REJECTED.
Candidate B: detour route, avoids event, but takes twice as long. habit_score=0.70, event_score=0.95. fitness = 0.70 × 0.95 = 0.665. MARGINAL.
Candidate C: known alternate route, avoids event, preserves time roughly. habit_score=0.90, event_score=0.98. fitness = 0.90 × 0.98 = 0.882. ACCEPTED.
Only Candidate C balances both reasons; the others fail on one dimension.

Multiplying the two scores ensures that a trajectory cannot pass by compromising heavily on one side. Both habit and event must be honored.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Extraction fails to name the real reason. If the method labels the habit-reason as purely 'work commute' instead of 'speed and safety,' then the alignment will not catch a trajectory that is fast but passes through a dangerous zone. The reason extraction must be precise.
Event constraint is ambiguous. If the event description is vague ('avoid downtown'), the LLM may interpret it differently in each iteration, and the self-alignment loop may fail to converge or accept contradictory trajectories.
Iterative alignment does not terminate. If the habit-reason and event-constraint are truly incompatible (person must reach an office that is completely inaccessible), the loop may run indefinitely without finding a trajectory that satisfies both.
Done right. When habit-reasons and event-constraints are clearly stated and genuinely compatible, the self-alignment loop converges quickly and produces trajectories that are both plausible and obedient to the event.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny mobility dataset with 10 people, each with 50 usual trajectories during normal times. Then simulate a single event (a neighborhood lockdown that forbids certain routes). Generate 20 candidate trajectories for each person. Compare: (1) a naive LLM that knows the person's routine but not the event, (2) a baseline that is trained on pre-event data only, and (3) ELLMob with explicit reason extraction and alignment.

accuracy_during_event = (trajectories that respect both habit-reason and event-constraint) / (total generated)

The demo must make impossible to miss that explicit reason extraction and alignment lets the model handle events it has never encountered before, by breaking the habit-only deadlock into two solvable logical constraints.
