Train Your Advisor: Steer Black-Box LLMs with Trained Advice

Train a small open model to generate custom advice that steers a frozen black-box language model to answer harder questions and solve tasks more efficiently.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Frontier language models like GPT-5 are locked: you cannot modify their weights, only send prompts and read answers. This paper shows how to train a small, open-weight helper model (an Advisor) to generate smart, task-specific advice that the black-box model can follow to do its job better, because specific, targeted hints help models focus on the right parts of a problem — improving accuracy by up to 27% and cutting the steps needed to solve software-engineering tasks by a quarter.

Zero assumption version

The black-box wall: you can only ask, never teach

Frontier language models are deployed as black-box services. You send a question, you get an answer. But you cannot change their weights, and you cannot retrain them. If the model makes mistakes on your specific task — missing rules, taking inefficient paths, not following your preferences — you have almost no way to fix it. Static prompts like 'be careful' or 'follow the rules' help a little, but they are the same for every question, so they miss the specific advice each question needs.

A naive approach: write a big clever prompt that covers every edge case. But this is manual, fragile, and does not learn. The paper's question: can we train a small model to learn what advice each question needs, and use that advice to steer the black-box model?

The core idea. Train a lightweight, open-weight Advisor model to generate dynamic, per-question advice that the black-box frontier model reads and follows, because task-specific guidance helps the model align its internal reasoning to the real problem at hand, improving both accuracy and efficiency.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Set up the student and advisor

Pick a small open model as the Advisor (the trainable part, because you need weights you can modify) and a frozen black-box model as the Student (the model you want to help).

Step 2: Feed the Advisor a question

The Advisor reads the task (e.g., 'solve this coding problem') and generates natural-language advice (e.g., 'First check edge cases, then write the core logic').

Step 3: Student uses the advice

The black-box Student reads the question and the Advisor's advice together, then generates an answer or tries a step in a multi-step task.

Step 4: Measure success

Check if the answer is correct (or if the task got closer to done). Assign a reward: higher if correct and efficient, lower if wrong or slow.

Step 5: Advisor learns

Update the Advisor's weights using reinforcement learning so it learns to generate advice that leads to higher rewards.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the Advisor's weights

The paper trains the weights of a small open-weight language model. This Advisor model starts untrained (generating vague, unhelpful advice like 'be thoughtful'). During training, its weights shift slightly so it learns to read each question and suggest exactly the right concrete guidance to help the black-box Student succeed.

the object it moves  =  the Advisor's weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: advice relevance and clarity

measured quantity  =  Advice quality signal

The paper does not measure advice quality directly. Instead it measures the effect, because the real test is whether the Student benefits: does the black-box Student's answer get better after reading the Advisor's advice? The hidden quantity is the Advisor's learned ability to predict, for each question, what kind of hint will nudge the Student toward a correct and efficient answer.

The Advisor does not have a numerical score for each piece of advice. Instead, reinforcement learning compares outcomes: questions where the Advisor's advice led to a correct answer or fewer wasted steps get a high reward signal, and the Advisor's weights adjust to produce similar advice in similar situations. Over time, the Advisor's internal representations (the weights) encode patterns like 'Rule-heavy questions need rule-checking advice' or 'Efficiency matters in SWE tasks, so suggest shortcutting'.

Why it works

Why it works: the steering loop

The black-box Student model has learned patterns from massive training. It knows how to solve many problems, but for each new task it needs a nudge — a hint that narrows its focus to what matters most. Generic prompts like 'be careful' are not specific enough; they do not pinpoint the exact issue. The Advisor learns to read the question (e.g., 'apply tax rules to this income') and generate a targeted tip (e.g., 'Check if the income crosses a bracket threshold first'). The Student, seeing this specific advice, adjusts its reasoning and produces a better answer, because a concrete, actionable hint lets the model skip over dead-end paths and focus its computation on the right reasoning.

Over training cycles, the Advisor's weights shift so it increasingly generates advice that the Student can follow to succeed. The feedback is closed-loop: if an Advisor suggestion leads to a correct answer, that suggestion's pattern gets reinforced in the Advisor's weights, because reinforcement learning updates weights to repeat successful patterns. If a suggestion fails, the Advisor learns not to repeat it. After training on hundreds of examples, the Advisor has internalized a library of 'what kind of hint works for what kind of question.' The key point: the frontier model's weights never change; only the Advisor is trained. This means the method is practical: you train once on a small student model (cheap) and then plug the trained Advisor in front of any frontier model to improve it, because the Advisor learns a general reasoning-aid strategy that transfers across models.

The learning equation

First in words:

The Advisor is trained with a reinforcement learning loss that combines correctness and efficiency. For each question, the Advisor generates advice, the Student produces an answer, and the training updates the Advisor's weights to maximize the reward (correctness plus efficiency bonus).

Then the compact form:

Loss = -E[R_correct + lambda * R_efficiency] where R_correct = 1 if answer is correct else 0, and R_efficiency = max_steps - actual_steps

Every symbol:

R_correctreward if the Student's answer is correct (1 or 0)
R_efficiencyreward based on how few steps the Student took to solve it
max_stepsthe maximum allowed steps for a task
actual_stepsthe number of steps the Student actually took
lambdahow much to weight efficiency versus correctness
E[·]the average over many training questions
Lossthe value the Advisor's weights are adjusted to minimize
A tiny worked example
Question 1: A tax rule problem with correct answer and 8 steps taken (max 15).
R_correct = 1, R_efficiency = 15 - 8 = 7, lambda = 1.
Total reward = 1 + 1 * 7 = 8. This advice was good; reinforce it.
Question 2: Same tax problem, student took 25 steps (too many, got distracted).
R_correct = 1, R_efficiency = 15 - 25 = -10, lambda = 1.
Total reward = 1 + 1 * (-10) = -9. This advice was inefficient; reduce its strength.
After many examples, the Advisor learns: 'tax problems need a 'check the threshold first' hint to avoid long searches.'
The frontier model (Student), given this trained advice, takes 8 steps instead of 25.

The Advisor maximizes a reward that counts both correctness and efficiency, so it learns to generate advice that makes the Student both more accurate and faster.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Advisor gives task-specific advice that breaks on other tasks. If you train the Advisor only on tax problems, the advice becomes too specialized ('always check the bracket threshold'). On coding problems, it will still output tax-specific hints, which confuse the Student. The paper mitigates this by checking that trained Advisors do not hurt performance on untrained benchmarks — but users should not expect an Advisor trained on one domain to magically help on completely different tasks.
The Advisor generates grammatically correct but nonsensical advice. Language models can produce fluent text that sounds authoritative but is wrong. An untrained Advisor often does this (that is why untrained Advisors sometimes hurt). Training filters out bad advice via the reward signal, but if the reward function itself is poorly designed, the Advisor can converge to giving advice that sounds good but misleads the Student.
Transfer fails if the Student model is too different. An Advisor trained with a small student model (e.g., Qwen 7B) may not transfer perfectly to a very different frontier model (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro). The paper shows transfer works in practice, but it assumes the two models operate on similar reasoning patterns. If the frontier model is fundamentally different in capability or style, the advice may not land.
Done right. Train the Advisor on a diverse set of examples from your target domain. Measure its performance on a held-out test set from the same domain. Before deploying to a frontier model, verify that the Advisor does not hurt performance on other benchmarks. Use a reward function that balances correctness and efficiency. Train for enough epochs (the paper uses 20) to let patterns stabilize.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy example: pick a simple domain (e.g., math word problems or logic puzzles). Train a small Advisor model on 50–100 examples where you label each question with 'this solved correctly in N steps' or 'this failed.' Run the Student alone (a frozen pre-trained model or a second copy of the same model) and measure accuracy and average steps. Then run the Student with the trained Advisor's advice prepended to each question. The demo should show two things: (1) accuracy improves (the Student gets more questions right, because the hint steered it away from mistakes), and (2) when measuring step-count, the Student solves problems more efficiently (fewer wasted tries, because it avoided wrong branches).

improvement_accuracy = accuracy_with_advisor - accuracy_without_advisor; improvement_efficiency = (avg_steps_without) - (avg_steps_with)

A good demo makes it impossible to miss: the same black-box model produces better answers faster when an Advisor reads the question first and gives it a hint, because the advice steers the model's reasoning toward the right solution path.
