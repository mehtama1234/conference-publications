PGC: Peak-Guided Calibration for AI-Generated Image Detection

Detect AI-generated images by finding local clues that gave them away, then using those clues to correct the global verdict.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Modern AI image generators put all their effort into making the main subject (the person, the object) look perfect, which means they skimp on the background and small details. That makes the image look real at first glance. A detector trained to spot AI-made images can get fooled because the realistic main subject drowns out the tiny flaws that actually reveal the image is fake. PGC finds those hidden clues by looking at what the generator struggled with, then uses those clues to override the false confidence.

Zero assumption version

The generator's dirty secret is hidden in plain sight

AI image generators like Stable Diffusion spend their computing budget wisely: they make the main subject (the person's face, the dog) as photorealistic as possible. The catch is that they have less computing left for the background, the sky, the grass. So the background ends up with visible artifacts — pixelation, impossible reflections, unnatural color edges — the telltale signs of a fake.

A human eye catches this instantly. But a detector trained to spot fakes by looking at the whole image at once gets fooled. The main subject is so perfect it drowns out the small flaws. The detector sees a hyper-realistic face and says 'real', even though the background is falling apart. This is the core problem: the very thing that makes the image look real (the perfect subject) acts like a wall that hides the very thing that exposes the image is fake (the broken background).

A naive solution would be to train a detector to look only at the background. That breaks too: some real images have boring backgrounds, and some fakes manage to polish the whole image if they use more computing.

In one sentence. The detector trusts the perfect subject and misses the flawed background.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Scan the image twice

One pass looks at fine spatial details (upsampling artifacts, pixel boundaries). Another pass uses a pretrained vision model (DINOv2) to spot semantic anomalies.

Step 2: Find the anomaly peaks

Identify the regions with the highest anomaly scores — the background patches where the generator visibly cut corners.

Step 3: Extract what the peaks know

Gather the learned features from just those peak regions, ignoring the rest of the image.

Step 4: Calibrate the global vote

Blend the peak features with the whole-image features, giving the peaks extra weight because they carry the real signal.

Step 5: Make the call

The adjusted evidence makes the detector say real or fake with the background clues now audible.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

PGC changes how the detector weighs different parts of an image. Instead of treating every pixel equally, it finds regions where anomalies are most severe and treats those regions as if they were spotlights on the truth. The object being moved is the aggregation strategy: from uniform averaging to peak-focused weighting.

the object it moves  =  aggregation strategy
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Anomaly concentration

measured quantity  =  Anomaly peak strength

Anomaly peak strength is the magnitude of the largest anomaly clues in a single region of the image. It measures where the generator's budget ran out most visibly. The paper estimates it by running spatial (CNN-based) and semantic (vision-model-based) feature extractors across the entire image, computing an anomaly score for each local patch, and identifying the patches with the top scores.

Why this matters: if the anomaly peaks are strong, they tell a clear story. If they are weak, the image might be an edge case (a real image with strange lighting, or a very high-quality fake). By measuring peak strength, the detector knows how confident to be when it overrides the global verdict.

Why it works

Why it works: the attention trade-off

The mechanism hinges on a hard truth about how AI image generators work: every byte of computing power spent on the subject's face is a byte not spent on the background. The generator has a fixed budget of transformations and neural-network layers. If it routes 80% of that budget into making the face photorealistic, only 20% remains for the background.

Real images are made by cameras that see everything equally. A real image's pixels all have consistent noise patterns, consistent grain, consistent color transitions from light to shadow. An AI-generated image will have high-fidelity subject pixels mixed with low-fidelity background pixels — a visible seam.

PGC exploits this by amplifying the voice of the low-fidelity regions. When the global detector (averaging the whole image) is fooled by the perfect subject, the local peaks shout the truth: the background does not match the subject's quality level. By forcing the detector to listen to those peaks, PGC makes it impossible for high-quality fakes to hide their resource allocation.

Math after the picture

The calibration equation

First in words:

Final score equals global score plus weight times peak anomaly strength, passed through sigmoid to get a probability between zero and one.

Then the compact form:

pred = sigmoid(global_score + lambda * peak_anomaly_strength)

Every symbol:

global_scorethe whole-image verdict from the baseline detector
peak_anomaly_strengththe maximum anomaly magnitude found in any local region
lambdahow much to trust the peaks versus the global opinion
sigmoid(x)a function that squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, representing a probability
A tiny worked example
Image A: global_score = 0.9 (looks real), peak_anomaly = 0.1 (no red flags).
pred = sigmoid(0.9 + 0.5 * 0.1) = sigmoid(0.95) ≈ 0.72 (still looks real).
Image B: global_score = 0.8 (looks fairly real), peak_anomaly = 1.5 (strong red flags).
pred = sigmoid(0.8 + 0.5 * 1.5) = sigmoid(1.55) ≈ 0.82 (now tilted toward fake).
Same global score, but the peaks change the verdict by making the detector listen.

Sigmoid turns the raw score into a probability; peak anomaly acts as a calibration lever that overrides an overconfident global verdict.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Real images with broken backgrounds. A real photograph taken in low light, or with motion blur, or with shadow artifacts might have regions that score high on anomaly. PGC could misclassify it as fake. The detector has to learn to distinguish between unnatural low-quality artifacts (made by a stingy generator) and natural variations (made by camera noise and lighting).
Fakes that polish everything equally. If a generator uses enough computing to make both subject and background look pristine, there will be no clear anomaly peaks. PGC degrades gracefully back to the global detector's verdict, which might still be fooled.
Adversarial perturbations. An attacker could deliberately craft anomalies in the peaks to trick the detector into saying fake when it is real, or real when it is fake. The paper shows PGC resists black-box attacks where an attacker does not have access to the model, but targeted white-box attacks where an attacker can see inside the model are not tested.
Done right. PGC works well across different generator types (GANs, diffusion models, commercial tools) because the underlying principle is fundamental: a computing budget bottleneck creates visible trade-offs. PGC works because it exploits a law of physics — no system can allocate infinite budget — not a quirk of one algorithm.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo would collect 100 images: 50 real photographs and 50 AI-generated. For each image, compute the global anomaly score (whole image), the peak anomaly score (worst region), and the PGC-calibrated score. Plot three curves: global accuracy, peak accuracy, and PGC accuracy. The demo should show that on commercial fakes (high-fidelity but uneven), PGC jumps ahead of both baselines.
