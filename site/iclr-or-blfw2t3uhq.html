Grounding or Guessing? Visual Signals for Detecting Hallucinations in Sign Language Translation

Measure how much a translation model is actually watching the video versus making up words from language patterns alone.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Sign language translation converts continuous hand and body movements into written sentences. Models that skip an intermediate step (called glosses—brief manual annotations of hand position and motion) often hallucinate: they produce smooth, correct-sounding sentences with no support from the video. This paper detects those hallucinations by measuring, for each word, whether the model looked at the video or relied on language habits.

Zero assumption version

Models watching the wrong thing

A sign language model translates video of people signing into written text. The challenge: meaning lives in tiny continuous movements—hand position, angle, motion speed. A naive approach maps these video frames directly to words, skipping any intermediate human-written labels.

The problem: the model learns to guess. During training, it sees thousands of videos paired with correct translations. It finds two ways to predict the next word: (1) look carefully at the video, or (2) notice that after certain contexts, particular words almost always follow. The second path is easier and faster because it relies on patterns seen thousands of times before (certain phrases recur in any language), requiring less computation than truly watching continuous hand motion. So the model gravitates toward it over time, because during training the learning process rewards whatever reduces prediction error most efficiently.

Result: the model generates fluent, grammatical sentences that sound right but are completely unsupported by what the hands are actually doing. These are hallucinations. You can spot them only if you know sign language and watch the original video.

In one sentence. We measure how much the model actually used the video (versus guessed from language patterns) by watching how its internal thinking processes change and comparing its behavior when the video is hidden.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Mask the video

Hide the video input and run the model. Store the internal states (decoder hidden layer and how the model focuses on video) at each word position.

Compare to normal

Run the model with the real video. Measure how much the internal states changed between normal and masked.

Score each word

For each word, combine two signals: (1) how much the hidden state drifted, and (2) how much focus moved to video positions.

Fuse into a reliability score

Blend the two signals with learned weights and a sigmoid (squashing) function to produce a 0–1 score per word.

Rank the sentence

Aggregate word scores into a sentence-level hallucination risk: high average reliability means the translation is grounded; low means it is guessing.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper builds a two-channel reliability scorer that runs as the model translates. For every word the model predicts, the scorer emits a confidence value between 0 and 1. That is the new thing: a number that summarizes how much the model was watching the video, not just whether the word is in the vocabulary.

the object it moves  =  Reliability score per word
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: visual dependence

measured quantity  =  Visual dependence (how much the model uses video vs. learned language patterns)

You cannot see inside the model. But the paper measures a quantity that reveals it: visual dependence. High visual dependence means the model is grounded—it is genuinely looking at the continuous hand movements. Low visual dependence means it is drifting to language habits.

The paper estimates visual dependence in two ways. First, feature-based sensitivity: if the model's internal hidden states (the learned representations it uses to think) change noticeably when you remove the video, then those states were depending on video. Second, counterfactual signals: compare the attention weights (which parts of the video the model is looking at) in the normal case versus when video is hidden or scrambled. A big drop in attention to video positions means that attention was genuinely looking at video, not just noise.

Why it works

Why it works: the two signals reveal genuine video use

Models that skip glosses learn to do two things at once: (1) watch the video and map hand positions to concepts, and (2) predict the next word from those concepts. But they also learn a shortcut: (3) predict the word from the language context alone, bypassing the video entirely. The model uses the shortcut when it is easier than genuine visual grounding.

The paper's two signals catch this. Feature-based sensitivity works because if the model truly needed the video to compute the hidden state at a particular word, then removing the video will make that hidden state noticeably different. It is like asking: if I blind the model right before it predicts this word, does its internal thinking change? If yes, it was depending on vision. If no, it was not.

Focus signals work because the model has a specific mechanism for looking at the video: in each decoder layer, a focus head computes how much to focus on each video frame. If a word is grounded, this focus will point to the video regions relevant to that word. If the word is guessed, the focus will be noisy or dispersed. By comparing focus under normal and masked conditions, we see which focus genuinely looked at the video versus which was just averaging over the whole video.

Gloss-based models (which get explicit hand-position labels as intermediate supervision) learn the visual path early and stick with it. Gloss-free models lack that guidance, so they wander toward the language shortcut more often. That is why the reliability score is more predictive in gloss-free models: in those models, the difference between grounded and guessed words is starker.

Math after the picture

The scoring equation

First in words:

Reliability score for word at step t equals sigmoid of (weight for sensitivity times sensitivity signal plus weight for attention times attention signal).

Then the compact form:

r_t = sigmoid(w_s * S_t + w_a * A_t)

Every symbol:

r_treliability score for word t, ranges 0 to 1
S_tfeature-based sensitivity: how much the hidden state changed when video was masked
A_tattention signal: how much the model focused on video positions decreased when video was hidden
w_slearned weight for the sensitivity signal
w_alearned weight for the attention signal
sigmoidsquashing function that converts any input into a number between 0 and 1
A tiny worked example
Suppose w_s = 0.6 and w_a = 0.4 (learned from data).
For word t=5, sensitivity signal S_5 = 0.8 (state changed a lot when video was hidden), attention signal A_5 = 0.7 (attention to video shrank a lot).
Combine: 0.6 * 0.8 + 0.4 * 0.7 = 0.48 + 0.28 = 0.76.
Apply sigmoid: sigmoid(0.76) ≈ 0.68.
Interpretation: this token has a 68% reliability score—fairly confident it is grounded in the video.
Compare to word t=6 with S_6 = 0.2 (state hardly changed), A_6 = 0.15 (attention did not move).
Combine: 0.6 * 0.2 + 0.4 * 0.15 = 0.12 + 0.06 = 0.18.
Apply sigmoid: sigmoid(0.18) ≈ 0.54.
Interpretation: this token has a 54% reliability score—weak grounding, likely a guess.

The two signals are blended with learned weights and squeezed through sigmoid to produce a probability-like score between 0 and 1.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gloss-based versus gloss-free transfer. A reliability scorer trained on gloss-based models (which get explicit hand-position labels) will not transfer perfectly to gloss-free models. This happens because gloss-free models tokenize at the subword level (breaking words into smaller pieces), while gloss-based models work word-by-word. The paper shows this transfer loss is smaller when going from gloss-free to gloss-free, because newer gloss-free models use the same tokenization.
Visual degradation confusion. If the input video is noisy or blurry (poor lighting, motion blur, partial occlusion), the model may low-score legitimate words because the video evidence is genuinely weak, not because the model is guessing. Reliability detects that the model is not using the video, but does not tell you why.
Learned language patterns can mimic grounding. Some words are so predictable from language context alone that the model will generate them whether or not the video supports them. For such words, the reliability score may remain high even if the model is guessing, because the language signal is so strong that it dominates both internal hidden states and focus.
Done right. Combine visual grounding signals with language-based signals (confidence, perplexity, entropy) to catch both types of hallucination: videos that the model ignores, and learned language patterns that are unsupported by video.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a sign language translation model (gloss-free, T5 or mBART backbone) on a small benchmark (PHOENIX-2014T or CSL-Daily). Train the reliability scorer on your own data (or use a pretrained one). Take a test sentence. For each word in the translation, compute the sensitivity signal (run the model with and without video, measure hidden state drift) and the focus signal (compare how much the model focuses on video under clean versus masked video). Combine into a reliability score. Identify the low-reliability words. Manually inspect the original video for those words: do they lack visual support? Compare low-reliability words to high-reliability words to show the difference in grounding.

hallucination_detection_rate = fraction_of_low_reliability_words_that_lack_visual_support / total_low_reliability_words

The demo must show that low-reliability words cluster in the translations that hallucinate, and high-reliability words cluster in correct translations.
