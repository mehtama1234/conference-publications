From Prior to Pro: Efficient Skill Mastery via Distribution Contractive RL Finetuning

Start with a broad pretrained robot policy and use focused reward feedback to squeeze it down into a high-skill specialized policy, all without forgetting the broad base.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot learning often starts with a pretrained policy trained on many diverse tasks, giving it broad behavioral coverage but lack of refinement for complex specific skills. This paper shows how to take that broad pretrained policy and squeeze it down into a specialized expert by adding a lightweight correction layer on top, trained with reward-based learning (called reinforcement learning) that amplifies high-success behaviors while staying close to what the pretrained policy already knows.

Zero assumption version

Broad Training Leaves Skills Rough

When you train a robot policy on many different tasks at once (like grasping a cup, moving a block, pushing an object), the policy learns to do all of them reasonably well. But reasonable is not expert. The policy is spread thin across all these tasks, so on any one hard skill it is not sharp enough.

A beginner's approach is to throw away that broad policy and retrain from scratch on just the one hard skill. But that fails: you waste all the diverse skills you already learned, and your robot now forgets how to do the easier tasks while struggling to learn the harder one from pure trial and error. Online robot learning is expensive—every failed attempt costs time and wear, so starting from zero is wasteful.

In one sentence. Keep the broad learned policy, but surgically sharpen it on one hard skill by layering a correction module on top, trained with rewards from actual robot attempts.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Start with pretrained

Load a policy trained on broad offline data (many tasks, many demonstrations).

2. Add a residual layer

Place a tiny learnable correction module on top of the pretrained policy.

3. Collect online feedback

Robot tries actions on the real task; record successes and failures (reward signal).

4. Train the correction module

Adjust it to amplify high-reward actions while staying near the pattern of behaviors the pretrained policy already knows.

5. Deploy the combined policy

Use pretrained policy plus correction module together to execute the hard skill expertly.

What the paper changes

What the Method Changes

The paper changes a lightweight correction layer that sits on top of the pretrained policy. This correction module is small (only a few adjustable numbers that the computer learns) and learns during online robot interaction to nudge the pretrained policy's actions in reward-improving directions.

The hidden quantity

How Much the Policy Shifts

measured quantity  =  How far the corrected policy drifts from the pretrained one

The key hidden quantity is the distance between the corrected policy and the pretrained policy—how much the learned correction module nudges things. If this distance grows too large, the robot forgets what the pretraining taught it; if it stays zero, no improvement happens. The paper keeps this distance small and controlled by adding a regularization term (a penalty that grows as the correction module's outputs get larger), which forces the learned correction to stay within the behavioral range of the pretrained policy.

This is called distribution contraction: the pretrained policy already covers a broad range of reasonable actions, and the correction module refines within that range rather than inventing entirely new action patterns. The paper measures and controls this by tracking how much the correction module outputs deviate from zero (zero means no correction, just use the prior).

Why it works

Why Sharpening Stays Stable

The pretrained policy is already good at covering a wide range of behaviors—it has learned from diverse offline data to handle many situations without crashing the robot or doing something silly. The problem is that this coverage is too broad; it smooths out the detailed, task-specific knowledge needed for complex manipulation.

By placing a small correction module on top and training it to amplify high-reward behaviors (actions that succeed), the paper adds targeted sharpening without destroying the broad safety and diversity learned in pretraining. The correction module is kept small (via an L2 penalty) so it cannot force the policy far outside the pretrained behavior distribution—it nudges, not overwrites. At the same time, a BC-loss filter (a rule that rejects corrections leading to untrustworthy value predictions) prevents the learned reward signal from sending the robot into unexplored territory where its estimates are unreliable. The combination—gradient-based reward optimization plus regularization plus value-consistency filtering—pulls the policy upward into expert territory without letting it drift into disaster.

Intuitively: the pretrained policy is a broad platform with many routes. The correction module carves out a sharp highway along the best-reward route, but does not build an entirely new platform. Robots learn faster (using fewer online trials) because they start from a stable, broad foundation. The specialist skill emerges from within the pretrained repertoire rather than being bootstrapped from noise.

Math after the picture

Training the Correction

First in words:

Minimize negative value of the combined action plus an L2 penalty on the correction's size, where combined action equals the pretrained policy output plus the learned correction.

Then the compact form:

min_θ E_{s∼D, z∼N(0,I)} [ -Q_φ(s, a) + β∥s_θ(s, z)∥²₂ ] where a = π_pre(s, z) + s_θ(s, z)

Every symbol:

θparameters of the learned correction module
Q_φ(s, a)predicted reward value: how good is action a in state s
s_θ(s, z)the correction module: small learned tweaks to the pretrained action
π_pre(s, z)pretrained policy output (base action)
afinal action sent to robot
βhow much to penalize large corrections (balances sharpening vs stability)
∥s_θ(s, z)∥²₂size of the correction (L2 norm): sum of squared correction values
E_{s∼D, z∼N(0,I)}average over states from the dataset D and random noise samples
A tiny worked example
Suppose in state s, pretrained policy outputs action π_pre = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2] (a list of movement amounts).
Correction module learns s_θ = [0.1, 0.0, -0.05] (small nudges).
Combined action: a = [0.6, 0.3, 0.15].
Critic evaluates: Q(s, a) = 8.5 (good result).
Correction penalty: ∥[0.1, 0.0, -0.05]∥² = 0.01 + 0.00 + 0.0025 = 0.0125.
With β=1.0, loss = -8.5 + 1.0*0.0125 = -8.4875 (high value minus tiny penalty).
If correction tried to grow to s_θ = [1.0, 0.5, 0.3] (much larger), penalty becomes 1.34, pushing loss to -7.66 (worse).
The β term keeps the correction small enough to stay within pretrained behavior, preventing drift.

The first term pulls the robot toward high-reward actions (learning the task), and the second term holds it close to the pretrained distribution (stability).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Correction grows too large. If the penalty weight (a number that controls how much we punish large corrections) is too small, the learned correction nudges actions far from the pretrained behavior pattern. The robot then ignores the pretraining and behaves like it trained from scratch—slow, unstable, high sample cost.
Value predictions become untrustworthy. Early in training, the critic's reward estimates are noisy. If the robot acts on those noisy estimates without filtering, it chases phantom high rewards that do not actually exist, leading to erratic behavior. The BC-loss filter catches this by rejecting actions where the value prediction disagrees with a conservative baseline.
Pretraining data quality is poor. If the pretrained policy was trained on messy or off-task data, the broad base it learned is weak. Finetuning then inherits those bad habits and cannot fully overcome them with limited online trials. High-quality offline pretraining data is essential.
Done right: correction stays calibrated. When the penalty weight is tuned correctly and the BC-loss filter is active, the correction stays small and focused, the pretrained base acts as a reliable safety net, and the robot reaches expert performance on the hard skill without catastrophic forgetting of the prior knowledge.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple robot manipulation task in simulation: a pick-and-place problem where the robot must grasp and move an object to a target. Pretrain a diffusion policy on a diverse offline dataset (grasps from many angles, places into many locations, with varying object shapes). Then finetune with online RL on one hard subgoal: pick-and-place with a narrow target zone. Track forgetting (success rate on a prior diverse task set) and sharpening (success rate on the hard target skill) during finetuning. The demo should show that residual finetuning maintains prior skill (high success on the diverse task set) while gaining expert skill on the hard target, outperforming both naive retrain and no-finetuning baselines.

Forgetting = (success_prior_before - success_prior_after) / success_prior_before; Sharpening = success_hard_task; Total sample cost = number of online robot trials

The core result: residual finetuning reaches expert performance on a hard skill in far fewer trials than retraining from scratch, while keeping prior skills intact.
