VINCIE: Learning Image Editing from Video

Train an image editor to handle multi-step edits by learning from plain videos, not paired before-and-after images.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Image editing models today need hand-built pairs of images (one before an edit, one after) and separate expert tools for each type of edit. VINCIE shows you can skip all that: train a single model directly on raw videos. The model learns what happens when a scene changes by watching natural video sequences, and ends up able to edit images step by step in response to user requests.

Zero assumption version

Editing models need expensive paired data

Today's image editors are built on hand-crafted pairs: a photo before someone draws on it, and the result after. Collecting thousands of these pairs is slow and expensive. Even harder: you need separate expert models for segmentation (finding what to edit), inpainting (filling in the change), and other specific tasks. A beginner might think: just collect lots of before-and-after pairs and train on those. That works, but it does not scale—videos are plentiful on the web, paired edits are not.

The real bottleneck: if a model needs to edit an image three times in a row (user says 'add a cat', then 'make it blue', then 'move it left'), existing methods struggle because they were trained on single-step edits, not sequences of edits. And if you want the model to understand that the same object appears in frame 1, frame 2, and frame 3 of a video as it moves, you need that consistency baked into the training signal somehow.

The bottleneck. Train on native videos as raw sequences, not paired edits. Let the model learn what changes between frames and how to control those changes with text.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Sample video frames

Pick a few consecutive frames from a video where a real scene changes naturally.

Annotate the change

Use a pre-trained model to label what object moved or changed, and mark where the edit happened.

Train three proxy tasks

Teach the model to predict the next frame, identify segmentation now, and predict segmentation in the next frame.

Focus on past information only

Arrange the model so it can only look at previous images and instructions, mimicking how editing happens step by step.

Scale to millions of videos

Web videos are abundant; the method scales automatically as you add more data.

What the paper changes

What changes: the training signal

The paper changes where the training data comes from. Instead of labeling paired before-and-after images (the old way), it builds a sequence from natural videos. Each video frame becomes part of an interleaved list: text instruction, image, segmentation mask, next image, next segmentation mask. The model learns to predict the masked parts given the visible parts. That is the entire shift—the training data source switches from curated pairs to raw video sequences.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frame consistency signal

measured quantity  =  consistency across edits

The paper measures something implicit: how well the model preserves objects and their identities across multiple edits in a row. In a video, the same cat appears in frame 1, frame 2, frame 3—the same entity, in slightly different positions or appearances. An editing model that understands this will not randomly redraw the cat each time you give a new instruction. The paper does not compute a number for this, but the multi-turn editing benchmark reveals it: the success rate at turn 5 is much lower than at turn 1, because consistency breaks down if you do not model it.

The paper estimates this by seeing how often a multi-turn edit succeeds. If you ask for edit 1, then edit 2 on the result, then edit 3, does the model keep the same cat in each step? The 25% success rate at turn 5 is the proxy. Training on videos implicitly teaches this, because videos naturally show the same objects persisting across frames.

Why it works

Why it works: sequences teach transitions

Video frames are naturally ordered. Frame 1, 2, 3 show the cat moving. By training on these sequences and asking the model to predict the next frame given the previous ones plus a text instruction, you teach it the mechanics of change. A paired dataset ('before' and 'after' image, no in-between) never shows the intermediate steps or the full context of the scene. A video does: it is a sequence of consistent contexts.

The three proxy tasks work together: predicting the next image teaches the model how pixels shift and change. Predicting segmentation now teaches it to identify what is changing. Predicting segmentation next teaches it to anticipate what the next region of interest will be. Causal attention (the model only looks backward in time) enforces the editing direction: you give instructions and images in order, and the model predicts the result in order, step by step. This structure naturally handles multi-turn editing because the model has been training on multi-step sequences from the start.

Why this beats paired data: a single video frame-pair tells you 'the cat moved from left to right', but it loses the context (what was the cat doing before? what is the rest of the scene?). A video sequence keeps all that. The model learns implicitly that objects persist, text instructions matter, and changes unfold over time. Scaling to 10 million video sessions gives the model hundreds of millions of example transitions, each coherent and diverse. Paired data of that size does not exist and would be unaffordably expensive to create.

The learning equation

First in words:

Loss = mean squared error on three tasks: next-image pixels, current segmentation, and next segmentation. The model minimizes the sum of these three losses together.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_NIP(I_next | context) + L_CSP(S_t | I_t, context) + L_NSP(S_next | context)

Every symbol:

L_totalthe total penalty to minimize during training
L_NIPnext-image prediction loss—error when predicting the next frame
I_nextthe actual next image from the video
contextthe previous images, text instructions, and segmentation masks
L_CSPcurrent segmentation prediction loss—error when identifying what to edit now
S_tthe segmentation mask for the current frame
L_NSPnext segmentation prediction loss—error when anticipating what to edit next
S_nextthe segmentation mask for the next frame
A tiny worked example
Imagine a video frame where the model must predict three things about the next frame.
L_NIP (next-image loss) = 0.08 (pixel values are slightly off; the cat's tail is in the wrong place).
L_CSP (current segmentation loss) = 0.05 (the model correctly identified 95% of the cat, but missed a paw).
L_NSP (next segmentation loss) = 0.12 (predicting where to edit next is harder, because the cat will be in a new pose).
L_total = 0.08 + 0.05 + 0.12 = 0.25 penalty to minimize.
During training, the algorithm nudges the model weights in the direction that reduces all three losses. Over millions of videos, the model learns to predict frames, segments, and their transitions accurately.

The three tasks act as mutual teachers: predicting the next image forces the model to understand segmentation (what is moving?), and segmentation tasks force the model to track objects across time.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Subject position shifts cause inconsistency. When the cat moves across the frame between video frames, the model can confuse the same cat in a new position with a different cat. The paper addresses this with spatial region-of-interest tracking, but imperfect tracking still breaks multi-turn coherence.
Segmentation mask quality limits editing precision. The masks come from a pretrained vision model, not ground truth. If the model misidentifies the region to edit, the editing fails silently.
Causal attention makes the model slow. Block-wise causal attention is more efficient than full attention (which lets every word-piece look at every other word-piece), but still more costly than unidirectional models, because checking which past word-pieces to focus on requires extra computation. Scaling to 10 million videos requires significant compute because each video session processes multiple images and masks.
Works best when videos are natural and coherent. Highly stylized or artificially edited videos may confuse the model, because it learned from natural scene transitions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple web interface where you upload an image, type an editing instruction (e.g., 'add a hat'), and see the model produce the edited image. Then ask for a second edit on the result ('make the hat red'), and verify the hat stays in the same place and the original image content remains unchanged. Repeat for turn 3 or turn 4 to show that multi-step editing works. Record which edits succeed and which fail, and compare to a baseline trained only on paired data.

Success rate = (number of edits where the output is visually correct and consistent) / (total number of edits requested)

The demo proves the central claim: because VINCIE learned from video sequences, it can handle multiple edits in a row without forgetting or contradicting the previous edits. A model trained on pairs alone fails by turn 3 or 4.
