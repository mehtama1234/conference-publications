A self-driving car model that learns to imagine the future in a compressed space and uses those imagined scenes to plan better actions.

When a self-driving car decides what to do, it needs two skills: predicting what the world will look like next (world modeling) and choosing the best action now. Zero assumption version

Why separate prediction and planning breaks autonomous driving

A self-driving car has two jobs at every moment: predict what the road and other cars will do next, and decide which steering and throttle command is safest. Today, most cars do these two jobs in separate brains. The prediction brain imagines the future scene. The planning brain picks an action. But the two brains do not talk to each other deeply—the planner does not read the prediction brain's internal thoughts about why the future will unfold a certain way.

A beginner might try to run prediction and planning side-by-side: predict future frames, then search over possible actions to find the one that leads to the best imagined outcome. But this is slow and clunky because the prediction brain runs in pixel space (millions of numbers), so imagining ten seconds ahead fills memory—storing raw image data is expensive. The planner then has to decode pixels to extract what matters, which is inefficient and error-prone: the planner guesses which parts of the prediction matter for steering, wasting effort on road texture when it should focus on the position of that oncoming truck.

The problem in one sentence: The planner and predictor use different mental representations, so predicting the future does not directly change how the plan is made.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compress the world

Predictor learns a compact mental representation of the scene from images.

Predict future in that space

Predictor rolls forward this compressed representation to imagine next moments.

Planner reads the prediction brain

Planner takes predictor's internal compressed thoughts (lists of numbers), not decoded images.

Test actions in imagination

Planner asks: if I steer left, how does that change the predictor's mental picture?

Pick the safest steering

Planner chooses the action that leads to the safest imagined scene, because steering that leads to close oncoming traffic gets lower scores than steering that keeps distance.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes the bridge between prediction and planning. Instead of the planner reading pixel images from the predictor, the planner reads the predictor's internal compressed thought-lists (lists of numbers encoding what the predictor learned about the scene) directly.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: scene representation alignment

measured quantity  =  representation alignment score

The paper's core insight is measuring whether the predictor's internal compressed thoughts (lists of numbers) actually encode the information the planner needs. A thought-list is well-aligned if it captures road position, vehicle locations, and motion—all in one compact set of numbers. A poorly-aligned thought-list wastes space on irrelevant details like pavement texture.

The paper uses shared training: both the predictor and planner tune the same compressed representation simultaneously. As they train, the representation becomes aligned because both systems learn to emphasize the features that help their own task. The planner learns to value road edges and traffic; the predictor learns to keep track of them too.

Why it works

Why it works: shared compression gains alignment

The planner needs to answer questions like: if I steer left, will I hit the oncoming car? To answer this, it must simulate future scenes. But simulating full pixel-images is expensive and wastes computation on irrelevant details. The insight is that the predictor already solves a related problem: given the current image, predict the next image.

If the predictor and planner both work in the same compressed thought-space, they develop a shared language. When the predictor learns to encode road position in one thought-list, the planner learns to read that same thought-list to check if steering left would collide. They are forced to agree on what matters, because they share the same internal representation.

The mechanism: training the planner and predictor together means their errors push back against each other. If the predictor wastes space on irrelevant details, the planner learns not to trust those details and ignores them. If the planner needs road position to make good steering choices, the predictor learns to emphasize road position in its thought-space because doing so helps the planner succeed. Over time, the thought-space organizes itself around what is actually useful for safe driving.

The core equation

First in words:

Total loss is the prediction error plus the planning error, both measured in the shared thought-space. The prediction error asks: did the next real world-state match my imagined next thought-vector? The planning error asks: did my chosen action lead to a safe outcome in the imagined future?

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_pred(z_next, z_pred) + L_plan(a_chosen, z_future)

Every symbol:

L_totalcombined loss (error) for both prediction and planning
L_predprediction loss: how wrong the predicted thought-vector was
z_nextthe actual compressed thought-vector of the next moment
z_predthe predictor's guessed compressed thought-vector
L_planplanning loss: safety and comfort of the chosen action
a_chosenthe steering and throttle command the planner picked
z_futurethe imagined thought-vectors over the next few seconds
A tiny worked example
Imagine predicting one moment ahead. Real next thought-vector is [0.8, 0.3, 0.6] (road ahead, left lane free, slow oncoming traffic).
Predictor guessed [0.75, 0.35, 0.65]. Prediction error = (0.8-0.75)^2 + (0.3-0.35)^2 + (0.6-0.65)^2 = 0.0025 + 0.0025 + 0.0025 = 0.0075.
Planner chose steering angle 5 degrees. Simulation in that predicted thought-space says: road stays clear (good), left lane opens up (good). Planning loss = 0.1 (small penalty because outcome is safe).
Total loss = 0.0075 + 0.1 = 0.1075. Both systems learn from this: predictor improves prediction, planner learns that this steering angle is safe.

Sharing the thought-space means both systems optimize for the same compressed representation, so they naturally align on what matters for safe autonomous driving.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Thought-space becomes too compressed. If the encoder squeezes the world too tightly, both predictor and planner lose important detail (like the exact position of a cyclist). The car might miss small obstacles.
Predictor overfits to common scenes. If training data is mostly highway driving, the predictor learns to encode highway features very precisely but fails to generalize to crowded city intersections where pedestrians move unpredictably.
Planner cannot distinguish prediction from reality. The planner must remember that it is reading imagined thought-lists, not actual sensor readings. If it treats all thought-lists equally, it might steer aggressively based on a hallucinated obstacle.
Done right. The encoder size and training data balance are tuned so the thought-space captures essential driving features without overfitting to noise or hallucinating absent threats.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple autonomous-driving simulator with a 2D bird's-eye-view. The car sees obstacles, lane markings, and traffic. Train a separate-system baseline: one neural network for predicting future obstacles, another for planning steering. Train the unified model: shared encoder, predictor, and planner all using the same compressed thought-space. On unseen road layouts, measure which system avoids collisions more reliably and whether the unified system's predictions correlate with the planner's steering choices.
