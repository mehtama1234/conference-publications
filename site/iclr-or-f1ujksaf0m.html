Modality Alignment across Trees on Heterogeneous Hyperbolic Manifolds

Align text and image by giving both tree-like hierarchies and embedding them into curved spaces that match their shape.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Vision-language models (models that read both text and images) struggle because they handle the two types of data unevenly: they break text into a tree of ideas, but squash each image into a single feature. This paper solves it by building matching tree structures for both, then placing those trees into specially curved spaces designed to preserve hierarchy. The spaces have different shapes depending on the data, and the paper finds the middle ground between them.

Zero assumption version

Text and images are treated unequally

Today's vision-language models break a sentence into many pieces (words or phrases), each with its own meaning, arranged in a tree from general to specific. But they treat an image as one blob — a single summary number for the whole picture. When the model tries to match them, it is comparing a detailed outline against a single dot.

A beginner might try: take the image feature, add more copies of it, and call it a tree. But copies are all identical — they contain no hierarchy. The real problem is that the image features are extracted without guidance from the text, so they capture the wrong details. The misfit wastes the model's effort.

The gap: Images are flattened to one feature; text gets a detailed tree. The mismatch breaks alignment.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Text guide

Text embeddings (list of numbers for each word) point to which image regions matter.

Visual selection

Cross-attention (one-way focus mechanism: text phrases point to image regions they care about) selects visual features coarse-to-fine guided by text.

Build both trees

Image tree and text tree are constructed with matching shape: abstract to concrete.

Two curved spaces

Each tree embeds into a hyperbolic space (curved like a saddle) with the right curvature for that tree.

Find middle space

Compute a third curved space (a manifold: a curved surface with a particular shape) halfway between them using a distance measure, then align both trees there.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes how the model extracts and compares visual features. Instead of one number per image, it builds a tree of numbers, and instead of extracting them blindly, it uses text to guide which details to capture at each level.

the object it moves  =  image feature hierarchy
The hidden quantity

The hierarchy depth mismatch

measured quantity  =  curvature of the feature space

A hyperbolic space (a mathematically curved surface that looks like a saddle or a funnel) naturally preserves hierarchies because distance grows exponentially with depth. A flat space does not capture this naturally. Trees with different shapes need spaces with different curvatures — one space might be very curved for text trees (which are deep and branchy) and less curved for image trees (if they are shallower).

The paper estimates this by measuring how tree-like the extracted features are: does the distance between a parent and child follow the expected exponential pattern? It then adjusts the curvature for each modality separately, and finds a middle-ground curvature that lets both trees align without either one being forced into the wrong shape.

Why it works

Why matching shapes fixes alignment

Without guidance, the image feature extractor pulls out random visual details — edges, colors, patches — none organized by semantic meaning. Cross-attention (a learned focus mechanism that lets text questions select which image regions to look at) reverses this: text cues tell the model which visual details matter for each concept. That creates a text-aligned hierarchy in the image features.

Once both modalities have hierarchical features, a flat space (the usual straight-line space with even distances) is a bad fit. In flat space, a general concept and a specific concept look like ordinary nearby points — there is no structure saying that hierarchy. A hyperbolic space (curved inward like a saddle, distance growing exponentially with depth) automatically encodes hierarchy: if you move from general to specific, the cost accumulates exponentially, so the space naturally reflects the tree. But text and image have different tree shapes, so they need different curvatures. The paper finds the best middle-ground curvature — a third space that sits halfway between them — so both trees can align without distortion. This is proved to exist and be unique.

The alignment equation

First in words:

distance on middle manifold = expected distance from text space to middle + expected distance from middle to image space, weighted by how well each tree fits its own space

Then the compact form:

D_middle(T, I) = min_M [ E_q[ d_text(T, M) ] + E_q[ d_M(I, image_space) ] ]

Every symbol:

D_middledistance measure in the intermediate aligned space
Thierarchical feature tree from text
Ihierarchical feature tree from image
Mthe intermediate (middle) manifold being optimized
d_textdistance in the text's hyperbolic space
d_imagedistance in the image's hyperbolic space
E_qexpected value over the distribution of tree pairs
Tiny worked example
Imagine text tree has 3 levels (general, medium, specific) and embeds in a space with curvature 0.8.
Image tree has 2 levels (coarse, fine) and embeds in a space with curvature 0.3.
Distance from general -> medium in text space: about 1.0 unit.
Distance from medium -> specific in text space: about 1.5 units (exponential growth).
Distance from coarse -> fine in image space: about 0.5 unit (less curved space, slower growth).
Middle manifold optimizes: curvature = (0.8 + 0.3) / 2 = 0.55, aligning both trees at this midpoint.
After alignment, text and image hierarchies can match: specific concepts pair with fine visual details.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Curvature mismatch. If the inferred curvatures for text and image are too far apart, the middle manifold is far from both, and alignment breaks.
Poor text guidance. If text embeddings are weak or do not align with visual semantics, cross-attention selects the wrong image regions, and the image tree becomes incoherent.
Tree depth mismatch. If one modality naturally has much deeper hierarchy (many levels) and the other is shallow, the middle manifold compromises, and both lose structure.
When alignment works. Text embeddings are strong, image regions are semantically aligned with text phrases, and tree depths are similar. The middle manifold is close to both original spaces, and both hierarchies project faithfully.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a miniature version on a small taxonomic dataset (e.g., a subset of ImageNet with labeled category hierarchies). Extract text trees from category names and descriptions. Extract image trees by applying cross-attention (a focus mechanism that runs on transformer layers: neural network chunks that process data in stages) at multiple layers, guided by the text. Embed both into hyperbolic spaces, fit the middle curved space, and measure alignment. Test on unseen classes (zero-shot) and on a different dataset (cross-domain). The demo should show that matched hierarchies outperform single-feature baselines in both transfer settings, because hierarchical structure captures the semantic relationships that single features lose, and shared shape ensures alignment does not require distortion.

alignment gain = (accuracy with trees) - (accuracy without trees), measured as percentage point improvement on zero-shot and cross-domain tasks

The visual story: trees that match in shape align better than a tree paired with a single point (because mismatched shapes force distortion during alignment), and that alignment holds when both are embedded in curved spaces that respect their shape (because curvature that matches tree-ness makes the embedding faithful).
