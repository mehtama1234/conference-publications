QuantSparse: Compressing Video Diffusion Transformers

Combine size-reduction and attention-trimming without them breaking each other by teaching the trimmed model to match the original one's hidden layers.

Zero assumption version

Shrinking video models breaks them in two ways at once

A video diffusion model (a machine that generates a video frame-by-frame by gradually cleaning up random noise) lives on specialized graphics chips and takes days to run. To deploy it, engineers try two tricks: quantization (store numbers as smaller integers instead of full decimals) and attention sparsification (skip most of the expensive attention math, keeping only the connections where one part of the video truly affects another).

Each trick alone loses video quality—numbers round off, important attention connections vanish. But someone naive combines them anyway, hoping the efficiency gains will multiply. Instead, they collide: the rounded numbers become worse signals for the attention math, and the sparse attention amplifies the rounding errors. The video quality crashes. Worse, the two effects feed each other: bad attention signals make the rounded numbers seem even more wrong, and rounded numbers make sparse attention land in the wrong places.

The core problem. Quantization and sparsification each reduce quality when used alone, and combining them makes both worse at the same time—like trying to drive a car with a broken engine and missing wheels at once.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change the diffusion process itself. Instead, it changes two things about the model: first, the weights (the numbers inside the model that tell it what to pay attention to) are shrunk and stored as smaller integers; second, the attention paths (the connections that show how much one part of the video should influence another) are pruned to only the most critical ones. The trick is doing both at the same time without them canceling each other out.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Attention alignment drift

measured quantity  =  Attention alignment drift

When you quantize a model (round the numbers), the attention math shifts—where the model looks changes slightly. When you prune attention (remove paths), you lose the information those paths carried. Together, they cause attention to shift in unpredictable ways. The paper measures this drift by comparing where the compressed model's attention lands versus where the full model's attention lands.

The paper fights this drift in two ways: Multi-Scale Salient Attention Distillation watches the full model's hidden layers (the layers between the input and output) at multiple detail levels and teaches the compressed model to match them, keeping attention aligned. Second-Order Sparse Attention Reparameterization uses mathematical tricks to estimate what the pruned attention paths would have contributed, so the remaining paths can absorb that work.

Why it works

Why it works: Three layers of rescue

Naively combining quantization and sparsification fails because the rounded numbers and pruned attention pull the model in opposite directions. The paper uses three mechanisms to align them:

First, Multi-Scale Salient Attention Distillation: the full model's hidden layers (the layers between input and output) hold information about where to pay attention. The paper copies the full model, saves its hidden-layer outputs at multiple levels of detail, and trains the compressed model to match those hidden outputs exactly, because by forcing agreement on the intermediate logic the paper can hide the rounding errors in how the results are combined. This is called distillation. Since the hidden layers capture where attention should go, matching them keeps the compressed model's attention in the same places despite the quantization noise.

Second, Second-Order Sparse Attention Reparameterization: when you prune attention paths, information vanishes. But the vanished information is not random—it has structure captured in second-order statistics (rougher patterns built from pairs of changes). The paper fits a mathematical model to estimate what those pruned paths would have contributed, then adjusts the remaining sparse attention to absorb that work. This recovery happens automatically during training without adding extra numbers to store.

Together, these two fixes make quantization and sparsification cooperate instead of fight. The distillation keeps attention aligned to the full model's intent, and the reparameterization recovers what sparsification throws away.

The distillation loss

First in words:

The loss combines: quantization penalty (how rounded the numbers are) plus distillation penalty (how much the compressed model's hidden layers differ from the full model's) plus sparse attention recovery penalty (how well the reparameterization fits second-order residuals).

Then the compact form:

Loss_total = L_quantize(theta_q) + L_distill(H_student, H_teacher) + L_sparse(W_sparse, residuals_2nd_order)

Every symbol:

theta_qthe quantized weights (numbers rounded to integers)
H_studentthe hidden-layer outputs from the compressed model
H_teacherthe hidden-layer outputs from the full model
W_sparsethe sparse attention weights (which connections remain)
residuals_2nd_orderthe second-order patterns of what sparsification discarded
L_quantizepenalty that increases with how far the rounded numbers are from the original
L_distillpenalty that increases when student and teacher hidden layers differ
L_sparsepenalty that increases when the reparameterization poorly fits the discarded information
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight was originally 3.847. Quantized, it becomes 4 (rounded). Quantization penalty: |3.847 - 4| = 0.153.
The full model's hidden layer at a certain step outputs [0.51, 0.49, 0.52]. The compressed model outputs [0.48, 0.50, 0.51]. Distillation penalty: sum of squared differences = (0.51-0.48)^2 + (0.49-0.50)^2 + (0.52-0.51)^2 = 0.009 + 0.001 + 0.001 = 0.011.
If pruning removed 30% of attention paths, the reparameterization tries to fit a model to the second-order changes in what those paths would have contributed. If the fit explains 85% of the variance, sparse penalty = 0.15 (lower is better). Total loss = 0.153 + 0.011 + 0.15 = 0.314. Training nudges the model to reduce this loss.

The three penalties work together: the quantization penalty keeps numbers close to the original, the distillation penalty keeps hidden layers aligned, and the sparse penalty lets the remaining attention absorb the pruned paths' work.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Distillation data stale. If the full model's hidden-layer reference is out of date or computed on a different task, the distillation teaches the wrong patterns. The fix: recompute the full model's outputs on the same data and reuse them consistently.
Reparameterization underfits discarded information. If the mathematical model fitted to second-order residuals misses important structure, the sparse attention cannot recover it. This happens when sparsification is too aggressive (pruning more than 70% of paths). The fix: use moderate sparsification rates or increase reparameterization model capacity.
Quantization too coarse. If numbers are rounded to integers only (8-bit quantization), the distillation cannot hide all rounding artifacts. The fix: use 16-bit or mixed-precision quantization (8-bit for some layers, 16-bit for critical ones).
Done right. When distillation data is fresh, sparsification is moderate (30–50% pruning), and quantization uses 16-bit, the method keeps video quality high while achieving a 3.68 times smaller storage footprint and 1.88 times faster execution, because the distillation teaches the sparse quantized model to replicate the full model's hidden logic, and the reparameterization ensures the pruned attention paths' information is recovered by the remaining paths.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Start with a small pretrained video diffusion transformer (e.g. a model trained on short clips). Quantize it to 8-bit integers and prune 40% of attention connections. Then train with distillation loss: compute the full model's hidden outputs on a test set, and train the compressed model to match those hidden outputs while also generating correct videos. Run a side-by-side visual comparison: full model video vs. QuantSparse video. Measure video quality (PSNR or perceptual similarity) and timing.

Quality_improvement = PSNR_QuantSparse - PSNR_naive_combined; Speed_gain = time_full_model / time_QuantSparse

The demo must show that the compressed model's videos are visually close to the full model's, despite quantization and sparsification. The difference should be subtle (a bit of softness or slight color shift), not catastrophic blur or flicker.
