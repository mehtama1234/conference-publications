ViTSP: Vision Language Models Guide Large-Scale TSP

A framework uses a pre-trained image-understanding model to spot promising regions in a drawn map of cities, then runs a classical solver on those pieces to find better routes for thousands or tens of thousands of stops.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

The Traveling Salesman Problem asks: what is the shortest route that visits every city once and returns home? With thousands of cities, the problem explodes — there are more possible routes than atoms in the universe. Classical solvers bog down, heuristic tweaks require expert tuning, and neural models trained on one city set fail on another. ViTSP sidesteps training by borrowing a pre-trained image-understanding model (one that already learned to recognize shapes and patterns in pictures) to look at a visual map and spot the promising small pieces. These pieces get solved cleanly with a classical optimizer, and the pieces are stitched together into a whole-map solution.

Zero assumption version

Three methods, none scale to real-world size

Classical exact solvers (algorithms that find the mathematically best route) are beautifully precise but grind to a halt with 10,000 cities. They explore too many possibilities.

Heuristic methods (hand-designed shortcuts that find good-enough routes fast) work at scale but require an expert to tune their parameters for each problem type — change the city layout or the cost model, and the tuning fails.

Learning-based methods (neural networks trained on examples) run fast and need no manual tuning, but they learn only the patterns in their training data. Show them a new city set or a much larger one, and they flounder — the patterns have shifted.

The trap: three solutions, but none handle both large scale and unfamiliar layouts. Use a pre-trained image model to visually identify small high-quality chunks of the big map, then solve those chunks cleanly and assemble the global solution.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Visualize the map

Draw the cities as points on an image or heatmap.

Step 2: Extract visual features

Feed the image into a pre-trained vision language model (a neural net that has already learned to understand pictures and text together from billions of examples).

Step 3: Ask the model for clusters

Use the model's internal understanding to identify regions that cluster together or form natural subproblems.

Step 4: Solve each cluster optimally

Run a classical exact solver (like LKH-3) on each small-scale cluster; these solvers are fast enough on 50-500 cities.

Step 5: Merge routes and return

Chain the optimal local tours together into a global tour covering all cities.

What the paper changes

What changes: the route-finding strategy

Instead of training a custom neural network or manually tuning parameters, the paper shifts the burden onto visual understanding. It borrows a model that already knows how to see and interpret patterns (trained on images and descriptions), applies that model to a map representation of the cities, and uses the model's judgment to partition the problem into solvable pieces.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: cluster quality and cohesion

measured quantity  =  How well the vision model partitions the cities

The paper does not explicitly measure a single number called 'cluster quality,' but its core success hinges on whether the vision model identifies clusters of cities that are genuinely close together and naturally form a good local tour.

The paper estimates this implicitly by checking if the final tours are short. A vision model that carves the map into poor clusters — like putting far-apart cities in the same group — will fail at the root: the downstream solver cannot fix a fundamentally bad partition. So the paper's measure of cluster quality is indirect: the quality of the final tour reveals whether the visual partitioning was sound.

Why it works

Why this works: reuse learned vision over training from scratch

A neural network trained only on small TSP instances (say, up to 1,000 cities) has learned patterns specific to that training range. Show it 10,000 cities and it chokes because the distribution has shifted — the model's internal weights are tuned to the old pattern.

A pre-trained vision language model, by contrast, has been exposed to billions of real-world images and learned general shape-recognition, clustering, and spatial reasoning. When you show it a new TSP map (even one with vastly more cities), its learned intuition about how things cluster together in space transfers directly, because the visual pattern is the same: dense regions and sparse regions look the same regardless of whether there are 100 or 10,000 cities.

Classical solvers are brittle but optimal at small scales. By partitioning the big map into small clusters (each solvable by a classical solver), the framework avoids the explosion of the search space while keeping the optimality guarantee within each cluster. The final tour is not globally optimal, but it is locally optimal in every piece.

Math after the picture

The cost formula: travel distance plus partition penalty

First in words:

total_tour_length = sum of distances along the route + small penalty if clusters are poorly stitched

Then the compact form:

L_total = sum_edges dist(city_i, city_j) + lambda * penalty(cluster_junctions)

Every symbol:

L_totalthe total tour length in distance units
dist(city_i, city_j)the straight-line distance between city i and city j
sum_edgesadd up all the jumps along the tour
lambdaa small weight; how much we penalize poor stitching between clusters
penalty(cluster_junctions)extra distance incurred when jumping from one cluster to another
A tiny worked example: three clusters, stitching cost
Cluster A has 50 cities; internal tour distance = 150 units.
Cluster B has 60 cities; internal tour distance = 180 units.
Cluster C has 40 cities; internal tour distance = 120 units.
Jump from A to B = 10 units. Jump from B to C = 15 units. Jump C back to start = 8 units.
Sum of internal routes: 150 + 180 + 120 = 450 units.
Sum of inter-cluster jumps: 10 + 15 + 8 = 33 units.
Total tour: 450 + 33 = 483 units.
If lambda = 0.1 and junctions are poor, penalty adds maybe 5 units -> final 488.
If the vision model identifies clusters well, junctions are natural and penalty is near zero.

The classical solver finds the best path within each cluster, and the vision model's partition minimizes wasted distance at cluster boundaries.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Vision model misses the true structure. If the map has an unusual layout (cities arranged in a thin spiral, or in a fractal pattern the vision model has never seen), the model's partitioning may be wrong. For example, it might group far-apart cities because they appear visually adjacent in the compressed visualization.
Cluster boundaries are poorly chosen. If the vision model cuts the map in a way that splits tight groups apart, the inter-cluster jumps will be long and wasteful. The local optimal tours within clusters cannot recover from a bad global partition.
The visualization itself is misleading. TSP cost may not be Euclidean distance (e.g., driving distance over roads). If the visual representation does not match the cost model, the vision model's spatial reasoning becomes irrelevant.
Done right: vision models trained on real-world maps and layouts. When the pre-trained model has seen similar spatial distributions during pre-training, it transfers its understanding directly. No retraining, no domain tuning, just borrowed insight from a bigger teacher.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small working instance: generate 500 cities scattered on a 1000-by-1000 grid, with a known optimal tour (or a known near-optimal solution from LKH-3). Feed a visualization of that instance to a pre-trained vision language model (you can use a freely available model like CLIP or Qwen-VL). Extract what the model considers to be interesting regions or clusters by asking it to annotate regions, or by measuring which parts of the image the model spent the most internal effort thinking about (the model's focus). Partition the 500 cities into 3-5 clusters based on the model's output. Run an exact solver on each cluster. Stitch the cluster tours together. Measure the final tour length and compare it to the optimal (the gap as a percentage). The demo must show that a pre-trained model's visual intuition produces a reasonable partition without any training on TSP examples.

optimality_gap (shown as a percentage of the optimal distance)

The metric must show that the vision-guided partition produces a sub-1% gap on a 500-city instance in a single forward pass (no retraining) and that naive neural methods or random partitions fail by comparison.
