Learning Unified Representation of 3D Gaussian Splatting

Instead of feeding raw 3D shape parameters directly to neural networks, convert each shape into a color field on its surface, then learn compressed codes of those fields.

Paper explainersAll themesRepresentation & Self-Supervised Learning

3D Gaussian Splatting is a fast way to reconstruct scenes by painting thousands of 3D blurry spheres. But when you try to let neural networks learn to modify or generate these spheres, the raw shape parameters (center, rotation, scale) break training because different numbers can paint identical pictures. This paper fixes it by representing each sphere not as raw numbers, but as a color field painted on its mathematical surface—a representation that is stable, unique, and learnable.

Why raw sphere parameters break learning

3D Gaussian Splatting paints a 3D scene using thousands of soft, blurry spheres. Each sphere is stored as a bundle of numbers: a center location (3 numbers), a rotation (4 numbers, called a quaternion), a size scale (3 numbers), a color (3 numbers), and how transparent it is (1 number). This works great for rendering—the computer draws the scene fast.

But when you want a neural network (a learned function) to produce these numbers—say, to edit a scene or generate a new one—the learning breaks. The core problem is that many different number-bundles paint identical pictures. For example, negating all 4 numbers in a quaternion (flipping signs) is mathematically identical to the original rotation, yet produces the exact same visual. When the network trains on both versions, it gets conflicting signals: the same painted picture says the numbers should be positive and the numbers should be negative simultaneously. Training gets stuck, wandering in circles.

Even worse, the numbers themselves live in different worlds. A center lives in normal 3D space; a quaternion lives on a curved surface (a 4D sphere); a scale and color are positive numbers only. Stuffing all these different kinds of numbers into a standard neural network encoder—which assumes they are all like a simple number list—breaks their internal structure.

The network cannot learn from parameter collisions, so training fails. Replace raw parameters with a representation that has one unique mapping: the color field on the sphere's mathematical surface.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Define the sphere surface.

For each Gaussian sphere, compute its iso-probability surface—the mathematical boundary where the density is equal everywhere.

Step 2: Paint color on the surface.

Define a color field on that surface that encodes both the sphere's color and view-dependent appearance.

Step 3: Sample the field.

Sample thousands of points on the surface and read their colors, creating a high-dimensional but structured representation.

Step 4: Compress with variational autoencoder.

Pass the sampled surface through a variational autoencoder (a learnable compressor) to get a fixed 32-number code.

Step 5: Train networks on codes.

Now neural networks receive these 32 stable numbers instead of colliding raw parameters, and training converges cleanly.

What the paper changes

What changes: from raw parameters to surface field codes

The paper replaces the input to the neural network. Instead of feeding raw Gaussian parameters (center, rotation, scale, color, opacity), it feeds a learned 32-number code that was extracted by compressing a color field sampled from the sphere's mathematical surface. The code is produced by a variational autoencoder, which is a type of compressor that learns to squeeze high-dimensional data into a small fixed size.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: collision uniqueness

measured quantity  =  representation uniqueness

Collision uniqueness measures whether different inputs to the learned function can produce the same output. With raw parameters, many different number-sets paint identical spheres—collisions are rampant. With the surface-field representation, the mapping is one-to-one: each distinct surface maps to exactly one 32-number code. There are no collisions.

The paper estimates this by checking: does negating a quaternion (sign flip) produce the same code? Does swapping two mathematically-equivalent rotations produce the same code? With raw parameters, yes—conflicts. With submanifold fields, no—each surface is unique.

Why it works

Why it works: moving to a collision-free space

The core mechanism is geometric: the surface of a Gaussian sphere is a well-defined mathematical object. Two different Gaussian spheres have different surfaces. The color field on that surface uniquely describes the sphere. Because surfaces are unique, and the field on each surface is unique, the combined representation has no collisions.

When you sample thousands of points on the surface and read the color at each point, you get a high-dimensional fingerprint. Two spheres with identical visual appearance but different mathematical structures will have different sampled colors or colors distributed differently on the surface, because the surfaces themselves differ. Feeding this sampled field through a variational autoencoder (a learned compressor) creates a learned 32-number code. Because the input has no collisions, the codes are stable: the same sphere always produces the same code, and different spheres produce different codes.

During training, the network learns a path from input codes to output codes without conflicting instructions because each code originates from exactly one sphere. The Manifold Distance metric (which measures distance using optimal transport, a way of comparing how surface colors are rearranged) further helps by respecting the curved structure of the surface field space rather than treating all changes as simple straight-line distances. This keeps the training signal aligned with what matters on the surface.

The representation formula

First in words:

For a Gaussian sphere with center and covariance, the submanifold field representation is defined as a color field on the iso-probability surface, then compressed through a variational autoencoder into a 32-dimensional code.

Then the compact form:

A tiny worked example
Imagine a Gaussian sphere with center at origin and covariance creating a small tight ball.
Step 1: Extract the boundary surface where density drops to 50 percent of peak.
Step 2: Sample 1000 points on that surface. Read color at each point: [0.9, 0.2, 0.1], [0.8, 0.3, 0.1], and so on, a thousand color triples. Total: 3000 numbers.
Step 3: Feed these 3000 numbers into a point-cloud encoder (PointNet). It produces a 32-number bottleneck.
Step 4: A decoder reconstructs the surface from the 32 numbers: 1000 reconstructed colors.
Step 5: The loss compares original versus reconstructed using Manifold Distance, which measures similarity while respecting the surface field structure.
A different Gaussian sphere produces a different surface shape, so its sampled colors differ, so the 32-number code differs. No collisions.

The code compresses the surface field down to 32 stable numbers, one unique code per sphere.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Autoencoder bottleneck too small. If the 32-number code is too short, the compressor loses important detail about the sphere's color or shape, and the decoder cannot reconstruct accurately. The training loss rises, and the codes become lossy approximations rather than faithful fingerprints.
Surface sampling too coarse. If you sample too few points on the surface, you miss important structure in the color field. Two truly different spheres might look similar after undersampling, and collisions re-introduce themselves at a different level.
Training without Manifold Distance metric. If you use straight-line distance between reconstructed codes instead of Manifold Distance, the loss does not accurately capture what matters on the surface. The training signal becomes misaligned with the underlying structure, and codes fail to stabilize.
Done right. Oversample the surface with thousands of points, use a 32-D bottleneck, and measure reconstruction error with Manifold Distance to keep the representation faithful and learnable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a variational autoencoder on a random set of 1000 generated Gaussian spheres. Train the encoder-decoder on surface-field representations for 10,000 steps. Then: (1) generate a held-out sphere that the autoencoder has not seen, convert it to a surface field, encode it, and decode it back. Measure reconstruction error. (2) Take two different spheres with similar visual appearance but different parameters, encode them both, and measure the code-space distance. (3) Take a sphere, encode it 100 times with slight noise in the sampling, and verify the 100 codes cluster tightly—the representation is stable.

reconstruction_error = Manifold_Distance( original_surface_field, decoded_surface_field ); code_stability = std_dev( codes_for_same_sphere_multiple_samples ) — should be near zero

The demo must prove that: (a) the surface field can be accurately reconstructed from the 32-number code, and (b) the same sphere encoded multiple times produces nearly identical codes. Collisions are gone.
