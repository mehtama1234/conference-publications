TwinVLA: Data-Efficient Bimanual Manipulation with Twin Single-Arm Vision-Language-Action Models

Instead of training a new bimanual model from scratch on expensive bimanual data, duplicate a working single-arm model and let the two copies coordinate through shared attention, keeping the strong single-arm knowledge intact.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Teaching a robot to use both arms together usually means collecting expensive recordings of two-arm movements and training a giant model from the ground up. TwinVLA takes a simpler path: start with a model already trained to control one arm well, copy it into a second instance, then teach the two copies to notice what the other is doing. The payoff is real bimanual robots that work better because they inherit the strong one-arm knowledge and coordinate via a small learned layer, and use far less data because you skip the enormous bimanual pretraining step.

Zero assumption version

The bimanual trap: fine-tuning alone breaks what already works

A robot can learn to manipulate things with one arm when trained on hundreds of thousands of one-arm demonstrations. But when you ask it to use both arms—say, holding an object steady with one hand while the other hand manipulates it—the model struggles. The naive fix: collect expensive video of both arms in action, then teach the model from scratch. This requires new human demonstrations, more compute, and a risk that you lose the strong one-arm skills the original model already has.

If you just fine-tune the existing single-arm model on bimanual examples, it often breaks: the model's attention mechanism (the part that decides what to look at) gets rewired by the new arm-specific information, and it forgets how to coordinate between the two arms. The model has to re-learn coordination from a blank slate instead of building on existing knowledge.

The core problem. Existing single-arm VLAs (vision-language-action models) do not coordinate across two arms; retraining from scratch wastes the strong one-arm knowledge already learned.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take two copies of a pretrained single-arm VLA.

Duplicate the entire model weights; both start with strong learned knowledge.

Wire the two copies through shared attention.

Insert a shared self-attention layer that processes signals from both arms together, using a causal mask so left and right arms can coordinate.

Re-weight the shared modality attention.

Protect the original learned patterns by scaling up attention to shared modalities (vision, language) so the model does not forget them during coordination learning.

Route arm-specific decisions with a mixture-of-experts gate.

A lightweight gate learns which arm should take the lead on each prediction step, preventing the two copies from interfering.

Fine-tune on small bimanual dataset.

Train only the new coordination layers and arm-specific parts; the core single-arm knowledge stays nearly frozen.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper modifies the attention mechanism (the part of the model that decides what information to look at when making a decision) to include signals from both arms at once. It also adds a mixture-of-experts routing layer that decides how much each arm's information should influence the next action.

the object it moves  =  attention routing and arm coordination
The hidden quantity

The attention-preservation score: keeping old knowledge alive during coordination

measured quantity  =  pretrained attention weight magnitude

When you wire two copies of a model together via new shared attention, the model risks losing the original learned patterns. The paper measures this by tracking how strongly the attention layer still weights the original shared modalities (vision, language) relative to the new arm-specific tokens. A high score means the model is still listening to what it learned before.

During fine-tuning, the paper re-scales (manually boosts) these shared-modality attention weights before they flow through the new coordination layers. This acts like a brake because the higher weight makes vision and language signals drown out arm-specific noise, so the model keeps its old habits longer instead of scrambling to re-learn them. As a result, the model learns coordination faster (focusing only on the new job) and avoids forgetting (the old signals stay strong). The paper shows this one change cuts initial fine-tuning loss by 40% because the model starts with good recovery pathways and boosts final performance by 1-4% because the coordination layer builds on a stable foundation.

Why it works

Why it works: preserving old knowledge while learning coordination

The mechanism is separation of labor: keep the single-arm model intact, and only teach it HOW to coordinate. When you duplicate a pretrained single-arm VLA, each copy already knows how to read the image, understand language, and predict one arm's actions. The two copies do not need to re-learn any of that.

What they DO need to learn is coordination: how to make one arm's action depend on what the other arm is seeing and doing. The paper adds this as a new layer on top—shared attention over signals from both arms, plus a mixture-of-experts router. Because this coordination layer is new and small, and because the underlying single-arm models stay mostly frozen, the model learns coordination quickly on small bimanual datasets.

The attention re-weighting method is vital: without it, when you add new arm-specific signals to the shared attention layer, the model starts ignoring the old vision and language signals because they are now competing for attention with new information and the new information is unfamiliar so it grabs the model's focus. Re-weighting keeps those old patterns loud by giving them extra weight in the scoring process, so the model remembers what it learned and builds coordination on top (learning only the new routing layer) instead of rebuilding all the old knowledge from scratch.

The flow-matching action prediction with shared coordination

First in words:

The model trains by predicting a flow (a smooth direction) from a noisy action chunk to the true action chunk, conditioned on the VLM output and coordination gate. The loss penalizes how far the predicted flow is from the target, scaled down by a mixture-of-experts weight that varies per token.

Then the compact form:

loss = E[ || v_theta(A_t_noised, h_t, d_t) - u_target ||^2 ] where v_theta predicts the flow, h_t is the VLM encoding (shared-attention output), d_t is the MoE gate decision, and u_target is the reference direction from noise to true action.

Every symbol:

v_thetathe model's learned function that predicts the flow direction
A_t_noisedthe action chunk after adding noise (the model learns to denoise)
h_tthe encoded observation from the VLM (image, language, proprioception, after shared attention)
d_tthe mixture-of-experts routing weight at time t (how much each arm leads)
u_targetthe true flow direction: the path from noise to target action
E[ ]the average loss across many training examples
A tiny worked example
Suppose the true action is close (gripper opens 10 degrees). The noisy action has the gripper at 45 degrees (far and wrong).
The flow u_target points from 45 degrees toward 10 degrees, a distance of 35 degrees.
The model predicts a flow v_theta. If it predicts 40 degrees (overshoot), the error is (40-35)^2 = 25.
If the MoE gate says 'left arm leads' (d_t = 1.0 for left, 0.0 for right), only the left-arm loss contributes.
If the gate says 'both arms equally' (d_t = 0.5 for both), both losses are weighted down, and the model learns slower but more stably.

The flow-matching loss trains the model to smooth out noisy actions into the true trajectory by penalizing prediction errors, teaching the model the clean direction from noise to truth; the MoE gate weights each arm's contribution by learning per-time-step importance scores, so the model learns which arm should lead each phase of the task (e.g. left gripper closes first, then right arm pushes) without the two arms' outputs corrupting each other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forgetting the single-arm knowledge. If you do not re-weight the shared-modality attention, the new coordination tokens drown out the vision and language signals. The model forgets how to read the image. Removing re-weighting alone drops performance by 1-4% and raises initial loss by 40%, which is why the paper includes it.
The two arms interfering instead of coordinating. Without the mixture-of-experts gate, both arms' tokens mix freely in attention. A token meant for the left arm's gripper might get corrupted by right-arm proprioception. The MoE gate keeps arm-specific decisions separate until coordination is needed.
Bimanual fine-tuning data still required. The method still needs SOME bimanual examples to teach coordination. If those examples do not cover the task, the model cannot learn it. However, this dataset is 10x smaller than monolithic retraining.
Done right: start from strong single-arm skills. TwinVLA's strength comes from the fact that the underlying single-arm VLA was trained on hundreds of thousands of public single-arm demonstrations. If that foundation is weak, the coordination will be weak. But reusing a strong foundation means coordination learning is fast and stable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple two-arm manipulation task: e.g., a robot holds a block steady with one gripper while the other gripper pushes the block across a table. Start with a pretrained single-arm VLA from OpenVLA or similar. Duplicate it to create two instances. Add a minimal shared attention layer with re-weighting and an MoE gate. Fine-tune on 100-500 bimanual demonstrations of the push-hold-push task. Measure success (did the block move to the goal?) and arm coordination (did the holding arm stay still while the pushing arm moved?).

success_rate = (tasks_completed / total_tasks) × 100; coordination_quality = (hold_arm_position_variance - initial_variance) / initial_variance × 100, where lower variance means better holding

The key numbers to watch: success on bimanual tasks should reach 80%+ without catastrophic forgetting of single-arm skills (hold single-arm success above 85%), all from fewer than 500 bimanual training examples.
