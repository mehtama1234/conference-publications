Score-Based Density Estimation from Pairwise Comparisons

The paper estimates a full probability density from pairwise preference data by learning the score of the winner density and then correcting it with a position-dependent tempering field to recover the hidden belief density.

Paper explainersAll themesProbabilistic modeling

Suppose an expert cannot assign numeric probabilities directly, but can repeatedly choose which of two options is preferred. Those pairwise comparisons reveal something about the expert's hidden belief density, meaning the probability density that describes which outcomes the expert finds plausible, but not in a direct way. Preferred samples are biased toward high-density regions, so the observed winner density is a distorted version of the target belief. This paper formalizes that distortion. It shows that the score of the hidden belief density and the score of the observed winner density are collinear, with the scaling determined by a position-dependent tempering field. The method then learns the winner score with score matching, estimates the tempering field under a Bradley-Terry preference model, and de-tempers the score to reconstruct the full belief density. A diffusion model trained on these corrected score signals can then sample from complex multivariate beliefs using only hundreds to thousands of pairwise comparisons.

Zero assumption version

Why preference data does not directly give a density

A density tells you how probability mass is distributed over a continuous space. Pairwise comparison data only tells you which option won inside each pair. If one sample is chosen over another, that means the expert preferred it under some hidden preference-scoring rule, but it does not tell you the density value itself.

A naive strategy would treat the winners as direct samples from the target density and fit a generative model to them. That fails because winners are selection-biased. Regions that already have high hidden probability produce winners more often, so the winner density is more concentrated than the belief density.

The central question is therefore structural: what exact mathematical relationship links the unobserved belief density to the observed winner density? Once that link is known, density estimation becomes possible.

The key relation. The target belief density is not observed directly. What you observe is a tempered winner density whose score points in the same direction but with a position-dependent scale factor.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect comparison data

Sample candidate pairs and ask an expert or preference oracle which one is preferred.

Model the winner density

Treat the marginal density of winners as an observable target and learn its score function with score matching.

Estimate the tempering field

Under the Bradley-Terry model, derive or estimate the position-dependent factor that links winner score to belief score.

De-temper the score

Divide out the tempering effect so the recovered score points in the direction of steepest increase of the hidden belief density.

Train a diffusion sampler

Use the recovered score inside a score-based generative model or diffusion process to sample from the estimated belief density.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the estimation target from pairwise choice probabilities to the score field of a continuous density. Instead of predicting who wins each comparison only, it reconstructs the full shape of the hidden belief probability pattern.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Tempering field

measured quantity  =  position-dependent scale between winner and belief scores

A score function is the direction and rate of fastest increase of the log density. It points toward increasing probability mass and tells a score-based generative model how to move samples. The paper proves that the score of the winner density and the score of the hidden belief density are aligned in direction. They differ by a multiplicative field that depends on location.

That field is hidden because pairwise comparisons do not expose it directly. Yet it is exactly what must be known to undo the preference-selection bias. If the field is greater than one in some region, the winner process has sharpened the score there more strongly; if smaller, the sharpening is weaker. Estimating this field correctly is what turns winner modeling into belief-density estimation.

Why it works

Why score estimation makes this possible

Score-based models do not need normalized density values. They only need the score field, which is often easier to estimate in high dimensions. That fits the preference setting because pairwise data does not naturally give normalized probabilities anyway.

The paper's first insight is to move from belief density to winner density. Winners are observable because every comparison produces one. If you aggregate those winners, you get a real density in data space. A score-matching objective can then learn how that winner density changes locally. The second insight is that, under a random utility preference model such as Bradley-Terry, the winner score is not arbitrary. It is a tempered version of the belief score. That gives a direct route back to the hidden target.

Once the corrected score is available, standard score-based sampling machinery applies. The paper uses diffusion-style training on tempered samples generated by score-scaled annealed Langevin dynamics. The important point is that pairwise comparison data is not used to supervise the final density directly. It is used to infer a score field that can drive a generative sampler.

Winner score, belief score, and de-tempering

First in words:

Learn the score of the observable winner density, then divide by the local tempering field to recover the score of the hidden belief density.

Then the compact form:

s_w(x) = tau(x) s_p(x), so s_p(x) = s_w(x) / tau(x)

Every symbol:

s_w(x)score of the winner density at point x
s_p(x)score of the latent belief density at point x
tau(x)position-dependent tempering field produced by the preference mechanism
xa point in the continuous data space
Bradley-Terry modelpairwise preference model used to derive or estimate tau(x)
score matchingtraining objective used to fit the winner score without normalized densities
A tiny worked example
Suppose at a point x the learned winner score is s_w(x) = [2.0, 1.0].
The estimated tempering field there is tau(x) = 1.25.
Then the recovered belief score is s_p(x) = [1.6, 0.8].
This means the winner density and belief density point in the same direction, but the winner density has been sharpened by the preference mechanism.
A Langevin or diffusion sampler using the corrected score follows the hidden belief shape rather than the over-sharpened winner shape.

The paper's theory provides the justification for this correction. Without the tempering field, fitting winners alone would recover the wrong density.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Preference model mismatch. The tempering-field estimator relies on assumptions such as Bradley-Terry-style pairwise choice behavior. If the expert's selection process departs strongly from that model, the de-tempering correction can be biased.
Sparse comparisons leave the local density shape underdetermined. In high dimensions, a small number of pairwise outcomes may not constrain the winner score well enough in all regions, especially away from frequently preferred areas.
Score estimation errors propagate into sampling. Diffusion and Langevin procedures depend directly on the recovered score field. If the winner score or tempering field is noisy, generated samples can drift toward incorrect modes.
Done right. The method is most attractive when numeric density supervision is unavailable but pairwise preferences are cheap, because it converts those preferences into a full generative object rather than a ranking-only model.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create a two-dimensional hidden target density and a preference oracle that selects winners according to a Bradley-Terry rule. Generate pairwise comparisons, fit the winner score with score matching, estimate the tempering field, and sample from the recovered belief density with Langevin or diffusion updates. Compare against a baseline that fits only the winner density. Plot both resulting sample clouds against the true target density.
