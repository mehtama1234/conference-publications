Optimal Ad Scheduling with Psychological Behavior Models

Ads work best when spaced to ride three psychological waves: getting familiar, staying fresh, and reinforcing through the right pauses.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Deciding when to show ads is hard. Simple rules like showing them evenly spaced do not work well, because how much someone likes your brand changes over time in patterns rooted in human psychology. This paper models three real psychological forces at work (how repetition builds familiarity, how people get tired of the same thing, and how time away actually strengthens memory), then solves for the exact ad times that ride these waves best.

Zero assumption version

Ad timing is stuck on guessing

Advertising companies show ads to people using simple rules: space them evenly in time, or cap the total number per day. These heuristics ignore how people's interest in a brand actually changes over hours and days.

What a beginner tries first: show ads as often as allowed. This fails because people get annoyed or forget what the ads are selling. Spread them out evenly? That also fails, because some times in a person's day matter more than others—a pause that has worn away your memory is wasted, and a repetition at the wrong moment feels pushy.

The real ad timing depends on three invisible forces shifting how interested someone is at each moment. The paper models user interest as a function of ad history, then finds the exact times to show ads that maximize total interest.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure three psychological forces

Mere exposure: seeing an ad repeatedly makes it feel familiar, so the person cares more about it (but only up to a point, because eventually familiarity tops out). Hedonic adaptation: each time the person sees the ad, the boost in interest gets smaller because the surprise and the newness have already been spent, leaving less capacity for the emotion to lift. Operant conditioning: when time passes without the ad, the person forgets about it slightly, so the next ad lands with a bigger effect than it would have right after the last one.

Model user interest as a curve over time

Interest starts high, drops when you do not see the ad, rises when you see one (but less each time), and is shaped by all three forces at once.

Find the times that maximize total interest

Given you can show exactly N ads in a time window, calculate which exact moments will add up the highest total interest.

Prove the solution is nearly perfect

The algorithm is guaranteed to find a schedule within a tiny amount of the theoretical best—the gap shrinks exponentially as N grows.

Compare against simple heuristics

Evenly spaced ads, uniform frequency caps, and other standard rules are shown to be much weaker than the optimal schedule.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the ad times themselves—not the number of ads, not their creative content, but exactly when each one appears. Instead of guessing based on rules, it calculates the precise moments within a time window when showing an ad will deliver the highest lift in user interest.

the object it moves  =  ad times
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: user interest over time

measured quantity  =  user interest curve

User interest is a single number that starts high, drops when the person does not see the ad for a while, rises when they see it again (but not as much as the first time), and is pulled up and down by three invisible forces. The paper measures it as a curve—interest at every moment from now to the end of the time window.

The three forces that shape it: mere exposure (seeing an ad over and over makes it feel familiar, lifting interest upward because humans naturally like what they know well, but with a limit because too much of anything becomes stale); hedonic adaptation (the lift from seeing an ad gets smaller each time because the newness and surprise fade, leaving less room for the emotion to spike); operant conditioning (a period of no ads lets the person forget parts of what they saw, so the next ad lands with more punch because it reaches someone whose memory has partly reset). The paper estimates all three from data or assumes sensible shapes for them.

Why it works

Why the optimal schedule works

When you fix the number of ads to N, the optimal times to show them depend entirely on one of the three psychological forces: operant conditioning (time decay). The paper proves this, which means the best schedule is one where each ad arrives at exactly the right moment—not too early (so the decay from the previous ad has done its job), not too late (so the person is still interested enough to feel the effect).

Here is the causal chain: (1) fix N ads; (2) operant conditioning says the power of an ad depends on how long it has been since the last ad; (3) so we space the ads to give each one time to recharge the user's sensitivity; (4) this maximizes total interest because each ad hits when the person is most able to feel its effect; (5) the algorithm finds these moments in quasi-linear time (a tiny amount of computation) by exploiting the structure of the decay curve.

Math after the picture

The optimization equation

First in words:

Total interest is the sum of interest changes at each ad time, where each change depends on when the previous ad was shown and the three psychological functions. We maximize this sum over all possible ad times.

Then the compact form:

I_total = sum of [interest_boost(t_i, t_{i-1}) - interest_decay(t_i - t_{i-1})] for i = 1 to N

Every symbol:

I_totaltotal user interest across all ads shown
t_itime when ad i is shown
interest_boost(t_i, t_{i-1})how much showing an ad at time t_i lifts interest, given the last ad was at t_{i-1}
interest_decay(gap)how much user interest drops over a time gap (shaped by operant conditioning)
Nfixed number of ads to show
A tiny worked example
Suppose we show 2 ads in a 10-hour window. First ad at time 2: boosts interest by 50. Decay from time 2 to time 7 (5-hour gap): interest drops by 20.
Second ad at time 7: boosts interest by 45 (less than the first, due to adaptation). No further decay in this example.
Total interest: 50 + 45 - 20 = 75. If we moved the second ad to time 6 (4-hour gap), decay would be 15, but the boost might be 48 instead (slightly higher). Total: 50 + 48 - 15 = 83 (better).
The algorithm finds the exact time that wins.

The equation shows that to maximize interest, we trade off giving ads time to recover (bigger gaps) against showing them when the person still remembers the previous one (smaller gaps).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The three psychological functions are guessed, not measured. If the shapes of mere exposure, hedonic adaptation, or operant conditioning are wrong for the real audience, the optimal schedule will be wrong too. The algorithm is only as good as the psychology model it is given.
The model assumes user interest can be measured and predicted. In reality, user interest is hard to observe directly. It must be inferred from clicks, dwell time, or purchase behavior, which are noisy and do not perfectly reflect what the model assumes.
The optimal schedule is fragile to the chosen time window. If the analysis is done for a 24-hour window but people's interest really cycles over weeks, the ads will land at wrong moments. The time window must match the real psychology.
Done right. Measure the psychological functions for your actual audience. Validate the model against holdout data. Re-optimize the schedule as you gather new behavioral signals. Treat the schedule as a starting point to test, not a final answer.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulation where a user's interest follows the three psychological rules. For a small number of ads (say 3–5), compare three strategies: (1) uniform spacing, (2) random times, and (3) the optimal times from the algorithm. Run each strategy 100 times with different random audience members, measuring final interest and also counting how many times a person clicks the ad. The demo should show that the algorithm's schedule wins on both metrics consistently.

average_final_interest = mean of user interest level after all ads are shown; click_rate = number of clicks divided by total ads shown

The demo must make it impossible to miss that riding the three psychological waves (optimal spacing) beats simple rules by a large margin—often 30% or more.
