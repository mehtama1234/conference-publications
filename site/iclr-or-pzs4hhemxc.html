Calibrating Verbalized Confidence with Self-Generated Distractors

When an LLM says it is confident, ask it the same question with slightly different claims, and watch how it contradicts itself to detect when it is really just guessing.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models can tell you how confident they are in their answers, but they are often overconfident. An LLM might say it is 90% sure about something when it is actually just guessing. This breaks trust because a human user thinks the model knows the answer when it does not. This paper shows that overconfidence happens because LLMs become unusually gullible when they do not really understand the topic.

Zero assumption version

Overconfidence ruins trust in LLM answers

When you ask an LLM a factual question, you want to know: does it really know the answer, or is it just guessing? The model can verbalize its confidence (say "80% sure"), which seems helpful. But in practice, LLMs report high confidence on answers they get wrong.

A naive fix: just ask the model multiple times and see if it gives the same answer (called self-consistency). This works better than a single answer, but it does not directly tell you if the model is overconfident. You are measuring consistency, not actual knowledge.

The core problem. LLMs are suggestible. When faced with a claim they do not understand, they confidently affirm it rather than saying "I do not know."

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Generate distractors

Ask the LLM to create alternative claims related to the question (e.g., different birth years for the same person).

Ask confidence on each

Prompt the model: Is this claim true? Get a yes-no probability for the original claim and each distractor.

Sum the confidences

Add up all the yes-probabilities across the original plus distractors. If the model understands, this sum should be around 1.

Normalize each confidence

Divide each individual confidence by the total. This removes the suggestibility bias.

Compare to baseline

Use generator-validator disagreement: if the model generated the answer but is unsure validating it, that is a red flag.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how you extract confidence from an LLM. Instead of taking the model's stated confidence at face value, you measure its behavior across multiple related claims and rescale based on that pattern.

the object it moves  =  verbalized confidence scores
The hidden quantity

Suggestibility: how gullible the model is

measured quantity  =  suggestibility (total confidence bias)

Suggestibility is a hidden property of each LLM: how much it says yes to claims when it does not actually know. When an LLM does not understand a topic, it does not stay quiet; instead, it confidently affirms contradictory claims. A highly suggestible model says yes to many incompatible alternatives at once.

The paper measures suggestibility by summing all the yes-probabilities when asked about multiple related claims. On questions the model got wrong, highly suggestible models produce a high total confidence (summing to 3 or 4 out of 10 alternatives), while on questions the model got right, the total is closer to 1.

Why it works

Why normalizing by total confidence works

An LLM's true hidden knowledge is spread across all the claims it can make about a topic. If the model truly knows the answer, it will concentrate high confidence on the correct claim and low confidence on the distractors. The total adds to about 1.

When the model does not know, it cannot tell the claims apart. It says maybe yes to all of them. The total adds to 3, 4, or higher. This spreading is the signature of suggestibility. By dividing each claim's confidence by that total, you undo the spreading. A confidence of 0.4 out of a total of 0.5 becomes 80% (very confident). But 0.4 out of a total of 3 becomes 13% (low confidence), revealing the model's real uncertainty.

Math after the picture

The normalization equation

First in words:

Normalized confidence for a claim equals the raw verbalized confidence divided by the sum of all confidences in the claim set.

Then the compact form:

f_norm(c) = f_VC(c) / sum_c f_VC(c)

Every symbol:

f_norm(c)normalized confidence for claim c after correcting bias
f_VC(c)raw verbalized confidence for claim c from the model
sum_c f_VC(c)total confidence across all claims in the set (includes c and all distractors)
A tiny worked example
Question: Was researcher Kang Ji-hwan born in 1980? Model generates: yes (1980).
Distractors: Was he born in 1985? Yes with 0.40 confidence. Was he born in 1990? Yes with 0.35.
Raw confidences: claim 1980 = 0.45, claim 1985 = 0.40, claim 1990 = 0.35.
Total confidence = 0.45 + 0.40 + 0.35 = 1.20 (model spread its confidence too thinly).
Normalized confidence for 1980 = 0.45 / 1.20 = 37.5% (honest, lower than raw 45%).
If model had known the answer, total would be near 1.0, and normalized would match raw.

When the model is suggestible and spreads confidence thin, the normalization shrinks all scores, revealing actual uncertainty.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Distractors are too easy or too hard. If all distractor claims are absurd, the model rejects them easily, and the method does not detect suggestibility. If they are indistinguishable from the correct claim, the method cannot separate them. Distractors must be plausible alternatives.
The model enters a fixed response pattern. Some models always say yes at 60%, or always say no. They do not actually vary confidence based on understanding. Normalization cannot fix this; it just rescales the flat pattern.
Generator-validator disagreement is not informative. If the model generated the answer but then validates it with high confidence, the method has no signal that something is wrong. The method only catches cases where the model changes its mind.
Large distractor sets work better than small ones. Generating 10 claims instead of 5 gives more stable estimates of total confidence. However, 10 claims require 10 times more forward passes (when the model answers each claim), so there is a budget tradeoff.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple test: pick 20 factual questions where you know the ground truth (use TriviaQA or SimpleQA). For each question, have an LLM generate an answer, then use DINCO to score confidence. Generate 5-10 distractor claims (either randomly or via the LLM). Compute normalized confidence for each question and check: do questions the model answered correctly have higher normalized confidence than questions it got wrong? Measure calibration using expected calibration error (ECE): bin confidence scores from 0% to 100%, and check if the fraction of correct answers in each bin matches the average confidence in that bin.

ECE = average across bins of abs(average_confidence_in_bin - fraction_correct_in_bin)

The demo should show that raw verbalized confidence is miscalibrated (high confidence on wrong answers), and DINCO makes confidence calibrated (confidence matches actual accuracy).
