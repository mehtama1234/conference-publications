Video-LevelGauge: Positional Bias in Video Language Models

Video language models answer the same question differently depending on where in the video it is asked, revealing hidden positional biases invisible in overall benchmarks.

Zero assumption version

Benchmarks miss the real problem

Video language models (software that watches videos and answers questions) have improved over time by learning from thousands of video-question pairs. We have benchmarks that measure overall accuracy — ask 100 questions, count the right answers, report a score. That feels thorough. But it hides a nasty habit: the model gives different answers to the same question depending on where in the video the question points you, even when the content is identical. It might nail questions about the first minute, bomb questions about the middle, and nail them again at the end, because during training the model saw patterns where important moments and questions clustered at the start or end. The benchmark score averages over all those positions and misses the pattern entirely.

A beginner might think: just train longer, get more data, build a bigger model. But if the model only has a bias because of imbalance in its training data — humans showing it video-text pairs where important moments cluster at the beginning or end — then just scaling won't fix the underlying habit. You need a tool to see the bias first, measure it precisely, and track whether your fixes actually work.

In one sentence. The paper builds a test set where you can move a question to different positions in a video without changing what it asks about, and measure whether the model's answer changes just because of position.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect videos and write questions

Gather 438 videos, write questions about specific moments using GPT-4o captions.

Insert probe at fixed positions

Take one question, embed it at start, middle, end, or near related frames.

Build context the way real models see it

Interleave video and text (realistic retrieval-augmented and dialogue scenarios).

Run model on same question at each position

Ask the video model 27 times per probe, one model at a time, record the answer.

Compute bias metrics and pattern type

Calculate how much accuracy swings by position. Label the shape: head bias, neighbor bias, or balanced.

What the paper changes

What moves: probe position

The paper doesn't change the model's weights (its learned settings). It doesn't retrain anything. Instead, it moves a single question to different spots in the input sequence — before certain frames, after related content, at the middle of a long sequence, at the end — and watches whether the model's answer flips. The object is the position of the question in the video-text stream, a simple mechanical thing, but it exposes invisible mechanical bias in how the model attends to information.

the object it moves  =  probe position in video stream
The hidden quantity

Positional performance drift

measured quantity  =  accuracy swing across position

When you move a question to different positions, the model's accuracy doesn't stay constant. It drifts. The paper names this drift — how much the answer changes when only position changes — and gives it a measured value: a composite score combining standard statistical metrics (variance across positions, the peak-to-trough gap, and how often the model shows the same bias pattern).

To estimate it, the paper runs each probe at nine different configurations (start of context, end, middle, next to related frames, etc.), records the right-or-wrong answer at each position, and computes three numbers: the spread of accuracy across positions, how tall the spike is if the model peaks at one position, and whether multiple models show the same shaped bias. Combining these three gives a single bias score — higher means more bias, lower means the model treats all positions equally.

Why it works

Why the bias lives in how the model reads

Video language models read sequences of video frames and text by focusing on certain parts selectively — a process called attention (the model learns which parts of the input to look at more carefully). Early in training, models often see data where important information is at the start (title, overview) or where the human asked the question near the key frame. Over time, the model settles into a habit: look harder at the start, or look harder at frames right before the question. When you later ask an identical question at a different position, the model looks at the wrong parts of context because its learned reading habits fire the same way — and the answer flips. Commercial models have seen longer, more balanced training data, so they learned to read all positions equally. Open-source models, trained on shorter clips or simpler data, developed position-dependent reading habits that work well on average but break when you move things around.

The fix is not to retrain the model on different data — that is slow and expensive. Instead, this benchmark lets you see the bias first, track how it changes as you modify the training process (longer videos, better sampling, interleaved text-video data), and verify that your fix actually works, because you can measure the bias before and after. Without it, you just see the overall score stay flat and guess what helped.

Math after the picture

Bias composite metric

First in words:

bias_score = (max_accuracy - min_accuracy) / max_accuracy + variance + skewness_magnitude

Then the compact form:

B = (max(A_p) - min(A_p)) / max(A_p) + var(A_p) + |skew(A_p)|

Every symbol:

A_paccuracy at each probe position p
max(A_p)highest accuracy across all positions
min(A_p)lowest accuracy across all positions
var(A_p)spread of accuracy values (how jumpy they are)
|skew(A_p)|how lopsided the peaks are (0 means symmetric, high means one-sided)
A tiny worked example
Model X tested at nine positions: accuracy at start 85%, pos2 84%, middle 72%, pos7 75%, pos8 78%, end 86%, repeat-start 85%, random 73%, after-frame 80%.
Max accuracy: 86%, min accuracy: 72%, spread = (86 - 72) / 86 = 16.3%.
Variance of [85,84,72,75,78,86,85,73,80] = 38.4 (high, jumpy).
Skewness shows peaks at ends (start and end are high), middle is low = +0.8 (right-skewed, not symmetric).
Bias score = 0.163 + 0.384 + 0.8 = 1.347. (A commercial model might score 0.05, showing no position-dependent swings.)

The metric captures three intuitions: peak height shows the model has a strong preference, spread shows how fragile it is, and skewness shows if the preference is one-sided (head bias) or symmetric.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Test set not diverse enough. If all videos are shorts or all are long movies, the benchmark only exposes biases relevant to that length. Testing across multiple context types (dialogue, retrieval-augmented generation, pure long-form video) is needed because each scenario teaches the model different position-dependent habits.
Model can exploit the benchmark itself. If a model is trained specifically to pass this benchmark, it might learn to detect the fake probe structure (alternating video-text-video patterns) and handle only that setup differently, so the benchmark measures the model's ability to game this specific test, not true position-independence. Probes must be hidden from training data entirely because if the model has seen these exact videos and questions during training, it can memorize the right answer without actually reading the video; then the test proves nothing about real position-independence on new content.
Human annotation can introduce position bias too. If human raters write clearer questions for certain positions, the benchmark measures the raters' bias, not the model's, because the model might learn to exploit those uneven question qualities instead of fixing position bias. Human refinement must treat all positions equally.
Done right. Maintain separate test sets, curate questions position-agnostically, validate against hallucinations or information leakage before releasing, and retest as new models arrive.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test: collect five videos, write ten questions about mid-video moments that don't mention time or order. Embed each question at three positions: immediately after the related frame, at the start, at the end. Run an open-source video language model on all three versions of each question. Record right-or-wrong for each. Compute the bias metric for that model on your five videos. Then run the same test on a commercial video model. Plot the two models side by side.

bias_swing = max_position_accuracy - min_position_accuracy

The demo makes it impossible to miss: if bias_swing is large for the open-source model and small for the commercial model, positional bias is real and measurable, because the same question got different right-or-wrong answers in the same video just from moving position. This difference proves the open-source model learned position-dependent reading habits during training, while the commercial model's longer exposure to diverse positions eliminated that bias.
