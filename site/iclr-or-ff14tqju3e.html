Faithfulness Under the Distribution: Attribution Evaluation via Diffusion

When checking if attribution methods are truthful, reconstruct masked image regions using diffusion instead of zeroing pixels, keeping inputs realistic.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Attribution methods claim to show which parts of an image matter to a model's answer. Testing this claim (called faithfulness evaluation) means erasing pieces and watching the answer change. But standard erasure methods—like zeroing pixels—create unnatural images that look nothing like the photos the model saw during training. This breaks the test, because the model's behavior on fake inputs does not reflect its behavior on real ones. The FUD framework reconstructs erased regions using diffusion models (machines that paint missing pieces realistically) instead, keeping inputs realistic. When inputs stay realistic, the model behaves normally and its confidence drop honestly reflects how important the erased region truly was.

Why standard attribution tests break

Attribution methods point to pixels (or regions) that most influence a model's answer. To test whether these pointers are truthful, you mask (hide) those pixels and check if the model's confidence drops. If confidence drops sharply, the method was right; if it barely drops, the method lied.

The catch: when you zero a pixel, you make an image the model has never seen during training. Real photos never have pure black holes. So the model, now confused by this unnatural input, may behave in weird ways—its confidence may drop not because the attribution was wrong, but because the image is out of distribution (unlike anything the training data contained). This corrupts the test.

A beginner might try: zero pixels, measure confidence drop, declare the attribution faithful. But that measurement is unreliable because the zeroed image is fake, and the model's response to fake inputs does not prove anything about real-world attribution.

In one sentence. Attribution evaluation metrics fail when they use unnatural perturbations that push images out of the training distribution.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Get an attribution.

A method (like Grad-CAM) marks which pixels matter.

Step 2: Mask those pixels.

Hide them (set to zero or blur).

Step 3: Reconstruct with diffusion.

Use a diffusion model to paint plausible pixels in the masked region.

Step 4: Measure confidence drop.

Feed the reconstructed image to the classifier and measure how much the answer changed.

Step 5: Judge faithfulness.

A sharp drop means the attribution was truthful; a tiny drop means it was not.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The FUD framework swaps out the perturbation method. Instead of zeroing or blurring (the naive, distribution-breaking move), it uses a diffusion model to fill in the masked region with pixels that look natural and belong in the image. This one change—from heuristic erasure to learned reconstruction—is the whole idea.

the object it moves  =  perturbation method (zeroing to diffusion reconstruction)
The hidden quantity

Distribution alignment: how close the perturbed image stays to realistic photos

measured quantity  =  distribution alignment (how natural the perturbed image looks)

The paper measures an invisible quantity: how far an image has strayed from the kinds of images the model saw during training. A zeroed image (full of black holes) sits far from the training manifold—it is out of distribution. A diffusion-reconstructed image sits close to it—it looks like a real photo, just with a plausible guess in the erased region.

The paper does not explicitly compute this quantity (that is hard), but it uses diffusion—which by definition learns the shape of the training data distribution—to keep perturbations in-distribution. Cleaner tests come from inputs the model actually expects to see, not inputs that confuse it.

Why it works

Why the method works: the causal chain

Classifiers (like image recognition models) are trained to handle images from the training distribution. When you give such a model an image from outside that distribution—one that looks weird or unnatural—its behavior becomes unpredictable and does not reflect what the model would do on real data. Zeroing pixels creates such an alien image; the model's confidence drop tells you nothing about whether the attribution was right, because the model is also confused by the fake input.

Diffusion models learn the shape of the training distribution—they learn to draw pixels that look like real photos. When you ask a diffusion model to fill in a masked region (given the unmasked context), it samples from the learned distribution, creating a plausible continuation. The resulting image looks natural—it sits inside the distribution the classifier knows. Now when the classifier's confidence drops, the drop reflects the true importance of the erased region, not the model's confusion about fake pixels.

In short: diffusion reconstruction keeps both the perturbation and the model's response honest. The drop is real, not an artifact of out-of-distribution inputs.

Math after the picture

The measurement formula

First in words:

Faithfulness drop equals the confidence on the original image minus the confidence on the diffusion-reconstructed image.

Then the compact form:

Faithfulness = Conf(x_original) - Conf(x_reconstructed)

Every symbol:

Conf(x_original)model confidence on the unmodified image
Conf(x_reconstructed)model confidence on the diffusion-filled image
Faithfulnesshow much confidence dropped (higher drop means attribution was more faithful)
A tiny worked example
Original image: model confidence on dog = 85%.
Mask the dog region (where the attribution pointed).
Diffusion fills the mask with plausible pixels (say, trees, sky, or another animal).
Reconstructed image: model confidence on dog = 35%.
Faithfulness score = 85% - 35% = 50% drop.
A 50% drop is strong evidence the attribution was right (the dog region really did drive the 'dog' answer).

The bigger the confidence drop, the more faithful the attribution. Because the reconstructed image is realistic, the drop is honest, not an artifact of out-of-distribution confusion.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Diffusion model itself is weak. If the diffusion model does not know how to paint realistic pixels (trained on poor data, or the original image domain is unfamiliar), the reconstruction will be off. The filled region may still look unnatural, corrupting the test just as much as zeroing. The method only works if the diffusion model is good.
Diffusion introduces its own bias. A diffusion model may favor certain kinds of pixels (e.g., it might always fill with average textures, or it might inherit biases from its training data). These biases could make masked regions less salient than they truly are, making faithfulness scores artificially low even though the attribution was right.
Mask design still matters. Whether you mask a single pixel, a region, or a semantic object (e.g., the entire dog) changes the test. The paper does not solve all mask-design choices; it only fixes the perturbation method. Other evaluation choices still linger.
Done right. Train the diffusion model on the same data as the classifier, mask semantically coherent regions (not random pixels), and verify that the diffusion samples look plausible before claiming the test is honest.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small experiment: pick a trained image classifier and an attribution method (e.g., Grad-CAM). Generate a few test images. For each image, apply the attribution method to find important regions. Then run two evaluation paths: (1) the standard zeroing method, and (2) the FUD diffusion method. Measure the confidence drops under both methods. The demo should show that diffusion-based drops are more stable and correlate better with ground-truth importance (if you have labels for which regions truly matter) compared to zeroing-based drops, which are noisy and unreliable, because diffusion keeps the input realistic so the model is not confused by fake pixels, while zeroing breaks the image and muddles the test with artificial confusion.

Fidelity gap = Faithfulness(diffusion) - Faithfulness(zeroing)

The demo should make it impossible to ignore: diffusion-reconstructed perturbations yield more honest and reliable attribution evaluations than zero-based perturbations, because the model behaves normally on in-distribution inputs.
