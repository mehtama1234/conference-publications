Identifiable Markov Switching Models with Instantaneous Effects

Learn which causal connections change when a system switches between hidden states, and prove you got them right even when changes happen fast and variables affect each other instantly.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Real-world data often flips between different stable patterns—like glucose levels bouncing differently in healthy people versus those with diabetes, or stock prices rising in one market regime and falling in another. These regime-switches are hidden: you only see the time series numbers, not which regime is active at each moment. This paper shows how to find those hidden regimes AND recover the causal connections that operate within each regime, even when the system jumps between regimes frequently and variables influence each other instantly (on the same time step).

Why regime switches are invisible

Time series data like daily temperatures or patient glucose readings are not born from a single fixed machine. Instead, they jump between different operating modes. A diabetic patient's blood sugar responds differently to meals during controlled periods versus stressful days. A financial market prices assets one way in stable times and completely differently during a crash.

The naive approach: assume one causal model (e.g., glucose depends on food, insulin depends on exercise) and fit it to all the data. This fails because the real model changes. On day 1, glucose might rise sharply with food; on day 2, the same food causes almost no rise. The single model averages these patterns and explains neither.

The harder problem: you cannot see which regime is active. You see glucose numbers, but not a flag saying whether the patient is stressed or calm. Regime switches may happen frequently and variables may affect each other instantly (same time step), creating tangled simultaneous dependencies. Learning which hidden regime caused each observation, AND what causal rules apply in each regime, is the problem.

The method in one sentence. Recover hidden regimes and their causal structures by setting up a mathematical proof that the regimes and relationships are uniquely determined by the observed data, then use that to build a detector.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Define identifiability

Prove mathematically that if you had infinite noise-free data, the regimes and causal structures are the ONLY explanation that could produce it.

Step 2: Use FlowMSM to detect regimes

Feed the time series into a detector that assigns each time step to its hidden regime using the identifiability proof as guarantee.

Step 3: Run causal discovery inside each regime

For each detected regime, apply standard causal discovery (find which variables cause which) on that regime's subset of data.

Step 4: Recover regime-dependent causality

You now have separate causal models per regime, capturing how relationships change between operating modes.

Step 5: Certify the answer

The identifiability theorem guarantees your answer is correct (assuming model assumptions hold), not just one possible guess.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard causal discovery assumes one fixed causal model and finds relationships (X causes Y, Z causes W). This paper changes what you are discovering: instead of one global causal model, you discover multiple causal models, one per hidden regime, that switch on and off as the system jumps between states.

The hidden quantity

The identifiability certificate

measured quantity  =  Identifiability: uniqueness of regimes and causal relationships

Identifiability means: given infinite noise-free observations, there is exactly ONE assignment of regimes and ONE set of causal structures that could produce the data. If two different regime assignments or two different causal models could explain the same data equally well, the problem is not identifiable.

The paper proves identifiability by showing that under certain conditions (noise that follows standard probability patterns, no feedback loops between variables on the same time step except through the regime itself, temporal dependencies tracked properly), the true regimes and causality are mathematically forced to be the answer—no ambiguity.

How the paper estimates it: FlowMSM uses this identifiability guarantee to guide its search. By working backward from the data, it assigns each time step a regime using a probabilistic model (tracking which regime is most likely at each step), then applies standard causal discovery to each regime's data.

Why it works

Why discovering regime-specific causality works

The core observation: if two regimes have different causal structures, they will produce different statistical patterns in the data. For example, in regime A, changes to food instantly ripple through glucose; in regime B, they do not. That difference leaves a fingerprint. A sufficiently sensitive causal discovery algorithm can spot which subset of the data came from regime A (high food-to-glucose coupling) versus regime B (low coupling), and recover the regime labels from that detection.

Complicating factor: variables can affect each other on the same time step (instantaneous effects, e.g., glucose and insulin both change in response to a stressor without one causing the other in the normal sense). This creates cycles and ambiguity. The paper proves that even under these cycles, the regime boundaries and causal relationships are still uniquely identifiable—because the regime-switching signal acts as a "circuit breaker" breaking the cycles across regime boundaries.

The method works because regimes are like switches in a control panel: the same physical variables behave differently when the switch flips. By learning BOTH which data points belong to which regime AND the causal model within each regime jointly, the problem becomes rigid enough to have a unique solution.

Math after the picture

The identifiability condition

First in words:

The latent regimes and their causal relationships are identifiable if the data are generated by switching between regimes with distinct causal structures, each regime has independently distributed noise from the exponential family, and regimes evolve as a Markov chain (next regime depends only on the current regime).

Then the compact form:

P(X_t | Z_t, X_{t-1}, Z_{t-1}) = f(Z_t) * g(X_t | Z_t, X_{t-1})

Every symbol:

X_tobserved values (e.g., glucose) at time step t
Z_thidden regime (e.g., 1 = healthy mode, 2 = stress mode) at time t
f(Z_t)probability of regime Z_t given the previous regime
g(X_t | Z_t, X_{t-1})the regime-specific causal model: how current values depend on previous values and the current regime
Markov assumptionnext regime depends only on current regime, not all history
A tiny worked example
Say regime 1 (healthy) has rule: glucose_t = 0.8 * glucose_{t-1} + 0.5 * food_{t-1}, and regime 2 (stressed) has rule: glucose_t = 0.95 * glucose_{t-1} + 0.05 * food_{t-1}.
Observation: days 1-3 show glucose rising 0.5 per unit food, days 4-6 show glucose rising 0.05 per unit food.
Inference: days 1-3 must be regime 1 (high coupling), days 4-6 must be regime 2 (low coupling).
Identifiability guarantee: no other regime assignment and no other causal structure could produce this pattern—the answer is unique.

The equation expresses how current observations depend on the hidden regime and past data; identifiability proves the regimes and causal structure inside each are uniquely recoverable from this dependence pattern.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Regimes are too similar. If two regimes have nearly identical causal structures, the data gives no signal to distinguish them. FlowMSM will misassign many time steps, degrading the final causal recovery. The identifiability proof assumes regimes are sufficiently distinct; it does not apply if they overlap.
Too many regime switches. If the system switches regimes every time step (or randomly), you have almost no data within any single regime to discover its causal structure. Causal discovery needs multiple examples of the same regime to learn its pattern. Frequent switches starve each regime of data.
Feedback loops across regimes. If regime Z influences X, and X influences Z, the system becomes circular. The identifiability proof assumes this is handled correctly (the regime-switching acts as a break); if the dynamics are more tangled, the proof no longer guarantees uniqueness.
Regimes and causality properly separated. When regimes are distinct, switches are sparse (multiple time steps per regime), and there are no hidden feedback loops, FlowMSM provably recovers both the regime labels and the correct causal structures in each regime.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic time series with exactly two hidden regimes: regime A where variable 1 causes variable 2, and regime B where variable 2 causes variable 1 (an inverted causal structure). Make regime switches visible but not too frequent (e.g., regime A for steps 1-20, regime B for steps 21-40, regime A again for steps 41-60). Run FlowMSM on this data, asking it to recover both the regime labels (which steps are A vs. B) and the causal structures (1 causes 2 in regime A, 2 causes 1 in regime B).

regime_accuracy = steps_with_correct_label / total_steps; causal_accuracy = (number of correct causal edges in regime A + number of correct causal edges in regime B) / total_edges

The demo proves that FlowMSM correctly identifies the time of each regime switch AND recovers the inverted causal relationship between the two regimes, something impossible without regime-aware causal discovery.
