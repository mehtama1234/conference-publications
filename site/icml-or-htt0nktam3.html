ProSAR: Prototype-Guided Semantic Augmentation for Time Series

Instead of guessing which parts of a time series to keep when creating training pairs, use learned example patterns (prototypes) to guide which parts matter.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you teach a model to understand time series data (like stock prices or sensor readings), you create two slightly different versions of the same series and ask the model to recognize they are the same thing. But time series are messy and noisy. Random edits often destroy the actual pattern you care about. ProSAR uses explicit learned patterns called prototypes to decide what to keep and what to discard, and refines those patterns as it learns.

Zero assumption version

Augmentation destroys the signal

When you train a model to understand time series, you need two slightly different versions of the same series and ask the model to recognize they are the same. The trick is: make the versions different enough that the model learns something deep, but similar enough that they still represent the same real pattern underneath.

The naive approach: randomly delete parts of the series, stretch or compress it, add noise. This works for images where a cat is still a cat even if you flip it or darken it. But a time series is fragile. If you delete the 3 a.m. spike in power usage or stretch the seasonal rhythm, you have destroyed the actual meaning. A random edit that ignores what matters is not helping — it is sabotage.

The problem. Random augmentations destroy the semantic structure of time series because they do not know which parts carry meaning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Start with learned prototypes

The model holds learned example patterns (time series templates) of what real data looks like.

Step 2: Measure prototype match

For each piece of the input series, score how well it matches each prototype pattern.

Step 3: Augment selectively

Keep parts that match prototypes, discard parts that do not—this preserves semantic structure.

Step 4: Train the encoder

Pass augmented pairs through the model, learn representations that recognize the same series.

Step 5: Refine prototypes

Cluster the learned representations and update the prototypes to match what the model actually learned.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves the decision of what to keep and what to discard from random guessing to guided selection. It swaps unthinking augmentation for prototype-aligned augmentation.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Prototype-semantic coherence

measured quantity  =  Prototype-semantic coherence

This is a measure of whether the augmented views stay faithful to the underlying semantic structure of the time series. High coherence means the edits you make preserve the real patterns; low coherence means they destroy them.

The paper estimates this by checking how well each time segment matches the learned prototypes. Segments that score high (match a prototype) are kept; segments that score low (are noise) are discarded. The prototypes themselves are refined by clustering the learned representations, so the feedback loop keeps prototypes synchronized with what the model actually considers semantic.

Why it works

Why it works

The root cause of the naive method's failure is that it treats all parts of the time series the same. A random deletion hits semantic structure and noise equally. ProSAR solves this by making the prototypes the final judge of what is semantic and what is not.

Here is the chain: (1) Prototypes are learned representations of real patterns that occur in your dataset. They start as random guesses but improve over time because the feedback loop updates them to match what the model actually learns to recognize as important. (2) When you augment, you score each segment against all prototypes — high scores mean this segment looks like a real pattern. (3) You keep high-scoring segments and discard low-scoring ones. Because the prototypes encode what the model has learned to be important, this strategy preserves meaning while removing noise. (4) After training, you cluster the model's learned representations and update the prototypes to stay in sync. This feedback loop ensures prototypes keep pointing at what actually matters to the model, not what mattered to some human heuristic.

The equation

First in words:

The penalty for augmentation is: Information kept minus a weight times Information discarded. Information is measured against learned prototypes. Higher prototype match means the segment contributes real signal, not noise. Keep segments with high prototype match; discard segments with low match.

Then the compact form:

L_aug = I_kept - lambda * I_discarded, where I_kept = sum_i score_i(t_i, P) and I_discarded = sum_j score_j(n_j, P)

Every symbol:

I_keptsum of prototype-match scores for kept segments
I_discardedsum of prototype-match scores for discarded segments
score_i(t_i, P)how well segment t_i matches prototypes P
lambdaweight controlling how aggressively to discard low-scoring parts
Pthe learned prototype patterns
A tiny worked example
Segment 1: matches Prototype A with score 0.9. Keep it. Contributes 0.9 to I_kept.
Segment 2: matches Prototype B with score 0.2. Discard it. Contributes 0.2 to I_discarded.
Segment 3: matches Prototype A with score 0.85. Keep it. Contributes 0.85 to I_kept.
I_kept = 0.9 + 0.85 = 1.75. I_discarded = 0.2. With lambda = 1.0, penalty = 1.75 - 1.0 * 0.2 = 1.55.
If you had kept Segment 2 instead (not discarded it), penalty = 1.75 + 0.2 - 1.0 * 0 = 1.95—lower quality.

The prototypes are the judge: high match means keep, low match means discard. The penalty grows when you keep things the model considers noise.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Prototypes start bad. If the initial prototypes do not capture real patterns, augmentation in early rounds will be poorly guided. The paper handles this through the feedback loop: as the model learns what patterns actually matter, those learned representations are clustered and fed back to refine the prototypes, so they become more accurate with each round.
Overfitting to prototypes. If prototypes become too specific to the training set, augmentation will be too aggressive (discard too much signal). A penalty term that discourages sharp, overly specific prototypes can prevent this.
Prototype collapse. All prototypes might converge to the same pattern, making augmentation too uniform. A diversity penalty on prototypes prevents this.
Done right. With a good feedback loop and diversity constraints, prototypes stay diverse and well-matched to real patterns in the data. Augmentation then reliably preserves semantics while removing noise.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test: take a time series dataset with known patterns (for example, daily temperature with a clear seasonal cycle). Train a contrastive model using naive random augmentation and measure how well it forecasts on a held-out test set. Then train the same model using ProSAR with learned prototypes and measure again. Prototype-guided augmentation should give better test accuracy because it preserves the seasonal rhythm and avoids deleting parts of the signal that the model needs to learn the real pattern, whereas random augmentation destroys signal indiscriminately and leaves the model learning noise.

Improvement = (accuracy_ProSAR - accuracy_naive) / accuracy_naive × 100%

The demo must make it impossible to miss: because prototypes preserve semantic structure while random augmentation destroys it, models trained with prototype guidance learn to forecast and classify correctly on held-out data while models trained on random augmentation do not.
