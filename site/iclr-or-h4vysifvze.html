DiffTrans: Reconstructing Transparent Objects

A method that traces light rays through transparent objects to figure out their exact shape and material properties by running the physics backward.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

When you photograph a glass ball or a water bottle from many angles, you can see the light bend inside the object. DiffTrans is a system that simulates light bouncing through the material, then adjusts the object's shape and material until the simulated photos match what the camera actually saw. It splits the problem into three parts: first, use the silhouettes to get an initial guess at the shape; second, recover where the light is coming from in the scene; third, repeatedly trace rays and tweak the shape, refraction, and absorption until the reconstruction is exact.

Zero assumption version

Why transparent objects are hard

Light does not stop at a glass surface; it bends (refracts) as it enters and again as it exits. The amount of bending depends on a hidden number called the index of refraction. As light travels through the material, it also gets dimmer (absorbed) depending on the color and thickness. When you photograph a wine glass or a clear bottle from multiple angles, the pixels show you where light came from, how it bent, and how much color was lost. But you never see those three hidden pieces directly.

Older methods forced restrictive assumptions: the object must be a uniform shape, or it must be perfectly clear with no color absorption, or it must have only a flat surface without internal complexity. These assumptions break down in real scenes. When you try to optimize the shape and materials without respecting the physics of light (how rays actually bounce and bend), the reconstruction becomes noisy and wrong—fuzzy surfaces instead of clean edges, and materials that do not match reality.

The method in one sentence. Trace rays through the object forward and backward, adjusting the shape and material properties until the simulated light paths match all the camera photos.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Initial shape from outlines (FlexiCubes + silhouette masks)

Build a coarse mesh from object outlines; refine with smoothness constraints to avoid bumpy noise.

Environment estimation (radiance fields)

Measure how light is distributed in the scene so refracted rays know what light to bounce back.

Recursive ray tracing (forward simulation)

Send rays through the mesh; track refraction angles, color absorption, and exit paths to compute expected pixel colors.

Optimization loop (gradient-based updates)

Compare simulated pixels to real photos; nudge shape, refraction index, and absorption rate to shrink the error.

Regularization (smoothness + mesh quality)

Penalize bumpy surfaces and extreme material values to keep the mesh clean and materials realistic.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes

The paper changes three quantities together: the shape of the mesh (which vertices move where), the index of refraction (how much light bends), and the absorption rate (how much color is lost per unit distance). All three are optimized end-to-end using ray tracing. Unlike methods that guess the shape without simulating light, or methods that recover material but ignore shape details, DiffTrans treats all three as one coupled problem.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Recursive ray intersection

measured quantity  =  Which rays bounce and where they exit

When light enters the object, it refracts (bends). It then travels through the material in a straight line, getting dimmer as it goes. It hits an interior surface, refracts again, and exits (or bounces to a different face if the topology is complex). Older methods either ignore these internal bounces, or use a feed-forward neural network guess without enforcing physics. DiffTrans computes the EXACT ray path using classical optics: it solves for refraction angles using Snell's law, tracks absorption distance inside the material, and accumulates the radiance (light energy) from reflected and refracted components.

The paper calls this the 'recursive differentiable ray tracer.' 'Recursive' means it follows each ray through all its bounces until it exits. 'Differentiable' means every step of the simulation is mathematically reversible: if you nudge the refraction index by 0.01, you can compute how much each pixel changes. The optimizer uses these tiny computed changes to decide which direction to tweak the index and the mesh.

Why it works

Why tracing rays and differentiating works

The core insight is that light obeys predictable physics, and you can run that physics backward via calculus. When the optimizer tweaks a weight (the refraction index or the position of a mesh vertex), the ray tracer recalculates what the photo should look like, compares it to the real photo, and computes a gradient: a number that tells the optimizer whether that tweak helped or hurt. By making tiny tweaks in the direction of smaller error, the optimizer converges to a shape and material that looks like the real object.

Why is this better than guessing? Because it enforces the constraint that light must obey Snell's law (the bending rule), must follow straight paths inside the material, and must respect absorption (color loss with distance). A naive optimization that only cares about 'make the pixels match' could converge to a fake shape with fake materials. By requiring the simulation to use real physics, the method finds a shape and material that are not just pixel-perfect, but physically plausible. The regularizations (penalizing bumpy surfaces, extreme material values) further steer the optimizer away from noise.

Math after the picture

The ray tracing equation

First in words:

radiance_out equals the blended reflection (fraction R-prime times reflected-light) plus transmission (fraction T-prime times refracted-light-minus-absorption-loss). The absorption loss follows Lambert-Beer law: intensity fades exponentially with distance and material color.

Then the compact form:

L_out = R' * L_reflect + T' * (L_refract * exp(-absorption_coefficient * distance))

Every symbol:

L_outthe light that exits the object and reaches the camera
R'the fraction of light reflected (depends on refraction angle and material)
L_reflectthe light that bounced off an interior surface
T'the fraction of light transmitted through (absorbed and exiting)
L_refractthe light that refracted into the material
absorption_coefficienthow strongly the material absorbs light (hides the color)
distancehow far the ray traveled inside the material
exp(...)the exponential: a value that shrinks with distance, modeling color loss
A tiny worked example
Ray enters glass at 45 degrees. Refraction index n = 1.5 (typical glass). Snell's law gives the refraction angle inside = 30 degrees.
At the air-glass interface: R' = 0.04 (4% reflected), T' = 0.96 (96% transmitted and enters the glass).
Ray travels 2 cm inside the glass before exiting. Absorption coefficient = 0.05 per cm. Loss factor = exp(-0.05 * 2) = exp(-0.1) ≈ 0.905 (about 10% of light absorbed, 90% reaches the exit).
At the exit surface (glass to air): again R' = 0.04, T' = 0.96. So the light that makes it back to the camera = 0.96 * 0.90 * 0.96 ≈ 0.83 (83% of the original light). The optimizer compares this to the camera pixel value and adjusts refraction index or absorption if needed.

Every bounce and absorption in the ray tracer can be differentiated: each step tells the optimizer which direction to tweak the refraction index or surface position to shrink the error. These small tweaks compound over thousands of iterations.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Silhouette ambiguity. If the object has a complex outline (many thin parts), the silhouette alone under-specifies the 3D shape. The method mitigates this by using regularization, but a thin strand or a sharp protrusion can still be missed if no photo views it directly.
Ambiguous material values. High refraction index and high absorption can both hide internal details in the same way. The optimizer may converge to a false solution (unrealistic material) that matches the photos. Progressive training (first solve for shape with smooth materials, then refine materials) helps because it anchors the shape first, constraining the space of materials the optimizer can explore; this reduces the number of equally-good-looking-but-wrong solutions.
Occlusion and self-shadowing. If part of the object is hidden from all camera views, or if internal faces shade each other in a complex way, the ray tracer has no signal to reconstruct those regions. The method relies on silhouettes and inter-reflections to infer them; dense multi-view data is needed.
Done right: use many views, initialize carefully, regularize smoothly. When the input has many camera angles covering the object, the method initializes the mesh with FlexiCubes, and the training respects smoothness, the optimizer converges to a solution where the shape is clean, the refraction index is plausible (typical glass is around 1.5, water around 1.33), and the absorption matches the object's color and opacity.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple test: render a synthetic transparent object (a glass sphere or a clear bottle) from 10–20 different camera angles using a physics engine (like Mitsuba or pbrt). Feed the rendered photos and silhouettes to the method. Run the optimization for 2000 iterations. Extract the reconstructed mesh, refraction index, and absorption values. Compare them to the ground truth (what you know the object's shape and materials really are).

mesh_accuracy = (vertices_match / total_vertices) * 100%, refraction_error = |reconstructed_IoR - ground_truth_IoR|, absorption_error = (visual_difference_in_color_loss / max_possible_difference) * 100%

The demo must show a side-by-side render of the reconstructed object under new lighting conditions (different from the training camera views), compared to a ground-truth render under the same lighting. If the method worked, the new-lighting render should look nearly identical to the ground truth, because the refraction and absorption are accurate enough to transfer. A naive per-pixel optimization or a shape-only method will fail visibly: incorrect refraction angles, wrong colors, or visual artifacts when the camera angle or lighting changes.
