SAGE: Learning from Simplified Practice Worlds

A robot learns navigation by first rehearsing in simple physics worlds, then transfers that learned understanding to complex real rooms.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots in the real world need to navigate complex indoor spaces, but collecting training data from real robots is expensive and time-consuming. Most systems try to learn from photorealistic computer simulations, but policies trained there often fail when deployed on actual robots. SAGE flips the approach: instead of learning from detailed photorealistic simulations, the system has the robot practice in simplified physics-grounded worlds first, then transfers what it learned to real navigation.

Zero assumption version

Real robots are stuck learning from the wrong training worlds

A robot needs to move through rooms, understand language instructions, and navigate to target objects. The obvious approach is to collect lots of real-world video footage of robots navigating, then train the robot to mimic that behavior. But real-world data collection is expensive: you need actual robots, actual rooms, and human supervision at every step.

So engineers try a second approach: train the robot in a computer simulation instead. The simulation is photorealistic—it looks like real photos—so the thinking goes that policies learned there should transfer. But this fails reliably. Policies trained in those detailed simulations often crash into walls or get lost in real rooms, because the simulation, no matter how realistic it looks, differs from reality in ways the training never prepared for.

A beginner might assume the simulation just needs to be even more photorealistic. Instead, the real problem is that the robot is overfitting to surface details (exact lighting, exact object shapes) instead of learning the underlying physics: things collide, gravity works, doors have specific widths. Those deep principles matter more than photorealism.

In one sentence. The system practices in simple physics worlds first, then transfers the learned physics knowledge to real navigation.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Genesis phase

Create diverse synthetic tasks in a simple physics-grounded world, using collision-free paths and random start-goal positions.

Collect experiences

Run those tasks and record what the robot sees, the optimal path it should take, and low-level actions needed.

Evolution phase

Train the robot's policy using reinforcement learning with hybrid sampling: some experiences labeled as helpful guides, others as standard training.

Adaptive clipping

Automatically adjust how tightly the policy is constrained by guided experiences, avoiding over-reliance while staying stable.

Navigation phase

Deploy in real rooms by retrieving similar past experiences from the robot's memory, then using them to generate safe subgoal waypoints.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes what training world the robot learns from. Instead of training in photorealistic simulation (which looks like reality but fails to transfer), SAGE trains in a simplified physics-grounded world where collision, traversability, and shapes and sizes are enforced, but visual details are stripped away. This shift is deliberate: the robot learns the causal rules (things collide, paths are constrained by physics) rather than memorizing visual appearance.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: experience relevance weight

measured quantity  =  Clipping bound for experience guidance

SAGE introduces a hidden quantity it must measure and adjust: how much to let retrieved experiences influence the policy at each training step. The paper calls this the clipping bound, written as epsilon (the maximum fraction by which the policy can change when guided by retrieved experiences). If this value is too low, the robot learns slowly because it ignores useful guidance. If it is too high, the robot becomes over-dependent on past experiences and loses the ability to generalize to new situations.

The paper estimates this value adaptively by monitoring validation performance: the system measures the best reward the policy has earned so far, compares it to a target, and automatically relaxes or tightens the bound to balance learning speed against stability. This measurement happens every five training steps, so the system continuously diagnoses whether the current experiences are helping or hurting.

Paper reading. The paper defines eta as a dynamic learning-rate modifier for the clipping bound, computed from validation reward and a target reward score. As validation reward rises toward the target, eta decreases, tightening constraints; if reward plateaus, eta relaxes, allowing the policy more freedom. The paper uses an initial value of 0.8 and a minimum of 0.0.
Why it works

Why it works: three reasons

First, simplified physics worlds sidestep the sim-to-real gap. A photorealistic simulation looks like reality but breaks in unexpected ways (lighting, texture, exact shapes and sizes matter). A physics-grounded sandbox is intentionally simplified—no photorealism, just collision and traversability rules. Those rules are universal: they apply in simulation and in the real world alike. When the robot learns 'walls block paths, doors have widths', that knowledge transfers because the physics is the same.

Second, the adaptive clipping mechanism solves a classic reinforcement-learning problem: balancing exploration and exploitation. Raw reinforcement learning is greedy—it chases immediate rewards. Retrieved experiences act as stabilizers, nudging the policy toward actions that worked in similar past situations. But if those nudges are too strong, the policy never explores alternatives. The adaptive schedule lets early training trust guidance heavily (fast learning), then gradually loosens the constraint (more exploration). The paper measures this balance by watching validation reward: as reward climbs, the system knows the policy is learning, so it can afford to relax.

Third, retrieval at deployment time is a safety valve. In complex real rooms, the policy never encounters all the situations it trained on. By retrieving semantically similar past navigation sequences from memory, the system grounds the policy's next-step decision in concrete examples: 'in this kind of hallway before, we went this direction.' The paper confirms this works by showing that semantically mismatched retrieval (randomly selected unrelated experiences) fails badly, while relevant retrieval succeeds. The system automatically learns what 'similar' means by training on the experiences together.

Math after the picture

The adaptive clipping schedule

First in words:

The clipping bound (maximum allowed policy change) is set by measuring whether the policy's reward is improving toward a target, and automatically relaxing or tightening accordingly.

Then the compact form:

η_t = max(η_min, η_init × (1 − min(R_val^(t), R_target) / R_target))

Every symbol:

η_tThe clipping bound at training step t (how much guidance constrains the policy)
η_initStarting value for the clipping bound (paper uses 0.8)
η_minMinimum clipping bound, never tighten past this (paper uses 0.0)
R_val^(t)Best validation reward score observed up to step t
R_targetGoal validation reward (paper uses 1.5)
A tiny worked example
Suppose η_init = 0.8, η_min = 0.0, R_target = 1.5, and at step 30 the system has achieved R_val^(30) = 0.75.
Compute: min(0.75, 1.5) / 1.5 = 0.75 / 1.5 = 0.5.
Then: η_30 = max(0.0, 0.8 × (1 − 0.5)) = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4.
At step 60, the system reaches R_val^(60) = 1.5 (the target).
Compute: min(1.5, 1.5) / 1.5 = 1.5 / 1.5 = 1.0.
Then: η_60 = max(0.0, 0.8 × (1 − 1.0)) = 0.8 × 0.0 = 0.0.
Interpretation: Early on (η = 0.4), the policy is moderately constrained by guidance. At the target (η = 0.0), guidance is loosened fully, letting the policy explore freely.

As the policy achieves higher validation rewards (climbing toward R_target), the clipping bound shrinks from η_init down to η_min, automatically shifting from guided learning to free exploration.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Over-reliance on retrieved experiences. If the adaptive clipping bound stays too high during training, the policy memorizes the retrieved trajectories and loses generalization. New environments it never trained on will fail. The fix: monitor validation reward to detect when the policy has stopped improving, then lower the bound to force exploration.
Irrelevant retrieval. If at deployment time the system retrieves experiences that do not match the current scene, the guidance becomes harmful. The policy chases guidance that points the wrong direction. The paper tests this explicitly and confirms it fails. The fix: only retrieve semantically similar past trajectories, using the learned representation to define similarity.
Simplified worlds that are too simplified. If the practice world removes important physics (e.g., thin doorways, narrow staircases, friction-dependent motion), the policy will not learn to handle those cases. Transfer will fail. The fix: ensure the simplified world captures all the constraints that matter in the real world, even if visuals are stripped away.
Done right. When the simplified world enforces the right physics, adaptive clipping balances the learning stages, and retrieval pulls in relevant past experience, the policy transfers successfully to real navigation. The paper achieves 53.21% success rate in real indoor rooms with real robot deployment, confirming the mechanism works end-to-end.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small navigation environment with just two types of spaces: an open hallway and a room with obstacles. Create a simplified physics-grounded practice world where those spaces are represented as abstract traversable and blocked regions (no photorealism, just collision rules). Train a policy using the three-phase SAGE process: (1) Generate random navigation tasks in the practice world with A* optimal paths. (2) Collect those trajectories as experiences, split them into 'guided' (labeled as helpful) and 'standard' (raw training data). (3) Train using adaptive clipping, monitoring validation success rate. Then, deploy the learned policy on the real layout (or a high-fidelity simulation distinct from the practice world), and measure whether the policy successfully reaches target objects without crashing into obstacles.

Success Rate = (number of navigation tasks completed without collision) / (total navigation tasks attempted)

The demo must make clear that the policy learned correct spatial reasoning (how to move without collision) despite never seeing photorealistic images, because it learned the underlying physics in a simplified practice world first.
