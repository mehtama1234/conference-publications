Zero assumption version

Why real graphs break old methods

Most graph learning algorithms assume that neighbors are similar. They collect information from all neighbors equally, like averaging their labels. This works for real networks like social graphs where friends tend to share interests.

But many real graphs are heterophilous: adjacent nodes (the things the graph connects) carry different labels. In social networks, people often follow accounts they disagree with. In molecules, adjacent atoms are different elements. In recommendation networks, a user follows items outside their usual taste.

When you average signals from opposite-label neighbors, two bad things happen. First, nodes with many connections (called hubs) drown out the signal from less-connected nodes, making their labels over-confident. Second, when you stack multiple layers of averaging, distant signals get blended together until they look identical, destroying the information you needed.

In one sentence. The paper learns different strengths for different neighbor connections and applies multiple frequency filters at different graph scales.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn which neighbors matter

The model learns a signed affinity (positive or negative strength) for each edge, based on node features and graph structure.

Build a soft hierarchy

Using these affinities, group nodes into levels: coarse scales near the top, finer detail lower, keeping each node's local neighborhood intact.

Apply Haar filters at each level

At each level, apply learnable mathematical filters in the frequency domain (a transformation that separates smooth patterns from sharp details).

Skip-connect all outputs

Combine the results from every level back to the original graph using skip connections, so no single scale drowns out the rest.

Train the whole system

The affinities, filters, and combining weights are all learned end-to-end from labeled examples.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves from fixed, uniform neighbor aggregation (summing all neighbors equally) to learned, signed affinities (each neighbor gets its own learned weight, which can be positive or negative).

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frequency-wise importance

measured quantity  =  frequency importance across scales

The paper measures how important each frequency band (smooth patterns vs. sharp details) is at each hierarchical level of the graph. In signal processing, frequency means how fast the signal wiggles: low frequency is smooth and slow, high frequency is wiggly and sharp.

The method estimates this by observing which frequency components (computed via the Haar basis at each level) contribute most to the final prediction. Nodes that are far apart in the graph might have sharp, high-frequency differences; nodes that are near might share smooth, low-frequency similarities. By learning to weight each frequency band separately at each scale, the model avoids the trap of blending distant signals and the trap of hub domination.

Plain reading. A frequency is just a pattern that wiggles at a certain speed. Smooth changes are low frequency; sharp jumps are high frequency. Real graphs contain both kinds of patterns.
Paper reading. The Haar basis is an orthonormal (mutually perpendicular, unit-length) set of basis functions that separate a signal into frequency bands at different scales. The paper learns a separate filter for each band at each hierarchical level.
Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Old methods fail because they treat all neighbors as equally important and average them once, or they stack layers until distant, opposite-labeled nodes get blurred together. This paper fixes both by learning three separate things.

First, by learning signed affinities instead of uniform aggregation, the model learns that some neighbors push the prediction one way (positive affinity) and others push it the opposite way (negative). This breaks the hub domination: a node with 1,000 connections is not automatically 1,000 times more influential if most of those connections are negative or weak.

Second, by building a soft graph hierarchy (grouping nodes into levels that respect the graph's structure and learned affinities), the method ensures that local neighborhoods stay local at the finer scales, and only the coarsest scale blends distant information. This prevents oversmoothing because a node's label is never directly averaged with a node that is far away.

Third, skip connections from all scales back to the original graph mean that every layer of processing—from the very smooth, global view down to the very local, detailed view—contributes to the final prediction. If one scale starts to obliterate the signal, another scale can rescue it because they are never merged into a single, irreversible blended representation.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

For each level in the hierarchy, compute the learned frequency-domain filter applied to the graph signal (the node features projected onto the Haar basis), then sum the filtered outputs from all levels, weighted by learned skip-connection weights.

Then the compact form:

y = sum_{l=1}^{L} alpha_l * W_l * Phi_l * H_l * x

Every symbol:

yfinal node predictions
Lnumber of hierarchical levels
alpha_llearned skip-connection weight for level l
W_llearned frequency-domain filter at level l
Phi_lHaar basis matrix at level l (orthonormal)
H_lsoft hierarchy (grouping matrix) at level l
xoriginal node features
A tiny worked example
Suppose a 12-node graph is split into 3 levels: coarse (4 nodes), medium (8 nodes), fine (12 nodes).
Level 1 (coarse): Haar basis has 4 basis functions (patterns). Filter W_1 learns to preserve smooth patterns. alpha_1 = 0.3.
Level 2 (medium): 8 basis functions, captures more detail. W_2 learns to catch mid-range changes. alpha_2 = 0.4.
Level 3 (fine): 12 basis functions, keeps local neighbors. W_3 learns local precision. alpha_3 = 0.3.
y = 0.3 * W_1 * smoothoutput + 0.4 * W_2 * detailoutput + 0.3 * W_3 * localoutput.
Three scales add up to 1.0, so predictions are a balanced blend of all three views.

Each level processes the graph at its own scale via the Haar basis, and skip connections mix them back together without losing information from any view.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Imbalanced hierarchies can skew the blend. If one level has far more nodes than the others, the skip-connection weights might concentrate there because a level with more nodes contributes more signal to the sum. A carefully initialized alpha (a list of numbers, one per level) helps by pre-balancing, but dense subgraphs can still dominate because they have more edges flowing through them. Adaptive weighting per node might help by detecting over-contribution, but this is not tested in the paper.
Very large hub nodes still noisy. Signed affinities reduce hub dominance, but if a hub node has 10,000 neighbors and 90% are weak, it still aggregates 1,000 contributions because even weak affinity times many neighbors is still large total influence. For extreme real-world graphs (social networks with celebrity accounts), this might still hurt because the celebrity's true label is buried in noise. More aggressive negative weight learning helps by forcing the model to learn stronger negative weights when it detects confusion, but this is a manual trade-off because it can also make the model too confident.
Haar basis assumes certain structure. The Haar basis is good for smooth, locality-preserving hierarchies, but if your graph's true structure does not respect hierarchy (e.g., a highly tangled citation network with cycles everywhere), the soft hierarchy might not align with the data because the hierarchy's groupings do not reflect which nodes are actually similar. A learned basis could work better because it would reshape itself during training to match the graph's actual patterns. The paper uses the fixed Haar basis for computational efficiency.
Done right. When the graph is moderately large, has some local structure (nodes are not random), and has genuine heterophily (adjacent nodes often differ), HMH learns strong, stable predictions without oversmoothing or hub bias.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic heterophilous graph: 1,000 nodes split into 10 clusters of 100, but connect each node to exactly 10 random nodes outside its cluster (these are the heterophilous edges). Assign labels so same-cluster nodes are usually the same label, and different-cluster nodes are usually different.

Train HMH and a baseline (e.g., GCN with all neighbors equally weighted) on this graph. The demo should show three things: (1) HMH learns higher accuracy because it weights inter-cluster edges lightly and intra-cluster edges strongly, (2) HMH does not suffer from oversmoothing because predictions at different scales are blended, not collapsed, and (3) HMH is faster than baselines because the Haar basis is sparse and learnable.

forgetting_reduction = (baseline_oversmoothed_accuracy - baseline_from_scratch) - (HMH_oversmoothed_accuracy - HMH_from_scratch)

The demo must show that HMH's predictions remain distinct (high accuracy) even after many layers, while the baseline collapses to random guessing (oversmoothing) by layer 8 or so.
