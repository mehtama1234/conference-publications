Removing knowledge from trained models

These papers explore how to selectively delete information or capabilities from already-trained models—forgetting copyrighted content, removing biased patterns, or erasing harmful behaviors—without breaking the rest of the system.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A trained model has absorbed its training data into its weights. If that data includes copyrighted books, someone might demand you remove them. If the data includes personal information, privacy law might require you to delete it. If the model has learned biased patterns, you might want to remove those behaviors. But the knowledge is distributed across all the weights. You can't just delete one neuron. If you try, you might break other things the model learned. And if you retrain from scratch without that data, it takes time and computational resources. So researchers ask: can you selectively remove knowledge from a trained model without retraining?

Why is this hard? Because machine learning is a many-to-many mapping: different training examples affect many parameters, and each parameter is affected by many training examples. The knowledge you want to remove is woven through the network in complex ways. If you naively delete it, you will likely degrade the model's overall performance or introduce artifacts that users notice. If you try to be surgical, it's hard to know whether you got everything or if the model still retains the knowledge in hidden representations. And you want the model to forget the specific thing while remembering everything else. This is like trying to remove a thread from a sweater without unraveling the whole thing.

Researchers approach unlearning from several angles. One is gradient-based: use optimization techniques to find weight updates that reduce the model's performance on data you want to forget while preserving performance on data you want to keep. Another is to use auxiliary models or classifiers that can distinguish desired from undesired knowledge, then optimize against them. A third is to understand the mechanistic circuits that encode unwanted knowledge and surgically remove them. Some papers also study what constitutes successful unlearning: how do you verify that a model has truly forgotten something and isn't just hiding the knowledge or reproducing it in a different form? The goal is to make machines that can be corrected and updated, rather than requiring complete retraining when something needs to change.

How the work breaks down

When you train a model, information gets baked into the weights in intricate ways. Researchers tackle unlearning from distinct angles: making efficient methods that work with just the data to unlearn (gradient-based approaches), verifying that unlearning actually succeeded (testing and attack scenarios), adapting techniques for specific model types like diffusion models or large language models, and understanding the fundamental limits of what can be forgotten without breaking the entire system.

Gradient optimization and weight updates

The core challenge: how do you find weight changes that make a model forget specific information while keeping everything else intact? When you push back against the model's gradient (a technique called gradient ascent), you can steer the weights away from unwanted knowledge. But this is fragile — sometimes the model just hides the knowledge rather than truly forgetting it, or it forgets too much and breaks other behaviors.

The Smoothing paper addresses the problem by softening how the model focuses on different parts of information during unlearning, finding a gentler path through weight space that successfully forgets without damaging other knowledge. Label Smoothing Improves Machine Unlearning shows that a simple training trick (distributing the target label's probability across several classes instead of putting it all on one) substantially improves how well unlearning works, because it prevents the model from creating brittle, fragile representations that break easily. Machine Unlearning under Retain–Forget Entanglement tackles the trickiest part of the problem: some knowledge is tangled with other knowledge, so removing one thing rips out something you wanted to keep. This paper proposes methods to carefully disentangle them, then unlearn the bad part while preserving the good.

These three papers share a view: unlearning is an optimization puzzle where the shape of weight space matters. By changing how you smooth the model's focus on different information or distribute probability during training, you can make the unlearning path safer and more reliable.

Diffusion models and image generation

Diffusion models work differently than language models: they generate images by gradually denoising random noise, adding layers of control at each step. When you want to remove a concept like a dangerous artistic style or explicit content, you need to find where in that diffusion process the unwanted concept lives and surgically remove it.

Co-occurring Associated REtained concepts in Diffusion Unlearning addresses a subtle problem: when you erase one concept (like nudity), related concepts sometimes get erased too (like certain body types), which isn't fair. This paper proposes CARE, a method that tracks which concepts go together and tries to erase only the truly unwanted one while preserving related safe variations. ForceForget: Reinforcement Concept Removal for Enhancing Safety in Text-to-Image Models uses a reinforcement learning approach to push the model away from generating unsafe images while keeping normal generation quality high. It works by treating unlearning as a reward signal: penalize the model when it produces unsafe content, reward it when it produces safe content.

Co-occurring Associated REtained concepts in Diffusion Unlearning and ForceForget recognize that diffusion models need domain-specific strategies: the process of generating step-by-step through noise gives you natural places to intervene that other models don't have.

Multimodal and language-specific unlearning

When a model has learned across multiple input types (text and images together in a vision-language model) or across multiple languages, unlearning becomes harder. Information can hide in different modalities or transfer between languages, so you have to be more aggressive to truly forget.

ASRU: Activation Steering Meets Reinforcement Unlearning for Multimodal Large Language Models proposes a two-part strategy: first, steer the model's internal activations (the numbers flowing through the network) away from unwanted knowledge, and second, use reinforcement learning to optimize against that knowledge. On the Qwen3-VL vision-language model, this achieves 24.6% better unlearning while keeping generation quality 5.8 times better than naive approaches, because combining both steering and reinforcement learning tackles the problem from multiple angles at once. DRAGON: Guard LLM Unlearning in Context via Negative Detection and Reasoning works in a different regime: when you can only show the model a few examples of what to forget (few-shot unlearning), a practical constraint in many real deployments. It detects when the model is about to reveal forgotten knowledge and blocks it with a reasoning step. Influence-Disentangled Federated Training: Learning Models That Are Easy to Unlearn takes a radically different approach: train the model from the start in a way that makes future unlearning easy. By disentangling which weight changes came from which training examples, unlearning later becomes as simple as subtracting those weight changes — no retraining needed.

The three papers represent a spectrum: ASRU handles multimodal complexity head-on, DRAGON works with minimal data at unlearning time, and Influence-Disentangled shows that you can design models to be unlearnable from the start.

Testing and verification of true forgetting

After you unlearn something, how do you know it's actually gone? The model might just be hiding the knowledge, suppressing it temporarily, or reproducing it in a different way. Testing real forgetting is harder than testing performance on typical tasks.

Anchored Decoding: Provably Reducing Copyright Risk for Any Language Model provides a way to verify you've removed copyrighted material. If a model can generate long passages from a copyrighted book, that's a red flag. By anchoring the model's generation to safe units of text (tokens) and decoding carefully, you can eliminate up to 75% of the measurable copying without destroying normal fluency. Gaussian certified unlearning in high dimensions uses formal mathematics (hypothesis testing) to prove that unlearning actually succeeded with high confidence, and it does this efficiently with just a single Newton step instead of the two-step process used before. ReTrace: Reinforcement Learning-Guided Reconstruction Attacks on Machine Unlearning plays an adversary: it tries hard to recover information the model supposedly forgot. By running reconstruction attacks (trying to reverse-engineer the training data), this paper tests whether unlearning holds up under aggressive attack or whether the knowledge leaks back out. Finding that it achieves 73.1% instance-level recovery reveals that many unlearning methods are weaker than they appear.

These three papers establish that verification is essential: without testing for hiding, regression, or leakage, you can't be sure unlearning actually worked.

Efficient and practical unlearning

In practice, you often can't spend days retraining a huge model just to remove one piece of information. You need methods that are fast, work without perfect data, and scale to real deployments.

Adversarial Reinforcement Learning for Diffusion Language Models extends reinforcement ideas to diffusion-based language models, solving credit-assignment challenges (determining which weight updates matter) that appear when you layer adversarial training on top of diffusion goals. Batch and Sequential Unlearning for Neural Networks proposes algorithms that work in two scenarios: when you get a batch of data to unlearn all at once, or when data arrives one sample at a time and you have to unlearn it immediately. DUET: DISTILLED LLM UNLEARNING FROM AN EFFICIENTLY CONTEXTUALIZED TEACHER uses a smaller teacher model to guide unlearning of a larger model, cutting data requirements by orders of magnitude while keeping quality high. Divergence Decoding: Inference-Time Unlearning via Auxiliary Models takes a completely different approach: instead of changing model weights, keep the original model and use a small auxiliary model at generation time to steer outputs away from forbidden knowledge. This is a plug-and-play strategy that doesn't require retraining.

These papers span the practical spectrum: some fix efficiency during training, others avoid training entirely, and some solve the real-world problem of sequential data arrival.
