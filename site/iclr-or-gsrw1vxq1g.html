Uncovering Robot Vulnerabilities through Semantic Potential Fields

Train a separate reinforcement learning policy to actively search for failures in robot manipulation policies by treating vision-language embeddings as a potential field.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot manipulation policies often fail in the real world when they encounter situations they were not trained on. Testing these failure modes safely and cheaply is hard, because you do not know what situations matter. This paper solves that by training a second policy whose only job is to find failures: it moves through a learned semantic space by treating regions with high failure rates as targets to navigate toward and regions with high success rates as regions to avoid.

Zero assumption version

Why robots fail in the wild

A robot arm trained to pick up a cup works fine in the lab, but then fails when the lighting is dimmer, the cup is a different color, or the table surface is different. These real-world variations are everywhere, but testing every one physically is expensive, slow, and risky.

A naive approach would be to guess which variations might break the robot (darker lighting, different object colors) and test them by trial and error. This is impractical: you do not know which variations matter, you waste time testing irrelevant ones, and every physical test risks breaking something.

The core challenge: How do you find the specific situations that will make your robot fail, without expensive physical testing and without knowing what to test?

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Build a semantic map

Train a vision-language encoder (image + text) on a small set of labeled robot rollouts (which succeeded, which failed).

Step 2: Make the map smooth

Use a contrastive loss so nearby regions of the space have similar rewards: success stays clumped, failure stays clumped.

Step 3: Treat the map as a scoring field

The encoder output becomes a potential field: high scores for failure regions, low scores for success regions.

Step 4: Train a searcher

Teach a separate deep RL policy (RoboMD) to move through this field: it maximizes failure while staying curious about uncertain regions.

Step 5: Deploy and retrain

Query the searcher to find new failure modes, then use them as examples to retrain the original robot policy.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces a second reinforcement learning policy, named RoboMD. Unlike the original robot policy (which learns to complete a task), RoboMD learns to actively find failures in that original policy. It does this by probing the original policy while systematically changing the environment (different colors, lighting, object shapes) and measuring what breaks.

The hidden quantity

The semantic potential field

measured quantity  =  Smooth failure-success scoring across variations

The paper measures a hidden quantity: the semantic potential field. This is a smooth, continuous scoring function that maps any visual scene (or variation of it) to a number: how likely that scene will cause the robot to fail. It is computed by encoding the scene into a vision-language embedding (combining image and text information) and then passing it through a learned classifier.

This field is not a lookup table; it is continuous and smooth. If a dark-red cup fails, then a slightly lighter red cup and a slightly darker scene should score nearby. This smoothness is enforced by training the embedding with a contrastive loss: success examples cluster together, failures cluster together, and regions blend smoothly in between without gaps.

Why it works

Why this finds failures without physical testing

The insight is to separate the search problem from the manipulation problem. The original robot policy is frozen; you do not retrain it just to test. Instead, you train a second policy whose only job is to search. This searcher runs in a learned semantic space, not in the real physical world, so it is fast and safe.

The semantic space is the key. By training a vision-language encoder on success-failure labels, you create a smooth scoring field where similar situations (dark lighting, similar colors, similar shapes) are grouped nearby. A deep RL policy can then navigate this field efficiently: it learns to move toward regions with high failure rates, guided by the structure of the embedding. Regions where the original policy has never been tested (uncertain regions) are also explored, because the searcher rewards both failure and uncertainty.

This is efficient because the searcher does not have to guess or test randomly. The embedding structure guides it: if red cups fail, the searcher learns to try reddish variations without being explicitly told. The smoothness of the field (enforced by contrastive loss) means the searcher gets gradient-based guidance—which direction to try next—even for situations the original training data never covered.

Math after the picture

The smooth field equation

First in words:

The embedding is trained to minimize a combined loss: binary cross-entropy on success-failure classification plus a contrastive loss term that pulls failure examples together and success examples together. The reinforcement learning policy then navigates this embedding space by maximizing task-based failure reward plus uncertainty bonuses.

Then the compact form:

Loss = BCE(y, classifier(e(s))) + λ * L_contrastive(e(s)) ; RL reward = R_task(a) + β * H(a)

Every symbol:

ytrue label: success (1) or failure (0)
e(s)embedding of the scene s (a list of numbers)
classifier(e(s))learned function predicting success or failure from embedding
L_contrastivecontrastive loss pulling similar examples together
λweight controlling how smooth the embedding is
R_task(a)reward for taking action a (negative if failure occurs)
H(a)uncertainty bonus: rewards actions in unexplored regions
βweight on how much to reward curiosity
A tiny worked example
Scene 1 (red cup, dark light): embedding = [0.8, 0.2], classifier score = 0.9 (likely fail), BCE loss = -log(0.9) = 0.105
Scene 2 (blue cup, bright): embedding = [-0.7, -0.3], classifier score = 0.1 (likely succeed), BCE loss = -log(0.9) = 0.105
Contrastive loss pulls [0.8, 0.2] toward other failures, [-0.7, -0.3] toward other successes, creating smooth clusters.
RL policy exploring finds Scene 3 (red cup, medium-light, uncertain): H(a) is high, so it tries this variation even though prediction is near 0.5.

The contrastive loss enforces Lipschitz continuity, ensuring smooth gradients for the RL policy to navigate efficiently through situations the original training never covered.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Poor initial embedding training If the vision-language encoder is trained on too few success-failure examples, the semantic space will be noisy and rough. The RL policy will then get bad gradients and explore randomly. Solution: collect enough labeled rollout data upfront.
Weak contrastive loss If the contrastive loss is too weak (weight too small), the embedding will not be smooth. Success and failure clusters will overlap, and the RL policy cannot distinguish between safe and unsafe regions clearly. Solution: tune the weight to enforce clear separation while staying smooth.
RL policy gets stuck in local minima The searcher policy might find some obvious failures but miss subtle ones if it converges to a local optimum in the semantic space. Solution: use the uncertainty bonus H(a) aggressively to force exploration, and run multiple seeds.
Done right Train on diverse success-failure rollouts, tune the contrastive loss for smooth clustering, run the RL searcher with both task reward and uncertainty bonus, and validate discovered vulnerabilities on the real robot before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that the RL searcher (RoboMD) finds failure modes that random or heuristic testing misses. Start with a simple manipulation task (a robot arm picking up objects). Train a vision-language embedding on a small labeled dataset of success and failure rollouts. Then run the RL searcher policy and compare the discovered vulnerabilities to those found by random baseline testing.
