Tether: Autonomous Play with Correspondence-Driven Trajectory Warping

A robot learns to mimic demo actions in new scenes by visually matching keypoints and bending demonstration trajectories to fit what it sees.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots cannot learn quickly without human demonstrations. Tether solves this by letting a robot watch a handful of videos, then reuse those actions in new scenes automatically. It works by finding visual landmarks (like a cup's handle) that stay the same across videos, measuring where they moved, and using that measurement to bend the old actions into new actions that work in the new scene. Over 26 hours, one robot fills a data bucket with 1000+ good trajectories without human help because the warping policy can run hundreds of times per hour (each warp-and-execute cycle takes seconds), and the vision-language model filters out failures automatically, leaving only successful trajectories in the dataset.

Zero assumption version

Robots need experiences to learn, and collecting them by hand is exhausting

Today's robot policies need hundreds of human-guided demonstrations to learn even one task. A person has to grab the controller, run the same pick-and-place motion 50 times, then switch to the next task. Multiply that by a household with a dozen tasks, and you are spending weeks guiding robots by hand.

A beginner might think: just copy the same video action directly to a new scene. But that does not work. If the cup in the video was at the edge of the table and now it is in the center, running the exact same arm motion crashes the gripper into thin air. The arm needs to know the cup moved and adjust its path.

The paper's contribution in one sentence: instead of copying the motion directly, find visual landmarks in both videos, measure how they shifted, and use that shift to mathematically bend the old motion into a new motion that tracks the cup to its new spot.

In one sentence. Match keypoints in two scenes, measure the gap, and warp demo actions to close that gap.

What it actually does

Take a small demo video set

Find visual landmarks in the new scene

Run visual correspondence (machine vision that spots the same semantic features across images) to locate cup handle, table edge, in old and new scenes.

Measure the shift

Calculate the 3D displacement: how far left/right, up/down did each landmark move?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the action sequence

The old way: store demo actions as raw numbers (arm angles at each timestep) and hope they work anywhere. The paper's way: store demos plus a rule that says how to warp them. The rule reads: find the landmark, measure its displacement in 3D space, add that displacement to every waypoint in the path. This transforms the action sequence from a fixed list into an adaptive list that reshapes itself to fit each new scene.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: keypoint displacement in 3D space

measured quantity  =  Keypoint displacement (the 3D shift of visual landmarks)

When you move a cup from left to center, the cup's handle moves with it. The paper measures this movement in three dimensions (left-right, forward-back, up-down). This measurement is the hidden quantity: it tells the method how much to adjust the reaching motion.

The paper estimates this by running visual correspondence (a neural network that finds matching features in two images), then triangulating from the two camera viewpoints to compute the 3D location of the landmark in each scene. Subtract the old location from the new location: that is the displacement (a list of three numbers: left-right, forward-back, up-down). Then apply it to every waypoint in the action path.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

The core insight is that semantic landmarks (a cup, a table edge, a gripper shape) stay visually similar even when the scene layout changes. Because they are anchored in the same object, they move together. So if you measure how one landmark shifted, you can use that shift to adjust the reaching motion: add the displacement to every waypoint and the arm's path bends to track the landmark to its new location.

Why does this work in new scenes? Because it does not depend on a neural network learning the 3D shape of the table and objects. Instead it uses direct measurement: find the same feature in two images, compute where that feature sits in 3D space in each image (by using two camera angles to triangulate), subtract the positions, and apply the difference to the motion. This is physically sound and works even when the scene has objects the demo never saw (new distractors, new object instances). The method is also honest about its limits: if a landmark is hidden or the visual match fails, the correspondence gives up and the robot skips that task.

Once the robot has a source of working trajectories (via this warping policy), it enters an autonomous loop: run the warped action, check if it succeeded (via a vision-language model that understands task completion), save the successful trajectory, and repeat. After 500 tries, train a neural imitation policy on all the successful trajectories. This neural policy is more flexible than the rigid warping rule but needs data to learn. The warping policy feeds it data by running the same task 500 times and collecting all successes. More examples teach the neural policy because it is a pattern-matching system that learns by fitting the same neural weights to many examples; more examples constrain the fit better, because the weights must now match trajectories in many different poses and contexts, not just a few. So the neural policy generalizes: it can predict reasonable arm moves even in scenes it has not seen during training.

Math after the picture

The warping equation

First in words:

For each waypoint in the demo path, add the measured displacement of the landmark to shift it to the new scene. If two landmarks give slightly different displacements (which can happen if the scene has rotated as well as translated), average them. Then interpolate the actions based on the new waypoint positions.

Then the compact form:

waypoint_new[t] = waypoint_demo[t] + mean(displacement_landmarks)

Every symbol:

waypoint_new[t]the new arm position at step t in the new scene
waypoint_demo[t]the arm position at step t in the original demo
displacement_landmarkslist of 3D vectors: each is (new landmark pos) minus (old landmark pos)
mean(...)average of the displacement vectors
A tiny worked example
Demo waypoint 1 (cup grasp): arm at (10, 20, 5) in cm
Landmark (cup handle) in demo: (8, 19, 5); in new scene: (23, 25, 5)
Displacement: (23-8, 25-19, 5-5) = (15, 6, 0) cm
New waypoint 1: (10, 20, 5) + (15, 6, 0) = (25, 26, 5) cm
Demo waypoint 2 (cup lift): arm at (10, 20, 15)
New waypoint 2: (10, 20, 15) + (15, 6, 0) = (25, 26, 15) cm
The entire reaching path is shifted 15 cm right and 6 cm forward to track the cup.

The warping is a simple addition: take every arm waypoint and shift it by the measured landmark displacement. This bends the path from tracking the old cup location to tracking the new one.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Landmark is hidden or missing. If the cup is hidden behind a wall or the new scene has no cup, visual correspondence cannot find the landmark. The warping policy gracefully fails on that task and the autonomous loop tries the next task. No crash, but no data from that attempt.
The scene rotated as well as shifted. If the table was rotated 30 degrees, a pure translation (adding the same displacement to every waypoint) is insufficient. The paper handles this by computing multiple landmark displacements and averaging them; if the scene is complex, the warp may be imperfect. The paper notes this as a limitation.
Correspondence is wrong. The visual matching algorithm sometimes locks onto the wrong feature (e.g., two bowl rims look alike). A false correspondence gives a false displacement. The paper includes a safety layer (checking that the computed waypoint is not in collision) to catch egregious errors.
Done right: small datasets work. With 10 or fewer demos, direct neural learning overfits and fails. Tether's warping uses the geometric structure of the scene instead, so it generalizes to new scenes and new object instances without retraining.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple toy robot arm or simulate one. Collect 5 short demo videos of the same pick-and-place task (e.g., pick a cup, move it left). Load the demos. Now move the cup to a new location (left-center). Run visual correspondence to find the cup in both the demo and the new scene, compute the 3D landmark displacement, warp the demo trajectory by that displacement, and execute the warped path on the simulated arm. Measure success: did the arm reach the cup at its new location?

Success rate = (number of times arm reached the landmark at new location) divided by (total execution attempts)

If the warped motion reaches the moved landmark at the new location, the method is working. If the demo-only baseline fails at the same task in the new scene, the warping has bridged the gap with no retraining.
