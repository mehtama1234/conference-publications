Free Point-wise Anomaly Detection via Fold-bifurcation

Detect anomalies in time series by tracking how close a system is getting to a breaking point, using stress signals from a normal prediction model.

Anomaly detection in time series means spotting when something goes wrong — a machine overheating, a financial market crashing, a sensor giving garbage. The old way requires hundreds of labeled examples of failures, which you usually don't have. This paper reframes the problem: instead of learning to spot anomalies, track how much stress the system is accumulating toward a tipping point, using only normal data.

Zero assumption version

Spotting trouble without seeing failures

When you see a time series (a stream of measurements over time), you want to flag the moment something breaks: a machine starting to fail, a patient getting sicker, a market glitching. The naive try: train a classifier to spot bad points. But that needs many examples of actual failures, which are rare and expensive to collect.

A beginner might say: forecast what should happen next, and flag big prediction errors as anomalies. This sounds reasonable but fails quietly. It mixes two different jobs: a small error might mean the forecaster is bad, or it might mean the data is legitimately anomalous. If you average errors over windows (groups of points), you hide individual mistakes — a big error in one point can be hidden by a small error in the next point, so the window-level score looks good even though you missed an anomaly.

The core problem. You have only normal data, and you need to catch the moment stress builds toward a failure, point by point.

What it actually does

Learn to predict the next reading from past ones, using only the healthy measurements.

Measure how far each real reading diverges from what the forecaster expected.

Feed stress into a bifurcation model

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most anomaly detection methods treat the problem as classification: learn a decision boundary between normal and anomalous points. This paper changes the object: instead, it explicitly models and outputs a risk state (a single number) that rises as the system accumulates stress toward a critical transition.

the object it moves  =  risk state from stress
The hidden quantity

The stress signal and risk state

measured quantity  =  Accumulated stress toward a tipping point

The paper introduces two new quantities. First is the stress signal: how far each reading deviates from the forecaster's prediction. It is measured from the absolute error or squared error at each point. Second is the risk state: a single cumulative number that integrates all the stress signals using a fold-bifurcation-inspired ODE (a mathematical model of how quantities change when pushed toward a threshold). The risk state starts near zero during healthy operation and climbs when stress accumulates.

The paper measures stress as the magnitude of prediction errors. It estimates risk by solving the ODE forward in time, letting each new stress input nudge the risk state closer to the breaking point. This is entirely automatic — no labeled anomalies are needed.

Why it works

Why tracking stress toward a tipping point catches anomalies

Normal operation has rhythm: a forecaster trained on healthy data learns the pattern and predicts well. Anomalies break that pattern. When the system starts to fail, prediction errors grow; the forecaster, trained on normal data, has no reason to expect the new readings.

But prediction error alone is ambiguous: it could mean the model is weak, or the data is truly anomalous. The paper reframes the problem: instead of deciding is-this-bad-or-not, track how much stress is building toward a breaking point. A fold bifurcation (a type of tipping point where systems remain stable until a sudden threshold is crossed, then fail) is a real phenomenon in many systems. By integrating stress into a risk state via an ODE (a system of equations modeling how quantities change over time) tuned to this shape, the method makes the accumulation explicit. The risk state rises slowly at first because the ODE has a cubic damping term (r cubed) that keeps the rate of change slow when risk is small, then faster as stress compounds because that damping term grows and the stress input dominates. Anomalies show up as sustained elevation of risk, not as random spikes. Since the risk model is fit only on normal data, it learns what the healthy accumulation looks like, making the threshold calibration automatic and requiring no tuning settings.

The payoff is point-wise precision: each reading is judged by its stress and the risk state at that instant, without needing a window of context to hide mistakes. This aligns with how real operators think: a dangerous state is not just one bad reading, but a trend of rising stress that threatens stability.

Math after the picture

The fold-bifurcation ODE

First in words:

The risk state r evolves according to: dr/dt = mu - r cubed plus stress input. The cubic term creates the bifurcation shape. Stress modulates mu (the bifurcation parameter), pulling the system toward instability.

Then the compact form:

dr/dt = mu(t) - r^3 + s(t)

Every symbol:

rrisk state (a single scalar, the quantity being tracked)
ttime (or index in the sequence)
mu(t)bifurcation parameter, driven by accumulated stress
s(t)stress signal at time t (prediction error magnitude)
r^3the cubic damping that creates the fold bifurcation shape
A tiny worked example
Start: r = 0, threshold = 1.5, normal stress is small (s = 0.1 per step).
Step 1: stress s = 0.1, bifurcation parameter mu = 0.2. dr/dt = 0.2 - 0^3 + 0.1 = 0.3. New r = 0 + 0.3 * dt ≈ 0.03 (using dt = 0.1).
Steps 2-4 (normal): s = 0.1 each. r slowly climbs: 0.06, 0.09, 0.12.
Step 5 (anomaly begins): s jumps to 0.6. mu increases to 0.8. dr/dt = 0.8 - 0.12^3 + 0.6 ≈ 1.39. New r = 0.12 + 0.139 ≈ 0.26.
Step 6: s = 0.7, mu = 0.9. dr/dt = 0.9 - 0.26^3 + 0.7 ≈ 1.58. New r ≈ 0.42.
Step 7: s = 0.8, mu = 1.0. dr/dt = 1.0 - 0.42^3 + 0.8 ≈ 1.73. New r ≈ 0.59.
After a few more high-stress steps, r crosses the threshold 1.5 and an anomaly is flagged. The cubic term keeps r from exploding indefinitely, modeling saturation at the bifurcation boundary.

The ODE models how pressure (risk) builds up from stress, with a tipping point encoded in the cubic term.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Forecaster trained on contaminated normal data. If the normal-data set includes hidden anomalies, the forecaster will learn them and fail to raise stress. Careful data cleaning is required before training.
Sudden, unpredictable anomalies with no gradual stress buildup. Some failures are instantaneous (e.g., a sensor dies and outputs zero). The fold-bifurcation model assumes stress accumulates over time. A single, isolated shock may not cross the threshold if the stress at that one point is below the level calibrated on normal data.
Multiple anomaly types with different stress signatures. If some anomalies emerge slowly and others quickly, or if different modes of failure have very different stress profiles, a single threshold may not fit all. The method assumes one risk trajectory per system.
Done right: train on clean normal data, validate the forecaster separately, and check that the calibrated threshold separates normal from anomalous in a held-out test set. The method's strength is automatic calibration (no tuning needed) and point-wise precision; its limits are honesty about data quality and anomaly diversity.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time series with two regimes: a healthy stretch (200 points of normal behavior) and a degradation stretch (50 points where readings drift and variance increases). Train a simple forecaster (e.g., an autoregressive model or LSTM) on the healthy stretch only. Compute stress signals (absolute forecast errors) and integrate them via a fold-bifurcation ODE. Calibrate the risk threshold at the 95th percentile of risk during the healthy period. Then, on the degradation stretch, measure point-wise recall: what fraction of the actually-anomalous points cross the threshold? Compare against a baseline (flagging high prediction errors directly). Show that risk state smooths out the noise and catches the sustained elevation that marks true failure.

Point-wise recall = (anomalies flagged) / (true anomalies in test set); False-positive rate = (false alarms on normal data) / (normal points in test set)

The demo proves that risk state, accumulating via a fold bifurcation, separates sustained degradation from random forecast errors.
