Optimal Sparsity of Mixture-of-Experts Models

A model with many dormant experts that only a few activate when answering a question is fundamentally different from one where all experts work, even if both predict the next word equally well.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Mixture-of-Experts models split their brains into many specialized workers, but only activate a small number to answer each question. This paper discovers that reasoning tasks and memorization tasks need opposite sparsity setups: memorization wants as many workers available as possible, while reasoning wants fewer workers and more data to learn from. The twist is that two models with identical next-word-prediction accuracy can differ wildly in reasoning ability if one has more dormant experts.

Zero assumption version

Scaling laws miss a new dimension

For years, researchers have predicted model performance by looking at training loss (how well the model predicts the next word on its training data). A simple rule emerged: bigger model plus more data gives lower loss, which was believed to give better reasoning because a model that predicts words accurately will also reason better on hidden test problems. But Mixture-of-Experts models introduced a new knob: sparsity, which is how many of the model's expert workers are actually switched on.

The catch: a naive approach treats all path to lower training loss as equally valuable. Build a MoE with 100 experts where only 10 are active per question? Check the training loss. Build a different model with 20 experts where all 20 are active? Check the training loss. If both achieve the same training loss, you might assume they will reason equally well. The paper shows this is dead wrong for reasoning.

A beginner might try to fix this by just making models bigger, hoping that more raw capacity will automatically improve reasoning. That breaks because adding more dormant experts actually hurts reasoning performance once you get past a sweet spot.

The core insight. The number of experts actively computing during reasoning matters independently of how well the model predicts the next training word.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the interpretation of what makes a model good at reasoning. Instead of assuming training loss alone predicts performance, it shows that the amount of computation actively used during pre-training and when answering questions is a separate, independent variable.

the object it moves  =  active compute utilization
The hidden quantity

Active FLOPs: the engine power actually running

measured quantity  =  Active FLOPs

Floating-point operations per word that are actually computed. When a Mixture-of-Experts model activates 10 out of 100 experts per word, it performs fewer FLOPs than a model that activates 20 out of 20 experts, even if the total number of neural network weights is identical. The paper shows that reasoning performance tracks active FLOPs, not total weights or training loss alone.

The paper estimates active FLOPs by counting: (number of experts that activate per token) times (computation per expert) times (number of tokens seen). Then it plots how downstream reasoning accuracy correlates with this value, holding training loss fixed.

Why it works

Why more compute during training makes reasoning better

Reasoning requires learning abstract patterns that only emerge with repeated exposure. When you reduce the number of active experts, you reduce the computation applied to each token during training. Less computation means less opportunity for the model to develop the intricate pathways needed for multi-step reasoning. On the other hand, memorization tasks (like recall trivia facts) need parameter capacity more than compute — they benefit from having more weights available to store more distinct patterns.

The key mechanism: a model with high sparsity can still achieve low training loss by using its few active experts very efficiently. But efficiency at predicting the next word is not the same as depth of reasoning. Reasoning tasks require the model to learn dependencies that span many reasoning steps, and that learning only happens if the model computes enough operations during pre-training to discover those dependencies. Simply increasing total parameters without increasing active compute does not help because the dormant experts are never trained. Conversely, a model with more active experts per token gets more training signal flowing through more pathways, enabling richer reasoning.

Math after the picture

The active FLOPs formula

First in words:

reasoning_accuracy increases with active_flops_during_training, independently of pre-training_loss

Then the compact form:

reasoning_accuracy ≈ f(active_flops) where active_flops = (num_experts_active) × (flops_per_expert) × (num_training_tokens)

Every symbol:

active_flopsfloating-point operations per token that actually run
num_experts_activehow many experts fire for this token (e.g., 10 out of 100)
flops_per_expertcomputation cost of one expert processing one token
num_training_tokenstotal tokens seen during pre-training
reasoning_accuracyfraction of reasoning problems solved correctly
A tiny worked example
Model A: 100 experts, 10 active per token, 1 million training tokens, 10 FLOPs per expert = 10 × 10 × 1M = 100M active FLOPs.
Model B: 20 experts, 20 active per token, 1 million training tokens, 10 FLOPs per expert = 20 × 10 × 1M = 200M active FLOPs.
Both achieve the same training loss. But Model B, with 2x the active FLOPs, solves 15 percent more reasoning problems correctly because it had more computational work flowing through its brain during learning.

This equation captures why identical training loss does not guarantee identical reasoning: the compute intensity during pre-training is an independent variable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Assuming training loss equals reasoning ability. Two models with identical next-word-prediction loss can have vastly different reasoning performance if sparsity differs. Do not rely on training loss alone.
Adding more experts without increasing active compute. Simply making the model wider without ensuring more experts compute per word will not improve reasoning; it may hurt it because the new dormant experts are never trained during pre-training and thus do not participate in learning reasoning patterns, while the few active experts become harder to train as the model grows.
Assuming post-training or test-time compute erases the sparsity difference. The paper tested reinforcement learning and self-consistency sampling; neither eliminated the active-FLOPs effect. Pre-training sparsity is the dominant factor.
When sparsity is chosen right. Reasoning benefits from balancing total parameters and active parameters so that active FLOPs are high during pre-training, then keeping that sparsity pattern fixed through post-training.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train two Mixture-of-Experts models: Model A has 64 experts with top-4 routing (4 active per token), Model B has 16 experts with all active (16 active per token). Under equal total compute budgets, ensure Model A and Model B reach nearly identical training loss on a simple next-token prediction task. Then evaluate both on a math reasoning dataset (grade-school word problems). The demo must show that Model B, with more active experts, solves more problems correctly despite identical training loss.

reasoning_gap = accuracy_high_active_flops - accuracy_low_active_flops

The demo's core finding: two models with the same next-word accuracy do not reason equally; the one that computed more during training thinks deeper.
