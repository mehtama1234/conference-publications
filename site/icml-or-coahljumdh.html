Ellipsoidal Time Series Forecasting

Forecasting the shape of future data by learning how vectors stretch and rotate locally, not just their average values.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When you predict the next few steps in a time series, the usual approach predicts the average value. But the actual pattern can twist and stretch in complex ways. This paper learns those twists and stretches by treating forecasting as an optimal transport problem—moving probability mass from a starting distribution to ellipses (stretched circles) that describe what the data will look like. This gives you not just a single prediction but the shape and direction of uncertainty.

Zero assumption version

Forecasting needs shape, not just center

Most time series forecasting predicts a single next value: if today is sunny, tomorrow is probably sunny too. But real data has structure beyond the average. It twists, stretches, and shrinks in directions that matter—some aspects vary wildly while others stay stable. A model that predicts only the average misses this texture.

If you naively train a model to minimize prediction error (the gap between guessed and actual), it learns to hit the center but has no idea about the local shape. It might predict a reasonable average temperature tomorrow but be shocked when the actual variance is very high. On nonstationary data—where the pattern itself shifts over time—a model that learned average behavior on calm data will fail catastrophically when the world suddenly becomes wilder or stickier.

The paper's move, in one sentence: Learn the local shape of the data—how vectors get stretched and rotated—by modeling the Jacobian (the instantaneous change-map) as an ellipsoid, not just predicting an average.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Observation: local change has shape

Small differences in input produce different outputs, and that mapping can stretch or shrink.

2. Jacobian lives in a special space

Brenier's theorem says we can write the stretching-map as a factorization.

3. Model the Jacobian directly

Instead of learning a dense matrix and then factoring it, build the Jacobian as a product of rotations and stretches from the start.

4. Optimal transport as loss

Measure how well the learned ellipsoid transports the input distribution to the actual output.

5. Extract interpretable diagnostics

Read off local stretching factors, spectral radius, and volume change directly from the factorization.

What the paper changes

The thing the paper changes

The object is the Jacobian—the matrix describing how the data locally twists and stretches. Instead of learning a dense unstructured matrix (which you would then have to factor after training), the paper builds it as a product of structured pieces from the start: orthogonal rotations (which never change angles or lengths) and diagonal stretches. This avoids wasted computation and keeps interpretability high.

the object it moves  =  Jacobian structure (how change is encoded)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: local shape distortion

measured quantity  =  Spectral stretching factors

The hidden quantity is how much the data gets stretched in each direction—the spectral radius and the stretch factors of the ellipsoid. These are the stretching factors (technically called eigenvalues) of the Jacobian. Think of it this way: if you have a ball of possible inputs, the Jacobian distorts that ball into an ellipsoid (a stretched circle). The stretching factors measure how much longer or shorter each axis of the ellipsoid becomes compared to the original ball.

The paper measures these stretching factors directly from its factorization. Instead of computing a matrix and then factoring it (two steps), the factors are baked in. The model learns the stretching factors and rotation angles (the tunable numbers that the training process adjusts); they pop out of the computation without extra work.

Why it works

Why modeling the shape fixes forecasting

The core reason is this: when data changes over time in nonstationary ways (the statistics of the past do not apply tomorrow), a model trained only on average values has no defense. It learned the center of the old data cloud but not its shape or texture. When the new regime arrives and data starts stretching wildly in new directions, the model's predictions become increasingly wrong because it never learned to expect that kind of variation.

By learning the local Jacobian—the instantaneous stretching map—the model builds an internal picture of how fragile or stable the data is locally. If the Jacobian has high stretching factors (the data expands a lot in some directions), the model now knows this and can express its uncertainty honestly. Brenier's theorem lets you encode this Jacobian as a product of rotations and stretches, which means the model learns these factors explicitly rather than leaving them hidden in a dense matrix.

The optimal transport formulation adds one more layer: instead of just guessing a point, the model generates an ellipsoid (a stretched Gaussian), and the loss measures how well that ellipsoid transports the input distribution to match the real output. This pushes the model not to guess the shape randomly, but to match it to the actual data shape. On nonstationary data—where the shape itself changes—learning the shape explicitly helps because the model is forced to track which directions are wild and which are calm, so when the regime shifts, it has already learned to notice shape, not just average.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

The Jacobian matrix is written as a product of orthogonal factors (rotations) and diagonal stretches, then the loss measures the Wasserstein distance (a transport cost) between the predicted ellipsoid and the actual output distribution, penalizing both position error and shape error.

Then the compact form:

L = W_2(N(0, Sigma_pred), data_samples) + regularization on stretching factors

Every symbol:

W_2Wasserstein distance (a way to measure how different two distributions are)
N(0, Sigma_pred)a Gaussian (bell curve) with mean zero and covariance (spread) Sigma_pred
Sigma_predthe covariance of the predicted ellipsoid (built from the learned Jacobian)
data_samplesthe actual data points in the next time step
regularizationa penalty that prevents stretching factors from becoming extreme (too large or too small)
A tiny worked example
Suppose input perturbation is 1 unit, and learned stretching factors are [2, 0.5, 1].
The Jacobian stretches the input into directions: 1 × 2 = 2 units (stretched), 1 × 0.5 = 0.5 units (shrunk), 1 × 1 = 1 unit (unchanged).
The predicted ellipsoid has axes lengths [2, 0.5, 1]. Actual output has axes [2.1, 0.48, 0.95].
Wasserstein distance measures this mismatch: small gap means the predicted shape is close to the real shape.
If stretching factor became 10, a regularization penalty would nudge it back toward reasonable values (perhaps 2–3).

Building the Jacobian as a product of rotations and stretches ensures stretching factors come out directly, so no extra factorization is needed after training.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Factorization becomes unstable if the penalty on stretching factors is too weak. If the stretching factors grow unbounded (the penalty that prevents them from getting too large is small or zero), the learned Jacobian becomes nonsensical—rotations flip or stretches become huge—and the model predicts rubbish.
Nonstationary patterns the model never saw will still break it. If the data's shape changes in a way completely outside the training regime, the model has no learned knowledge to fall back on. Fern handles more kinds of shocks than average-only methods, but it is not immune to all surprises.
Computational cost rises if the Jacobian is full-rank. On very high-dimensional data, even the factorization costs compute. The paper uses Householder reflections (a trick to compute rotations cheaply), but this is still not free.
Done right: tune the penalty strength and validate on held-out nonstationary test sets. The penalty weight on stretching factors is a knob: turn it down and the model adapts freely to new shapes; turn it up and the stretching factors stay conservative. You need to find the sweet spot where the model captures real variation without stretching factors exploding to unrealistic sizes. Then test on data regimes the model never saw to find breaking points before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy time series (e.g., a 2D signal that oscillates, then suddenly doubles its amplitude, then gets noisy). Split it into a calm training period and a wild test period. Train the naive average-only baseline and train Fern. Measure how well each one predicts on the wild test set. The demo should show that the naive method's predictions collapse (it predicts the old calm variance) while Fern's predicted ellipsoid stays honest—its width and direction adapt to the actual data shape.

shape_error = sum of (predicted_stretch_factor - actual_stretch_factor)^2 over all directions

When the data suddenly becomes wild, Fern's ellipsoid adapts its shape, but the naive model's point prediction remains blind—this is the core insight.
