Decoding brain signals and neural activity

Translating brain scans, EEG, and neural recordings into readable signals—what someone is thinking, intending, or perceiving—opening new ways to understand and help the brain.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When you think, your brain's neurons fire in patterns. When you move your hand, specific motor neurons activate. Researchers can record these electrical signals using electrodes (EEG, implanted sensors) or infer them from imaging (fMRI, PET). But the signals are noisy, and the brain is so complicated that no one has figured out a simple rule that translates the neural firing pattern into what you are thinking or wanting to do.

The challenge is that each person's brain is wired differently, the recording setup changes between sessions, and the same thought might activate different neurons at different times. A model that works for one person might completely fail for another. Yet the payoff is huge: if we can decode intention from brain signals, paralyzed people could control prosthetics with their thoughts, locked-in patients could communicate, and neuroscientists could understand how the brain encodes information.

This cluster trains neural networks to map from brain signals to behavior or intention. Some decode motor cortex recordings to predict which direction a person is planning to move, so a prosthetic arm can move before the person finishes the thought. Others decode visual cortex to reconstruct what someone is looking at or imagining. Still others use the signals to classify what a person is thinking about or predict their next decision. The technical challenge is working with small datasets (brain experiments are expensive and time-consuming), handling the unique wiring of each subject, and building models that can adapt to new people quickly. Papers here use transfer learning, personalization techniques, and carefully chosen network architectures that respect the structure of the brain.

How the work breaks down

The work splits into four directions: building models that can read brain signals accurately (so we understand what someone is thinking), scaling models to work across different people (because every brain wires differently), learning architectures that match how brains actually work (since biological systems might work fundamentally differently from standard networks), and using new geometric representations to capture the hierarchical structure in brain data better (which allows models to fit the true organization of neural firing patterns).

Reading and translating brain signals

The core challenge is taking raw electrical signals from the brain and translating them into something you can understand—what someone wants to do or what they're seeing. The signals are messy and complex, with many neurons firing at once. Researchers need methods that work well even with limited data, since recording from brains is expensive and time-consuming.

Beyond Grid-Locked Voxels approaches this by moving away from fixed grid structures used in traditional brain imaging. Instead of treating the brain as a grid of boxes, it learns continuous neural response functions that adapt smoothly across space. This cuts down the amount of training data needed dramatically while also letting the model adapt quickly when you test it on a new person. Continuous multinomial logistic regression (CMLR) takes a different angle: it builds a statistical model that can capture the fact that neural responses are multimodal—that is, the same brain signal might produce several different outcomes with different probabilities. When tested on large datasets of actual neural recordings from visual and motor cortex, CMLR consistently outperforms deep neural networks and machine learning methods like XGBoost because it respects the fact that neurons can produce multiple outcomes, suggesting that sometimes the structure of the data matters more than raw model complexity.

Adapting models across people and datasets

Every brain is wired differently—the same thinking pattern in one person's brain looks different in another's. EEG recordings (electrical signals from the scalp) also vary depending on electrode placement and recording conditions. Building models that generalize across these differences is critical for practical use, since you can't collect months of training data for every new person.

ECHO tackles the multi-task learning problem: train one big model on many EEG datasets at once so it learns general patterns that transfer to new data and new people. By using a contextual sequence-to-sequence design, ECHO learns what's specific to each recording session and what's shared across all brains. This lets it adapt much faster than training a separate model for each person or dataset because it has already learned the general principles across hundreds of subjects. Mind the State solves a related but different problem: it learns to translate from EEG (which is easy to record) to fMRI (which shows where blood flow increases in the brain, giving a picture of whole-brain activity). Once trained, this translation model can run on new EEG data to infer what's happening deeper inside the brain, even without actually doing an fMRI scan. The approach even hints that it could work for new tasks or clinical populations the model never saw during training.

Building artificial brains that learn like real ones

Real brains learn through gradual adjustments using only local information at each synapse—they don't compute error signals that flow backward through the network like artificial neural networks do. If we want to understand how brains learn and build more efficient brain-like computers, we need learning rules that are biologically realistic because otherwise we're missing something fundamental about how real neural tissue works. This means using spikes (discrete electrical pulses) instead of smooth strength values, and avoiding global operations that require unrealistic signaling across distant parts of the circuit.

Training Deep Normalization-Free SNNs shows how to train deep networks of spiking neurons stably, using lateral inhibition (where neurons turn off their neighbors) to keep the dynamics in check. This works without the usual normalization tricks that make training faster because lateral inhibition creates stability on its own, staying closer to what actually happens in neural tissue. Biologically Plausible Learning via Spike-Based Distillation achieves something surprising: it uses spike-based learning to match the performance of standard error-correction training on tasks like image recognition, generation, and time-series prediction, but does it with a learning rule that real brains could implement. Emergence of Spatial Representation explores a different angle—it shows how navigation abilities emerge naturally in a neural network inspired by the hippocampus, a brain region that stores spatial memories. With the right architecture (a sequence generator inspired by how hippocampal circuits work) and sparse input, the network learns to represent space the way the brain does, opening the door to understanding why brains are built this way.

New geometric ways to represent brain data

Traditional methods represent brain activity as points in flat space, but the structure of neural data might not fit flat space well. Hyperbolic space—which curves away from you like a saddle—can pack in much more hierarchical structure with less space. This helps machine learning models work better on brain data because hyperbolic space naturally captures the nested organization of neural systems and has more room than flat space for the same number of dimensions.

HEEGNet uses hyperbolic representations (a way to encode information into curved space) to transform EEG signals into a curved space before processing. This simple idea leads to superior results across multiple real-world EEG tasks: detecting visual responses, recognizing emotions, and working with implanted brain electrodes. Hyperbolic space for neural populations shows that when you represent the firing patterns of a population of neurons in hyperbolic space, you get a model with larger memory capacity than leading alternatives—and better accuracy when decoding what the brain is doing because the curved structure captures the nested organization of neural firing patterns. Model-Guided Microstimulation takes a major step toward practical applications: it uses a model trained on neural recordings to figure out exactly which neurons to stimulate to produce desired behavior. When tested on the visual cortex of macaque monkeys, this approach successfully steers complex visual behavior, suggesting that we're close to building prosthetics that respond to intention the way the brain intends. Finally, MnemoDyn learns dynamics from large-scale resting-state fMRI data (scans when the brain is doing nothing in particular)—this is the ultimate test of whether a model understands neural computation, since it must predict what naturally happens without external tasks. MnemoDyn outperforms sequence-to-sequence deep learning methods and generalizes well across different populations and scanning protocols while remaining computationally efficient because it respects the structure of how brains actually organize activity, showing that simpler structured models can beat more complex ones when you understand the problem deeply.
