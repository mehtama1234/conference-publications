How to Price Data: A Market Equilibrium Based Approach

Price data by modeling buyers and sellers as a strategic market for a non-rival good, then ask when a Nash-style equilibrium exists and can be computed.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Data marketplaces differ from ordinary goods markets in one decisive way: the same dataset can be sold to many buyers without being consumed. That breaks the usual supply-equals-demand logic used for rival goods such as food or hardware. This paper builds a new equilibrium concept for data pricing based on strategic stability rather than standard market clearing. It studies complementary data, where several datasets are needed together, and substitutable data, where one dataset can replace another. The paper proves a stark divide: equilibrium may fail to exist even in simple substitutable markets with linear utility, while complementary markets with Leontief utility admit existence, efficiency, and polynomial-time computation guarantees. It also studies how platform mediation and price discrimination can make optimal equilibria reachable.

Zero assumption version

Why ordinary equilibrium ideas do not carry over to data

For a rival good, one buyer's purchase reduces what remains for others. Prices can therefore adjust until demand matches supply. Data is different. If seller A owns a dataset and buyer 1 purchases access, buyer 2 can still benefit from the same dataset. The good is non-rival, meaning one buyer's use does not consume the item for others. That breaks the standard market-clearing story because there is no natural depletion event that balances demand.

A second complication is interaction between datasets. In some tasks two datasets are complements: neither is very useful alone, but together they unlock value. In other tasks datasets are substitutes: a buyer can choose the cheapest among several similar sources. A pricing rule must therefore handle strategic behavior by both buyers and sellers under non-rival supply and under different utility structures.

The key reframing. Price data through strategic equilibrium rather than physical market clearing, because data can be resold to many buyers and the main question is whether no participant wants to deviate at the posted prices.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Model buyers and sellers strategically

Treat each seller as choosing prices and each buyer as choosing data purchases that maximize utility, because data can be sold repeatedly and strategic deviation is the right stability test.

Separate complement and substitute cases

Analyze Leontief utility for complements and linear utility for substitutes, because the structure of buyer value changes the existence question.

Prove existence or impossibility

Show when equilibrium prices exist and when even very small markets fail to admit them, because feasibility depends on utility shape rather than on market size alone.

Compute equilibria in the tractable case

Give polynomial-time methods for the complementary-data regime, because existence is not enough if the platform cannot actually find the prices.

Study platform tools

Examine mediation and price discrimination, because a platform may recover optimal outcomes by shaping how prices are offered to different buyers.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the equilibrium target for data markets. Instead of asking for a standard market-clearing price, it asks for a strategic equilibrium tailored to non-rival goods and then characterizes exactly when that equilibrium exists.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  deviation pressure in the price system

The deciding hidden quantity is how much incentive each buyer or seller has to unilaterally change its decision at the current prices. A posted price list is stable only if every participant is already doing as well as possible given what the others are doing. For data, that stability test replaces supply depletion as the central organizing idea.

Utility structure controls deviation pressure. With complementary datasets, raising or lowering one price changes value in a coordinated way because the buyer needs the whole bundle, which can make stable prices possible. With substitutable datasets under linear utility, one seller can often undercut another and redirect demand without exhausting anything, which can destroy equilibrium entirely.

Why it works

Why complements admit equilibrium and substitutes may not

In a complementary-data market, a buyer values a collection of datasets in fixed proportions. That means partial acquisition gives limited value, and the main decision is whether the full bundle is worth the total price. This structure can stabilize seller incentives because cutting one price alone does not necessarily steal demand unless the whole bundle becomes attractive. The paper shows that this complement structure is strong enough to guarantee existence and efficient computation in general markets with homogeneous buyers.

In a substitute-data market with linear utility, the buyer can often swap between sellers one dataset at a time. Since the good is non-rival, a seller does not lose inventory by attracting one more buyer, so the pressure to undercut can persist without a natural stopping point. One seller lowers price, another responds, the buyer shifts again, and no posted-price profile leaves everyone content. The paper proves this impossibility even with two sellers and homogeneous buyers, which shows the failure is structural rather than a large-market pathology.

Platform intervention matters because the platform can alter the strategic game. If it mediates transactions or allows price discrimination, meaning different buyers can face different prices, it can suppress some profitable deviations and guide the market toward an optimal stable outcome. That is why the paper studies not only bare equilibrium existence but also what additional control a marketplace operator may need.

Math after the picture

A strategic stability condition

First in words:

An equilibrium price is one where no single participant can switch to another feasible action and obtain higher utility or profit while others stay fixed.

Then the compact form:

utility_i(current_prices, current_choice_i) >= utility_i(current_prices, alternative_choice_i) for every participant i and every feasible alternative

Every symbol:

utility_ithe buyer utility or seller profit for participant i
current_pricesthe posted data prices under consideration
current_choice_ithe action participant i currently takes at those prices
alternative_choice_iany other feasible action participant i could take
A tiny worked example
Suppose two datasets are useful only together for a buyer.
At current prices the buyer purchases both and gains utility 10.
If dropping either dataset lowers utility to 4, the buyer has no profitable deviation away from the bundle.
If neither seller can raise profit by changing price alone, the posted prices are strategically stable.
In a substitute case, one seller may cut price slightly and capture the same buyer without losing inventory, which is exactly the deviation pressure that can destroy equilibrium.

This stability inequality is the foundation of the paper's equilibrium notion for non-rival data goods.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The utility model can misrepresent buyer value. Real buyers may value data partly as complements and partly as substitutes, so a pure Leontief or pure linear model can miss the true strategic incentives.
Price discrimination can raise fairness concerns. Allowing the platform to tailor prices buyer by buyer may recover optimal equilibria, but it can also create policy or regulatory problems because similar buyers may pay different amounts.
Existence does not guarantee practical market acceptance. A mathematically stable price system may still be hard to deploy if sellers distrust mediation or if buyers cannot estimate the value of data before purchase.
Done right. Use the equilibrium analysis with a realistic utility model, test whether the market is complement-heavy or substitute-heavy, and add platform mediation only where uncontrolled deviations prevent stable pricing.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build two toy data markets. In the first, one buyer needs both sellers' datasets together to achieve value. In the second, the buyer values either dataset separately and will choose the cheaper one because either seller can satisfy the need on its own. Let sellers adjust prices iteratively in response to profit opportunities.

The demo should show that the complement market settles to a stable price pair, while the substitute market can cycle through profitable undercutting moves because no seller runs out of stock. Then add a platform rule such as mediated pricing or buyer-specific pricing and observe whether the substitute case becomes stable.

deviation_mass = count(profitable_unilateral_deviations_at_prices)

A convincing demo should drive deviation_mass to zero in the complement case and show why that can fail in the substitute case.
