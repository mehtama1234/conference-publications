AVGen-Bench: A Task-Driven Benchmark for Multi-Granular Evaluation of Text-to-Audio-Video Generation

Evaluate text-to-audio-video systems with task-specific checks that separate visual polish from whether the generated audio and video jointly obey the prompt.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Text-to-audio-video generation is harder to evaluate than text-to-image generation because there are at least three failure modes at once: the video can look good but miss the requested action, the audio can sound polished but mismatch the scene, and the two streams can disagree with each other. Many existing benchmarks flatten that complexity into one embedding similarity score between prompt and output. That is too coarse. An output can score well by matching general mood while still failing on the exact speech content, the requested object count, the timing between sound and motion, or the musical pitch pattern. AVGen-Bench builds a benchmark around tasks rather than around one generic similarity number, then evaluates systems at multiple granularities from perceptual quality to detailed semantic control.

Zero assumption version

Why joint audio-video generation needs a different benchmark

A text-to-audio-video prompt can specify several layers at once: what objects appear, what action happens, what sound is heard, whether speech is intelligible, whether music follows a requested pitch pattern, and whether the timing between sound and motion is correct. A single coarse similarity score cannot test all of that because many outputs match the general topic while failing one decisive detail.

This leads to misleading evaluation because a model can generate attractive clips with high aesthetic appeal and still fail the user's real request. If the benchmark measures only overall resemblance, that model looks stronger than it should. AVGen-Bench is designed to expose those hidden gaps by turning evaluation into a collection of targeted tasks.

The paper's move in one sentence. Judge generated clips with several specialized checks and multimodal judges so the benchmark can ask not only whether the clip looks and sounds good, but also whether the right audio and video events happened together in the right way.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Write task-driven prompts.

Construct prompts across 11 real-world categories so the benchmark spans speech, music, physical interactions, text rendering, and other multimodal demands.

Define granular evaluation axes.

Score perceptual quality, semantic correctness, controllability, and audio-video coordination separately because those properties can fail independently.

Run specialist evaluators.

Use lightweight models dedicated to narrow checks such as speech content or pitch behavior where a generic multimodal judge may be weak.

Run multimodal language-model judges.

Use broader multimodal judges for high-level semantic matching and cross-channel reasoning because those judges can combine audio and visual evidence.

Aggregate the profile.

Report a multi-part evaluation profile instead of one score so users can see whether a system fails on fidelity, control, or cross-channel consistency.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  joint semantic controllability across audio and video

The hidden quantity is whether the generated clip obeys the full prompt jointly across both streams. A model can satisfy the video half and fail the audio half, or match both streams separately while still breaking their coordination in time. That quantity is hard to see from a generic similarity score because overall style can dominate the metric.

AVGen-Bench exposes that quantity by decomposing it into targeted tests. Specialist models handle attributes with clear narrow criteria, such as whether spoken words are correct or whether musical pitch follows the request. Multimodal language-model judges then score higher-level combined semantics, where the judge must inspect both streams together. The benchmark treats those outputs as complementary because polished generation and faithful control are not the same thing.

Why it works

Why multi-granular evaluation reveals failure that one score misses

The benchmark separates perceptual quality from semantic control because those axes diverge in current systems. A generator can produce smooth frames and clean audio while still missing the requested relation between them. If those properties are collapsed into one score, the polished output can mask the semantic failure.

Specialist evaluators matter because some checks require narrow expertise. Speech correctness, text rendering, and musical pitch control have concrete failure patterns that broad multimodal judges may not score consistently. Narrow evaluators provide direct evidence for those attributes. Broader multimodal judges matter for cases where the key question crosses channels, such as whether a barking sound aligns with the dog on screen or whether a spoken line matches the visible speaker and action timing.

The task-driven prompt design matters because not every prompt stresses the same capability. A prompt about signage needs text rendering evaluation. A prompt about music needs pitch and rhythm checks. A prompt about collisions or object manipulation needs physical event reasoning. By matching the evaluation to the prompt type, the benchmark exposes the exact capability gaps rather than averaging them away.

Math after the picture

Score generation as a weighted profile, not one raw similarity number

First in words:

A simple summary is to combine several sub-scores, each tied to one property of the prompt, into one evaluation profile or weighted total.

Then the compact form:

score = sum_i w_i * s_i

Every symbol:

scorean overall benchmark score or summary total
s_ithe score for one attribute such as speech correctness, visual fidelity, or audio-video synchronization
w_ithe weight assigned to that attribute for the current evaluation setting
sum_isum over all evaluated attributes
A tiny worked example
Suppose a prompt asks for a person saying 'hello' while waving.
A model gets high visual quality and high audio quality, but the speech recognizer says the spoken word was not 'hello' and the multimodal judge says the wave timing does not match the speech.
A single similarity score might stay high because the clip still looks and sounds plausible.
The multi-part benchmark profile stays low on speech correctness and synchronization, which exposes the real failure.

The benchmark's main contribution is not one exact formula but the decision to score the relevant attributes separately before any final aggregation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Automatic judges can disagree with humans. A specialist or multimodal judge can still miss subtle context, so benchmark conclusions depend on whether those judges correlate well with careful human evaluation.
Aggregation weights can bias the ranking. If one evaluation axis gets too much weight, models optimized for that axis can look stronger overall because the aggregate score is dominated by that one axis even when they fail badly on another axis that matters to users.
Prompt categories may age quickly. As generators improve, new failure modes will appear. A fixed prompt set can become less diagnostic because models may saturate on the old tasks while still failing on newer forms of control.
Done right. The benchmark is most useful when judge quality is checked against humans, category coverage is refreshed over time, and the full score profile is reported rather than only one average.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small benchmark with prompts that each stress one joint capability, such as speech plus visible lip motion, music plus requested pitch change, or text rendering plus camera motion. Evaluate generated clips with separate modules for audio quality, video quality, speech correctness, synchronization, and a multimodal judge for overall semantic agreement.

The demo should show at least one model pair where coarse quality ranks them similarly but the detailed profile separates them sharply, because that proves the benchmark is measuring capabilities that a one-score evaluator would hide.

hidden_failure_rate = clips_with_high_quality_but_low_control / total_clips

A convincing result is a nontrivial hidden failure rate, because that shows why text-to-audio-video systems need multi-granular evaluation rather than one generic prompt-match score.
