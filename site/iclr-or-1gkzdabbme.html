Learning Mixtures of Linear Dynamical Systems via Tensor-EM

Start with a global algebraic guess using tensor math, then refine it locally with EM, to untangle multiple hidden dynamics in neural recordings.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

A neural recording looks different depending on what the animal is doing. The brain's activity follows different patterns for reaching left versus reaching right. A single dynamical model (a math machine that predicts what comes next) fails to capture this: it either averages the patterns and gets both wrong, or locks onto one. This paper shows how to automatically discover these hidden dynamics and assign each recorded trial to the right one, using a two-stage process that combines a global algebraic guess with local iterative refinement.

Zero assumption version

One model cannot fit many hidden dynamics

Neural recordings contain trajectories recorded under different conditions. A monkey reaching left follows a different brain-activity pattern than reaching right. A single linear dynamical system (a model that predicts the next brain state from the current one, like a video-game physics engine) averages all these patterns together, getting both conditions wrong.

A naive fix is to try random starting points and run EM (a method that guesses which condition each trial belongs to, then refits). This is fast but fragile: the guesses are often wrong, and EM gets stuck in poor local solutions. You end up with nonsensical clusters that do not match the true conditions.

The paper's move in one sentence. Use algebraic moment math (stacking data into 3D blocks and solving for structure) to make a globally correct initial guess, then feed that into EM to refine the details.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build 3D data blocks

Collect pairs of inputs and outputs across all trials; stack them into 3D blocks.

Decompose the blocks

Apply simultaneous block diagonalization to extract the mixture fractions and dynamics.

Convert to state-space form

Reshape the recovered blocks back into the form the Kalman filter needs.

Run Kalman filter

Assign each trial to a component based on likelihood; compute smoothed estimates of the hidden brain state.

EM refinement loop

Update all parameters (dynamics, noise, mixture weights) using closed-form formulas until convergence.

What the paper changes

What changes: the starting guess

The paper does not invent new models or new loss functions. It changes the initialization. Instead of starting EM from a random point (which often fails), it starts from a point computed by algebraic moment math (building 3D blocks of stacked data and solving for structure). This point is provably close to the truth, letting EM refine from a good foundation rather than wander in the dark.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: correctness of the initial guess

measured quantity  =  fraction of trials assigned to the true component

Before EM runs, how many trials did the algebraic initialization assign to the correct hidden dynamics? This number matters because EM refines from wherever you start. Start close to the truth, and EM polishes it. Start far away, and EM polishes a poor solution.

The paper measures this by comparing the algebraic-EM assignments to ground truth on synthetic data, and to supervised single-model fits on real neural data. The metric is simple: count the trials the method got right. Algebraic math, by looking at all the data at once and solving an equation, produces a high-quality initial guess that EM can then improve, because there is only one global solution rather than many local traps.

Why it works

Why it works: two strengths combined

Tensor math looks at the entire dataset at once and solves an algebraic equation. It has no concept of local optimization traps, so it naturally produces a global, well-founded estimate. However, tensors are brittle: noisy data distorts them, and they ignore the temporal structure that EM exploits.

EM iterates locally: it guesses which trial belongs to which component, then refits the model to match. It is flexible and exploits the temporal smoothness of neural data, but it gets stuck if the guess is wrong. The hybrid approach removes the worst problem: EM no longer starts blind. Tensor gives it a head start at 85% accuracy, and EM refines from there to 95%+, combining the global confidence of algebra with the local finesse of iteration.

Math after the picture

The tensor-moment equation

First in words:

For each component k, the third-order moment tensor is built from stacked input-output pairs, then simultaneously diagonalized to recover the mixture weight p_k and the system matrix A_k. The recovered estimates initialize the EM likelihood.

Then the compact form:

M = sum_k p_k (A_k ⊗ A_k ⊗ I) ; [U_1, U_2, U_3] from SMD(M) recovers {p_k, A_k}_k

Every symbol:

Mthird-order moment tensor built from input-output data
p_kthe fraction of trials belonging to component k
A_kthe state transition matrix for component k
U_1, U_2, U_3factor matrices recovered by simultaneous matrix diagonalization
⊗Kronecker product (a way to combine two matrices into a larger block structure)
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 100 trials. 60 follow dynamics A_1, 40 follow dynamics A_2.
The moment tensor M encodes p_1=0.6 and p_2=0.4, plus the matrices A_1 and A_2.
Simultaneous matrix diagonalization solves M = sum_k p_k (A_k ⊗ A_k ⊗ I).
Out comes an estimate: p_1_hat=0.58, p_2_hat=0.42, A_1_hat and A_2_hat close to truth.
This estimate, though not perfect, is already accurate enough that EM converges to the global optimum.

The tensor recovers the global truth without iterating; EM then refines from this strong start.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Tensor brittleness under high noise. Moment tensors amplify noise (a tensor built from noisy data is more distorted than the data itself). If your neural recording is very noisy, even the tensor initialization may be poor. The paper demonstrates that the method works up to moderate noise levels; beyond that, starting with EM alone may be safer.
Picking the right number of components K. The method assumes you know or guess how many hidden dynamics exist. If K is wrong, the initialization is meaningless. The paper uses validation-set cross-validation (measuring log-likelihood on held-out data) to pick K, but this is a separate step and not foolproof.
Kalman filter assumptions may not hold. The method assumes the dynamics are linear and the noise is Gaussian. Real neural data is often nonlinear and has outliers. When the model is misspecified, both initialization and EM can fail.
Done right: good initialization plus EM refinement. Algebraic initialization provides a starting point close enough to truth that EM converges to the global optimum without wandering, because EM is locally guaranteed to improve from any point near the optimum, and tensor math gets you there. Neither method alone achieves this: tensor alone is brittle to noise, and EM alone starts lost.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a dataset of 200 trials: 100 from a reaching-left dynamics, 100 from reaching-right. Each trial is a sequence of 50 time steps; the hidden state is 3-dimensional (e.g., arm position, velocity, acceleration). Run three methods: random-init EM, pure tensor, and Tensor-EM. Measure how many trials each method assigns to the correct dynamics. Measure the error in recovering the true A matrices. Plot both as functions of noise level (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%).

accuracy = (trials assigned correctly) / 200 ; error = mean( ||A_hat_k - A_true_k|| ) for each component

The demo must show that Tensor-EM maintains accuracy and low error across noise levels where random-init EM collapses.
