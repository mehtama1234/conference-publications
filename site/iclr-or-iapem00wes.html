Multimodal Knowledge Injection Without Forgetting

When a vision-language model learns new knowledge from images and text, it forgets old knowledge. This paper shows how to inject new facts while keeping the old ones intact.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Large vision-language models (models trained to understand both images and text together) absorb enormous amounts of knowledge during training. But the real world changes: new facts emerge, old facts turn out wrong. The paper asks: can we teach these models new knowledge without erasing what they already learned? It builds a test (MMEVOKE) that measures this, then finds methods that work: showing related examples while teaching, and using different processing paths for different types of knowledge.

The forgetting trap

Imagine you teach someone a new fact: 'In 2024, Alice became the mayor.' The problem: if you repeat that fact enough times while drilling it in, they start forgetting other facts they knew for sure: 'Alice works at the university.' or 'Alice's daughter is a doctor.' This is called catastrophic forgetting.

For vision-language models, it is even trickier because new knowledge comes bundled with pictures and text together. Existing methods only practice teaching purely new text facts into models. They ignore the real-world problem: how do you teach a model a new image-based fact (like recognizing a new building in a city) without breaking its ability to recognize things it already knows?

The core problem in one sentence. Models that learn images and text together forget old facts when learning new ones, and no test yet measures how bad this really is.

Teach models new facts and see how much old knowledge they lose.

Replay critical data

Show the model examples it saw before to keep those memories fresh.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of teaching new knowledge in isolation, the paper changes the training data itself: it augments it by mixing old facts with new ones, and adds expert-routing layers so different types of knowledge flow through different processing paths. It also replays old data to prevent decay.

the object it moves  =  training data and model architecture
The hidden quantity

Forgetting score: how much old knowledge decays

measured quantity  =  Forgetting rate

The hidden quantity the paper measures is forgetting: how much the model's accuracy on old facts drops after learning new facts. If a model answered correctly on 100 old facts before teaching, and only 70 after teaching new knowledge, the forgetting rate is 30 percent.

The paper estimates this by testing the model on facts it learned before, after exposure to new material. It also measures 'new knowledge injection rate'—how many new facts the model actually learned correctly—and 'balanced performance'—whether the model can do both tasks well or trades off one for the other.

Why it works

Why the fixes work

The core mechanism has two parts. First, knowledge-aware augmentation (mixing old facts into the lesson) works because the model's weights are like rubber bands tied between concepts. When you pull on the 'new fact' node hard, all the nearby nodes (related old facts) feel that tug and their weights try to resist. By showing old facts alongside new ones, you reinforce those old connections directly, so they do not decay. The model learns to keep both mental knots tied.

Second, mixture-of-experts works because not all facts use the same processing route. Imagine the model has three internal decision gates, and different types of knowledge (faces, dates, buildings) naturally prefer different gates. By making those gates explicit and learnable (an expert for each type), the model can strengthen its processing path for new knowledge without touching the path for old knowledge. Data Replay keeps old memories fresh by showing old examples again, preventing the weights from drifting away.

The forgetting equation

First in words:

Total performance loss is the drop in old-knowledge accuracy plus a penalty for low new-knowledge accuracy. We reduce this by adding replayed data to the training batch and routing new knowledge through expert channels.

Then the compact form:

loss = (accuracy_old_before - accuracy_old_after) + lambda * (1 - accuracy_new_after) + regularization_from_replay

Every symbol:

accuracy_old_beforemodel accuracy on old facts before teaching new knowledge
accuracy_old_aftermodel accuracy on old facts after teaching new knowledge
accuracy_new_aftermodel accuracy on the new facts just taught
lambdahow much we penalize failure to learn new facts (tradeoff weight)
regularization_from_replaypenalty reduction from showing old examples again during training
A tiny worked example
Before teaching: old-fact accuracy 95%, new-fact accuracy 0%.
After naive teaching: old-fact accuracy 70% (lost 25%), new-fact accuracy 88%.
Loss = 25 + 1.0*(1-0.88) + 0 = 25 + 0.12 = 25.12.
After teaching with augmentation + replay: old-fact accuracy 92% (lost 3%), new-fact accuracy 85%.
Loss = 3 + 1.0*(1-0.85) + (-2 replay bonus) = 3 + 0.15 - 2 = 1.15 (much better).

The equation shows that replay reduces forgetting by anchoring old connections, while new-expert routing lets new facts learn without overwriting.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Augmentation can repeat old facts too much. If you show old facts more than new ones, the model spends more training budget on old knowledge and barely learns the new facts, because the model updates by averaging signals across all examples—when old facts dominate the batch, new facts get drowned out. The mix ratio matters because you must balance protecting old knowledge against actually absorbing the new knowledge.
Experts can specialize and fragment. If each expert learns only one type of knowledge and never talks to the others, new cross-type reasoning (combining a face with a date) breaks. Experts need light connections too.
Replay without good storage is expensive. If you replay all past data for every new fact, storage and compute blow up. You must choose which old data is worth replaying—a pruning problem.
Done right. Tune augmentation ratio per knowledge type. Keep experts weakly connected via shared layers. Replay only high-value past examples, pruned by importance.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal demo: take a small pretrained vision-language model (or simulator), teach it 10 new image-text facts (e.g., 'this building is the new city hall'), then measure how many of 20 old facts it still remembers. Compare naive teaching (just hammer the new facts in) vs. augmented teaching (mix old facts into the batch) vs. augmented plus mixture-of-experts routing. Show that augmentation + routing wins.

retention_score = (accuracy_old_after / accuracy_old_before) * 100%, and learning_score = accuracy_new_after * 100%; aim for both >85%

The demo must make it obvious that mixing old reminders into new teaching is not wasteful—it is the foundation of learning without forgetting.
