Fairness via Independence: Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence

Use a single statistical measure of independence to make model predictions fair across all demographic groups.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Machine learning models often inherit unfairness from their training data: a loan model might deny loans to people in certain neighborhoods, or a hiring model might favor one gender over another. These biases happen because the model's predictions stay tangled up with sensitive information like race or gender. This paper presents a general method to untangle predictions from sensitive attributes by measuring independence in a single principled way, rather than using different fairness rules for different problems.

Zero assumption version

Fairness gets stuck between two traps

Imagine a bank uses a machine learning model to decide who gets a loan. If the training data reflects past discrimination, the model learns to replicate it: loan approvals end up correlated with race or gender. The bank wants to fix this, so it tries fairness rules built specifically for loan approval. But then the same model is used for job hiring, and those loan-specific rules do not work; a new set of fairness rules has to be written from scratch.

Alternatively, the bank could try to make the model's predictions independent of sensitive attributes—to tear apart the connection between the decision and the demographic group. But independence is slippery: there are many ways to measure how tangled two things are (using different statistical distance measures), and each one can give different answers. Choosing the wrong measure means the model looks fair on paper but stays biased in practice.

The challenge: Either build fairness rules for each new problem (brittle), or measure independence in a way that does not work reliably (unstable).

The hidden quantity

The Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence: measuring entanglement

measured quantity  =  Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence (prediction-attribute coupling)

The hidden quantity is the degree to which the model's predictions are entangled with demographic group membership. Imagine a graph: the x-axis is the demographic group (e.g., zip code, gender), the y-axis is the model's prediction (approve/deny, salary prediction). If predictions and groups are independent, the plot is a flat scatter; if they are tangled, the plot shows a clear slope. Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence (a mathematical tool from statistics) quantifies this slant: when it reads zero, the predictions are completely independent of the group, because there is no systematic relationship to measure. When it reads higher, the predictions are more tangled with the group, because some demographic splits have systematically different prediction averages.

The paper estimates this divergence directly from the model's outputs on the training data. It plugs in the predicted values and the sensitive attributes, computes the divergence, and uses that number as a signal for how unfair the model currently is.

Plain reading. Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence is a number that tells you whether two things (predictions and group) are moving together or separately. A high number means they are tangled together. A low number means they are independent.
Paper reading. CS Divergence measures how different the patterns of predictions are between demographic groups. When you compute it, you are checking whether Group A and Group B produce different approval or salary numbers on average. It gives mathematical guarantees about the fairness-accuracy tradeoff that are tighter (capture more of the real bias) than alternative measures like KL divergence or Hellinger distance, because those older measures let some systematic bias slip through undetected.
Why it works

Why untangling predictions works

Here is the causal chain: the model adjusts its weights during training. The loss function (the target the model is trying to minimize) normally only cares about prediction accuracy. If predictions are accurate but biased, the model is satisfied. The paper adds a second term to the loss: the Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence. Now the model has two masters—accuracy and independence. Whenever the model tries to boost accuracy by tightening predictions to a particular demographic group, the divergence term says no and raises the cost. This tug-of-war forces the model to find a middle ground: predictions that are accurate but independent.

This works better than alternative measures of independence because Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence can detect and measure every way that predictions differ between groups. Older measures (like KL divergence or Hellinger distance) are weaker, because they use formulas that average over the differences and can hide parts of the gap. Imagine two schools: one gives everyone a B, the other gives half A and half C. By average grade they look the same, but the variation is completely different. Older divergences sometimes miss this kind of spread-out bias. CS Divergence detects it because it directly compares the full patterns of prediction numbers. So when CS Divergence is low, the model is truly fair, not just fair on some incomplete measure.

Math after the picture

The fairness objective in one equation

First in words:

Total loss equals accuracy loss plus a penalty weight times the Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence between the predictions and the sensitive attribute.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_accuracy + lambda * CS_Divergence(predictions, sensitive_attr)

Every symbol:

L_totalthe combined training loss the model tries to minimize
L_accuracystandard accuracy loss, e.g. cross-entropy for classification
lambdaa knob controlling how much fairness matters (higher = fairer, lower = more accurate)
CS_Divergencethe Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence, measuring entanglement between predictions and demographics
predictionsthe model output for each example
sensitive_attrthe demographic group for each example, e.g. 0=Group A, 1=Group B
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model makes five predictions for a two-group population:
Example 1: prediction=0.8, group=A
Example 2: prediction=0.75, group=A
Example 3: prediction=0.2, group=B
Example 4: prediction=0.25, group=B
Example 5: prediction=0.3, group=B
Accuracy loss (how wrong the predictions are): suppose this is 0.15.
Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence (entanglement with group): the math computes this as 0.35 (high, because Group A predictions cluster near 0.8, Group B near 0.25).
If lambda = 0 (fairness ignored): L_total = 0.15 + 0 * 0.35 = 0.15.
If lambda = 1 (fairness weighted equally): L_total = 0.15 + 1 * 0.35 = 0.50.
Higher total loss pushes the model to adjust weights so Group B predictions rise toward 0.5, lowering the divergence.

The divergence penalty pushes predictions toward independence by raising the cost whenever accuracy and group membership stay tangled.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The fairness-accuracy tradeoff bites hard. Making predictions completely independent of demographics often means sacrificing accuracy. Group A and Group B may have genuinely different base rates in the real world; forcing predictions to be identical erases true information. The paper tries to find the sweet spot by tuning lambda, but there is no automatic answer.
Hidden attributes are invisible. The method only works if you declare which attributes are sensitive (race, gender, age, etc.). If a correlated attribute goes undeclared—say, zip code as a proxy for race—the model can still learn to use it, and unfairness leaks through the back door.
Cauchy-Schwarz Divergence assumes continuous or categorical predictions. The divergence formulation works for regression (real number predictions) or discrete categories. If the model output is a weird shape—e.g. a ranking or a tree structure—the divergence may not apply cleanly.
Done right: measure often and tune lambda per use case. The best practice is to measure fairness and accuracy on a held-out validation set, tune lambda to match the acceptable tradeoff for your domain, and regularly audit real-world outcomes to catch hidden proxies.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy classification task where a neural network predicts loan approval (yes/no) based on income and age, but the training data is biased: applicants in Group A (say, zip code 90210) are approved 70% of the time, while Group B (zip code 60601) are approved at only 20%, even with identical income and age. Train the model twice: once with no fairness penalty (lambda set to zero), once with fairness penalty enabled (lambda set to 0.5). Plot the approval rates by group on both models.

Fairness metric = |approval_rate_GroupA - approval_rate_GroupB|. Lower is fairer. Accuracy = fraction of correct predictions.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: the baseline model (lambda=0) shows the approval-rate gap of 50% or more across groups, while the fairness-penalized model (lambda=0.5) narrows it to under 10%, proving that the Cauchy-Schwarz penalty actually untangles predictions from demographics.
