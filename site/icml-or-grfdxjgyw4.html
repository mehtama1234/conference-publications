A Computational Framework for Evaluating Human-likeness in LLMs' Open-ended Human Behaviors

Evaluate whether agent behavior matches human behavior by comparing large behavior patterns and by measuring rationality, consistency, and diversity across open-ended tasks.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

This paper addresses a basic evaluation problem for agent simulations: when a language-model agent shops, answers questions, or moves through a city, how should we judge whether its behavior looks human? One-off challenge prompts are weak because they depend too much on the challenge writer and do not scale across many settings. The paper instead compares model behavior with large collections of real human behavior data and scores three properties that matter in open-ended activity: whether actions make sense for the situation, whether choices stay coherent over time, and whether the population of behaviors is broad enough to resemble real human variation.

Zero assumption version

Why simple human judgments do not scale

Many people judge human-likeness with a Turing-style test or a few hand-built scenarios. That approach can be biased because the chosen questions may favor some systems and miss important behaviors.

Open-ended human activity also has population structure. A believable agent should not only produce one plausible answer. It should produce patterns of action that resemble how many humans behave across many cases.

The paper's move in one sentence. Replace one-off human judgment with population-level comparison between agent behavior and large real human behavior data.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes evaluation from a few hand-built tests into a population-level comparison between model and human behavior.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Gap between model behavior patterns and human behavior patterns

The key hidden quantity is how far the overall pattern of model behavior is from the overall pattern of human behavior. A single realistic answer does not guarantee that the broader behavior pattern is realistic.

The framework estimates this distance by checking multiple principles. Rationality asks whether actions fit the situation. Consistency asks whether choices remain coherent over time. Diversity asks whether the model population covers enough of the variation seen in people.

Why it works

Why this works: population-level comparison reveals failures that sample-level tests miss

A few example interactions can make a model look convincing even when its wider behavior population is narrow, repetitive, or inconsistent. Population-level comparison is stronger because it asks whether the model reproduces the broad structure of human behavior across many cases.

Using several principles is also important. An agent can look rational in isolated steps but still fail because its decisions drift over time or because every agent instance behaves too similarly. The framework separates these issues so that evaluation is more diagnostic.

Math after the picture

Combine several behavior principles into one evaluation view

First in words:

A compact view is to give higher human-likeness to a model when its behavior distribution stays close to the human distribution on rationality, consistency, and diversity, while subtracting a penalty for mismatches.

Then the compact form:

human_like_score = rationality_match + consistency_match + diversity_match - distribution_gap

Every symbol:

human_like_scoreoverall evaluation of how human-like the model behavior is
rationality_matchhow often the model's actions make sense for the situation compared with people
consistency_matchhow well the model preserves coherent choices over time compared with people
diversity_matchhow much of the human variation the model population covers
distribution_gapremaining mismatch between the model behavior distribution and the human distribution
A tiny worked example
Suppose a shopping agent chooses sensible products in most cases, so rationality match is high.
But it repeats nearly the same preference pattern for every user, so diversity match is low.
It also contradicts its earlier choices in some long sessions, so consistency match is only moderate.
The overall score remains limited because looking plausible in one dimension is not enough.

The paper uses generic metrics under three principles rather than this exact single formula, but the formula captures how the framework combines those principles.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Human data can be biased or incomplete. If the collected behavior data reflects only part of the population, the framework may judge models against a narrow target rather than against people more broadly.
Good aggregate matching can hide bad individual cases. A model could match the overall behavior pattern while still behaving poorly for some subgroups, so population-level scores should be paired with subgroup checks.
Domain transfer is not guaranteed. Performance in shopping, question answering, or mobility may not predict performance in other human activities because each domain has its own behavior structure.
Done right. The paper is useful because it turns human-likeness into a scalable computational evaluation problem rather than a handful of ad hoc tests.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small evaluation setup with one open-ended domain, such as shopping. Gather a human behavior set from recorded sessions, then run several agent systems under the same task prompts. Measure situation fit, choice coherence across time, and population spread across many runs.

The demo should show that two agents with similar sample-level quality can differ sharply in population-level human-likeness because one may repeat narrow patterns while the other covers a broader range of coherent behavior.

gap = human_distribution_score - model_distribution_score

The demo must prove that human-likeness should be evaluated on broad behavior patterns, not only on a few handpicked examples.
