Building stronger models for graph data

Papers developing new types of neural networks that learn better from graph-structured data by improving how information flows between connected nodes and capturing long-range relationships.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

The core insight is that information in a graph lives in the connections, not just the nodes themselves. A person's interests may be similar to their friends' interests, a molecule's properties depend on how its atoms are bonded, and a webpage's topic relates to the pages it links to. So a model needs a way to collect information from neighbors and use it to make better predictions. Most early work did this by averaging neighbors' features at each layer—but averaging loses detail and doesn't capture the many subtle ways that different types of connections matter differently.

The hard part is doing this efficiently at scale while capturing long-range patterns. If you average neighbors, then average neighbors of neighbors, the signal can get diluted—like a game of telephone where the message gets garbled. Also, sometimes a node's most important influence comes from far away in the network, not from its immediate neighbors. And different edges may mean different things (a 'likes' edge is not a 'disagrees with' edge), but simple averaging treats all edges the same way without capturing these distinctions.

Papers in this subtheme propose new designs: better ways to aggregate neighbor information using attention—learning which neighbors matter most—spectral methods that think about the graph's mathematical structure, transformer-based approaches that let distant nodes talk directly so long-range dependencies aren't lost, and techniques to preserve information flow so distant patterns don't fade away. The goal is a model that captures both what each node is locally and how it fits into the overall shape and structure of the entire network, since this context shapes the node's role.

How the work breaks down

The work in this subtheme splits into five natural directions: (1) improving how neural networks handle long-range relationships in graphs, since local averaging alone misses distant but important connections; (2) handling heterophilic graphs where similar nodes are not necessarily connected, overcoming the assumption that neighbors resemble each other; (3) capturing geometric and spectral structure more faithfully through equivariance and frequency-based methods; (4) building more efficient and scalable architectures that work with large graphs without losing expressivity; and (5) designing specialized GNN approaches for particular problems like anomaly detection, link prediction, and dynamic graphs.

Fixing long-range signal propagation

When graphs get deep, information from distant nodes gets harder to reach. The LRIM benchmark paper tests both traditional message-passing and transformer-based graph models (which use learned weights to focus on important nodes, similar to how language models focus on important words) and shows they all perform far worse than they should on long-range tasks — even on carefully designed physics-based problems. This reveals a fundamental weakness: local information alone is not enough, because distant signals matter but don't get preserved through layers. The Message Passing on the Edge paper addresses this by rethinking which parts of the graph are worth learning on: instead of averaging all neighbors equally, it learns on edges themselves and achieves better ability to distinguish graphs than standard approaches while staying computationally efficient. The over-squashing paper tackles a different angle: it shows that existing methods to measure bottlenecks in graph networks miss 30-40% of the actual problems because they use measurements that can't detect all choke points, and it proposes a much faster way to find where information gets stuck. Finally, S³GNN combines two strategies — mixing information globally across the whole graph while keeping local message passing intact — achieving order-of-magnitude error reductions on long-range benchmarks with fewer internal numbers to learn.

These four papers converge on the same insight: deep graphs need both local detail and global context. The LRIM paper diagnoses the problem, the Message Passing on the Edge paper rethinks the architecture, the over-squashing paper clarifies where bottlenecks hide, and S³GNN shows a concrete solution that combines global and local mixing to fix the issue.

Learning universal representations across graphs

A major goal in graph learning is to build models that generalize across different graphs without retraining on each one. The Computational Reducibility paper approaches this by asking: can we pre-train on many graph problems and then transfer that knowledge to new targets? It shows that leave-one-out pre-training (training on all but one problem, then testing on that one without modifying the model) avoids harming performance from conflicts between tasks and actually speeds up learning on new problems because the model has already seen related patterns. The NodePFN paper takes a different path: it trains a single neural network function on synthetic graph distributions (generated graphs that exhibit a range of patterns) and then applies it directly to any new graph without any retraining — achieving 71% accuracy on 23 benchmarks across completely different graph types, so the model has learned patterns general enough to work on unseen graphs because it learned from diverse synthetic examples. GraphPFN extends this to a full foundation model, showing best-in-class results on both fine-tuning (training further on a specific task) and zero-shot in-context learning (using the model without any training on the target task) regimes.

All three papers share a goal: building models that work across many different graphs without starting from scratch each time. The Computational Reducibility paper shows transfer learning helps because shared patterns exist; NodePFN demonstrates you can skip training entirely on new graphs if you pre-train on diverse examples; and GraphPFN combines both strengths into a single pre-trained foundation model for arbitrary graphs.

Handling heterophilic graphs and flipped assumptions

Many successful graph neural networks assume that connected nodes are similar — neighbors resemble each other. But in real graphs, this often fails. Social networks have disagreements between friends; web pages link to opposites they critique; brain regions connect across different functional types. The Unified Framework for Deep Hypergraph Clustering paper tackles this on hypergraphs (where groups of nodes connect, not just pairs) and shows it can handle both homophilic (similar-neighbors) and heterophilic (dissimilar-neighbors) graphs better than alternatives by adapting how it clusters nodes in each setting. The Graph Homophily Booster paper takes a bold step: instead of designing new architectures, it transforms the graph itself to improve homophily — making neighbors more similar before applying any model, which outperforms 23 modern GNNs on heterophilic data because it fixes the input assumption rather than the model. The H³GNNs paper provides theory: it shows how to design models that work on both homophilic and heterophilic graphs by learning node encodings that separate structure from feature similarity, so the model adapts to whatever pattern is present in the data.

These three papers flip the assumption that neighbors are alike. The hypergraph clustering paper adapts architecture for both patterns; the homophily booster paper transforms the input graph itself; and H³GNNs builds a unified theory that handles both regimes through learned encodings.

Geometric, equivariant, and spectral approaches

Graphs embed relationships and physical structure — think of molecular atoms in 3D space or networks with inherent directional flow. Capturing this structure can unlock better learned patterns because the model learns to respect constraints that are already present in the data. The AdS-GNN paper demonstrates that conformal symmetries (mathematical properties about scaling and angles that don't change the shape) can be extracted from data, improving generalization on computer vision and physics tasks because enforcing these constraints reduces the model's job. The Foundations of Equivariant Deep Learning paper provides the first universal approximation theorem for sheaf neural networks (a proof that these models can learn any function that respects certain structure), unifying graph neural networks with sheaf theory and showing how to use category-based symmetries including non-reversible ones, so the model can exploit whatever symmetries actually exist and learn more efficiently because it respects them. The Full-Spectrum GNNs paper proves that learning the full frequency composition of a graph (what signals exist at each frequency band) lets you approximate any node-pair signal, and the approach scales efficiently to real benchmarks, especially on heterophilic graphs where classical methods fail. The Vector Diffusion Wavelets paper uses wavelets — the idea of decomposing signals into different frequency bands — to build geometric neural networks for point clouds, physics measurements, and other data that lives on curved surfaces.

These four papers all capture structure and spectral properties. AdS-GNN and Equivariant Deep Learning use symmetry; Full-Spectrum and Wavelets use frequency bands and scales. Together they show that thinking about what properties the data actually has — not just treating graphs as abstract node connections — lets the model learn more efficiently because it respects the constraints already present in the real-world structure.

Specialized GNNs for specific tasks and domains

Beyond general-purpose graph models, specialized designs solve particular problems. The MIMO-LP paper accelerates subgraph link prediction by 14-44x using a batching strategy that processes multiple inputs and outputs at once so the model doesn't need to repeat work for each edge, enabling fast-and-accurate learning to predict new edges. The Diffusion-based Combinatorial Optimization paper tackles constraint satisfaction: it uses diffusion models (the same process used in image generation) to generate solutions while adaptively controlling intermediate steps, keeping solutions feasible at every step because it learns to steer toward valid regions. The Discrete Prompting paper shows you can prompt graph neural networks the way you prompt language models — using discrete prompts (input pieces that tell the model what task to solve) that guide the model on few-shot tasks (learning from just a handful of examples), so the model can adapt to new tasks without retraining because it has learned how to interpret instructions, and this approach avoids the mismatch between training and testing that often breaks neural networks because the model sees prompt-based guidance during training too. The EquiCAD paper brings rotation-and-translation symmetry to 3D shape classification, so the model can recognize that a rotated shape is the same shape, which improves performance on real engineering shape datasets. The Error-Driven Graph Augmentation paper solves a physics problem: accelerating CFD (computational fluid dynamics) and structural dynamics simulations using graph networks that automatically identify physically challenging regions by looking at where the error is largest, so it allocates computation wisely. Finally, Smoothness Errors in Dynamics Models proposes a way to avoid error accumulation when predicting how systems evolve on mesh structures and in weather, outperforming both transformer-based models (which use learned weights to focus on important regions) and hand-crafted symmetric networks because it respects the continuity properties of physics.

These six papers show the breadth of graph learning: speeding link prediction, solving puzzles with constraints, prompting graphs like language models, classifying 3D shapes, accelerating physics simulations, and improving time-series forecasting on meshes. Each demonstrates that specialized design for the specific problem unlocks performance on real applications.
