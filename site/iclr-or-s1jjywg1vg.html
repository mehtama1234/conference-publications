Data-to-Energy Schrödinger Bridges

A method to learn how to transform one probability distribution into another when one (or both) is given only as an energy function, not as a pile of sample points.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Imagine you have a pile of starting images (a distribution—meaning the pattern of how often each image occurs) and want to transform them into ending images via a random walk. But what if the ending state is not available as examples—only as an energy function describing which states are low-cost? This paper solves that by generalizing iterative proportional fitting (a classical algorithm from transportation) to work without sample access.

Zero assumption version

Bridging distributions when one is locked away

A classical problem in transportation: transform a starting distribution into an ending one using a random walk that minimizes cost. This matters for diffusion models, sampling from physics simulations, and image-to-image translation. The catch: existing methods need actual sample data from both the starting and ending distributions.

But many real scenarios give only one distribution (starting images) plus an ending distribution as an energy function—a single number describing how cheap or expensive each state is. There are no ending-state samples to learn from. A naive approach: sample directly from the energy (but this is expensive and might get stuck). The paper's trick avoids sampling the target directly.

In one sentence. Learn the bridge by iteratively fitting forward and backward trajectories, using only starting samples and the ending energy.

What it actually does

Two neural networks; one learns forward drift, one learns reverse.

Forward step: drift toward low energy

Train forward net using trajectories from data and energy, minimizing variance of log-likelihood ratio.

Reverse step: prepare for forward motion

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of requiring samples from both distributions, the paper generalizes iterative proportional fitting (IPF) to accept an energy function for one or both endpoints. The algorithm learns two neural nets (forward and reverse drift functions) that compose a smooth random-walk trajectory from start to end, without ever needing end-state samples.

the object it moves  =  Iterative proportional fitting
The hidden quantity

The drift force—how hard to push in each direction

measured quantity  =  Drift force (direction and speed of each step)

Every random walk is made of tiny steps. The drift force (learned by the neural net) controls the direction and speed of each step at each time point. The paper measures how well the drift force aligns with the energy function: a good drift pulls states toward low-energy regions smoothly.

The algorithm estimates this by comparing the log-likelihood of trajectories under the forward drift to the log-likelihood under the reverse drift. A mismatch means the drifts are not in sync; closing that gap is what IPF does.

Plain reading. The drift is a learned direction (a vector—just a list of numbers describing which way to go and how hard to push) at each point in space and time, pulling random walkers toward zones where the ending energy is low.
Paper reading. The drift function is optimized via a log-likelihood objective (equation 6a, 8) that ensures trajectories sampled from the forward SDE (random differential equation) have high likelihood under the reverse bridge model.
Why it works

Why it works: iterative correction of drift

The core insight: a proper bridge has a property called balance—forward trajectories sampled from start should be easy for the reverse model to explain, and vice versa. IPF enforces this by alternating: train forward drift using the reverse net as a critic, then train reverse drift using real starting data. Each iteration shrinks the mismatch.

Why avoid sampling the ending energy directly? Because you would need millions of energy samples (slow and hard). Instead, IPF learns the bridge indirectly by playing forward-and-backward nets against each other until they agree on one smooth path. The off-policy tricks (replaying reverse trajectories, mixing on-policy and off-policy batches) stabilize this loop so learning does not collapse.

Math after the picture

The forward step objective

First in words:

The variance of the log-likelihood ratio, averaged over trajectories and starting points

Then the compact form:

L(θ) = E_{x_0} E_{τ^(1),...,τ^(N)} [ Var_i log(p_θ(τ^(i)|x_0) / p_φ(τ^(i)|x_1^(i))) + E_1(x_1^(i)) ]

Every symbol:

θparameters of the forward drift net
φparameters of the reverse drift net
τ^(i)a trajectory sample (a path from start to end)
x_0starting state sampled from data
x_1^(i)ending state after following trajectory i
p_θ(τ|x_0)likelihood of trajectory under forward drift
p_φ(τ|x_1)likelihood of trajectory under reverse drift
E_1(x)ending energy function (how good is state x)
Var_ivariance over N trajectories in a batch
A tiny worked example
Imagine two trajectories from a starting point (0):
Trajectory 1: log-likelihood ratio = 2.0, ending energy = 0.5, loss = 2.0^2 + 0.5 = 4.5
Trajectory 2: log-likelihood ratio = 0.5, ending energy = 0.3, loss = 0.5^2 + 0.3 = 0.55
Variance over batch = (4.5 - 2.5)^2 + (0.55 - 2.5)^2 ≈ 4.0 + 3.8 = 7.8
High variance means the trajectories disagree; training lowers this by adjusting θ, making all paths follow the same drift.

Minimizing this variance-based loss forces the forward drift to learn paths that the reverse net also approves of, without ever needing reverse samples.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Off-policy instability. If the replay buffer is too small or trajectories become stale, the forward net may diverge from the reverse net. Fix: use large buffers, refresh with Langevin updates (a light noise-based sampler), and mix on-policy and off-policy batches.
Energy function is badly scaled. If the ending energy has extreme values (very large or very small numbers), the variance-based loss becomes uninformative. Fix: normalize or rescale the energy before training.
Diffusion coefficient mismatch. The paper proposes learning the diffusion coefficient (how noisy each step is) jointly with the drift. If this is not tuned carefully, the reverse net may see trajectories that are too smooth or too wiggly to match.
Done right. When off-policy buffers are refreshed, energies are normalized, and both drift and diffusion are learned, the bridge converges to a stable transport that is fast, accurate, and does not require ending samples.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo: learn a bridge from a starting distribution (e.g., a 2D Gaussian centered at the origin) to an ending distribution given only as an energy function (e.g., a mixture of two Gaussians far apart, defined by their energy values). Train the forward and reverse nets using IPF. Sample from the learned forward net. Measure: (1) how many samples land in low-energy zones, (2) how many ending-distribution modes are covered, (3) comparison to naive energy sampling.

Ending energy coverage = fraction of samples with energy < threshold / (true ending distribution quantile) * 100%

The key finding: IPF learns to reach the ending distribution without ever sampling from it directly, reaching low-energy regions in dozens of steps instead of thousands.
