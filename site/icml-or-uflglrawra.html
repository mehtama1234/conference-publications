The (Marginal) Value of a Search Ad: An Online Causal Framework for Repeated Second-price Auctions

Bid on the extra sales caused by winning the ad slot, not on total sales, and learn that marginal value online from second-price auction feedback.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Search advertising creates a causal problem, not just a forecasting problem. If a user would have bought anyway through the advertiser's organic result, paying to win the sponsored slot may add little value. A good bid should therefore reflect the difference between two outcomes: what happens if the ad is shown and what happens if it is not shown. This paper builds online bidding algorithms around that treatment effect and studies how the auction's second-price rule reveals information that makes learning easier.

Zero assumption version

Why total revenue is the wrong target

In a search auction, an advertiser sees an opportunity to bid for a sponsored slot next to the search results. A common bidding rule values that opportunity by the revenue observed after a click or after an impression. That is incomplete, because a user may still reach the advertiser through the unpaid organic result even if the advertiser loses the auction.

The real value of winning is therefore marginal value: the extra outcome caused by paid exposure. Formally, it is the difference between the outcome under win and the outcome under loss. If that difference is small, aggressive bidding wastes budget even when observed post-click revenue is high.

The challenge is online learning under partial feedback. On each repeated auction, the bidder chooses one bid and observes only the branch that actually happened. The learner must therefore estimate a causal effect while simultaneously competing in the auction.

The paper in one sentence. Treat the value of an ad as the gain caused by winning instead of the total revenue after winning, then learn bids that target that causal gain in repeated second-price auctions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define value causally

For each auction, the target is the outcome under win minus the outcome under loss, which means the learner focuses on incremental effect.

Choose a bid online

At each round, submit a bid based on the current estimate of that incremental effect.

Observe auction feedback

In a second-price auction, winning reveals the payment threshold set by the next highest bid, which carries more information than a simple win or loss indicator.

Update the causal estimate

Use the observed branch and the threshold information to reduce uncertainty about the value of similar opportunities.

Control regret over time

Design the learning rule so its cumulative loss against the best causal bidding policy grows at the rate proven to be optimal for the feedback model.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the meaning of ad value. Instead of using observed revenue after exposure, it uses the gain attributable to exposure relative to the no-exposure outcome.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: counterfactual gain

measured quantity  =  the outcome difference between win and loss

The auction only reveals one branch per round. You either win and observe the paid-exposure outcome, or you lose and observe the no-paid-exposure outcome. The missing branch is the counterfactual branch, which means the unrealized outcome under the opposite auction result.

The learner therefore must estimate the treatment effect for each opportunity: how much extra value winning causes beyond what organic exposure would already deliver. That difference is the hidden quantity the bidding rule actually needs.

Why it works

Why second-price feedback helps

Second-price auctions have a useful property: if you win, you pay the second-highest bid rather than your own. That payment reveals the exact threshold you had to beat on that round. The learner therefore gets more than a binary success label. It sees where the decision boundary was.

That extra information matters for regret because bidding is an online decision problem with censored outcomes. In a first-price setting, the learner often knows only that its own bid was above or below the market threshold. In a second-price setting, the realized payment gives the threshold directly on wins, which means the learner can localize the profitable bidding region faster.

The causal definition of value matters just as much. If the learner targets total revenue, it systematically overbids when organic outcomes are strong. By targeting incremental gain, the algorithm aligns spending with what paid exposure actually changes.

The paper combines both points: causal value sets the right target, and second-price threshold feedback improves how fast that target can be learned online because winning reveals the market threshold instead of only a win indicator.

The value to bid on

First in words:

For one auction opportunity, bid according to the expected treatment effect, which is the outcome under winning minus the outcome under losing.

Then the compact form:

marginal_value(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | x]

Every symbol:

xobserved auction context, such as the query and user features
Y(1)outcome if the advertiser wins the sponsored slot
Y(0)outcome if the advertiser loses and only the organic path remains
E[. | x]average over uncertainty conditional on context x
A tiny worked example
Suppose a query often leads to a purchase even without the ad because the brand ranks first organically.
If winning the ad yields average revenue of 10 dollars but losing still yields 8 dollars, the marginal value is 2 dollars.
A bidder using total revenue would treat the opportunity as worth 10 dollars and overpay.
A bidder using marginal value treats it as worth 2 dollars, which matches the extra gain created by the paid slot.

The online problem is to learn this conditional treatment effect while bidding repeatedly under auction feedback.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The causal assumptions may be wrong. If unobserved factors change both auction outcomes and purchase behavior, the estimated treatment effect can be biased, which means the bids still drift away from true incremental value.
Loss rounds reveal less threshold information. When the bidder loses, the exact market threshold may remain hidden, so learning can still be slower in regions where wins are rare.
Stationarity may fail. If user behavior, auction competition, or organic ranking changes quickly over time, the online learner may chase a moving target because past treatment-effect estimates expire fast.
Where the design is strongest. The method is strongest when paid and organic channels overlap materially, because that is when total observed revenue differs most from true marginal value.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a repeated search-auction simulator with both sponsored and organic paths. Each query context should have two hidden outcome rates: purchase probability if the ad wins and purchase probability if it loses. Let the user compare a revenue-based bidder and a causal bidder.

The demo should expose both auction formats, second-price and first-price, while holding the same hidden treatment effects fixed. Track spend, incremental conversions, and regret. This matters because the paper's main technical point is not only that the objective must be causal, but also that second-price feedback reveals extra information that shortens learning.

regret = oracle_incremental_value - learner_incremental_value

The demo must make visible that a high observed post-click revenue can still justify a low bid when the organic path already captures most of that value.
