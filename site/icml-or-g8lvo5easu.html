Video-Based Optimal Transport for Feedback-Efficient Offline Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning

Use a small set of human-labeled video segment preferences to spread labels across a large offline dataset by matching visually similar trajectories with optimal transport.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Preference-based reinforcement learning learns a reward model from comparisons such as which of two behavior clips a person prefers. The bottleneck is label cost: each new pair requires human judgment. VOTP starts from an offline dataset of videos and only a small labeled subset. It encodes each video segment with a video foundation model, meaning a large pretrained network that turns a clip into a feature representation, meaning a list of numbers that summarizes its visual content. It then uses optimal transport, meaning a method that matches mass between two sets while minimizing total pairwise cost, to connect unlabeled segment pairs to labeled ones inside that feature space. Those transport weights produce pseudo-labels for many unlabeled pairs. The reward model is then trained on both the human labels and the filtered pseudo-labels, so the policy can learn from far more comparisons than humans directly provided.

Zero assumption version

Why offline preference learning runs out of labels

Offline preference learning has plenty of behavior data but very few judgments about which behavior is preferred because asking humans to compare clips is expensive. A reward model trained on only a few labeled pairs can overfit or can fail to distinguish subtle but important differences in long trajectories.

The central question is how to reuse the unlabeled part of the dataset. A naive guess based on nearest neighbors can be brittle when each preference depends on several moments within a clip. VOTP instead treats pseudo-labeling as a structured matching problem over whole video segments, so label transfer depends on the global relation between many labeled and unlabeled examples, not on one nearest match.

The paper's move in one sentence. Embed video segments with a pretrained video model, solve a transport problem between labeled and unlabeled segment pairs, and turn the transport weights into pseudo-preferences for reward learning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect a small labeled preference set

Ask humans to compare a limited number of segment pairs and record which segment in each pair is preferred.

Encode labeled and unlabeled segments with one video model

Map every segment to a shared feature representation so visual similarity is measured in one common space instead of directly from pixels, because raw pixels can vary for reasons that do not matter for preference.

Build a transport cost between preference pairs

Compute how costly it is to match each labeled pair to each unlabeled pair based on the distances between their segment features.

Solve for transport weights and derive pseudo-preferences

Use optimal transport to assign how much each labeled pair should influence each unlabeled pair, then convert the weighted label transfer into a preference score.

Filter by confidence and train the reward model

Keep only pseudo-labeled pairs whose score is far enough from zero, because ambiguous pseudo-labels can blur the reward signal instead of sharpening it.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how unlabeled clips become useful. It does not guess rewards directly from the video encoder. It first transfers pairwise preference structure from labeled examples to unlabeled examples through a transport plan, then trains the reward model on the expanded comparison set.

the object it moves  =  pseudo-preference transfer from labeled to unlabeled video pairs
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  whether closeness in video feature space predicts closeness in human preference ordering

The transport solver never sees a true reward. It only sees feature distances and a small number of labels. The hidden assumption is that if two segment pairs are close in the video feature space, their preference relation is likely to be similar as well.

That assumption is weaker than saying one clip must have one fixed reward under all contexts. It only says preference structure is locally transferable. VOTP then protects itself by thresholding pseudo-label scores, so transfer is used when the transport plan produces a clear direction and is ignored when the match is weak.

Why it works

Why transport-based pseudo-labeling can cut label cost

A preference label is relational: it says clip A is preferred to clip B, not that either clip has an absolute score by itself. VOTP respects that structure by matching pairs to pairs instead of matching single clips to single clips. That matters because preference often depends on relative progress, failure, and completion across the two members of the pair.

Optimal transport matters because it distributes influence across many labeled examples. One unlabeled pair can borrow partial evidence from several labeled pairs, and one labeled pair can support several unlabeled pairs. This is more stable than one hard assignment, because visual data can contain several relevant variations at once.

The confidence threshold matters because transport can still produce uncertain scores when the labeled set does not cover the region well. Discarding weak pseudo-labels keeps the reward model from training on comparisons that are mostly guesswork.

Math after the picture

The pseudo-preference score

First in words:

score an unlabeled pair by summing the labeled preference labels weighted by the transport plan

Then the compact form:

s_u = sum_l T_(l,u) y_l

Every symbol:

s_uthe pseudo-preference score assigned to one unlabeled pair
T_(l,u)the transport weight from labeled pair l to unlabeled pair u
y_lthe human preference label for labeled pair l, such as left preferred or right preferred
A tiny worked example
Suppose one unlabeled pair receives transport weights 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2 from three labeled pairs.
Suppose the corresponding labels are left preferred, left preferred, and right preferred, which we encode as +1, +1, and -1.
The pseudo-score is 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.2 = 0.6.
If the threshold is 0.4, the pair is kept as a confident left-preferred example.
If the score had been 0.1, the pair would be dropped because the transferred evidence would be too mixed.

The score is not a reward by itself. It is an intermediate label transfer step that expands the training set for the reward model.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The video feature space may group clips by the wrong cues. If the pretrained encoder focuses on lighting, background, or camera changes more than task progress, transport will connect the wrong pairs and transfer the wrong preference labels.
The labeled set may cover too little of the behavior space. If the labeled pairs come from only one narrow region of the dataset, many unlabeled pairs will get weak or misleading matches because the transport plan has no close anchors.
Thresholding can become too strict or too loose. A low threshold keeps noisy pseudo-labels, while a high threshold discards most of the unlabeled data. The method depends on keeping the region where transfer is clear and dropping the region where transfer is ambiguous.
Done right. Use a video encoder that preserves task progress, measure pseudo-label accuracy on a held-out labeled set, and tune the confidence threshold so the expanded dataset grows without becoming dominated by uncertain transfers.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take an offline control dataset with short video segments, label a very small number of preference pairs, and compare three pipelines: direct reward learning, nearest-neighbor pseudo-labeling, and VOTP transport pseudo-labeling.

Show the learned reward on held-out labeled pairs and then train a policy from each reward model. The demo should make clear that VOTP works because it transfers pairwise supervision across the dataset in a structured way instead of treating each unlabeled pair as an isolated guess.

pseudo_label_accuracy = correct_pseudo_labels / evaluated_pseudo_labels

A convincing result is that transport pseudo-labeling keeps reward quality high even when the human label budget is very small, while direct supervised reward learning collapses under the same budget.
