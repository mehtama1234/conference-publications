Queue Length Regret Bounds for Contextual Queueing Bandits

The paper shows how to schedule jobs to servers while learning hidden service rates by measuring how long the queue grows compared to the perfect schedule.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Imagine a real queue: jobs arrive with different types, servers handle different job types at different speeds, and nobody knows how fast each server really is. A scheduler must pick which job goes to which server, all while learning the hidden speeds from watching what actually happens. Most scheduling theory assumes you know the speeds in advance. This paper tackles the harder problem: learning while scheduling, and measures success by how much longer your queue gets than the theoretically perfect queue would be.

Zero assumption version

Why scheduling with unknown speeds is stuck

In real data centers and hospitals, jobs queue up for servers. Each job has features (its type, size, urgency). Each server handles each job at a different speed, but you do not know these speeds up front. A naive scheduler might always pick the fastest-looking server for each job, but you only learn a server is actually slow after sending jobs to it and watching them finish slowly. By then, your queue has grown.

The deeper trap: if you and the oracle differ on which job to pick next, the queues differ. Your queue has different jobs sitting in it than the oracle queue has. This makes it impossible to compare fairly: maybe your queue looks longer only because you picked different jobs earlier, not because you learned badly.

The core problem. Schedule jobs to servers while learning unknown service speeds, and measure success not by final decisions but by how much longer your queue gets than the perfect oracle.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Phase 1: Pure exploration

Pick each server in round-robin order to measure its speed under each job type.

Phase 2: Epsilon-greedy learning

Pick the server that looks fastest for each job type, with occasional random picks to keep learning.

Measure speed using logistic model

The true speed follows a logistic curve determined by job features and a hidden number unique to each server.

Track confidence bounds

Keep upper and lower confidence bands on each server's speed; pick the server with highest optimistic upper bound.

Compare via policy-switching queues

Imagine a fake queue running your policy then switching to oracle; measure how much longer it is than the oracle queue alone.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper changes how you pick the next job-server pair. Instead of just guessing which server is fastest, you combine observed data (which pairs actually succeeded fast) with a learned model of how job features affect speed.

the object it moves  =  job-server pair selection rule
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: server speed under each job type

measured quantity  =  Logistic service rate per job-server pair

The hidden unknown is: how fast does server k actually finish a job with a certain set of traits? The paper models speed as a logistic curve (an S-shaped probability curve) that depends on how well the job traits match a hidden preference stored by each server. In plain words: imagine each server has a hidden preference list for job traits, and a job is fast or slow depending on whether its traits align with that preference.

The algorithm estimates this hidden preference by watching which job-server pairs succeed (finish in one time step) and which fail (stay in queue). Each success or failure gives a noisy hint about the true preference. The algorithm combines all hints to narrow its guess about the true preference, then uses the optimistic upper guess to make picks.

Why it works

Why the method works: coupling and regret decomposition

The decisive move is the policy-switching queue. You cannot compare your queue directly to the oracle queue at any single moment, because they hold different jobs (you picked differently earlier). Instead, imagine a fake queue that runs your policy for some number of steps, then switches to the oracle for the rest. This fake queue is a bridge between your queue and the oracle queue.

Using this bridge, you break regret into two parts: short-term (picking the wrong pair now makes one extra job wait) and long-term (that mistake changes which jobs sit in the queue later). The algorithm keeps the gap between your service rate estimates and the oracle's narrowing over time (once you learn a server is slow, you learn it is slow for all job types). The exploration phases ensure you gather enough information on each server before committing to picks that look best so far.

The regret decays as one over the fourth root of T because your confidence estimates shrink as you accumulate observations, so after the initial pure-exploration phase, your picks get better and better, and new mistakes become rarer.

Math after the picture

The regret equation

First in words:

Queue length regret equals the expected difference between your queue length at time T and the oracle queue length at time T. The oracle knows the true service rates and picks optimally. Your regret is bounded by a sum of exploration cost (how many wrong picks you make while learning) plus the gap between your estimated service rates and the truth, weighted by how long jobs stay in the queue.

Then the compact form:

Regret_T = E[ Q(T) - Q*(T) ], where Q(T) is queue length at time T under your policy and Q*(T) is queue length under the oracle policy

Every symbol:

Regret_Tcumulative queue length difference over all time steps
Q(T)queue length at the end under your learning algorithm
Q*(T)queue length at the end under the oracle with known speeds
E[...]average over all randomness (exploration, job arrivals, service outcomes)
Worked example with two servers and four jobs
Suppose at time T=5 your queue has 12 jobs waiting. The oracle queue has 4 jobs waiting.
Your regret is 12 - 4 = 8 job-steps of excess wait.
The algorithm guarantees this regret grows at most like log squared of T (for adversarial contexts) or T to the minus one-fourth (for random contexts).
For T=10000 rounds: log-squared bound is about log(10000)^2 = 160. The power-law bound is about 10000^(-0.25) = 5.6.
So for random contexts, the queue barely grows; for adversarial, it grows but logarithmically, not linearly.

The regret measures the total accumulated penalty from not knowing speeds up front. The bounds shrink over time because your learned estimates get more accurate, so you make fewer mistakes.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Exploration cost is unavoidable. You must try each server on different job types to learn, which means some early jobs go to slow servers. The initial regret cannot be avoided, only managed.
Adversarial contexts are much harder. If a malicious adversary can pick job features to trick the algorithm, regret grows as log squared of T, not the power-law decay. This is because each adversarial choice can undo learning from previous rounds.
The logistic model assumption can be wrong. If the true service rate does not follow a logistic curve (e.g., if server k is slow for job trait A but fast for job trait A plus a shift), the estimated preferences will be biased and the algorithm will keep picking suboptimally.
Done right: the policy-switching queue idea generalizes. The coupling trick unlocks fair comparison even when policies make different choices. This idea extends to multi-queue systems and other scheduling problems where you learn while acting.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Write a simple simulator with K servers, T time steps, and 20 synthetic job features. For each job, sample a server-specific hidden parameter theta for each server (say, 5-dimensional). The service rate is logistic(x dot theta). Simulate three policies: random picks, a greedy oracle with known theta, and the CQB algorithm. Plot queue length over time for each. The demo must show CQB's queue length tracking the oracle queue closely (not the random queue), especially after the pure-exploration phase ends.

Regret_demo = sum over t=1 to T of (queue_length_CQB(t) - queue_length_oracle(t))

The demo proves that learning service rates while scheduling keeps your queue almost as short as if you knew the rates up front. The regret should decay or stay bounded, not grow linearly over time.
