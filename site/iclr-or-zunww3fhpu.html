Latent Vector Fields in Neural Networks

A neural network encodes a hidden flow field in its latent space that reveals what the model has learned and whether a sample is unusual.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Neural networks squish high-dimensional data (like images) into compact lists of numbers (called a latent space—a compressed version of the original). This paper finds that when you repeatedly feed data through the network's encoder and decoder, the network traces out an invisible flow field in that space. Like water flowing downhill, different starting points drift toward stable resting places called attractors. These attractors act as landmarks that tell you what the model thinks is important, how it memorizes, and when it sees something it has never encountered.

Zero assumption version

Why we need to peek inside the network

Today, neural networks are black boxes. You train one on cats and dogs, it works on new pictures, but you cannot ask: Did it memorize the training set? Or did it learn what a cat really is? When you show it a penguin (something the training set never had), does the network know it is unusual, or does it confidently guess wrong?

A beginner might naively look at the numbers the network stores (its weights). But those raw numbers are like staring at a computer's electrical signals—you see 0s and 1s but not what they mean. We need a tool that reads the network's learned logic directly.

The paper's move in one sentence. Treat the network as a dynamical system: repeatedly passing data through the encoder-decoder pair reveals a flow field in the latent space, and the stable resting points of that flow are the network's implicit landmarks.

What the paper changes

What the paper measures

The paper does not train anything new. Instead, it extracts a hidden quantity that was already there: the vector field that emerges when you treat the encoder-decoder pair as a dynamical system and apply it over and over.

the object it moves  =  the latent vector field
The hidden quantity

The latent vector field: flow and attractors

measured quantity  =  the vector field and its attractors

The latent vector field is what you get when you repeatedly apply the encoder (compress the data) and then the decoder (decompress it). Start with any point in latent space. Pass it through decoder, then encoder again. It moves. Pass it through again. It moves again. After many iterations, it settles at a stable resting place—an attractor. The vector field is the combined map of all these flows; the attractors are the destinations.

The paper measures this field by running the encode-decode cycle on latent vectors and tracking where they go. It does not require any new training, just observation of behavior already baked into the network. Attractors near cluster centers (where training examples grouped together) reveal what the model learned; isolated attractors reveal what it memorized by accident.

Why it works

Why the flow field reveals what the model knows

When you train an autoencoder normally, the network learns to compress similar examples into nearby spots in latent space and decompress them accurately. This means the encoder-decoder pair implicitly defines a flow: if you start at one spot and apply encode-decode, you are nudged closer to the cluster of similar training examples. Apply it again, nudged closer again. Repeat enough times, you land on an attractor.

Why does this work? Because the training process optimizes the encoder and decoder so that they undo each other (input goes in, comes out almost identical). This means the composed map Decoder(Encoder(z)) acts like gravity pulling points toward learned cluster centers and memorized modes. The structure of the flow encodes the structure of what the model learned—without requiring any new analysis. Samples the network never encountered during training have no patterns to guide them, so the flow field does not know where to pull them; they wander in unpredictable directions.

Math after the picture

The vector field equation

First in words:

the composed map is decoder applied to encoder applied to latent vector z, iterated: z-next equals decoder(encoder(z)). After many steps, z settles to a fixed point (attractor) z-star where z-star equals decoder(encoder(z-star)).

Then the compact form:

z_{t+1} = D(E(z_t))

Every symbol:

z_tposition in latent space at step t
E(z)encoder: compress the representation
D(z)decoder: decompress the representation
z_starfixed point (attractor) where z-star = D(E(z-star))
titeration step number
Tiny worked example
Start z = [2.0, 1.0]. Encoder shrinks it slightly: E([2.0, 1.0]) = [1.8, 0.95]. Decoder undoes: D([1.8, 0.95]) = [1.95, 0.98].
Next step: z = [1.95, 0.98]. Encode: E([1.95, 0.98]) = [1.87, 0.94]. Decode: D([1.87, 0.94]) = [1.92, 0.97].
After 100 steps, both coordinates plateau at [1.5, 0.8]—the attractor. The network has pulled the point downhill to a stable learned landmark.

Each iteration of the composed map nudges the point closer to a resting place, because training optimized the encoder and decoder to undo each other.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Attractors can be misleading if the model is broken. A poorly trained network might have attractors in random locations that tell you nothing about real structure. The vector field only reveals what the training actually encoded.
Distinguishing learned from memorized requires care. Both create attractors, but learned categories cluster near dense regions while memorized examples isolate at sparse points. Misreading the density can flip the conclusion.
Computing the full vector field is expensive. For large latent dimensions, running encode-decode many times per point, over many points, takes time. You need to sample smartly.
Applied correctly, it works. Track attractor density, test on train vs held-out data, and verify with actual model internals to distinguish memorization from learning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a small autoencoder on a simple dataset (e.g., MNIST digits). Compute the vector field by iterating Decoder(Encoder(z)) on a grid of points in latent space. Plot the trajectories; attractors will appear as darkened pools where flow lines converge. Show three things: (1) Attractors in the dense cluster center for learned digit categories. (2) Isolated attractors for training images the network memorized. (3) Chaotic flow for inputs the network never saw (e.g., noise, images from a held-out class). Measure convergence rate (how many steps to reach each attractor) and trajectory stability (how perturbed inputs behave). The demo should make it impossible to miss: the vector field is a fingerprint of what the model learned and memorized.

convergence_rate = median(steps to reach attractor) per input category; trajectory_stability = std_dev(endpoint distance) when perturbing start by small epsilon; OOD_divergence = mean(final distance from any attractor) for OOD samples minus learned-category samples.

A learned category has fast, stable convergence to a cluster-center attractor. Memorized examples have slow, isolated convergence. Unseen samples never converge and wander far from all attractors.
