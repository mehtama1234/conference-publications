Null-Space Constrained Policy Optimization for LLM Safety

Keep an AI model's math and coding skills sharp while teaching it safety rules, by preventing safety training from breaking the neural paths that power general abilities.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models are trained on billions of diverse texts, giving them broad skills like math, coding, and following instructions. When we then teach them safety rules using reinforcement learning (reward-based nudging where we steer the model toward safe outputs), the neural weights that let them do general tasks often get damaged, like stepping on the same ground twice erases the first footprint. This paper introduces a method that protects the core abilities while adding safety guards.

Zero assumption version

The alignment tax: safety training erases general skills

Modern large language models start with broad abilities: they can solve math problems, write code, and follow complex instructions. These skills live in the model's neural network weights (the numbers that let the model work), distributed across thousands of microscopic decision points.

When safety engineers want to make a model refuse harmful requests, they use reinforcement learning to nudge the weights toward safer outputs. But here is the trap: nudging weights in one direction to fix safety often nudges them away from the directions that make math or coding work. It is like tightening one screw on a machine and accidentally loosening another. The model becomes safer but forgets how to do algebra. This loss of general skill is called the alignment tax.

A naive approach is to mix safety data with general-task data during training, hoping the model learns both. This requires huge amounts of annotated examples and still does not reliably save the old skills.

The core problem. Reinforcement learning for safety damages the neural patterns that enable general abilities like math and code, because the update nudges weights in directions that disrupt the decision paths those abilities rely on.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect two kinds of feedback

Gather examples where the model succeeds on general tasks and examples showing what safety looks like.

Measure general-task directions

Measure which direction to nudge weights for math, coding, instructions. Call this the general direction.

Measure safety-update directions

Measure which direction to nudge weights to refuse harmful requests. Call this the safety direction.

Project safety into null space

Remove any part of the safety direction that overlaps the general direction. Keep only the orthogonal piece.

Update with the projected safety direction

Nudge weights only in directions that add safety without erasing general ability.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the direction of safety updates

The paper does not change how often the model trains or what data it sees. It changes one thing: the actual direction in which safety updates nudge the neural weights. Instead of letting safety feedback steer weights along any path it wants, the paper constrains it to move only in directions that do not overlap with the general-task paths. This is like putting guardrails on a road so safety training cannot swerve into the lane that handles math.

The hidden quantity

The null space: directions that preserve general skill

measured quantity  =  Null space of general-task directions

The hidden quantity this paper measures is the null space: the set of all directions in the weight space where moving does not change performance on general tasks. Think of a terrain with a hill. Inside that terrain, there is a path running horizontally around the hill—moving along this path does not carry you up or down (does not hurt general tasks). Moving off the path tips you up or down (damages general ability). The null space is this horizontal path.

The paper estimates the null space by collecting small updates for general tasks, then computing which weight directions are perpendicular (at right angles) to those updates. Take the directions that point uphill for general tasks and find all directions at right angles to them. Only the null space is safe for safety training.

Plain reading. The null space is the set of all weight changes that do not help or hurt the model's general abilities—a safe zone where safety training can operate freely without collateral damage, because moving in these directions is mathematically perpendicular to the paths that general tasks use.
Paper reading. Formally: given a table G of general-task directions, the null space is the set of all weight directions x such that multiplying G^T by x gives zero. A weight update in this space adds no component along any general-task direction.
Why it works

Why it works: orthogonal updates leave old paths alone

The mechanism is based on a fact from linear algebra: if you move in a direction that is perpendicular to another direction, you do not travel along that other direction at all. Imagine a compass rose: moving north does not move you east. The weight space in a neural network has thousands of directions, but the principle is the same.

General tasks like math or coding use specific paths through the weight space. These paths are defined by the directions in which small updates improve performance on those tasks—the directions that make the model answer correctly more often, because nudging weights this way causes better answers. If safety training nudges weights only along directions perpendicular to these paths, the updates never touch the paths. It is impossible to damage a path you never touch. The safety nudging still works—it pulls weights in ways that improve safety, because those pulls happen in unused dimensions that do not interfere with the skill paths. The model learns the new safety rule while keeping the old math and coding neurons exactly where they were.

The paper proves mathematically that this perpendicular constraint actually preserves the model's original capabilities: because updates are perpendicular, the performance (error) on general tasks does not increase. At the same time, the paper ensures that safety training still makes progress—the safety nudge still points in the right direction on the safety objective, just a safer direction that protects general skills.

Math after the picture

The equation: projecting onto the null space

First in words:

The projected safety update is the original safety update minus all the parts that point toward general tasks.

Then the compact form:

g_safety_projected = g_safety - G^T (G G^T)^-1 G g_safety

Every symbol:

g_safetyThe gradient (direction to nudge) for safety improvement
g_safety_projectedThe safety gradient after removing overlaps with general tasks
GMatrix of general-task gradients (rows are different task directions)
G^TThe transpose of G (same information, rows become columns)
(G G^T)^-1A small matrix used to calculate the overlap precisely
G G^T (G G^T)^-1 GThe projection operator: extracts the parts of g_safety that overlap general tasks
A tiny worked example
Suppose there are only two weights, w1 and w2.
General-task gradient: G = [8, 0] (improving math means nudging w1).
Safety gradient: g_safety = [6, 4] (safety wants to nudge both).
The null space of G is the direction [0, 1] (only w2 is orthogonal to the math path).
The overlap of g_safety with the general direction is the [6, 0] part.
After projection: g_safety_projected = [6, 4] - [6, 0] = [0, 4].
Result: safety training nudges only w2, leaving the math weight w1 untouched.

The projection removes every component of the safety update that travels along the general-task paths, leaving only the orthogonal part to act.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Incomplete general-task coverage. If you do not measure gradients for all the important general tasks (e.g. you skip instruction-following), the null space will be too large and safety training can accidentally damage the unmeasured skills.
Redundant general-task directions. If the general-task directions are all very similar (measuring the same thing over and over), the null space shrinks and there is less room for safety nudges, possibly making safety training too slow or weak.
Noisy gradient estimates. If the gradient measurements from data are unstable or biased, the null space is computed incorrectly and the method loses its safety guarantee.
Done right. The paper shows that with representative general-task feedback and stable gradient estimates, the method reliably preserves skills while improving safety, even on new domains like code and reasoning that were not explicitly protected in the null space calculation.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A concrete demo would train a small language model (say, one with a billion internal numbers) on a math task until it solves algebra problems reliably. Then split into two branches: one branch trains on safety data using standard reinforcement learning, the other uses NSPO. Measure math accuracy, safety refusal rates, and code-problem accuracy on both branches after safety training. The NSPO branch should show math accuracy close to the pre-training level, while the baseline branch shows the typical 20–30 point drop.

Alignment tax = (math_accuracy_before_safety - math_accuracy_after_safety) - (safety_score_after - baseline_safety_score_before) / scaling_factor

The critical demo must make impossible to miss: two neural networks trained identically except for the safety update direction, same final safety performance, but NSPO keeps the math skills alive where standard methods kill them.
