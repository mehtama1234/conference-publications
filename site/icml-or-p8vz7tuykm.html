AdaNav: Adaptive Reasoning with Uncertainty for Vision-Language Navigation

An embodied robot learns to reason only when it is unsure, making decisions faster and better with limited training data.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot following directions like 'go past the kitchen and turn left' must understand the instruction and act on what it sees in each video frame, making decisions over many steps without forgetting the goal. Asking the robot to think through every step is slow and wasteful when it already knows what to do. AdaNav teaches robots to recognize when they are confused and only spend time reasoning in those moments.

Zero assumption version

Robots get lost when following spoken directions

A robot that follows words like 'walk toward the red door' must connect language to what its cameras see, moment by moment, while remembering where it started and what it came to do. Over long paths with many turns, this is hard.

One approach is to have the robot stop at every step, think hard about what the instruction means and what it sees, then decide the next move. That works well but takes a long time—the robot wastes thinking time on easy decisions it already understands. Another approach is to just react to the current frame without thinking ahead, which is fast but makes mistakes because the robot forgets context and misses hard turns.

In one sentence. Robots either think too much and move slowly, or act without thinking and make mistakes.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The robot decides whether to spend time reasoning at each step, rather than always reasoning or never reasoning. This decision is a toggle that can be on or off at any moment in the trajectory.

the object it moves  =  reasoning toggle
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Action Uncertainty

measured quantity  =  Action Uncertainty

Action Uncertainty measures how spread out the robot's guesses about the next move are. If the robot thinks the next action is clearly 'turn left', uncertainty is low (no confusion). If the robot thinks 'go straight' and 'turn left' and 'turn right' are all equally likely, uncertainty is high (lots of confusion).

The paper uses Action Uncertainty to decide when the robot should reason: high uncertainty means the robot is confused and should think, low uncertainty means the robot is already confident and can just move. Uncertainty starts as a guess from a fast model and gets refined through training so the robot learns which moments actually need thinking.

Why it works

Why thinking only when unsure works

A robot moving through a space faces two kinds of moments: obvious ones (the hallway branches left and the instruction says 'go left') and ambiguous ones (the hallway is narrow and the words 'proceed cautiously' could mean slow down or wait). On obvious moments, reasoning is wasted effort. On ambiguous moments, reasoning is the only way to avoid a mistake.

Uncertainty detects ambiguity because when many actions look equally plausible, the robot's confidence is spread thin. When only one action stands out, the robot's confidence is focused. By triggering reasoning only when uncertainty is high, the robot spends its thinking budget where it matters: at the confusing turns. High uncertainty means 'I have many guesses, not one clear answer.'

The paper trains the robot via reinforcement learning to refine when to reason. It starts with a guess ('if uncertainty is high, reason') and tries it out. When the robot takes a path and reaches the goal, it learns that the guessed reasoning trigger was good. When the robot gets lost, it learns to reason more at the moments before the mistake. Over time, the robot's trigger becomes accurate.

The equation for reasoning triggers

First in words:

The penalty for reasoning at the wrong times is the number of reasoning steps times a cost, plus how badly the robot performs on the task. Reasoning less is faster but makes more mistakes. The robot learns to balance both.

Then the compact form:

total_cost = reasoning_steps * reasoning_cost + navigation_loss

Every symbol:

reasoning_stepscount of moments when the robot stops to think
reasoning_costtime spent on reasoning per step (fixed penalty)
navigation_losshow often the robot fails to reach the goal
total_costwhat the learning algorithm tries to minimize
A tiny worked example
Path 1: Robot reasons 8 times, cost 8*2=16, reaches goal, loss 0. Total: 16+0=16.
Path 2: Robot reasons 2 times, cost 2*2=4, misses goal 3 times, loss 30. Total: 4+30=34.
Path 3: Robot reasons 5 times, cost 5*2=10, reaches goal, loss 0. Total: 10+0=10.
Path 3 is best: reasoning enough to succeed but not wasting effort.

The robot learns where reasoning is worth its cost by comparing paths and finding the sweet spot between thinking and acting.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Uncertainty threshold set wrong. If the robot reasons whenever uncertainty crosses a bad threshold, it might reason too much or too little. Early in training, guessing is hard.
Reasoning engine breaks. If the robot's reasoning module (the slower, more careful planner) gives bad advice, triggering it more often makes performance worse, not better, because now the robot follows bad guidance at more moments instead of fewer.
Limited training data. With only 6K paths to learn from, the robot might overfit when to reason. It learns the training scenes but fails on new buildings.
Done right. When the fast model gives good uncertainty estimates and the reasoning engine is accurate, the robot learns a trigger that trades speed for correctness only when it matters.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple maze navigation task: a robot starts at position (0, 0) and receives instructions like 'go to the top-right corner, but be careful of the walls on the left'. At each step, the robot receives its current scene (where it is, where walls block it) and sees a few action choices (up, down, left, right). It computes entropy over the action choices. When entropy is high (multiple paths look equally safe), it triggers a deliberate planner that reasons over all walls. When entropy is low (one path is clearly safe), it picks the highest-scoring action instantly.

success_rate = (paths_reaching_goal / total_paths) and average_reasoning_steps = (total_reasoning_moments / total_paths)

Show that the robot reaches the goal more often than the always-reasoning baseline, while using fewer reasoning steps than the always-reasoning robot, because the learned trigger sends reasoning only to moments that need it, skipping the easy parts.
