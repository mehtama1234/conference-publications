Breaking Scale Anchoring: Frequency Representation Learning for Super-Resolution Forecasting

When a model learns from low-resolution signals, it learns only the low frequencies; teaching it to notice and remember high frequencies lets errors shrink as resolution grows.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Physics simulations need models trained on low-resolution data to work well on high-resolution grids. But something unexpected happens: errors do not improve as resolution increases, because low-resolution training data simply cannot contain the fast, wiggly patterns that appear in high-resolution data — so the model never learns to predict them. The problem is fundamental: it is not a law of physics but a limit of what data the model saw. This paper shows that by teaching the model to represent frequencies explicitly and consistently across different resolutions, errors finally decrease as they should.

Zero assumption version

When training data cannot see the full picture

A physics forecasting model is trained on coarse data — say, a weather grid at 10 kilometer spacing. At test time, it is asked to predict on a finer grid with 1 kilometer spacing. The naive hope is that if you train on 10km and test on 1km, the error should shrink; you have more data to work with. But it does not. Errors stay roughly the same or get worse.

The root cause is in what frequencies (fast oscillations) the coarse training data can show. By the Nyquist theorem, coarse data at 10km spacing can only represent patterns changing every 20km or more, because you cannot extract signals faster than the sampling rate — a camera at 30 fps cannot see motion faster than 15 fps. Patterns faster than 20km are absent from coarse training data because they require a finer sampling rate to appear. The model, trained on 10km data, has never seen 1km patterns. When asked to forecast on 1km grids, it guesses, and errors stay large because the model never learned the high-frequency skills needed to predict them.

The problem is usually blamed on the model not generalizing. But that is misleading. The model learned well from the data it saw. The real issue is that the model ignores a whole class of patterns that exist in the high-resolution target but were absent in low-resolution training. No amount of standard training can fix that.

In one sentence. Models trained on low-resolution data lack the capacity to represent fast patterns that only appear in high-resolution data, so their errors stay anchored at low-resolution quality.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Represent signals by frequency bands

Decompose each forecast into labeled frequency bands: slow, medium, fast, very-fast.

Step 2: Train on multiple resolutions at once

For each training example, generate coarse and fine versions, forecast both.

Step 3: Measure frequency alignment

Compare frequency decompositions of coarse and fine forecasts; penalize mismatches.

Step 4: Frequencies stay stable across scales

Model learns that the same physical process has consistent frequency content everywhere.

Step 5: High resolution inherits low-resolution skill

Because frequencies are aligned, fine-grid forecasts reuse what coarse-grid training taught.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds explicit frequency decomposition (the breaking down of a signal into its component wave frequencies) to the model's internal learned patterns (weights), and adds a loss term (a penalty during training) that keeps the frequency content consistent across different resolutions. The specific change is architectural: the model learns to encode and compare frequencies as a first-class operation, not as an afterthought.

the object it moves  =  learned frequency representation
The hidden quantity

Spectral consistency: the invisible mismatch

measured quantity  =  Spectral consistency gap

Spectral consistency is the degree to which a forecast at coarse resolution and the same forecast at fine resolution have matching frequency patterns. If low-resolution forecasts are mostly slow patterns and high-resolution forecasts are mostly slow plus some fast patterns, then the spectral content has drifted. The paper measures this by decomposing each forecast using a mathematical tool (the Fourier transform, which breaks a signal into its constituent frequencies) and comparing the size of each frequency band between coarse and fine versions.

The paper estimates this gap by computing the energy in each frequency band and penalizing differences. This teaches the model to produce frequency patterns that scale consistently: if the low-frequency band has energy X at coarse resolution, the same band should have approximately proportional energy at fine resolution.

Why it works

Why encoding frequencies explicitly breaks the anchor

The core insight is that the model must learn to name and track frequencies across resolutions. Standard models learn end-to-end from data, but they have no explicit concept of what frequencies they are learning. A coarse model learns some pattern and the fine model learns a different pattern, and there is no feedback that they should be related.

Frequency Representation Learning (FRL) makes frequencies visible. By decomposing forecasts into frequency bands and comparing band energy across coarse and fine versions, the training loop now has a handle: nudge the fine-resolution model when its low-frequency band does not match the coarse one. This teaches the model that frequencies are conserved quantities, like mass in physics. Once the model understands that a physical process has consistent frequency content at all scales, the fine model can reuse all the low-frequency expertise from coarse training, and add new capability only for the high frequencies it can actually see.

The spectral consistency loss is the mechanism: it is a penalty that is nonzero whenever coarse and fine forecasts disagree on frequency content, and zero when they agree. Backpropagation (the process of pushing errors backward through the network to adjust weights) uses this penalty to steer the model toward frequency alignment. This is why error finally decreases with resolution: the fine model no longer starts from scratch on low frequencies; it inherits the learned low-frequency pattern from coarse training, proven by the spectral match.

Math after the picture

The spectral consistency penalty

First in words:

Loss equals prediction error on the forecasted values, plus a penalty term: lambda times the sum over all frequency bands of the squared difference between coarse band energy and fine band energy, weighted by how important that band is.

Then the compact form:

total_loss = forecast_error + lambda * sum_b w_b * (E_coarse_b - E_fine_b)^2

Every symbol:

total_lossoverall training penalty
forecast_errorhow wrong the predictions are
lambdaknob controlling spectral penalty strength
sum_bsum over all frequency bands
w_blearned importance weight for band b
E_coarse_benergy (size) of band b in coarse forecast
E_fine_benergy (size) of band b in fine forecast
A tiny worked example
Suppose lambda = 0.1, and we compare coarse and fine forecasts.
Low-frequency band: E_coarse = 80, E_fine = 85, w_low = 2.0.
Penalty for low: 0.1 * 2.0 * (80 - 85)^2 = 0.1 * 2.0 * 25 = 5.0.
High-frequency band: E_coarse = 5, E_fine = 20, w_high = 0.5.
Penalty for high: 0.1 * 0.5 * (5 - 20)^2 = 0.1 * 0.5 * 225 = 11.25.
Total spectral penalty = 5.0 + 11.25 = 16.25.
Backpropagation nudges model to reduce this penalty by aligning E_fine to E_coarse for each band.

The penalty is zero when coarse and fine forecasts have the same frequency signature, teaching the model that frequencies are a conserved property of the physics.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda set too high. If the spectral penalty weight lambda is too large, the model focuses only on matching frequencies and ignores actual forecast accuracy. The fine forecast may have perfect spectral alignment but be physically wrong.
Frequency bands too coarse. If the frequency decomposition is too coarse-grained (e.g., only one slow and one fast band), the model cannot distinguish which parts of the signal are truly misaligned. Too fine-grained and the signal is sparse in high bands, making the penalty noisy.
Physics without true scale invariance. Some physical systems do not conserve frequency content across scales; for example, turbulent flows can transfer energy between scales in ways that break the assumption. FRL will struggle if the problem does not actually have frequency-aligned structure.
Computational overhead small. The paper reports that frequency decomposition and the extra loss term add only modest overhead, a few percent, so training time is not a major barrier.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple 1D forecasting task: train a neural network to predict the next time step of a periodic signal (e.g., a sum of sine waves: sin(x) + 0.5*sin(3x) + 0.2*sin(7x), representing slow, medium, and fast components). Train on coarse samples (every 10th point). Evaluate on fine samples (every point). Run twice: once with standard loss (MSE), and once with standard loss plus spectral consistency loss. Plot error versus resolution.

resolution_improvement = (error_coarse - error_fine) / error_coarse; should be positive and large with FRL, near zero without.

The key result is that error shrinks visibly as resolution increases only when spectral consistency is enforced. Without it, error is flat or rises.
