A model of errors in transformers

LLM errors on arithmetic and repetitive tasks come from tiny attention mistakes that pile up, not sudden reasoning collapse.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When an LLM counts words (or tokens, the pieces a model breaks text into) or does arithmetic, it has to make the same tiny judgment over and over. Each judgment is the attention mechanism (the part that decides which previous words to pay attention to) deciding where to look. When attention makes a small error on one step, that error carries forward and compounds. This paper shows that these tiny accumulated errors are why LLMs fail on long repetitive tasks, not because the model suddenly breaks.

Zero assumption version

Tiny mistakes pile up into wrong answers

When an LLM counts a long list of identical tokens or performs step-by-step arithmetic, it must repeat the same decision many times. Each decision—which previous token to pay attention to—introduces a small chance of error. A naive model would say: just add up all these little error chances, and the total error grows. But LLMs fail catastrophically: a model that works perfectly on five arithmetic steps fails completely on fifty.

Common explanation: the model's reasoning suddenly breaks down on long sequences, as if hitting a wall. But this does not match what actually happens: errors do not appear out of nowhere. Instead, many small errors gradually push the model's internal state further and further away from the right answer, until finally the model produces the wrong output token.

The core mechanism: Small attention errors accumulate across repeated steps. When enough tiny errors add up, they cross a threshold that flips the answer.

What the paper changes

The thing the paper measures

The paper does not propose a new training technique or model architecture. Instead, it extracts a simple model using two values (called parameters: single numbers that control how a formula works) that predict when attention errors become large enough to cause the LLM to output a wrong word. The two values are the elementary noise rate (how often attention makes a small error on one step) and the number of plausible wrong-answer words the model could predict (how many answers are actually similar-looking to the model). These two numbers alone let you predict whether the model will succeed or fail on a repetitive task.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: cumulative attention drift

measured quantity  =  Cumulative attention error size

This is the total amount by which attention has strayed from the correct target token across all repeated steps. It is not a single binary mistake. Instead, imagine attention as trying to point at the right previous token, but each step it points slightly off. Over many steps, all these small pointings-off add up to a large total drift from where it should be.

The paper estimates this quantity by tracking how much the model's internal computation (the state it builds up through each repetition) has drifted away from what the correct answer would be. When this drift crosses a threshold—roughly the size of the separation between correct and plausible wrong answers—the model outputs a wrong token.

Why it works

Why tiny errors compound into big ones

Here is the chain of cause and effect. First: on each repetition, attention (which token to pay attention to) has a small chance of error. This is just noise—random fluctuation in how attention weights the tokens it sees. Second: each small attention error slightly corrupts the model's internal computation for that step. The model feeds this slightly wrong internal state to the next step as input. Third: the next step makes its own small attention error, but now it is starting from corrupted input. The two errors do not cancel out; they add up. Fourth: this repeats many times. Each step contributes a small error, and they all accumulate. Think of it like random steps on a line: take a small random step left or right at each iteration, and after many iterations you are far from where you started, even though each single step was tiny. Fifth: the model's internal state has now drifted far from what it should be for the correct answer. When attention tries to predict the next token, it looks at this corrupted state and picks from a list of plausible tokens. If the drift is large enough, the corruption makes a wrong token look more likely than the right one. The answer flips. There is no sudden switch in the model's reasoning. Just noise accumulating until the scale tips.

Math after the picture

The two-parameter prediction formula

First in words:

Accuracy equals one minus the probability that accumulated noise crosses a threshold. The noise grows with the elementary error rate and the number of plausible wrong answers, but shrinks as the model state space grows larger (more room for drift before hitting the wrong answer).

Then the compact form:

P(correct) ≈ 1 - erfc(gap / (2 * sqrt(n_steps * noise_per_step)))

Every symbol:

P(correct)The fraction of times the model outputs the right answer on this task
erfc(...)Error function (a standard curve that measures tail probability; grows from 0 to 1 as the value inside grows)
gapThe separation between the correct answer and the closest plausible wrong answer in the model's internal space
n_stepsHow many times the attention decision is repeated (task length or complexity)
noise_per_stepThe elementary error rate: how often attention makes a small mistake on one step
A tiny worked example: counting five tokens
Suppose noise_per_step = 0.05 (attention errs 5% of the time per step), n_steps = 5, gap between correct and wrong = 1.5.
Total accumulated noise grows like sqrt(5 * 0.05) = sqrt(0.25) = 0.5.
Signal-to-noise ratio = gap / (2 * noise_std) = 1.5 / (2 * 0.5) = 1.5.
A standard error function at 1.5 returns erfc(1.5) ≈ 0.08.
Predicted accuracy = 1 - 0.08 = 0.92 (92% chance of correct count).
Same model on 20-step task: noise_std = sqrt(20 * 0.05) = 1.0, ratio = 1.5/2 = 0.75, erfc(0.75) ≈ 0.29, accuracy ≈ 71%.
Longer task, same noise, lower accuracy. This matches observed LLM behavior.

The model says longer tasks and higher noise shrink accuracy through a simple mathematical rule, without needing to know every weight in the LLM.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Task structure must be genuinely repetitive. If the task changes strategy partway through (e.g., arithmetic that shifts from addition to multiplication), the two-parameter model does not apply. The assumption is that the same decision is repeated.
Noise rate must be stable across steps. If the model's attention noise varies (e.g., becomes more accurate later in the sequence), the model's prediction drifts from reality, because the formula assumes constant elementary noise per step and thus accumulates the wrong total.
The threshold must be well-defined. In some tasks, there is no clear separation between correct and plausible wrong answers in the model's internal space. When answers blur together, the gap shrinks and the model becomes unpredictable.
Done right: tasks with clear structure and stable noise. Counting, arithmetic, token repetition, and other mechanical tasks match the model's assumptions. The paper achieves excellent agreement on these tasks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny transformer on a simple repetitive task: count a sequence of identical tokens, or compute addition step-by-step. Measure the model's accuracy at different sequence lengths (5, 10, 20, 30, 50 steps). Independently estimate the elementary noise rate of the model's attention on this task (log attention weights and measure variance). Apply the paper's two-parameter formula using the observed noise and task length. Check that the formula's predicted accuracy closely matches the measured accuracy. Repeat on different tasks (modular arithmetic, parity, sorting) and different models to show the prediction holds generally.

error_in_prediction = |observed_accuracy - formula_predicted_accuracy|

When error_in_prediction stays below 5% across many tasks and model sizes, you have shown that tiny attention noise is the mechanism behind LLM failures on long repetitive tasks, not reasoning collapse.
