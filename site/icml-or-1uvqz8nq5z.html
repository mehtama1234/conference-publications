From Welfare to Utility: Generalized Objectives in Budget-Feasible Procurement

This paper redesigns procurement auctions so a budget-limited buyer can optimize value minus payment or value minus production cost, rather than only maximizing raw value bought.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Budget-feasible procurement is the problem of buying services from strategic sellers when the buyer has a hard spending cap. Classical work mostly asks one question: how much value can the buyer purchase before the budget runs out? This paper argues that the objective is often wrong. A buyer may care about utility, which means value received minus money paid, or welfare, which means value received minus the sellers' real production costs. Those objectives behave differently. A mechanism that maximizes value can overpay badly. The paper develops mechanisms for welfare in the worst-case setting and for utility when the buyer has a probabilistic cost model, then shows how to convert an expectation-level budget guarantee into a budget guarantee that holds on every realized cost profile.

Zero assumption version

Buying the most value is not the same as buying well

A buyer wants to procure items or services from several sellers. Each seller privately knows its own cost, so it may exaggerate that cost when reporting it. The buyer has a budget and assigns a value to getting each service. Earlier mechanisms usually tried to maximize total value purchased under the budget.

That objective ignores two things. First, if the buyer overpays, total value can look high while utility is poor. Second, total value also ignores whether the selected services were expensive for sellers to produce, which matters for welfare. Once the objective changes, the mechanism itself has to change, because the payment rule and the selection rule must now trade off value, payment, and cost in a different way.

The core problem. Value maximization can spend the whole budget on expensive deals that look large on paper but leave little actual gain after payment.

What it actually does

Separate objectives

Treat welfare and buyer utility as distinct goals instead of folding everything into raw value.

Design a worst-case welfare mechanism

In the worst-case setting with no cost model, give a constant-factor approximation for welfare.

Move to Bayesian utility design

When the buyer knows cost laws, which means probability models for seller costs, build a mechanism that optimizes utility under an expected budget limit.

Strengthen the budget guarantee

Modify that mechanism so the budget is respected after every realized set of seller costs, not just on average.

Generalize the template

Extend the design logic beyond these two named objectives.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the objective that defines a good procurement decision. Instead of asking only how much value is bought, it asks how much value remains after payment or after production cost.

the object it moves  =  the optimization target inside budget-feasible procurement
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: gain left after paying

measured quantity  =  Net objective value under strategic bids

The buyer never directly sees the number it truly cares about. If the target is utility, the key number is value minus payment, but payment depends on the mechanism and on strategic reports. If the target is welfare, the key number is value minus true production cost, but true cost is privately known to the sellers. The mechanism has to optimize a quantity that is partly hidden and partly changed by its own payment rule.

That is why objective design matters so much. A mechanism that ranks bids by raw value can choose a bundle that looks attractive before payment, then discover that little net gain remains. The paper builds rules where the score attached to each purchase reflects the actual target, so the mechanism is aligned with the quantity the buyer wants to maximize.

Why it works

Why it works: score purchases by the right net objective

The first insight is conceptual. A procurement mechanism is not just a way to select items; it is a joint rule for selection and payment. Once payment is part of the objective, the mechanism must account for it directly. Optimizing raw value while hoping payments stay small is not enough, because the payment rule can erase most of the gain.

The second insight is that welfare and utility require different information regimes. Welfare compares value with true production cost, so the paper seeks approximation guarantees in the worst-case setting, where no probability model for costs is assumed. Utility is harder in that setting because payments depend on strategic behavior in ways that can break any guarantee. The paper therefore moves utility to a Bayesian setting, where the buyer knows cost laws well enough to optimize expected performance.

The conversion from expected budget feasibility to ex-post budget feasibility matters because averages are not enough for a real buyer. A mechanism that overspends only on rare outcomes may still be unusable if the budget is legally or operationally hard. The paper shows how to modify the design so the buyer remains within budget for every realized profile while losing only a small amount of utility. That step turns a mathematically neat rule into one that fits an actual budget process.

Math after the picture

The equation: net objective, not raw value

First in words:

The buyer may optimize utility, which subtracts payments, or welfare, which subtracts true production costs.

Then the compact form:

utility = value_of_selected_services - payments_to_selected_sellers

Every symbol:

utilitythe buyer's net gain after paying sellers
value_of_selected_serviceshow much the buyer values the procured services
payments_to_selected_sellersthe money actually paid out under the mechanism
A tiny worked example
Suppose two services together are worth 15 to the buyer.
A value-only rule buys both and pays 14.
Raw value is 15, which looks high, but utility is only 1.
A utility-aware rule buys one service worth 11 and pays 6.
Raw value drops to 11, yet utility rises to 5 because the payment is much smaller.

The same purchase can look good under value maximization and poor under utility maximization. The objective decides which outcome is preferred.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The cost model can be wrong. Utility guarantees in the Bayesian setting depend on having a useful model of seller costs. If that model is poor, expected-optimal choices can misfire.
Utility without a cost model is fundamentally limited. The paper explains that without cost-law information, even a single seller can destroy useful utility guarantees, so the mechanism designer cannot wish that difficulty away.
Budget tightening can reduce gains. Forcing the mechanism to stay within budget in every realized outcome may lower the achievable utility because some aggressive but profitable outcomes must be excluded.
Done right. Match the objective to the buyer's real goal, use Bayesian information only when it is credible, and demand ex-post feasibility when the budget cannot be violated even rarely.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Create a small procurement game with five sellers, hidden production costs, reported bids, buyer values, and a fixed budget. Compare a value-only selection rule with a utility-aware rule and a welfare-aware rule. Run many cost realizations from a known cost model and track raw value, buyer utility, welfare, and whether the budget is ever exceeded. The demo should show that the best rule depends on the chosen objective because payment and hidden cost change what counts as a good purchase.

net_gain = buyer_value_of_selected_bundle - total_payment

The main point to surface is that objective choice is not cosmetic. It changes which sellers should win and which payments are acceptable.
