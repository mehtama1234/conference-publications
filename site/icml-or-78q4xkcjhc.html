A Diagnostic Study of Multi-Agent LLMs for Real-World Debates

Evaluate debates by measuring the quality of the interaction process, not only whether the agents converge on an answer.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

In real debates, especially on policy or planning questions, there may be no single ground-truth answer that makes evaluation easy. This paper shows that outcome-only scoring misses important failures. A debate can end in agreement and still be poor if one agent dominates, others stop engaging, or the group converges too early. The paper therefore measures the debate process itself because the path to the answer often reveals whether the discussion was actually useful.

Why outcome-only debate scores are incomplete

Many current evaluations check whether agents reached consensus, whether a majority vote selected one answer, or whether another model liked the final output. Those checks can miss process failures because they focus on the endpoint. A weak debate can still produce a plausible answer by chance, while a strong debate may expose disagreement productively before arriving at a careful conclusion.

The paper builds diagnostic measures for engagement, responsiveness, influence balance, stability, and agent utility. These measures matter because they expose how the discussion unfolded, which is often where failures such as domination, sycophancy, or premature agreement first appear.

The paper's move in one sentence. Judge debate quality by inspecting interaction patterns as well as the final answer.

What it actually does

Run multi-agent debates

Collect full transcripts from real-world discussion settings where simple answer checking is weak.

Measure process features

Compute interpretable indicators such as how much each agent contributes and how often agents respond to each other.

Measure final outcomes

Keep the usual end-result checks so that process and outcome can be compared.

Compare with human judgments

Test which metrics line up better with what people judge as a healthy and useful debate so that the evaluation reflects the discussion quality humans actually care about.

Diagnose failure patterns

Use the process metrics to identify cases of domination, disengagement, or premature convergence.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Debate health

Debate health is the overall quality of the interaction process: whether agents engage with the topic, respond to each other, share influence reasonably, and remain open to revision without collapsing too early. This quantity is hard to observe from only the final answer.

The paper approximates debate health with several transparent metrics. That decomposition is useful because one number alone would hide which part of the interaction failed.

Why it works

Why this works: weak discussions leave structural traces

Domination, sycophancy, and premature convergence are not random accidents. They change the shape of the transcript. A dominating agent speaks more and moves others without reciprocal influence. A sycophantic agent agrees without adding independent content. Premature convergence appears as quick agreement before key objections are examined. Process metrics work because these failures alter who speaks, who responds, and how opinions change.

That is why the diagnostics align better with human judgment. People notice whether a debate was balanced and responsive because those properties are part of what makes discussion trustworthy in settings where a final answer cannot be verified directly.

A compact summary is to reward engagement, responsiveness, and balance while penalizing one-sided influence. The exact paper uses several metrics, but this formula shows the intended direction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Metrics can be gamed. Agents might learn to mention each other's points more often without adding real substance, which could raise responsiveness scores without improving the debate.
Not every domain values the same process shape. A legal debate, a planning meeting, and a brainstorming session may need different balances of disagreement and convergence, so one universal threshold may be too rigid.
Human judgments are also noisy. If human raters disagree about what a healthy debate looks like, the alignment target becomes fuzzy because the benchmark itself is unstable.
Done right. The main gain is diagnostic visibility because a developer can now see why a debate failed, not only that it failed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Run a set of three-agent debates on questions that do not have one easy ground-truth answer. Save full transcripts and ask human reviewers to rate discussion quality. Then compare two evaluators: one that scores only the final answer, and one that also computes process metrics such as response rate, balance, and influence asymmetry.

The demo should show that process-aware scoring predicts human ratings better because it catches debates that sound polished at the end but were structurally weak throughout.

better_alignment = corr(process_eval, human_rating) > corr(outcome_only, human_rating)

The demo must prove that transcript structure carries real evaluation signal because the route to an answer matters in open-ended debates.
