Normalization-Free Spiking Networks with Lateral Inhibition

Replace artificial normalization with biologically realistic inhibitory circuits that regulate how strongly neurons fire.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Spiking neural networks are energy-efficient models inspired by how real brains compute: instead of always-on computations, neurons only transmit information via discrete spikes, consuming power only when they fire. But training deep ones has required artificial normalization (a math trick to keep neuron activity stable). This paper builds deep spiking networks without that trick, using the brain's own inhibitory circuits instead: excitatory neurons (the speakers) and inhibitory neurons (the silencers) work together to keep activity from exploding or dying out, just like the cortex does.

Zero assumption version

The normalization trap

In a spiking neural network, neurons fire discrete spikes (all-or-nothing events) only when their internal voltage crosses a threshold. Because power consumption in neural hardware is proportional to the number of spike events, not to the dimension of the network, spiking networks use far less energy than dense neural networks that compute on every layer for every input sample. But training deep networks of spiking neurons is hard: as signals pass through many layers, the voltage can either explode (growing so large every neuron fires constantly) or die (shrinking so all neurons stay silent). The standard fix is to add a normalization layer—a mathematical scaling step that artificially squashes the voltage back into a safe range. This works, but it does not exist in the brain.

A beginner might try training a deep spiking network layer-by-layer, assuming each layer stabilizes on its own. It breaks: as you add more layers, the dynamics of early layers feed into later ones unpredictably, and the cascade either saturates or vanishes. You are forced to choose: use artificial normalization (lose biological realism) or watch deep networks fail to train.

The paper's move in one sentence. Instead of normalization, use two teams of neurons—excitatory (E) and inhibitory (I) neurons arranged like brain cortex—to regulate activity dynamically through subtractive inhibition (cutting raw voltage) and divisive inhibition (scaling how responsive neurons are).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the simple feedforward layer with an excitatory-inhibitory (E-I) circuit: each layer now contains two populations of neurons (E and I) that communicate with each other. Excitatory neurons drive activity forward; inhibitory neurons regulate how strong that activity can grow. The key is that inhibition works two ways: subtractive (directly removing voltage) and divisive (scaling the sensitivity). This replaces what normalization used to do artificially.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: E-I balance

measured quantity  =  Expected net input to each excitatory neuron

The paper measures a quantity that does not appear in the network output but controls whether training succeeds: the expected voltage each excitatory neuron receives after combining excitatory input (from the previous layer) and inhibitory input (from the inhibitory neurons). If this expected value is far from zero, neurons either saturate (always firing) or stay silent (never firing), and training collapses. The paper estimates this by assuming each presynaptic (input) neuron fires at an average probability (a spike rate), then computing the expected currents mathematically.

The paper uses this hidden quantity to initialize weights wisely: set the synaptic weights so that the expected excitatory current approximately equals the expected subtractive inhibitory current. This makes the expected net input near zero at initialization, putting every neuron in the sweet spot where it can both fire and silence itself based on input, because a neuron near zero voltage can respond to small increases in excitation by firing, and also quickly silence itself when inhibition kicks in—this responsive middle ground is where learning can actually happen.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Without inhibitory control, signals in a deep network amplify chaotically: layer 1 output becomes layer 2 input, which grows further in layer 3, and so on. Artificial normalization clamps this artificially. The paper instead uses biology: excitatory neurons fire and send spikes forward; inhibitory neurons simultaneously fire in response and send two kinds of inhibitory current backward into the excitatory neurons. Subtractive inhibition directly reduces the excitatory voltage (like turning down a volume knob). Divisive inhibition scales the gain of the excitatory neurons (like adjusting the sensitivity of a microphone so loud sounds do not saturate it). Together, these two inhibitory pathways regulate activity to stay in a middle zone—active but bounded.

The second key is how to initialize and train this circuit stably. Initialization is critical: if weights are random, inhibition is weak, and neurons saturate immediately (either all fire or all silent). The paper's E-I Init scheme computes the expected firing probability from data, then sets weights such that expected excitatory and expected inhibitory currents approximately cancel—neurons wake up balanced, not drowning in signal or starving. During training, E-I Prop guards the learning signal flow: tiny divisive currents (near zero) cause numerical explosions during differentiation, so they are clamped; large inhibitory learning signals also destabilize training, so they are scaled down. With these guards, learning signals flow cleanly through many layers, and the network learns end-to-end without any artificial normalization trick.

Math after the picture

Equation and worked example

First in words:

The subthreshold membrane potential of an excitatory neuron at the next time step is the current potential, decayed by a time constant, minus any spikes emitted, plus excitatory and inhibitory input currents.

Then the compact form:

u_E[t+1] = (1 - 1/tau_E) * u_E[t] - theta_E * s_E[t] + I_EE[t] - I_EI[t]

Every symbol:

u_E[t+1]membrane voltage of excitatory neuron at next time step
tau_Etime constant controlling how fast voltage decays
s_E[t]spike output (1 if fired, 0 if silent)
theta_Espike threshold voltage
I_EE[t]excitatory input current (from previous E neurons)
I_EI[t]inhibitory input current (from I neurons), subtractive and divisive
A tiny worked example
Suppose tau_E = 2.0, theta_E = 1.0, u_E starts at 0.0.
At time 0: I_EE[0] = 1.6 (excitatory input), I_EI[0] = 0.6 (inhibitory suppression).
u_E[1] = (1 - 0.5) * 0 - 1.0 * 0 + 1.6 - 0.6 = 1.0 (exactly at threshold).
At time 1: neuron spikes (s_E[1] = 1), so next step includes - theta_E * 1 = - 1.0.
I_EE[1] = 1.5, I_EI[1] = 0.5 (inhibition adapts).
u_E[2] = 0.5 * 1.0 - 1.0 * 1 + 1.5 - 0.5 = 0.5 (voltage resets, neuron is alive for next cycle).
Without inhibition (I_EI = 0), voltage would grow: u_E[2] = 0.5 + 1.5 = 2.0, soon saturated. Inhibition keeps it bounded.

Inhibitory current (I_EI) acts as a dynamic brake: stronger excitatory input triggers stronger inhibitory response, preventing saturation and keeping neurons in the active range even over many layers, because the inhibition automatically scales up with the excitatory signal, so no matter how strong the input gets, the net effect stays bounded instead of exploding like it would without that negative feedback.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Naive initialization fails. If you randomly initialize weights without respecting E-I balance, neurons will either saturate (fire constantly) or go silent. The E-I Init scheme prevents this by computing expected firing rates from data and setting weights to cancel expected currents.
Numerical explosions in backward pass. Divisive inhibition can produce very small currents, which blow up when differentiated (the learning signal becomes huge). E-I Prop clamps near-zero values to the smallest positive value in a batch, keeping learning signals stable.
Inhibitory learning signals destabilize training. The inhibitory synaptic weights can produce disproportionately large learning signals (updates that are too big), throwing off the optimization. E-I Prop scales these learning signals down adaptively.
Done right: circuit adapts with learning. When E-I Init and E-I Prop are applied together, the circuit self-regulates during training. Weights evolve to maintain E-I balance while solving the task, allowing stable end-to-end training through arbitrarily deep networks.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a spiking network 5-10 layers deep. Train it on a simple classification task (e.g., CIFAR-10) or a neuromorphic dataset (e.g., DVS-Gesture, which records spikes directly from event cameras). Implement three variants: (1) standard feedforward SNNs with artificial batch normalization, (2) unconstrained E-I SNNs without E-I Init or E-I Prop (should fail or collapse), and (3) full E-I SNNs with both E-I Init and E-I Prop. Track the following metrics across training.

Training stability = whether network trains to convergence without numerical errors or collapse; Forgetting = initial accuracy on first batch vs. accuracy after 50 updates (should be small); E-I balance = average absolute net input per layer at initialization (should be < 0.1).

The demo must show: (a) unconstrained E-I fails or trains slowly, (b) E-I Init + E-I Prop matches normalization in accuracy with no artificial tricks, and (c) E-I circuit voltage profiles stay bounded throughout training, proving that inhibition dynamically stabilizes activity without external intervention.
