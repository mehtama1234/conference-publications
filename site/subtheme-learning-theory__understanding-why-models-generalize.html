Understanding why models generalize

Papers investigating the theoretical foundations that explain when and why machine learning models can successfully learn patterns from limited examples and perform well on new data.

What this is

This subtheme tackles the fundamental question: why does a model trained on a finite set of examples perform well on new examples outside the training set? When you show a model 100 photographs of dogs, it learns to identify a dog from the pattern of pixels it sees. But there are infinitely many ways pixels can arrange themselves, so why does the pattern it learned apply to the 101st dog? This is not obvious. A model could have simply memorized the 100 examples, and then failed on the 101st.

The difficulty comes from the gap between two worlds: the world of training data (the 100 examples you showed the model) and the world of new data (everything the model has never seen). If training and new data come from the same underlying distribution — if both sets of photos show real dogs under normal conditions — then there is hope that patterns learned from training transfer to new data. But if the new data is different (say, only dogs wearing costumes), then what the model learned might not apply. Learning theory tries to quantify this gap and find conditions where generalization is guaranteed.

Researchers study what properties make generalization possible. They examine the size and complexity of the model (simpler models tend to generalize better because they are less likely to fit noise), the amount and diversity of training data (more data, especially data that covers edge cases, helps), and the inherent structure of the problem (some tasks are easier to learn than others). They also analyze specific learning algorithms to predict how much the performance will improve when you add more data or a more complex model, because understanding these tradeoffs tells you how much training is required. The work involves statistical tools that measure how confidently a model's behavior on training data predicts its behavior on new data.

How the work breaks down

Generalization theory splits into several natural directions. First, understanding how data size and shifts in data patterns affect whether models work on new data—more data usually helps because it reduces the chance of fitting random noise, and knowing how much data is needed tells you whether a learning problem is feasible. Second, analyzing specific learning algorithms and model architectures to predict their behavior—different algorithms and designs have very different learning costs because some explore the hypothesis space more efficiently than others. Third, characterizing the role of model complexity and how to find the right balance—simpler models generalize better because they fit less noise and have fewer ways to memorize random patterns. Fourth, studying how networks learn and represent information in ways that enable transfer to new tasks—features that capture real patterns transfer to new data because the patterns exist everywhere, while features that capture noise do not generalize because noise is random and specific to each dataset.

Data shifts and performance on new patterns

A central question in generalization is: what happens when training data comes from one data pattern but you want the model to work on different data? This is the data-shift problem, and it is hard because the training examples may not represent all the patterns the model will encounter in the real world. When a model learns to identify dogs from regular photographs, it might fail on photos of dogs wearing costumes or in unusual lighting. Researchers need to prove under what conditions a model trained on one data pattern can still perform well on another.

The first paper on adversarial optimal transport solvers takes a statistical learning perspective on algorithms that move or transform data from one domain to another with minimal cost. It provides the first theoretical analysis of these algorithms, giving bounds on how well they generalize when moving data between two domains. The second paper tackles out-of-distribution generalization directly by studying when empirical risk minimization (the basic learning approach of training on available data) can work across distribution shifts. Under certain structure conditions, it shows the algorithm can achieve nearly optimal error rates without needing to know exactly how much the distributions differ. The third paper on performative learning addresses a unique scenario: when your model changes the world around it, so the distribution you learn from differs from the distribution you will encounter. This happens with recommendation systems or hiring algorithms. The theory proves bounds on generalization even when the act of deploying the model shifts the data distribution itself.

Sample complexity and learning algorithms

Sample complexity is the fundamental question of how much training data you need to learn reliably. More data usually helps because more examples reduce the chance of fitting random noise by accident. The question is: by how much? For some tasks, a little more data gives a big jump in performance because the underlying pattern is simple and easy to capture. For others, you need exponentially more data to make small gains because the pattern is complex or rare cases require special handling. Predicting these tradeoffs tells you how hard a learning problem actually is and whether collecting more data is worth the cost. Different types of models and learning tasks have dramatically different sample complexity, and theory aims to characterize these differences precisely.

For graph neural networks, the theory proves that generalization error decreases as the square root of the log of dimension over sample size for arbitrary graphs. This is much better when the graph has special structure: under homophily conditions (nodes with similar labels connect to each other), the error only needs dimension divided by sample size. This improvement happens because when similar labels cluster together, the network can learn the pattern more easily—it sees strong signal rather than noise. This directly explains empirical observations where GNNs work differently on different real-world graphs—networks with higher homophily let models generalize from fewer examples. For test-time scaling strategies like self-correction and reasoning, the theory proves sample complexity bounds that separate what works and what does not. Notably, it shows that when a model can correct its own outputs, it can learn multiple tasks at once without being told what the tasks are, opening a path to more flexible learning. For determining network width, the theory provides a method using a mathematical tool called the neural tangent kernel (which connects how changing weights changes the outputs to how learning happens) to identify the cardinal width where a network's generalization saturates. This avoids expensive training and lets you estimate how wide the network needs to be before wider networks stop helping.

Feature learning, representation quality, and grokking

When a model learns, it builds internal representations or features that capture patterns in data. The question is: when are these representations good enough to generalize? A model might seem to memorize training data perfectly but then fail on new data because its representations captured noise rather than real patterns. Understanding what makes representations transfer to new tasks matters for building models that work in the real world. Relatedly, grokking is a surprising phenomenon: a model trains on data until it fits training examples perfectly, then suddenly its test performance jumps dramatically. Why does this delayed learning happen, and what does it tell us about generalization?

The first paper provides an alternative theory to the neural feature ansatz, a popular model of how networks build representations. The new theory (FACT) matches learned features more closely, explains why the traditional theory usually works anyway, and captures grokking and sudden learning transitions in sparse parity problems. This addresses a major gap: most generalization theory does not explain grokking. The second paper studies networks that focus selectively on input parts and their storage capacity, proving explicit formulas for how much information such a network can hold in its value vectors (representations computed from inputs) when those vectors are not perpendicular to each other. It establishes that under non-perpendicular vectors, scaling is multiplicative, giving concrete predictions about capacity limits. The third paper analyzes contrastive learning, where a model learns by comparing positive and negative examples. Large negative sets improve generalization because they give the model more patterns to distinguish the positive example from, reducing the chance of overfitting to accidental similarities. It derives bounds with precise scaling laws showing how many negative examples are needed, and experiments on vision-language models confirm these predictions, showing theory-guided scaling works in practice.

Model architecture and automatic learning constraints

The architecture of a model—its depth, width, connectivity, and how information flows—shapes what it can learn and how well it generalizes. Some architectures have built-in biases that steer learning toward simpler patterns and away from overfitting, so the training process naturally avoids memorization. This automatic constraint on learning can happen through the training process, even without explicit penalties. Understanding these effects lets us design better architectures and predict their generalization behavior by reasoning through the architecture rather than by expensive trial and error, which saves both time and compute resources.

For heuristic learning in search problems, the theory proves that networks can learn admissible heuristics (lower bounds that guide search without lying) with strong generalization guarantees that depend on network width and depth. This extends bounds for goal-dependent heuristics, showing architecture affects sample complexity. For Schrodinger potential estimation in unpaired data, the theory establishes tight bounds on how well an algorithm can learn even when training data is not paired with test data. The convergence rates achieve near-optimal scaling with logarithmic factors, even for complex distributions like Gaussian mixtures. These results show that under the right conditions, learning from seemingly incomplete information can still succeed and its difficulty can be precisely quantified.

Modern neural networks and rapid task adaptation

Modern neural networks using selective focus mechanisms have become the dominant architecture, yet understanding why they generalize is still an open problem. In-context learning is a striking ability: such a network can perform a new task by seeing only a few examples in its input, without any weight updates. This is not learning in the traditional sense, yet it works remarkably well. Meta-learning asks: how can a model learn to learn, adapting quickly to new tasks? Theory needs to explain when and why these phenomena succeed, and what limits them.

For learning pairwise interactions in attention-style models, the theory proves dimension-free minimax rates: the complexity needed to learn depends on model properties but not on the number of input elements (tokens), the total number of dimensions, or the rank of weight matrices. This surprising result explains why attention mechanisms are efficient learners. For in-context learning viewed as Bayesian reasoning, the theory proves that these selective-focus networks implement optimal algorithms for learning new tasks when few examples are provided. Task uncertainty drops exponentially with just a few examples, clarifying why few-shot in-context learning works. For the sample complexity of attention itself, the theory shows attention maps converge to the true data pattern at a specific rate. On distributions with bounded support, convergence is inversely proportional to the square root of sample size, and this directly predicts slowdowns in downstream accuracy on real and synthetic data.
