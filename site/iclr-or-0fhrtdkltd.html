Understanding VLMs Spatial Mental Modeling Capability from Limited Views

Vision language models can imagine the full scene from a few views if first trained to make an internal map, then reason about it.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

A vision language model (a neural network that understands both pictures and words) cannot easily figure out what a room looks like when shown only one or two angles, the way you or I would. This paper introduces MindCube, a benchmark that measures this gap, then shows how to bridge it: train the model to first generate a mental map (like sketching a floor plan), then reason about invisible corners and how things would move.

Zero assumption version

The gap between pictures and imagination

When you see a room from one corner, your brain instantly builds a mental layout: you know there is a wall to your left, furniture in the center, a window somewhere. You have never seen those parts, but you reason about them. A vision language model (a neural network trained on images and text together) sees a picture and answers questions, but it processes only what is in the frame. Show it one corner of a room and ask what is behind you, and it guesses randomly.

A beginner might think: just show the model more views during training, and it will learn. But the real problem is deeper. The model learns patterns from pixels, not the spatial layout underneath. A pixel is just a color value at one spot. Pixels do not inherently carry information about where things are in space, or how they would look if you stepped three feet to the left. Without building an internal structured map—a symbolic place to store 'left wall is here, couch is there'—the model has nowhere to ground its reasoning.

The method in one sentence. Train the model to first generate a bird's-eye cognitive map from the views it has, then let it reason over that map to answer questions about unseen space.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Take multiple limited views

Feed the model two or three pictures of a scene from different camera angles, each showing only part of the room.

Step 2: Generate a bird's-eye cognitive map

The model produces a structured representation of the full layout, like a labeled floor plan, showing where walls, furniture, and objects are positioned.

Step 3: Ground the question to the map

When asked 'What is behind you?', the model refers to the map: 'The map shows I am in the corner, so behind me is the opposite wall.'

Step 4: Reason over the spatial structure

The model uses the map to reason about unseen angles, hidden objects, and how things would move if the camera turned or an object was pushed.

Step 5: Reinforce correct reasoning chains

Reinforcement learning teaches the model to prefer reasoning paths that match human spatial understanding, nudging it away from guesses.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change how the model processes pixels. Instead, it adds a new intermediate step: spatial reasoning through an internal cognitive map. Before, the model went straight from pictures to answers in one jump. Now it goes: look at pictures, generate a cognitive map, then use the map to answer the question. The map is a structured bottleneck that forces the model to reason about space and positions.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spatial coherence

measured quantity  =  spatial coherence

Spatial coherence is how well an internal representation preserves geometric relationships: if object A is left of object B in the map, then moving left should bring you closer to A. Humans use spatial coherence to navigate mentally. A model that has never been trained to track position, distance, or orientation has no way to maintain it. The paper measures spatial coherence indirectly through accuracy on position (where is the couch?), orientation (where would you be if you turned around?), and dynamics (if I move forward, what do I hit?).

The paper estimates spatial coherence by training the model to generate explicit cognitive maps and then checking if the maps answer spatial questions correctly. The benchmark, MindCube, has 21,154 questions designed to probe position, orientation, and dynamics reasoning. A model with high spatial coherence answers these questions accurately; one without coherence scores near 50% (random guessing on multiple-choice questions).

Why it works

Why the map unlocks spatial reasoning

A vision language model trained to classify images learns pixel patterns: 'this texture is a wall, this shape is a chair.' But pixels have no coordinates. If I ask 'Is the wall to your left or right?' the model has no way to answer, because it never learned to assign a position to the wall. It only learned to recognize it.

When the model is trained to first generate a cognitive map, it is forced to make spatial choices: this wall goes here, that chair goes there, the camera is positioned thus. The map becomes a bottleneck: every answer must route through spatial positions and angles. Once the map exists, reasoning becomes tractable. If I ask 'What is behind you?' the model checks the map: 'Behind me, opposite of where the camera points, is the far wall.' The map is not perfect—it is built from limited views—but it is a shared structured space where spatial questions ground.

Reinforcement learning then teaches the model to prefer maps and reasoning chains that match human spatial intuition. If the model generates a map that says 'the wall is in two places at once,' that map fails to answer spatial questions correctly, so reinforcement learning steers away from that solution. Over time, the model learns to generate coherent maps and reason over them consistently.

The training objective

First in words:

total loss equals map-generation loss plus reasoning loss plus a reinforcement learning bonus that rewards high spatial accuracy

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_map + L_reason + alpha * (accuracy - baseline)

Every symbol:

L_mappenalty for generating a spatial map that is inconsistent (e.g. objects in impossible positions)
L_reasonpenalty for reasoning steps that do not lead to the correct answer
accuracythe fraction of MindCube questions answered correctly (position, orientation, dynamics combined)
baselinethe average accuracy before reinforcement learning, around 37.8%
alphahow strongly to reward improvement over the baseline (the reinforcement learning weight)
A tiny worked example
Suppose L_map (map quality) = 0.4 and L_reason (reasoning mistakes) = 0.3, so L_total before RL = 0.7.
Before RL, accuracy = 38%. Baseline = 38%, so (38 - 38) = 0, no bonus.
After RL training, accuracy = 62%. Bonus = alpha * (62 - 38) = alpha * 24.
If alpha = 0.01, bonus = 0.24, so RL nudges the model toward that 62% solution.
Training updates the model to prefer maps and reasoning that jointly maximize map coherence + answer accuracy.
Later, with alpha boosted, accuracy reaches 70.7%. The map and reasoning are now locked together.

The training jointly optimizes map quality and reasoning accuracy, forcing the model to build a spatial representation that supports answering questions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The map is built from partial views. If the camera views miss an entire corner, the generated map will be incomplete or wrong. The model fills in the blank with its best guess, which may not match the true layout. This limits how accurate the reasoning can ever be, even with reinforcement learning.
The map can hallucinate objects that do not exist. To make the spatial layout consistent and complete, the model may invent an object or wall that was never seen, just to fill in the blank space. Downstream reasoning then reasons about things that do not actually exist.
Reinforcement learning can overfit to the benchmark. MindCube is one specific set of spatial questions. If reinforcement learning optimizes too hard on those exact questions, the model may fail on real-world spatial reasoning that does not follow the same pattern.
Done right. The model learns to generate sparse, minimal cognitive maps (only essential features), cross-check the map against all views before finalizing it, and test the map on a held-out set of spatial questions before using reinforcement learning.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal version: take a synthetic 3D room (e.g. a cube with furniture). Show a vision language model two camera angles (40% of the scene). First, ask it spatial questions without the map training (baseline). It will score around 38 to 45%, mostly guessing. Then, fine-tune the same model on a small set of synthetic rooms where you provide ground-truth cognitive maps and spatial question answers. Ask it the same questions again. It should score around 60 to 70%, a jump of 20+ points. Then compare: is the jump due to memorizing answers, or learning spatial reasoning? Test on a held-out set of rooms the model never saw during training. If accuracy stays high on unseen rooms, the model learned spatial reasoning, not memorization.

improvement = accuracy_with_map - accuracy_without_map

The demo proves that a cognitive map is the causal bridge: removing the map removes the accuracy gain; a model cannot achieve high spatial reasoning without first building an explicit internal spatial representation.
