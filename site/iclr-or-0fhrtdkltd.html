Map-Then-Reason: Teaching Models to Imagine Hidden Spaces

Vision-language models learn spatial reasoning by first writing a map, then reasoning about it step by step.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Humans can picture a room from just one view—we imagine walls, windows, and furniture we did not see. Vision-language models (large image-understanding programs) cannot do this. This paper teaches them to imagine by having them write down their mental picture as a map, then reason step by step using that map. The model's accuracy improves from 38 percent to 61 percent because intermediate maps force the model to externalize spatial reasoning rather than rely on shallow image patterns; pushing further to 71 percent with reward training refines this process by penalizing maps that do not support correct reasoning.

Zero assumption version

Models see like robots, not humans

Imagine you are standing in a room and can only look forward. You see a wall, a window, a chair. A human would instantly know: if I turn right and walk forward, will I get closer to the window? We do this by building a mental picture of the whole room in our heads. Vision-language models—the programs that read images and answer questions—fail at this. They answer almost randomly (near 38 percent on a tough test).

A beginner would think: just show the model more views of the room. Let it see left, right, back, all at once. The problem: that's not how humans actually see. Humans see one view, then imagine the rest. If you teach the model to memorize all four views, it never learns to imagine.

The model cannot picture what it did not see. When the model sees only one view of a room, it has no internal map of the whole scene.

What the paper changes

What changes: the training pipeline

Most approaches feed images directly to a language model. This paper inserts an intermediate step: the model must first output a cognitive map (object positions and coordinates), then output reasoning chains, then output answers. The model learns to do all three at once.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spatial structure in the map

measured quantity  =  Isomorphic map rate

The paper measures whether the model's cognitive map actually matches the true scene layout. An isomorphic map is one where the object positions and relative distances are structurally the same as the real room, even if drawn on a different grid. The paper tracks this metric: what fraction of generated maps are structurally identical to ground truth?

During training, the model learns to generate maps that are not just plausible but actually match the scene structure. The paper uses this as a diagnostic: strong maps predict strong reasoning and strong final answers. Weak maps (where objects are in the wrong relative positions) lead to wrong answers.

Why it works

Why it works: scaffolding thought step by step

Spatial reasoning happens step by step, not all at once. Humans rotate, walk, check distance. The old approach forced the model to guess the answer directly from one image. It never learned to step through the space because it was never asked to.

This paper makes the model externalize its thought process. First, it writes down the spatial layout (the map). When the model generates a map, it must actually understand where objects are relative to each other—it cannot memorize a shortcut. Second, it writes step-by-step reasoning in English (turn right, move forward, is the window closer?). Third, it reads its own reasoning to pick the answer. Because the model trained on maps and reasoning together, correct maps enable clearer reasoning, and reasoning that works forces the map to be accurate. The two steps reinforce each other.

The training objective: learn two tasks at once

First in words:

Total loss = (loss for predicting correct map) + (loss for predicting correct reasoning) + (loss for predicting correct answer)

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_map + L_reason + L_answer

Every symbol:

L_mappenalty when the generated map does not match ground truth positions
L_reasonpenalty when the step-by-step reasoning does not match human explanations
L_answerpenalty when the final answer is wrong
L_totalcombined loss that the model tries to minimize
A tiny worked example
Suppose the model generates a map with objects at wrong positions: L_map is high (say 2.5).
Weak map leads to confused reasoning: L_reason is also high (say 1.8).
Confused reasoning picks the wrong answer: L_answer is high (say 1.2).
Total loss = 2.5 + 1.8 + 1.2 = 5.5.
Now suppose the model generates a correct map: L_map drops to 0.3.
Good map enables clear reasoning: L_reason drops to 0.4.
Clear reasoning picks the right answer: L_answer drops to 0.1.
Total loss = 0.3 + 0.4 + 0.1 = 0.8.
By training on all three losses together, the model learns that correct maps unlock correct reasoning.

The model minimizes all three penalties at once, so it learns to generate good maps, good reasoning, and good answers in parallel.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong map, right answer by luck. If the generated map is incorrect but happens to support correct reasoning by coincidence, the model may learn bad maps. The fix: weight map accuracy heavily during training.
Frozen image encoder limits perception. The model uses a frozen (non-trainable) image encoder from earlier work. If that encoder misses spatial cues in the image, the downstream map will be wrong no matter what, because the map can only be built from the features the encoder provides. Unfreezing or replacing the encoder would expose more spatial information for map generation.
Limited to structured object layouts. The benchmark tests scenes with 2-4 objects in clear spatial arrangements (rooms, outdoor scenes). Cluttered, complex scenes or dynamic actions might break the approach.
Reasoning chains are long and slow. Generating maps and reasoning chains adds tokens (text) to the output. This is slower than direct prediction. In real-world deployment, the extra latency may not be worth the accuracy gain.
Done right. When the training data includes accurate maps and reasoning chains, and both are weighted equally, the model learns coherent spatial understanding that transfers to new scenes and new questions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a lightweight demo with 20 test scenes (a mix of indoor and outdoor). For each scene, show one view of the image and ask a spatial question: where is the object? If I move here, am I closer? The demo should compare two models: (1) a baseline that predicts directly from the image, and (2) the map-then-reason model. Show the generated map, the reasoning chain, and the final answer side by side.

Accuracy = (number of correct answers) / (total questions)

The demo must make clear that intermediate maps and reasoning chains are not just padding—they are real, checkable steps that the model relies on. When you hide the map, accuracy plummets. When you show the map and reasoning, you can see why the model chose its answer, and it is usually right.
