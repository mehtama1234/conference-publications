Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion for Goal-Oriented Robot Navigation

A robot fills in the unobserved parts of a map using diffusion to guess what obstacles and objects are hidden, so it can find a goal even when most of the world is unknown.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots need to find a specific object in a room they have never seen before. The main obstacle: they can only see a small area around themselves, so the rest of the map is blank. Most navigation systems fail when the map has big gaps. This paper proposes a method that uses a diffusion model (a technique for gradually building images by removing noise) to intelligently guess what occupies the unseen regions, making navigation possible even with incomplete maps.

Zero assumption version

Robots are blind to the unseen world

A robot standing in a hallway can see maybe 10 feet ahead and to the sides, but the rest of the building is invisible. To find a goal object (say, a cup on a table), the robot needs a map of where obstacles and objects are located. Most systems assume the map is complete or heavily pre-observed.

A naive approach: ignore the blank regions and navigate only in the seen area. This fails immediately when the goal is in the unseen part. A better approach: mark unseen regions as unknown, then guess what they contain. But the guesses are often inconsistent (the system might predict a wall here and open space there, contradicting the physics of how buildings are actually laid out) because simple guessing rules do not learn what realistic buildings look like.

The core problem. Robots cannot navigate to goals in unseen regions because they lack a way to predict what those hidden areas contain.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Input the partial map

Start with a bird's-eye-view map showing known obstacles and objects, with blank spaces for unseen regions.

Add noise to unobserved labels

In the unknown areas, assign random guesses (noise) for what could be there (walls, objects, empty space).

Iteratively refine via diffusion

Run a learned model that removes noise step by step. At each step, it updates the guesses to be more consistent with the known boundaries and layout rules.

Enforce structural coherence

The model uses a prior (a learned pattern about how obstacles typically arrange) to ensure the filled-in map respects edges and connectivity.

Feed the completed map to navigation

Once the diffusion is done, use the full map (known + completed labels) with any existing navigation strategy to reach the goal.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: unobserved map labels

The paper substitutes a learned diffusion model in place of simple placeholder logic or heuristic guessing for the missing parts of a map. Specifically, it changes what occupies the blank regions: instead of leaving them empty or guessing randomly, it generates obstacle and semantic labels (categories like wall, table, floor) that are consistent with the visible boundaries and structure.

the object it moves  =  unobserved map labels
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: structural consistency in map completion

measured quantity  =  structural coherence

Structural coherence is a measure of whether the completed map respects the natural layout of the environment. In a real building, if you see a wall on one side and open space on the other at the boundary between seen and unseen regions, the unseen region should not have a disconnected floating obstacle in the middle (that would be physically impossible). The paper measures this implicitly by training the diffusion model on large datasets of real maps, so the model learns that certain layouts are far more probable than others. The model also explicitly incorporates an obstacle prior (a pattern learned from known obstacles) to weight the denoising process, pulling the guesses toward realistic configurations.

The paper estimates coherence indirectly through the quality of navigation results: if the completed map is structurally sound, the robot reaches the goal; if it is incoherent, navigation fails. The method also samples multiple completions and picks the one with the highest probability (the ones the model is most confident about).

Why it works

Why it works: diffusion as a learned prior

The core insight is that a diffusion model trained on real maps learns what realistic layouts look like. When you start with noise in the unobserved region and iteratively remove that noise, the model nudges each guess toward plausible configurations. This is fundamentally different from random guessing: the model has seen thousands of real buildings and knows that walls cluster, obstacles do not float randomly, and open spaces tend to connect.

The paper adds a second layer: it explicitly uses the visible boundary (where the seen region meets the unseen region) as a structural anchor. If the boundary shows a wall edge, the denoising model is encouraged to respect that edge and extend it coherently into the hidden area. It also incorporates an obstacle prior that weights the denoising toward confirmed real-world obstacle distributions. The combination means the guesses are both realistic (learned from data) and locally consistent (anchored to what the robot actually sees).

Math after the picture

The diffusion denoising equation

First in words:

At each step of denoising, the model predicts the noise added at that step and subtracts it from the current estimate. The prediction is guided by the partial map (the known region), an obstacle prior that encodes typical obstacle patterns, and a weight on how much to trust each constraint. The output is a less-noisy estimate of the complete map.

Then the compact form:

m_t-1 = (m_t - sqrt(1 - alpha_t) * epsilon_theta(m_t, c)) / sqrt(alpha_t) + lambda * prior(m_t, obstacles_seen)

Every symbol:

m_tthe map estimate at denoising step t
m_t-1the map estimate after one denoising step (improved)
alpha_ta scheduling factor that controls noise levels across steps
epsilon_theta(m_t, c)the diffusion model's prediction of the noise in the current map, conditioned on known region c
lambdaa weight controlling how strongly the obstacle prior influences the denoising
prior(m_t, obstacles_seen)the learned pattern encoding realistic obstacle arrangements, anchored to visible boundaries
A tiny worked example
Suppose the known region at a boundary shows a wall (high obstacle value = 8.0). At step t, the unseen region guesses a random value 3.2.
The diffusion model predicts noise of 2.1 and alpha_t = 0.9, so the denoised estimate = (3.2 - sqrt(0.1) * 2.1) / sqrt(0.9) = (3.2 - 0.66) / 0.95 ≈ 2.8.
The obstacle prior also prefers walls to be connected, so it adds 0.5 * (boundary_wall - guess) = 0.5 * (8.0 - 2.8) = 2.6.
After constraint: 2.8 + 0.5 * 2.6 ≈ 4.1 (moved toward the boundary wall, still refining).
After many steps, the guess converges to ~7.8, closely matching the known boundary wall. Structure is preserved.

The equation shows how diffusion removes noise iteratively, while priors anchor the guesses to real layouts and visible boundaries.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Hallucinated obstacles in truly empty space. If a large region is genuinely open, the diffusion model might predict obstacles incorrectly because it learned from datasets where open areas often contain clutter. The fix: weight the obstacle prior less in regions where the boundary clearly shows open space.
Boundary edge confusion. If the robot's sensor reading of the boundary is noisy or misaligned, the completed map will propagate that error into the unseen region. The robot's position estimate must be accurate.
Semantic inconsistency across the boundary. If the known region shows a table and the unseen region is supposed to be floor, but the model predicts the unseen as table, the boundary becomes incoherent. The method mitigates this by training jointly on semantic and obstacle labels.
Done right. Train the diffusion model on maps collected from the same environment type (indoor offices, warehouses, etc.) and validate that boundaries match real-world continuity before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small simulated environment with a known floor plan. Mask 60 to 80 percent of the map as unseen and run the PLMD method to complete it. Simultaneously, run a baseline that either ignores unseen regions or fills them randomly. Place a goal object in the unseen region and measure whether each navigation strategy reaches it within a step budget.

success_rate = (times_goal_reached / total_trials) × 100%; completion_coherence = measured_distance_from_completed_to_ground_truth_map

If PLMD works, the completed map will be physically valid (no floating walls, connected spaces), and the robot will navigate to the goal reliably. The baseline will fail or crash into hallucinated obstacles.
