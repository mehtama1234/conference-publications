Robust Spiking Neural Networks Against Adversarial Attacks

Spiking neural networks become harder to attack by moving neurons away from their firing cliffs.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Spiking neural networks are brain-inspired: instead of outputting continuous numbers like regular neural networks, they pulse (fire) in discrete spikes. This uses far less power, but they are fragile. When attackers add small noise to the input, threshold-neighboring neurons (those sitting right at their firing cliff) flip from firing to silent or vice versa, breaking the whole network. This paper shows you can harden them by pushing those neurons far from their cliffs.

Zero assumption version

The fragile cliff at the neuron's threshold

A spiking neuron collects incoming signals into a tank called a membrane potential. When the tank reaches a cliff (the firing threshold), the neuron spikes. When it spikes, the tank empties and starts filling again. The problem: neurons whose tank level sits exactly at the cliff are jittery. A tiny noise bump sends them over the edge to fire, while an equally tiny downward nudge silences them. Directly trained spiking networks (trained using standard automated learning methods) develop many of these cliff-sitters because the training process naturally settles neurons near decision boundaries, which are unstable.

An attacker adds just a few percent noise to the input image. Because cliff-sitters are so sensitive, this noise flips them from firing to silent or firing to silent in reverse. The flipped neurons send completely different signals downstream. The entire decision collapses, and the network misclassifies. A gradient (the direction to nudge weights during training) is a measure of how much changing a weight affects the final error. Standard training methods compute these gradients, and cliff-sitting neurons make gradients very large, giving attackers a clear roadmap for where to perturb.

The move: Penalize neurons for sitting near the firing cliff; push their tank levels far away from the threshold.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Measure distance to cliff

For each neuron, check how far its tank level is from the firing threshold.

Penalize cliff-sitting

Add a loss penalty: the closer you sit to the cliff, the bigger the penalty grows.

Retrain weights

A training algorithm (using a technique called backpropagation, which traces errors backward through the network) adjusts weights to reduce the penalty, pushing tanks away from thresholds.

Add probabilistic noise

Replace hard deterministic spiking with slightly randomized spiking in critical neurons.

Test against attack

Test on adversarial inputs; neurons now resist flipping under small perturbations.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes

The paper changes the training loss function. Standard training minimizes only the classification error. Threshold Guarding Optimization adds a second term: a penalty that grows when any neuron's membrane potential sits too close to its firing threshold. This forces the network to reorganize its internals to keep neurons far from the cliff.

the object it moves  =  Training loss function
The hidden quantity

Distance to the firing threshold

measured quantity  =  Membrane potential margin

This is simply how far each neuron's tank level sits from the firing cliff. A neuron with a tank at level 5 and a firing threshold at 10 has a margin of 5. A margin of 1 means you are balancing at the edge; a margin of 10 means you are deep in safe territory.

The paper estimates this for every neuron at every time step during training. For neurons that creep close to the threshold (margin less than a tuning value delta), the loss function penalizes them, growing the penalty quadratically as they approach the cliff. This pushes the network to learn weights that keep margins large.

Paper reading. The paper proves mathematically that threshold-neighboring neurons set the upper bound on how strong an adversarial attack can be. By moving neurons away from thresholds, you reduce the gradient magnitude and shrink that upper bound.
Why it works

Why pushing neurons away from the cliff makes attacks fail

An adversarial attack works by finding a direction in input space where small changes trigger huge cascading errors. Neurons sitting at the firing cliff are the gatekeepers: they flip state easily, and downstream neurons amplify that flip into catastrophic failure. When you penalize cliff-sitting, the network learns to reroute decisions through neurons with large margins. Those neurons are stiff: small input noise does not budge their tank level enough to flip them. They act like shock absorbers.

The paper adds a second layer: noisy spiking. Instead of a deterministic cliff (spike if V > threshold), it becomes probabilistic: spike more likely if V is well above threshold, but never guaranteed. This randomness breaks the attacker's ability to carefully aim a tiny perturbation at a deterministic flip point. Even if the input changes by exactly the amount the attacker computed, the neuron's firing is still random, throwing off the attacker's plan.

Math after the picture

The penalty for sitting near the cliff

First in words:

Loss equals the original classification error plus lambda times the average penalty across all neurons and time steps, where the penalty for each neuron is the maximum of zero or delta minus the absolute distance from threshold.

Then the compact form:

TotalLoss = Loss(x) + lambda * (1/(N*T)) * sum over all neurons i of max(0, delta - |V_i - V_th|)

Every symbol:

TotalLossThe combined error to minimize during training
Loss(x)How wrong the network's prediction is
lambdaHow strongly to enforce the cliff-avoiding rule
V_iThe membrane potential (tank level) of neuron i
V_thThe firing threshold (the cliff)
deltaThe safe distance; neurons must stay at least this far from the cliff
NThe number of neurons in the layer
TThe number of time steps in one forward pass
A tiny worked example: two neurons, one time step
Neuron A: tank level V_A = 7, threshold V_th = 10, delta = 3.
Distance from cliff: |7 - 10| = 3. Penalty: max(0, 3 - 3) = 0 (safe).
Neuron B: tank level V_B = 9, threshold V_th = 10, delta = 3.
Distance from cliff: |9 - 10| = 1. Penalty: max(0, 3 - 1) = 2 (unsafe, incurs penalty).
If lambda = 0.5 and we have 2 neurons and 1 time step, additional loss = 0.5 * (1/2) * (0 + 2) = 0.5.
Gradient descent sees this 0.5 penalty and adjusts weights to move neuron B farther from the cliff.

The penalty only activates for neurons closer than delta to the threshold; safe neurons incur zero penalty, so training does not waste effort on them.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Lambda too large ruins learning. If the penalty weight (lambda) is too aggressive, the network spends all its energy staying away from cliffs and forgets how to classify. Training stalls or diverges.
Delta (safe distance) mismatch. If delta is too small, neurons creep back to cliffs. If too large, the network lacks degrees of freedom and clean accuracy drops.
Noisy spiking breaks speed. Adding randomness to spike firing can slow down the prediction phase (when you run the finished model on a new input) and waste the energy efficiency that makes spiking networks attractive in the first place.
Done right: adaptive lambda. The paper uses a dynamically adjusted lambda during training, starting small and ramping up as the network stabilizes. This balances defense against attack and accuracy on clean examples.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train two spiking networks on a simple task (e.g., MNIST digit classification). Train one normally (baseline). Train the other with Threshold Guarding Optimization by adding the membrane potential constraint to the loss function, which forces neurons far from their cliffs, and by enabling noisy spiking, which randomizes firing near the threshold. Why this works: cliff-sitters are fragile; pushing them far away makes them rigid and hard to flip.

Now expose both to adversarial attacks to measure who breaks first. Use two attack methods: FGSM (a fast perturbation method) and PGD (a slower iterative method). The baseline network's cliff-sitters will flip easily, because small input changes push neurons over the threshold and change their spiking pattern. The threshold-guarded network's neurons sit far from their cliffs, so those same small perturbations do not reach the threshold and the spiking pattern stays the same, defeating the attack.

Robustness gain = (adversarial success rate before) - (adversarial success rate after)

The demo must show that threshold-guarded neurons remain spiking in the correct pattern even as adversarial noise grows, while the baseline network's spiking pattern collapses, leading to misclassification.
