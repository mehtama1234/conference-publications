Continuous Space-Time Video Super-Resolution with 3D Fourier Fields

Represent video as waves spread over space and time, so you can upsample at any scale without the errors that come from trying to guess missing frames.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Video files are blocky because cameras capture pictures at fixed moments and with limited detail. To make videos smoother and sharper, older methods try to freeze one frame at a time, then guess where the action moved to in the next frame. This guessing breaks easily. This paper instead treats the whole video as one continuous wave pattern across space and time, letting the system answer what the picture looks like at any moment and zoom level without any guessing.

Zero assumption version

Why old methods break

Today's cameras record video by snapping pictures at fixed times and at fixed pixel grids. To make a video sharper or smoother, you need to invent pixels the camera never took. Old methods do this by guessing: they look at one frame, guess where things moved, and paste that knowledge onto the next frame. This is called frame warping, and it is fragile — if the guess about motion is even a little wrong, the next frame looks wrong.

A beginner might try: take each frame, make it sharper separately, then run them back together. But this fails because sharpness added frame-by-frame is choppy — the sharpness does not match between frames, and the picture flickers.

Better attempts separate the job: one network makes each frame sharper, another fills in new frames in between. But this approach fails because the networks fight each other — sharpening one frame wants to keep edges crisp, while frame-filling wants to blur across time to bridge the gap smoothly. Opposite goals trained at once cause the solution to be mediocre at both. They also cannot predict what a brand new moment in time should look like without first inventing intermediate frames, because neither network ever learns how things actually move or change in the real video.

The core problem: You cannot make a video both sharp and smooth if you process time and space as separate problems, or if you guess where things moved.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

The paper switches from separate spatial and temporal networks to a single spatio-temporal Fourier Field — a representation that treats space and time as one unified 3D volume. Instead of guessing where things move, it predicts the wave coefficients that describe the whole video at once.

the object it moves  =  the video representation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: local wave phase

measured quantity  =  phase shift and scale per location

The Fourier Field encodes video as a sum of up-and-down oscillating waves at different speeds. At each point in the 3D space-time grid, the method learns a phase shift (how far ahead or behind each wave oscillates at that location) and a scale (how tall each wave is). These two numbers determine what color or brightness the video has at that spot.

The neural encoder estimates these phase shifts and scales by looking at the low-quality input across a large spatio-temporal window. This is the hidden work — the encoder is learning where motion happens and how to represent fine detail as wave behavior, all implicitly encoded in those phase shifts.

Why it works

Why it works: unified 3D waves

Old methods fail because they separate space and time, or because they guess motion. This method works because it treats space and time as one 3D volume and expresses it as a sum of waves. Here is the mechanical chain:

Chain 1: No broken motion guessing. Instead of estimating optical flow (guessing where pixels moved), the method directly learns phase shifts — implicit motion information baked into the wave structure. A sine wave that peaks at different times at neighboring spatial locations naturally encodes motion, without ever computing a direction-and-speed number explicitly.

Chain 2: Sampling at any resolution. A sine wave is defined for all real numbers, not just integers. To upsample by two times or four times, you just ask the wave formula for values at coordinates between the originals, like 1.5 or 2.3. No frame interpolation network needed.

Chain 3: Sharp plus smooth together. Fourier waves naturally capture both fine spatial detail (high-frequency sine waves) and smooth motion over time (phase shifts that vary slowly). They are not fighting each other; they are part of one model.

Chain 4: Anti-aliasing built in. When sampling at a new resolution, the method multiplies by a Gaussian blur curve (point spread function) before summing. This cancels high-frequency noise that would make the output sparkly or aliased, directly fixing an artifact that plagues other upsampling methods.

Math after the picture

The equation: a sum of waves

First in words:

Video is estimated as the sum of sine and cosine waves, each with its own frequency, phase shift at each location, and amplitude scale. At each point in space-time, multiply the wave by a Gaussian to kill high-frequency noise, then sample.

Then the compact form:

V_out(x, y, t) = sum_k a_k(x, y) * sin(2*pi*f_k*x + phi_k(x,y)) * cos(2*pi*g_k*y + psi_k(x,y)) * cos(2*pi*h_k*t + theta_k(x,y)) * Gaussian(x,y,t)

Every symbol:

V_out(x, y, t)the output color or brightness at position (x,y) and time t
a_k(x, y)the amplitude scale of wave k at location (x, y), predicted by encoder
f_k, g_k, h_kthe frequency (how fast the wave oscillates) in x, y, t directions for basis k
phi_k(x,y), psi_k(x,y), theta_k(x,y)the phase shift (where in the cycle the wave starts) at this location
Gaussian(x,y,t)a smooth blur weight that kills high-frequency noise, ensures anti-aliasing
A tiny worked example
Suppose we want the brightness at position (2.5, 3.2, 0.4) in a low-res video upsampled 2x.
Wave k=1 oscillates with frequency 1 in x: phase 0.5. At x=2.5, the sine part = sin(2*pi*1*2.5 + 0.5) = sin(5*pi + 0.5) ≈ sin(0.5) ≈ 0.48.
Wave k=1 oscillates with frequency 1 in y: phase at (2.5, 3.2) is 0.3. The cosine part = cos(2*pi*1*3.2 + 0.3) = cos(6.4*pi + 0.3) ≈ cos(0.3) ≈ 0.96.
Wave k=1 oscillates with frequency 0.5 in time: phase at this location is 0.1. The time cosine = cos(2*pi*0.5*0.4 + 0.1) = cos(0.4*pi + 0.1) ≈ cos(1.35) ≈ 0.21.
Amplitude a_1(2.5, 3.2) = 0.6, Gaussian weight ≈ 0.8. Contribution = 0.6 * 0.48 * 0.96 * 0.21 * 0.8 ≈ 0.055.
Sum all k waves, get final brightness ≈ 120 (on 0-255 scale). Because the Gaussian is baked in, no sparkle or aliasing artifacts appear.

The phase shifts and scales are learned by the encoder from the low-resolution input, letting the system automatically find which wave frequencies describe the video best.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Ringing near sharp edges. Fourier methods sometimes make halos around very sharp edges (a phenomenon called Gibbs ringing). The Gaussian anti-aliasing filter reduces this, but cannot eliminate it completely on ultra-sharp boundaries like text.
Motion outside the training window. The encoder looks at a fixed window of past and future frames to predict phase shifts. If an object moves faster than expected or farther than the encoder's receptive field, the model has not seen that motion and may fail to extrapolate smoothly, because the wave pattern was learned only from the limited input window, so predictions beyond that range are unreliable.
Occlusions and disocclusions. When something moves and reveals a new part of the scene (a disocclusion), no single wave pattern can describe it across time. The method must learn to 'break' the wave smoothness there, which requires explicit supervision.
Done right. When trained on videos with diverse motion, lighting, and scene changes, the model learns to adjust phase shifts adaptively and produces video that is both sharp and smooth across all scales.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A runnable demo should take a low-resolution video clip (say, 240 pixels wide at 30 frames per second), feed it through the trained model, and produce a high-resolution version (1920 pixels wide, or 120 frames per second). The output should show two things at once: (1) frame-by-frame close-ups showing that edges are sharp and textures are crisp, not blurry, and (2) a fast-motion scene (a ball rolling, a person walking) that looks smooth when played back, without jitter or ghosting.

sharpness loss = sum of differences between upsampled frames and ground truth; smoothness = temporal consistency score (how similar neighboring frames look when aligned); speedup = time to upsample 100 frames at 4x spatial scale.

The demo must make it impossible to miss: a single unified Fourier model produces video that is sharp, smooth, and computed fast, without ever guessing motion or interpolating frames.
