Pulp Motion: Synchronized Actor and Camera Generation

When a camera and an actor move together, they must stay coordinated on screen, so generate them both at once using on-screen position as the glue.

Movie scenes have two movers: the actor and the camera. If you generate them separately, the camera might zoom on empty space while the actor walks off-frame. This paper treats them as one problem: given text, create an actor's motion and a camera trajectory that stay in sync on screen, like a cinematographer and actor rehearsing together.

Zero assumption version

Two movers, one screen

A movie scene has an actor moving and a camera moving. If you generate their paths separately (actor motion from text, camera from text), they often clash: the camera is pointed at where the actor WAS, or the actor walks out of frame. The beginner's fix is to generate one, then adjust the other by hand. But that is slow and manual.

The real problem: the camera and actor do not live in the same coordinate system. The camera is in the world. The actor is also in the world. But what matters on screen is how the actor LOOKS in the camera's viewpoint, which is called the on-screen framing or the projected position. That framing depends on BOTH the actor's position AND the camera's aim. If you ignore this link, they diverge.

The method in one sentence. Use the on-screen position of the actor (where they appear in the camera) as the shared target, and generate the actor and camera together toward it.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Learn a shared compact code

Train an autoencoder that can encode both actor motion and camera paths into a single compact representation (a short list of numbers that captures the essence of each motion).

Connect to on-screen framing

Build a simple linear function that predicts the on-screen framing from the compact codes.

Sample from text

When given text, generate a candidate compact code, compute its implied on-screen framing, and check coherence.

Steer the sampling

If the framing looks off, nudge the sampling toward better framing because the method's guidance function lowers the score for incoherent framings, making the diffusion model less likely to sample them.

Decode actor and camera

Convert the refined compact code back into actor motion and camera trajectory that stay synchronized.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of generating actor motion and camera trajectory independently, the paper generates them jointly, using the on-screen framing as their shared target. The key innovation is treating the on-screen position (the actor's projection into the camera view) as an auxiliary bridge between the two modalities, so they pull each other toward coherence.

the object it moves  =  actor and camera compact codes
The hidden quantity

On-screen framing: where the actor sits in the camera view

measured quantity  =  On-screen framing (projected position)

On-screen framing is the answer to: if I project the actor's joints onto the camera's viewpoint, where do they land on the image plane? This is a concrete 2D position on the screen (or 3D position in the camera space). It is not the actor's position in the world, and not the camera's position in the world, but their INTERSECTION: how the camera sees the actor.

The paper computes on-screen framing from both the actor motion and the camera trajectory. It is then used as the target: during generation, the method steers both the actor and the camera to keep this framing coherent and on-center, so the actor stays visible and cinematographically pleasing.

Why it works

Why decoupling fails and why joint generation fixes it

When you generate actor and camera separately, each generator is blind to the other. The actor generator sees the text and predicts motion in world coordinates. The camera generator sees the text and predicts a trajectory. Neither knows where the other will go. Collisions and off-frame moments are likely.

The paper fixes this by adding an intermediate target: the on-screen framing. Before training, the method learns how framing depends on both the actor and camera latent codes (this is the lightweight linear function). Once this link is learned, generation works differently: at each step, the generator proposes latent codes, the method computes the implied on-screen framing, and if the framing is bad (off-center, clipped, or clashing with the text), the generator steers away from that region of the latent space. Both the actor and camera shift together toward a framing that is on-screen and matches the text. The result is coordinated motion.

Math after the picture

Combining actor and camera in latent space

First in words:

On-screen framing equals a linear map applied to the joint latent codes of the actor and camera.

Then the compact form:

framing = W * [latent_actor, latent_camera] + b

Every symbol:

framingthe on-screen position of the actor in camera view (a 2D or 3D coordinate)
latent_actorthe compact encoding of the actor's motion
latent_camerathe compact encoding of the camera trajectory
Wa learned weight matrix (how much each latent dimension affects framing)
ba learned bias (the baseline framing when all latents are zero)
A tiny worked example
Suppose latent_actor = [0.5, 0.2] (actor moving slightly left and forward) and latent_camera = [0.1, 0.3] (camera tilting up and zooming).
Suppose W = [[2, 1], [1, 3]] and b = [0, 0].
Then framing = [[2, 1], [1, 3]] * [0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.3] + [0, 0] = [2*0.5 + 1*0.2 + 1*0.1 + 3*0.3, 1*0.5 + 3*0.2 + 0.1 + 3*0.3] = [1.0 + 0.2 + 0.1 + 0.9, 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.1 + 0.9] = [2.2, 2.1].
If the on-screen framing target is [2, 2], then the current sample is close (off by 0.2 in each dimension). The sampling can stay or nudge slightly.

The linear map is learned during training and acts as the bridge: it says how much the actor and camera motions contribute to on-screen position.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Linear map is too rigid. If the real relationship between latent codes and on-screen framing is nonlinear, the linear function cannot capture it. The method might steer toward a framing that cannot be reached. A fix is to use a small neural network instead of a linear map, trading simplicity for accuracy.
Latent space is tangled. If the actor and camera latents are not well-separated in the autoencoder, nudging the camera latent might accidentally shift the actor motion. A fix is to enforce orthogonality between the two latent subspaces during training.
Text and framing goals conflict. A text prompt might say the actor hides behind a pillar, but the framing goal is to keep the actor centered. The method must balance these goals. If framing is weighted too heavily, the text is ignored. If text is weighted too heavily, framing breaks.
Done right. The method learns framing as a secondary auxiliary task, so text remains primary. The framing constraint only nudges when safe, allowing text-coherent motions that also stay visible on screen.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a scene generator that takes text like 'an actor walks stage left while the camera follows' and generates both the actor skeleton motion and the camera path. The output should be a side-by-side video: the 3D world view (actor and camera positions) and the screen view (what the camera sees). Measure the on-screen position of the actor (the projected framing) and confirm it stays in a good region (e.g., within the center 80% of the image) across the entire clip.

coherence = (frames_where_actor_is_visible) / (total_frames) * 100, where visible means the on-screen position is inside the camera frustum and not clipped

The demo must show that joint generation achieves >90% visibility (most frames show the actor on-screen), while separate generation dips below 70%, confirming that framing coherence is the essential bridge.
