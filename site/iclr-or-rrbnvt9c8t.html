Pulp Motion: Framing-aware multimodal camera and human motion generation

When generating human motion and camera moves, use the on-screen frame position as a bridge so the actor and camera naturally coordinate.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Movie makers know a secret: the camera and actor do not move independently. The camera follows the actor, and the actor performs for the camera. But most AI systems generate human motion and camera trajectories separately, missing this tight link. This paper solves that by treating on-screen framing (where the actor's body lands in the camera's view) as the bridge that keeps both modalities honest.

Zero assumption version

Two separate outputs go out of sync

Today, if you ask an AI to generate a human walking across a room and a camera following that walk, the system treats them as independent problems. The human motion model says, 'walk forward.' The camera model says, 'pan left.' But nobody checks if the actor is still in the frame.

A naive approach would be to generate the human first, then condition the camera on it. This breaks because the human model never learned to perform for a camera; it just makes a motion that fits the text. Then the camera model struggles to frame something not built for framing. Or you could train a single generator on both at once—but paired human-camera training data is rare and incomplete. The model sees only a small slice of how actors and cameras actually coordinate, so it learns a weak and brittle pattern. It never learns the real rule: actor and camera are dance partners, one responding to the other.

In one sentence. Generate human motion and camera moves together, using where the actor appears on screen (on-screen framing) to keep them coordinated.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode both modalities

A joint encoder takes raw human pose and raw camera trajectory, producing two internal number-lists.

Compute on-screen framing

Apply a simple linear map to the two latent codes to produce the third: where the human lands on screen.

Train the full pipeline

Teach the autoencoder to reconstruct the original human and camera from the latents, using the framing latent as an internal signal.

Steer generation with framing

During generation, nudge the diffusion model along the framing direction to prefer outputs where the actor stays centered.

Refine off-frame limbs

If an arm or leg leaves the frame, run a local inpainting pass to fix just that region.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of generating human and camera independently, this paper changes the sampling process to steer both toward outputs that keep the on-screen frame coherent. The key move: introduce a third internal number-list representing on-screen framing, and use the direction of steepest improvement for that number-list to guide the generation.

the object it moves  =  sampling direction
The hidden quantity

Framing distance: how far the actor drifts out of frame

measured quantity  =  on-screen framing distance

Framing distance measures how far out of the camera's view the human body drifts. It is the gap between where the human's joints project onto the screen and where they should be to stay centered and in-frame.

The paper estimates it by training a joint autoencoder to map the human and camera latents to this framing signal via a lightweight linear transformation. The paper never directly encodes video frames; instead, it learns framing purely as a computed bridge, supervised only by how well the full motion reconstructs.

Plain reading. Framing distance measures how far the actor's joints stick out beyond the screen edge. If the distance is zero, the actor sits perfectly in frame. If it is large, the actor's limbs have drifted out of view. Smaller is better because we want the actor on screen, not out of sight.
Paper reading. The paper learns a linear map W from concatenated human and camera latents to a framing latent z, whose reconstruction error implicitly supervises whether the actor stays on screen.
Why it works

Why linking through framing works

The human motion model and camera model are both trained as diffusion models (where the model learns to gradually remove noise from random motion). During generation, they run in parallel, each following the text prompt. But without any shared signal, they drift apart: the human walks left while the camera aims right.

This paper inserts a shared signal—the on-screen framing latent—that both models can feel. When the joint autoencoder trains, it learns a simple linear mapping from the human and camera latents to where the body lands on screen. Then, during generation, the diffusion model receives a nudge along the framing direction: shift your latent code toward configurations where the actor stays centered. Both the human motion and camera trajectory feel this nudge, because they both feed into the framing calculation. The result: they coordinate naturally, because they are steered toward a shared goal.

Math after the picture

The framing bridge equation

First in words:

reconstruction loss = (human reconstruction error) + (camera reconstruction error) + (framing reconstruction error), where framing is a linear map from human and camera latent codes

Then the compact form:

L = ||D_x(z_x) - x_raw||^2 + ||D_y(z_y) - y_raw||^2 + ||D_z(W[z_x; z_y]) - z_raw||^2

Every symbol:

x_raworiginal human pose sequence
y_raworiginal camera trajectory sequence
z_xlearned human motion latent code
z_ylearned camera trajectory latent code
z_rawon-screen framing ground truth
D_x, D_y, D_zdecoders that reconstruct each modality from its latent
Wlightweight linear transformation matrix
[z_x; z_y]concatenate the two latent codes
A tiny worked example with three joints
Suppose the human latent z_x is [1.2, 0.8] and camera latent z_y is [0.5, 1.1].
Concatenate: [1.2, 0.8, 0.5, 1.1].
Apply linear map W (a 4x2 learned grid of numbers) to get framing latent z_computed = [0.9, 1.3].
True framing from video is z_true = [0.95, 1.25].
Framing loss = (0.9 - 0.95)^2 + (1.3 - 1.25)^2 = 0.0025 + 0.0025 = 0.005.
This loss tells the encoder: learn z_x and z_y such that their linear combination predicts framing accurately.
During generation, nudge the sample away from high framing loss, naturally steering actor and camera together.

The linear map W is tiny compared to the full models, so it trains fast and generalizes across different architectures.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Latent space mismatch. If the human and camera latent codes do not overlap (different encoders, different scales), the linear map cannot bridge them. Fix: train them jointly from the start.
Framing ambiguity in the data. If the training videos have highly varied framing (sometimes tight, sometimes wide), the linear map learns an average and underspecifies correct framing. Fix: standardize framing conventions in the dataset or add explicit camera zoom as a controllable setting.
Unusual prompts far from training examples. If the text describes an action very different from the training set (e.g., complex acrobatics), the diffusion model may generate poses that are anatomically wrong or impossible to frame nicely. The framing bridge cannot fix impossible poses.
Done right. Train on paired data with consistent framing conventions, use the linear map during generation to guide both modalities, and fall back to inpainting for rare edge cases where limbs leave the frame.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a text-to-motion generator that outputs both human pose and camera trajectory. Give it a prompt like 'actor walks from left side of room to right side, confident stride.' Measure two things: (1) Does the actor stay in frame for the entire motion? Count the percentage of frames where all joints project within the screen boundary. (2) Do the camera and human motion agree with the text? Use a pretrained text-encoder to score how well the motion matches the prompt and how well the camera trajectory matches it. Run this on three setups: human motion only, camera only, and both together with framing guidance. The framing guidance should show higher framing quality (actor in frame) without sacrificing text alignment.

framing_success = (frames where all joints in frame) / (total frames); text_alignment = cosine_sim(text_encoding, motion_encoding)

The demo must show that linking through on-screen position lets the system obey both the text instruction and the cinematography rule: keep the actor in frame.
