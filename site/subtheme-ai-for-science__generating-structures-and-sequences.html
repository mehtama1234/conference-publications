Generating structures and sequences

Using AI to create new molecules, proteins, and complex structures from scratch by learning patterns from existing data, rather than simulating every atom.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A protein is a long chain of amino acids folded into a specific shape that determines what it does. A small molecule is atoms bonded together in a precise pattern. Both of these are sequences and structures that nature built to solve a problem: bind to a target, break down a poison, copy DNA, fight an infection. Drug discovery is about finding new proteins or molecules that solve a medical problem — but the space of possibilities is unimaginably huge. A hundred atoms can be bonded together in a trillion different ways.

Traditional drug discovery is to synthesize candidates in the lab one at a time and test each one. That is expensive and slow. What if we could generate promising structures on a computer first, test them in simulation, and only synthesize the winners? The challenge is that generation has to respect chemistry: atoms bond only to certain other atoms with certain angles and distances, and the resulting structure has to be stable — not blow apart in a solvent or fall apart in the body. Naive AI just generates gibberish atoms that violate chemistry entirely.

This cluster uses generative models — networks that learn the patterns in databases of known proteins, known drugs, known molecules — and generate new ones that are similar but different. Some generate them one step at a time, checking chemical validity at each step. Some learn a continuous space where similar structures are close together, so morphing between known proteins smoothly generates plausible new ones. Others combine generation with scoring: generate many candidates and rank them by computed toxicity or activity before synthesis. The papers here differ in how they handle the constraints: do they build chemistry rules in, or teach the model to learn them?

How the work breaks down

Work in this subtheme splits into three main directions: generating and simulating the dynamics of proteins and other biological macromolecules that fold and move in time; designing small drug-like molecules that can bind to targets or avoid toxicity; and modeling systems where structure matters but generation must respect physical laws like conservation of mass or energy.

Protein dynamics and structure generation

A protein is not a static shape—it moves, vibrates, and samples different configurations depending on temperature and surroundings. If you want to design a new protein that folds correctly or works in the real world, you need to generate both structure and the way it moves over time. These papers tackle the challenge of learning from trajectories and coordinates of known proteins to generate new ones that have realistic motion and don't collapse or misfold.

The Align Your Structures paper uses structure pretraining to condition generation of molecular dynamics trajectories, fixing errors in atom positions and energy that naive models make on datasets like QM9. La-Proteina builds on flow matching to generate full all-atom protein structures up to 800 residues while ensuring they are co-designable (can have their sequences redesigned without losing fold), solving the hard problem of staying valid at scale. Learning Escorted Protocols learns efficient switching pathways for multistate free-energy estimation, letting us compute how hard it is to transform one structure into another without simulating every step. BioDynaSpec improves 250-frame trajectory generation using harmonic guidance, cutting error by 60% compared to earlier diffusion approaches. MarS-FM generates molecular dynamics by learning the structure of Markov state models, reproducing structural properties like radius of gyration and secondary structure better than earlier generative methods. Multi-state Protein Design uses DynamicMPNN to handle proteins that need to fold into multiple different shapes depending on context, beating single-state methods by up to 25%.

These methods agree on a core idea: learn the low-dimensional space where proteins live (their dynamics and structures), then use generation to explore that space smoothly. They differ in whether they generate one frame at a time or whole trajectories together, and whether they score the atomic positions or trust the learned model directly.

Molecular docking and ligand binding design

Drug design starts with a target protein shaped like a pocket or cleft that binds a bad actor—a virus protein, a tumor signal, a pathogenic enzyme. You want to find small molecules that fit that pocket tightly and don't bind to off-target proteins that would cause side effects. Classical methods use physics simulations to score each molecule, but they are slow and can miss the good candidates. Generative models can propose candidates first, and deep learning can score them faster than classical physics because neural networks evaluate millions of configurations in seconds rather than simulating quantum mechanics for each one.

SigmaDock uses SE(3) diffusion on fragment building blocks to pose-dock ligands into protein pockets, achieving 79.9% success on PoseBusters where molecules are both correctly positioned and pass chemical validity checks—and it beats classical physics-based methods. Refine Drugs, Don't Complete Them builds molecules fragment-by-fragment using discrete flow matching, letting chemists guide generation toward existing scaffolds rather than starting from scratch, which improves docking scores because the model learns chemical validity through the scaffold, not by trial and error. Pallatom-Ligand generates full ligand-bound protein complexes with all atoms using diffusion, controlling both the global protein fold and where the ligand sits at atomic resolution. Steering Large Language Models guides LLM-based drug design through Thompson sampling on real docking scores, beating all earlier methods in average docking score with smooth improvement. Towards Sub-Second Molecular Docking reduces a diffusion model by removing redundant weights and shrinking weight sizes to dock an entire 256-atom complex in 0.17 seconds on a single GPU, making generation fast enough for real-time screening so thousands of molecules can be ranked per second.

All of these papers move docking away from classical brute-force simulation toward learned scoring and smarter generation order—either fragments first or joint structure-ligand co-generation. The goal is speed and chemical validity without sacrificing binding specificity.

Synthesizable molecules and retrosynthesis

Making a drug in the lab means executing a multi-step chemical synthesis—buying starting materials and running reactions in a specific order. A molecule might be theoretically perfect but impossible to synthesize in five steps with common reagents. Retrosynthesis is the reverse problem: given a target molecule, work backward to find a plausible sequence of reactions and starting materials. Generative models must both dream up new structures and ensure they are actually reachable in the lab.

Exploring Synthesizable Chemical Space iteratively refines pathways to molecules that can actually be made, achieving the highest reconstruction rate and synthesis success on benchmark tasks. FragFM generates molecules by treating them as hierarchies of fragments rather than individual atoms, which gives better property control than atom-by-atom methods because building from fragments preserves chemist intuition, and includes a new benchmark for natural product–like molecules. Order Matters in Retrosynthesis uses reaction-center guidance to predict which bonds break in a target molecule, then backtracks through plausible reactions, reaching 61.2% top-1 accuracy on USPTO-50k and matching large language models with far less training data. When Single Answer Is Not Enough rethinks retrosynthesis benchmarks to match how chemists actually work—often there are many valid synthesis routes that all reach the same goal, so scoring by exact match is too strict and unfairly penalizes models finding alternate valid paths—and trains models that outperform LLM baselines as a result.

These papers shift the goal from generating any structure to generating structures that a chemist can make, often by explicitly learning the language of reactions and synthetic accessibility rather than just optimizing for molecular properties.

Constrained physics and field generation

When you generate the future state of weather, fluid flow, or a quantum system, the output must obey conservation laws—mass, energy, and momentum cannot be created from nothing. Naive generative models ignore these constraints and produce physically impossible states. The challenge is to bake conservation into the learning process itself, either by building in hard constraints or teaching the model to learn them from data.

P3D is a highly scalable neural surrogate for PDEs (partial differential equations), generating probabilistic samples of turbulent flows that capture the statistics of real turbulence. Strictly Constrained Generative Modeling uses split augmented Langevin sampling to ensure diffusion models generate fields that satisfy energy and mass conservation exactly, substantially improving forecast accuracy because violations in conservation laws amplify errors over time. GenCP trains on decoupled data but infers coupled physics—two fluids flowing together—and provides error bounds, letting you use cheaper training data because the model learns to couple the solutions when they interact. A paper on physics versus statistical patterns trades off between matching physics laws and matching what you see in real data, finding trade-offs where you win on some PDE benchmarks and lose on others (a physics-statistics tension, not a clear winner). Physics-Informed Distillation of Diffusion Models distills a constrained diffusion model into fewer steps while maintaining conservation, outperforming recent baselines in both accuracy and constraint satisfaction.

These papers converge on the idea that generation must either encode physics as hard constraints in the network architecture or learn physics-aware scoring functions. The trade-off is between staying faithful to equations versus staying close to real data.

Sequence and signal generation in biology

Cells talk through genetic signals: they express genes in response to stress or drugs, they perturb their neighbors, they change state over time. Predicting what happens when you knock out a gene or add a drug is essential for understanding disease and testing therapies. Brain signals like fMRI and EEG carry information about perception and cognition. The challenge is generating realistic sequences and signals that respect the constraints of biology—gene expression cannot go negative, and sequences that don't occur in nature are biologically implausible.

Functional MRI Time Series Generation uses wavelet-based transforms and spectral flow matching to generate realistic fMRI timeseries that capture the temporal dynamics of brain activity and improve downstream brain network classification because fMRI signals vary in frequency content and time alignment. scDFM uses flow matching on the full shape of cell responses (rather than point predictions) for single-cell perturbation prediction, improving performance by 19.6% on combinatorial drug interactions where one gene interaction affects multiple pathways because modeling the full response shape captures how genes buffer or amplify each other. Beyond Independent Genes learns module-inductive representations that capture how genes work together, outperforming methods that treat genes independently by 6.7% on unseen and combinatorial perturbations. Chamaileon generates sequences of molecular binders that adapt to diverse protein conformations and multi-target requirements, benchmarked on a new cross-context dataset. RIDER uses reinforcement learning guidance with diffusion to generate 3D RNA structures that are structurally different from native sequences while maintaining fold validity, improving structural similarity by over 100%.

These papers treat biology sequences and signals as something to sample from in multiple ways, not optimize naively. What makes a sequence plausible depends on what you see in training data, not just on abstract chemical rules, which is why learned models outperform hand-coded scoring functions because they can capture correlations and constraints that humans don't codify explicitly.
