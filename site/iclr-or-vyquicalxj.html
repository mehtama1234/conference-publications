Cross-Modal Redundancy and Vision-Language Geometry

A sparse autoencoder that enforces energy matching across image and text embeddings reveals which features truly align, which are unique to one modality, and how to edit embeddings honestly.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Vision-language models (like CLIP) align pictures and text brilliantly, but the inside of that shared space is a mystery. This paper opens the box: it adds one simple constraint (energy should match across modalities) to a sparse autoencoder (a tool that breaks down number-lists into human-readable building blocks called atoms), then reads off what lives where. The result: clear atoms carrying shared meaning, separate atoms for image-only and text-only quirks, and a recipe for editing number-lists without breaking alignment.

Zero assumption version

Vision-language models hide what is truly shared

CLIP and similar models take an image and a caption, map each to a list of numbers (an embedding), and learn to make the lists close when they describe the same thing and far when they do not. It works, but we cannot see inside. What atoms of meaning live in that shared number-space? Which atoms are truly shared (image and text agree)? Which are quirky to one side (image artifacts, text grammar)? If you try to edit a number-list (e.g., shift values to change meaning), you risk breaking the alignment.

A naive approach: unpack the embedding with a standard sparse autoencoder (a tool that breaks down a list of numbers into a small set of human-readable atomic features). But this often fails — the autoencoder extracts quirky atoms that look nothing alike across modalities. You cannot tell which atoms are truly shared versus which are just modality-specific noise. The picture stays hidden.

In one sentence. Enforce that truly shared atoms have the same average magnitude (energy) in both modalities when training the sparse autoencoder.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take an embedding

Start with the raw list of 768 (or 1024) numbers from CLIP.

Decompose into atoms

A sparse autoencoder rebuilds the list using a small set of learned features (atoms).

Measure energy per atom

For each atom, sum up its magnitude in the image path and the text path separately.

Penalize mismatch

During training, nudge atoms to have equal energy across modalities if they are truly shared.

Sort atoms into three groups

High-energy atoms in both modalities are shared concepts; atoms active in only one are modality quirks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper adds a single line to the sparse autoencoder training recipe: a soft energy-matching penalty. Instead of just reconstructing the embedding accurately, the autoencoder now also learns to make atoms that fire in both image and text paths have the same average magnitude. That one constraint cascades: it reorganizes which atoms are learned, without harming accuracy.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: bimodal versus unimodal atoms

measured quantity  =  Alignment energy (per-atom cross-modal consistency)

An atom is a learned feature (a list of numbers, like a mini-embedding) used to reconstruct the full embedding. For each atom, the paper measures its average magnitude when it fires in image embeddings versus text embeddings. If these two averages are roughly equal, the atom is bimodal (truly shared). If one is much larger or only one fires, the atom is unimodal (modality-specific).

The Iso-Energy Assumption says: if a concept is truly shared, it should have equal energy (average magnitude) in both modalities. Without this constraint, an autoencoder will happily learn atoms with wildly mismatched energies. The constraint forces the autoencoder to either make an atom truly shared (high energy on both sides) or relegate it to modality-specific duty (high on one side only). This splits the solution into three clear groups: bimodal atoms (the bridge), image-only atoms (vision quirks), and text-only atoms (language quirks). The paper measures this by tracking which atoms the constraints separate.

Why it works

Why energy matching reveals true alignment

Start with the problem: a standard sparse autoencoder breaks down each number-list into atoms, but atoms learned from mixed image and text data often split strangely. One atom might fire hard in images but only whisper in text. It looks like that atom is not truly shared, even if it carries real meaning. Because the autoencoder has no incentive to make atoms match across modalities, it learns whatever is convenient.

The paper adds a soft penalty: during training, compute the average magnitude of each atom in image embeddings and in text embeddings, then nudge those two averages to be equal. This does not force them to be identical, but it pulls them toward each other. Now the autoencoder faces a choice: either make an atom truly shared (fire at similar strength on both sides) or specialize it (fire hard on one side, soft on the other). Under this pressure, atoms naturally sort into three groups: the shared group (high energy on both sides), the image-only group (high in images, near-silent in text), and the text-only group (high in text, quiet in images). The constraint does not break reconstruction because atoms can still combine to rebuild the number-list accurately; it just reorganizes which atoms play which role. The result is a much clearer picture: you can read which atoms are truly shared and which are tied to one modality.

Math after the picture

The training loss equation

First in words:

Total loss is the standard reconstruction loss plus a weighted penalty that aligns atom energies across modalities

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_reconstruction + beta * (-trace(Z_image * Z_text_transpose) / batch_size)

Every symbol:

L_reconstructionStandard autoencoder reconstruction: rebuilding the embedding from atoms
Z_imageThe atom activations (codes) for image embeddings (matrix of size batch × num_atoms)
Z_textThe atom activations for text embeddings (same shape)
betaThe weight of the alignment penalty (how strongly to enforce energy matching)
trace(Z_image * Z_text_transpose)Sum of products of atom magnitudes; high when atoms are similarly active in both modalities
batch_sizeNumber of image-text pairs in one training step
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 4 atoms and 2 image-text pairs.
Atom A: image magnitude 8.0, text magnitude 8.0 -> product 64.0 (highly aligned, contributes strongly to trace)
Atom B: image magnitude 6.0, text magnitude 0.1 -> product 0.6 (misaligned, hardly contributes)
Atom C: image magnitude 0.2, text magnitude 6.0 -> product 1.2 (misaligned, opposite direction)
Atom D: image magnitude 0.5, text magnitude 0.5 -> product 0.25 (both quiet, weakly aligned)
Sum of products (trace) for this pair: 64 + 0.6 + 1.2 + 0.25 = 66.05.
The negative trace goes into the penalty: -trace = -66.05. When atoms align well, trace is large, so the penalty pulls that direction during training.
A misaligned pair (e.g., A: 8 vs 0.1, B: 0.1 vs 8, others quiet) would give trace ≈ 0.8 + 0.8 = 1.6, much lower penalty, so training adjusts atoms to increase alignment.

The penalty nudges atoms toward matching energy by summing up how often atoms fire in sync across modalities, without requiring them to be identical.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Beta tuning is tricky. If the energy-matching penalty (beta) is too small, atoms do not align and you see no geometric structure. If too large, the autoencoder is forced to sacrifice reconstruction to chase alignment, hurting downstream performance. The paper tunes beta carefully with cross-validation.
Works only on models with separate image and text paths. The method assumes image and text go through separate paths (like CLIP) so you can measure atoms separately in each. On models where image and text are processed together, the separate image and text codes do not exist, and energy matching may not reveal the same structure.
Removing unimodal atoms is risky. The paper shows you can filter out image-only and text-only atoms to close the modality gap. But if those atoms carry genuinely useful information (e.g., image-specific visual features needed for fine-grained tasks), filtering them hurts performance on vision-only downstream tasks.
Done right. Tune beta on validation data, confirm that the learned bimodal atoms are semantically sensible (manually inspect a few), and test downstream tasks before assuming alignment improvements transfer.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a standard sparse autoencoder on CLIP embeddings (image-text pairs from a small dataset like COCO or Conceptual Captions). Record which atoms are high-energy in images, high-energy in text, and high-energy in both. Then train an Aligned SAE (same architecture, but with energy matching) on the same data. Compare the atom organization: does the aligned version produce a cleaner separation into bimodal and unimodal groups? Does the modality gap shrink? Can you manually inspect a bimodal atom and confirm it represents a real shared concept (e.g., 'dog' fires in both the dog-photo embedding and the text embedding for dog-related captions)?

Bimodal fraction = (num atoms active in both image and text paths above threshold) / (total atoms); Modality gap = mean L2 distance between aligned image and text embeddings

The demo should show that energy matching causes atoms to reorg cleanly into shared and modality-specific buckets, and that this reorganization shrinks the gap between image and text embeddings without sacrificing accuracy.
