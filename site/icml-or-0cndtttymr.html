FOCA: Future-Oriented Conditioning for Data-Efficient VLA Adaptation

Show the robot's vision-language model what tasks should look like in the near future, so it learns faster from fewer demonstrations.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A robot's eye and brain (a vision-language-action model, or VLA, trained on vast robotic videos) can do many tasks, but only if you give it hundreds of examples. FOCA cuts that need drastically by forcing the model to predict what the task should look like at future time steps, and to align those future predictions with the actual end goal. The result: robots learn new tasks with as few as twenty examples instead of three hundred.

Zero assumption version

Why robots need so many examples

A robot's visual brain (a model trained on thousands of hours of video) can recognize what things are and what language means. But when you give it a new task, like stacking cups or opening a drawer, it has trouble learning from just a handful of examples. The model sees each new task as something completely different from its training, and without hundreds of repetitions, it makes mistakes.

A beginner might think: just show the model some examples of the task, and it will copy what it sees. This fails because the model treats early actions and late actions the same way. It does not know that reaching toward a cup is a step in a longer story about stacking, and a reach at the wrong moment in that story is a completely different action.

The bottleneck. The model does not understand the task's long-horizon structure from few examples because it only sees isolated states and actions, not the arc of the task unfolding over time.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Explicit future prediction

Given the robot's view and the instruction, predict what the world will look like 5-10 frames ahead.

Implicit goal alignment

Also nudge the model's internal representation toward the actual goal state the demonstrations reach.

Internal representation separation

Keep the prediction branch and goal branch separate inside the model so they do not interfere. Each branch learns in its own part of the model's internal memory (the learned numerical representations that encode meaning).

Synthetic co-training

Generate realistic fake robot videos with a world model and co-train on them too, filling data gaps.

One learned model

The fine-tuned vision-language model now reasons about task structure and learns new tasks with twenty examples.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes the signals that steer the robot's learning. Instead of only showing the model what actions to take, it also shows what the task should look like in the next few moments, and nudges the model's internal state toward the final goal. These are added as two new training objectives that run alongside the action-learning objective.

the object it moves  =  the learning signals
The hidden quantity

Task-structure encoding in internal representation space

measured quantity  =  How much of the task's narrative the model encoded

When the robot's brain learns a task from examples, it can either memorize isolated actions or it can build a map of how the task unfolds over time. FOCA measures this implicitly by tracking whether the model's internal representation (the latent space, an internal numerical sketch of meaning) moves toward the correct goal state when it processes examples. If the representation drifts toward the goal, the model has encoded the task's narrative. If it stays random, the task is still opaque.

The paper estimates task-structure encoding by checking whether the implicit alignment loss shrinks (meaning representations move closer to goal states) and whether the model needs fewer demos. When task encoding is stronger, the model's internal state moves much faster toward goal observations during training, so the alignment loss drops sharply. This sharp drop directly means the model has learned the task structure, so it needs far fewer new examples to adapt it to other tasks.

Why it works

Why future signals teach the task structure

Here is the concrete chain of cause and effect. When the robot's brain processes a demonstration, it naturally forms an internal mental state—a summary of the current situation in numbers. Usually, that summary is built only to predict the next action. But the next action alone does not reveal whether that action was a success or a failure, or what step of the task it was.

FOCA adds two new constraints during training. First, it forces the model to predict what the world will look like in a few steps ahead. This is hard to do unless the model understands what the task is trying to achieve. If the model only memorized arm positions, it cannot predict whether the cup will be stacked. Second, it pushes the model's current mental state toward the actual end-state of the demonstration. This teaches the model to recognize progress. Together, these two forces steer the model to build an internal representation that encodes the task's narrative: this state is the start, this action moves toward the goal, this is the finish. Once the model has internalized that structure, new examples slot into it immediately—the model only needs to learn the specifics (which cup, which drawer), not the entire task logic again.

Math after the picture

The training objective combining future signals

First in words:

The training minimizes two losses together: the diffusion loss (how far the predicted future actions are from the real actions), and the implicit alignment loss (how far the learned representation is from the goal state). The total loss balances them equally.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_fm + lambda times L_imp. L_fm = norm of (A_phi(s_t, a_tilde, rho, r_exp_t, r_imp_t) minus (a_t minus epsilon)). L_imp = norm of (r_imp_t minus g_t).

Every symbol:

L_fmdiffusion flow-matching loss (how wrong the predicted future action is)
L_impimplicit alignment loss (how far the latent representation is from the goal)
lambdaweight balancing the two objectives (typically 1.0)
s_tthe current state (what the robot sees)
a_tildethe predicted future action chunk (noised for the diffusion process)
rhothe diffusion time step (between 0 and 1)
r_exp_texplicit future-prediction tokens from the model
r_imp_timplicit goal-alignment tokens from the model
g_tthe goal state representation from the demonstration
a_tthe actual action the robot should take
epsilonrandom noise added during diffusion training
A tiny worked example
Example: At time step 1, the robot sees arm above cup.
Predicted action chunk: reach down, grab, lift. Real action: reach down, grab, lift.
Diffusion loss = (0.1 + 0.05 + 0.08) squared = 0.0289 (very small, good prediction).
Representation r_imp_1 = 0.2, 0.3, 0.1 (from the model's internal state).
Goal state g_1 = 0.5, 0.6, 0.8 (what the demo ends with).
Alignment loss = (0.2-0.5) squared plus (0.3-0.6) squared plus (0.1-0.8) squared = 0.09 + 0.09 + 0.49 = 0.67.
Total loss = 0.0289 + 1.0 times 0.67 = 0.7 (training nudges r_imp toward g).
After many steps: r_imp drifts toward g, model learns cup state equals progress.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Goal state never seen in short horizon. If the demonstrations do not reach the goal state (a cup is picked but not placed), the alignment loss has nothing to align toward. The model learns a phantom goal and fails on test tasks.
Explicit and implicit branches interfere. If the prediction branch and goal-alignment branch share the same internal learned numbers (numbers that the model uses to encode what it knows), they can cancel each other out or jam each other. The paper addresses this by masking them so each branch uses separate memory.
Synthetic video realism mismatch If the world model generates robot videos that look unrealistic (jerky, physics-wrong, bad lighting), co-training on them teaches the model to expect a certain pattern of appearance that does not match real robot videos. This causes real-world transfer to fail.
Structured by design. When the learned numbers are isolated, goal-alignment loss properly shaped, and world-model videos realistic, the model learns the task structure reliably and transfers to new robots.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small robotics task (e.g., a simulation where a robot arm must stack three cups in a small stack). Collect twenty demonstrations of a human or scripted agent completing the task. Split them into train and test. Train the base VLA model on your twenty demos using standard fine-tuning, and measure success rate on the test set. Then train using FOCA (add the explicit future-prediction loss and the implicit alignment loss). The demo passes when FOCA's test-set success rate is visibly higher (15-25 percentage points) and when you can show that the model's internal learned numbers move measurably closer to the goal state during training.

data_efficiency_gain = FOCA_success_with_20_demos minus baseline_success_with_20_demos

The invisible win is not the higher success rate; it is that the model's internal representations now encode the task narrative. Inspect the learned tokens and you should see that representations cluster by stage of the task (reaching, grasping, lifting, placing), not by raw pixel values.
