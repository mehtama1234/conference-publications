Beyond Short Steps in Frank-Wolfe Algorithms

Look ahead to guess the next gradient direction, then choose a step size that maximizes progress toward the true optimum using both lower and upper bounds on how far away you are.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

The Frank-Wolfe algorithm solves optimization problems (finding the best setting of weights or parameters) without needing to flip all your weights onto a constraint surface — it only needs to find the best direction to move. The traditional approach picks a fixed step size (how far to move) by ensuring the function shrinks monotonically. This paper shows you can do better: predict which direction the slope will point on the next step, because you can then aim your step more accurately instead of reacting to only where you are now, and choose your step size to maximize guaranteed progress measured by both how far you are from the answer and an upper bound on that distance, because tightening both bounds together gives you a reliable stopping signal you did not have before.

Zero assumption version

Frank-Wolfe hits a ceiling on progress

The Frank-Wolfe algorithm is used when projecting weights onto a constraint surface is expensive (imagine it takes hours to flip all weights exactly onto the allowed region). Instead, it only finds the best direction to step in (the most promising neighbor on the constraint boundary). The traditional version picks step sizes using a simple rule: make sure the function value shrinks after every step. This works, but leaves money on the table.

The problem: you are only watching how far below the optimum you sit (the primal gap: current value minus best possible). But there is also an upper bound on how far away you could be — a dual gap. The old rule makes primal progress but does not guarantee dual progress. This means you cannot easily tell when to stop: the algorithm might keep moving even though it has already converged to a good solution. You are flying blind without a reliable stopping signal.

In one sentence. Predict where the slope will point next and choose the step size that shrinks both the distance to the optimum and the upper bound on that distance.

Build an estimate of the best possible value you could achieve (from online learning theory).

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies two things: (1) it adds a prediction step (guessing the next gradient direction using online learning ideas), and (2) it replaces the step-size rule with one that optimizes the gap between a lower bound and an upper bound instead of just making the loss shrink.

The hidden quantity

The primal-dual gap: how much runway is left

measured quantity  =  primal-dual gap

The paper introduces the concept of a primal-dual gap — a single number that captures how far you are from done. The primal gap is the distance from where you are now to the true best point. The dual gap is an upper bound on that distance: a ceiling beyond which the optimum cannot sit. The gap between them is how much slack, how much room for error.

The algorithm computes a lower bound on the optimum (what we know the answer is at least as good as) and an upper bound (what we know the answer is at most as good as). At each step, it picks the step size that shrinks that gap the most. This gives a reliable stopping criterion: when the gap closes, you are done.

Why it works

Why optimism and dual gaps speed you up

The old Frank-Wolfe only looks at the gradient at the current point. It says: if I move in this direction, the function will shrink by roughly (this gradient magnitude squared), because the function is smooth. Then it picks a step size that guarantees that shrinkage happens, ensuring primal progress.

The new method does two things smarter. First, it predicts: using online learning theory, it estimates what the gradient will point toward on the next step. This is the optimism. It is like saying: I will not just react to where I am, I will aim for where I expect to land. Second, instead of only maximizing the shrinkage in the loss, it simultaneously tightens the primal-dual gap — the range between lower and upper bounds on the answer. This dual bound is an upper limit on how far from optimal you could possibly be. Squeezing both bounds together makes faster progress because each step shrinks both the actual distance and the uncertainty band, so you converge quicker, and it gives you a reliable stopping signal because when that gap closes to zero, you mathematically know the answer is optimal (you cannot improve further).

The step size is not constant. It is solved for at each step by maximizing the progress in the gap. This means the algorithm adapts: if the constraint set is very narrow in one direction and wide in another, the step size adapts to grab the best progress available. Critically, the paper proves this rule still maintains the same convergence rate as the old method (you are not trading away guarantees for speed), because the step size is always bounded conservatively enough that each step shrinks the gap. The result is faster practice because of the adaptation, while keeping the same convergence guarantees from the theory.

Math after the picture

The step size equation

First in words:

The step size a_t is chosen to maximize progress in the primal-dual gap. The gap at iteration t is bounded by a model derived from smoothness and curvature. The algorithm solves for the step size by optimizing this gap formula.

Then the compact form:

a_t = argmax_a { G_t^{primal-dual}(a) } where G_t = f(x_t) - f(x*) and dual bound = f(x_t) - (lower estimate of f(x*))

Every symbol:

a_tthe step size (how far to move) at iteration t
x_tcurrent weights or parameters
f(x_t)the loss or objective value at x_t
f(x*)the optimal value (what we are trying to reach)
primal gapf(x_t) - f(x*), how far below optimal we sit
dual gapthe upper bound minus the lower bound, uncertainty remaining
Lsmoothness constant (how fast the loss can change)
Ddiameter of the constraint set (max distance between any two points)
A tiny worked example
Current loss: f(x_t) = 10. Lower bound on optimum: 2. Upper bound on optimum: 8.
Primal gap = 10 - 2 = 8 (we are 8 units away from the best we know is possible).
Dual gap = 8 - 2 = 6 (the true optimum sits somewhere in that 6-unit range).
Gradient at x_t is 4 units long. Smoothness constant L = 2 (function curves tightly).
Old rule: step size a = 4 / (2 * 4) = 0.5 (ensures loss shrinks by at least a bit).
New rule: we try a = 0.5, 0.7, 0.9. For each, compute: gap_after = gap_before - L * a^2 + grad_pred_term.
Step 0.7 closes the gap the most (both primal and dual tighten). Choose a_t = 0.7.
New loss after step: f(x_{t+1}) ≈ 10 - 2.8 = 7.2. Dual bound tightens too. Repeat.

The key is that the step size adapts by solving a small optimization problem (maximizing gap shrinkage) instead of using a fixed formula.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Optimistic prediction fails on noisy data. If the gradient is very noisy or the function changes unpredictably, the lookahead prediction can miss badly. The algorithm may overshoot and waste steps. Mitigation: this paper assumes smooth functions; on highly noisy objectives, the gains shrink.
Step size depends on unknown constants. The algorithm solves for the best step size using the smoothness constant L and the constraint diameter D. If these are unknown or poorly estimated, the step size formula is wrong. Mitigation: line search (measured step size search) can replace the closed form, as the paper shows.
The dual bound can be loose. The upper bound on the optimum is derived from the proof, but it might be much higher than the true optimum. This means you converge in theory but the stopping criterion looks premature in practice. Mitigation: use the primal gap as a fallback stopping signal.
The paper bounds the step size to a simple closed form that is always sound. Even if the estimate is rough, the step size choice is guaranteed not to increase the gap (you never take a step that goes backward). The algorithm is conservative but correct.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple quadratic minimization problem: minimize the squared distance from a target, subject to each coordinate being between minus one and plus one. Choose the curvature matrix to be poorly-conditioned so that the loss surface is interesting (stretches differently in different directions). Run both the old Frank-Wolfe (fixed short steps) and the new optimistic Frank-Wolfe (primal-dual adaptive steps) for 30 iterations. Plot: (1) loss value over iterations, (2) the primal gap (current loss minus optimal), (3) the dual bound (upper bound on loss), and (4) the step size chosen at each iteration. The demo should show that the new algorithm reaches a given accuracy threshold in fewer iterations and that the dual bound tightens alongside the primal gap.

Iterations to convergence = min(t such that (dual_bound_t - lower_bound_t) < epsilon). Compare old vs new.

The tightest proof should be that the step size adapts smoothly based on the shape and size of the constraint set, and that the dual bound collapses as fast as the primal value, creating a real stopping signal that does not exist in the old method.
