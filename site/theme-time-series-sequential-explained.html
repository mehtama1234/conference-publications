Time Series & Sequential

How do we learn from past sequences to predict the future, spot anomalies, and understand what is really happening when the rules keep shifting?

What this whole area is about

Time moves in one direction, and the world leaves traces. Stock prices, weather, heartbeats, factory sensor readings—all are sequences of numbers recorded over time. The core problem is simple to state but hard to solve: given what we've seen so far, what comes next? This question appears everywhere. A hospital needs to predict patient vital signs to catch crises early, because early detection gives doctors time to intervene before the patient gets worse. A power grid needs to forecast electricity demand hours ahead to avoid blackouts, because generators take time to ramp up and you need to know demand in advance. A manufacturer watches vibrations in equipment to know when it will break, because you can schedule maintenance during downtime rather than facing an unexpected failure that stops production. All of these are versions of the same challenge: understanding patterns in sequences so that you can anticipate and prepare for what comes next.

Why is this hard? Real sequences are messy. They have trends that gradually shift direction. They have seasonal cycles—sales spike during holidays, temperatures warm in summer. They have sudden jumps and anomalies when something unexpected happens. They have gaps where measurements were missed. Most importantly, the patterns that worked yesterday sometimes stop working tomorrow. A model trained on last year's stock data often fails this year. A model that predicts weather for one city often fails for another. The underlying rules seem to change, or at least the data distribution shifts in ways that trick the model. And scale matters: you might have only a few weeks of data for a new factory, while another team has years of data. There is no one-size-fits-all solution.

Researchers approach this from multiple angles. Some build better forecasting models by learning which parts of the past matter most, because not all history is equally predictive. Others use generative models—the same technology that creates images from text—to synthesize realistic sequences or fill in missing data, because generating diverse plausible scenarios is sometimes more useful than predicting the single most likely future. Some focus on spotting when a sequence behaves abnormally, which matters just as much as predicting normal behavior because anomalies often signal danger or opportunity. Others try to connect sequences to language: a doctor's notes describe what the heart monitor sees, so text provides context and reasoning that raw numbers lack. Some build foundation models that learn general patterns once, then adapt to new sequences without starting from scratch, because training from scratch on new data wastes time and computational resources. Others strip down to minimal models for cases where data is scarce or hardware is weak, because sometimes you have to work with what you have. And some work on making these models explainable—showing which past values the model used, or why it flagged an anomaly—because decisions that affect people or systems need to be justified.

This matters because sequences are everywhere. Healthcare systems use time series to detect organ failure hours before it becomes obvious, so they can treat early when interventions are most effective. Power grids use forecasts to balance supply and demand, which prevents blackouts and keeps electricity flowing. Manufacturers use anomaly detection to schedule maintenance before a machine fails and stops production, which saves money by preventing downtime. Financial firms use predictions to manage risk, reducing losses and protecting capital. Climate scientists use patterns in historical data to understand how the world is changing, which informs policy and planning. Getting even slightly better predictions at scale saves millions of dollars and sometimes lives because small improvements compound across billions of decisions.

Subtheme

Predicting what comes next

This is the most direct form of sequence work: you have past values, you want to guess future values. For example, a hospital has a patient's blood pressure readings every minute for the last hour, and needs to predict the next hour. Or a warehouse has shipment volumes for every day of the last year, and needs to plan for next week. The naive approach—fit a straight line, or repeat the average—often fails because real sequences have structure that is more complex. Trends curve. Seasonal patterns repeat. Past shocks echo into the future. A good forecaster has to learn these patterns from data.

The challenge is that what matters changes. If you are predicting stock prices, the past hour might matter more than the past year, because markets react to fast-moving news. If you are predicting factory wear, the past week matters less than how the machine has been used over months. A single model that always uses the same look-back window often overshoots or undershoots. Additionally, real forecasts need to be not just point guesses but also ranges: the hospital needs to know not just 'blood pressure will be 120' but 'blood pressure will be between 115 and 125 with high confidence,' because low-confidence predictions are less useful for decisions.

Papers in this cluster explore neural networks that learn which parts of the past matter, including methods that assign higher weights to the time steps that most influence the future. Others decompose the sequence into trend, seasonality, and noise, and forecast each separately. Some use frequency analysis—converting to waves and modeling waves instead of raw values—because waves are often simpler to predict than raw noise. Hybrids combine neural and traditional statistical approaches. The goal is to extract the hidden structure that the raw numbers are masking, so that predictions work better across different scenarios and datasets.

Subtheme

Generating sequences with diffusion and flows

In recent years, a new family of generative models has emerged—diffusion and flow matching—that work well at creating realistic samples from complex distributions. These were first applied to images, where they produce photo-quality pictures. Now researchers are applying the same approach to sequences. The idea is to learn the underlying distribution of realistic sequences, then sample from it. This is useful in two ways: you can generate synthetic sequences for testing or training when real data is scarce, and you can fill in gaps in incomplete sequences by sampling likely continuations.

Why is this different from traditional forecasting? Traditional forecasting is deterministic or gives you a probability distribution over next values. Generative models, in contrast, learn the entire shape of realistic sequences. They can capture multimodal behavior—the possibility that a sequence might go up or down with roughly equal probability—in a natural way. They can also be guided or conditioned on partial information. For example, if you have a sequence with missing middle values, you can use these models to fill the gaps while respecting the overall shape and endpoints. And they tend to learn more diverse patterns because they are optimized to generate plausible sequences, not just the most likely one.

Papers in this area adapt diffusion and neural ODE (ordinary differential equation) models to sequences. Some condition on metadata—like 'this is a factory sensor, not a stock price'—so the model generates genre-appropriate sequences because different types of data have different statistical properties, and mixing them confuses the model. Others use these models as a bridge between datasets: train on one hospital's data, generate synthetic sequences from that distribution, then fine-tune on a new hospital's real data, which reduces the domain shift problem because you have learned a shared representation. Flow matching, a newer approach that is mathematically simpler than diffusion, is also being adapted to sequences because it is easier to scale and sometimes produces higher-quality samples.

Subtheme

Finding the unusual and the break

Every sequence has a baseline—the normal range of behavior. A healthy patient's blood pressure stays in a narrow band. A factory bearing vibrates at expected frequencies. Stock prices move within historical ranges. But sometimes something changes. A patient's blood pressure spikes. A bearing starts vibrating at a new frequency, signaling wear. A stock crashes on bad news. These events matter enormously for action: hospitals need to alert staff, factories need to schedule maintenance, traders need to reduce risk. The challenge is that 'unusual' is not always obvious. Is today's temperature 2 degrees warmer than average because the model is wrong, or because climate is shifting? Is a sensor reading an anomaly or just normal noise? Is a change temporary or permanent?

The problem has layers. Anomalies can be sudden spikes, slow drifts, or behavioral changes that are subtle. A sequence can have multiple normal modes—for example, traffic patterns differ on weekdays and weekends, and both are normal. Anomalies can be local, affecting just a small window, or global, shifting the entire distribution. Some anomalies are contextual: an absence of data in a hospital system is normal during maintenance but would be alarming in production. And rare events are, by definition, rare, so labeled training data is scarce. The model has to learn what normal looks like from mostly-normal data, then spot when something deviates.

Papers tackle this by learning baseline models of normal sequences, then flagging values that deviate far from the baseline. Some use reconstruction error: if an autoencoder encodes and decodes the sequence, large reconstruction error suggests anomaly. Others explicitly model change points—moments when the distribution shifts—and estimate where they occur. Some combine statistical tests with neural methods. A key insight is that anomalies are often domain-specific: in healthcare, you care about medical significance, not just statistical significance, so papers sometimes incorporate domain knowledge or causal reasoning to separate true anomalies from noise.

Subtheme

Pairing sequences with language

Numbers tell part of the story, but context is in text. A hospital chart has vital signs and also doctor's notes describing what is happening: 'patient admitted with fever, responding to antibiotics.' A factory has sensor logs and also maintenance records: 'bearing installed 200 hours ago, last lubricated Thursday.' Stock data has prices and also news headlines and earnings call transcripts. Language carries information that is hard to encode as numbers because context and causation are described in words, not as numerical features. Language models have become skilled at reasoning and synthesis, so a natural question is: can we use language to help time series models, since language encodes domain knowledge that numbers alone do not capture?

The connection is surprisingly deep. First, language can provide supervision. If a doctor annotates a time series—'this pattern signals sepsis'—that labeled data is precious for training. Second, language can augment learning. A model that sees both the time series and paired descriptions might learn more stable patterns because descriptions highlight what humans think matters. Third, language models themselves can reason about sequences. Given a price chart and news, a language model might generate a narrative: 'stock fell because earnings missed estimates.' This is reasoning, not just pattern matching. Fourth, language can be the output: instead of predicting the next price, predict a description like 'stock will be stable.' This bridges two modalities.

Papers in this cluster train multimodal models that jointly encode time series and text. Some use pretraining: learn general patterns from large unlabeled time series, then adapt to new sequences with small amounts of paired text and data. Others use language as a regularizer: if the model's internal state is interpretable and can be described in language, that forces the model to be more human-aligned. A few papers generate narratives from sequences—they produce coherent descriptions of what the sequence is doing. The underlying insight is that language forces structure and interpretability in a way that raw sequence modeling often lacks.

Subtheme

Learning patterns that transfer

A trained model on one dataset often fails on a new dataset. A forecasting model trained on 10 years of New York weather will give bad predictions for Boston. A model trained on one patient's heart rate patterns will not generalize to another patient because bodies are different. This is the transfer learning or generalization problem. Ideally, you could train once on a large, diverse dataset, then quickly adapt that knowledge to new sequences without retraining from scratch. This saves compute because you do not redo the expensive large-scale training, reduces data requirements because you reuse knowledge learned elsewhere, and enables models to be deployed faster because adaptation is quicker than full training.

Why is transfer so hard for sequences? Sequences carry many hidden factors. Weather patterns depend on latitude, time of year, and local geography. Heart rates depend on age, fitness, current stress, and medications. Factory sensor readings depend on equipment model, operating conditions, and maintenance history. A model trained on one distribution often overfits to spurious correlations in that distribution. For example, a weather model might learn that 'when the forecast says rain, the next day is cooler'—but that is because rain and cooler temperatures are correlated in the training location, not a causal rule. When transferred to a new location where the correlation differs, the model fails. Transfer learning requires learning the invariant patterns that actually generalize.

Papers build foundation models—large models trained on many diverse sequences from different domains and sources. The idea is that if a model sees sequences from factories, hospitals, finance, and climate science simultaneously, it learns general principles about how sequences work rather than domain-specific quirks. Then, for a new sequence, you fine-tune just a small part of the model or add a small adapter. Some papers also investigate what features transfer: do models that understand seasonal patterns transfer well, even if the specific seasons differ? Others use domain adversarial training, where you force the model to be unable to tell which domain a sequence came from, so it must learn domain-invariant representations.

Subtheme

Working with very few model weights

Neural networks have millions or billions of internal weights—numbers that determine how the network transforms input to output—and training them takes compute and data. But many practical sequence problems have tight constraints. An embedded device on a sensor has limited memory. A manufacturer needs predictions in milliseconds, not seconds. A startup has only weeks of data on a new product. In these cases, you cannot use a massive model. The question becomes: how good a forecaster can you build with minimal capacity? Sometimes this means going back to classics—linear regression, exponential smoothing, autoregressive models—which have few weights but strong mathematical foundations. Sometimes it means designing neural networks that are smaller or have special structure.

The trade-off is accuracy versus efficiency. A large model might forecast with 10 percent error, but a small model might have 15 percent error. For many applications, 15 percent error is good enough for decision-making, especially if the small model is 100 times faster or uses 100 times less memory because speed and resource efficiency matter when you are deploying to millions of devices or serving millions of users. The hidden challenge is that this trade-off is not smooth. A model with half the weights does not have exactly half the accuracy; sometimes it degrades sharply. So the research is about finding the sweet spot: the smallest model that still captures the key patterns in the sequence.

Papers in this area revisit statistical methods—like linear regression, exponential smoothing, and vector autoregression—and ask whether they can be competitive with neural methods in settings where data is limited and compute matters. Some propose hybrid approaches, combining a fast linear model for the trend with a small neural network for residuals. Others design efficient architectures: convolutional nets with fewer layers, or mechanisms that only look at a small window of past values instead of the entire history. Pruning is another technique—train a larger model, then remove connections or weights that do not contribute much, leaving a smaller model. The theme is pragmatism: accuracy is not the only metric that matters.

Subtheme

Understanding why the model predicts

A model that forecasts accurately is valuable, but a model that also explains itself is priceless. A hospital wants to know: which vital signs led the model to predict a crisis, because doctors need to understand the clinical reasoning. A factory wants to know: why did the model flag this sensor reading as anomalous, because maintenance engineers need to know if the flagging is real or a false alarm. A trader wants to know: did the model's price prediction come from technical patterns or fundamental data, because the trading strategy depends on the underlying logic. Understanding the model's reasoning builds trust because humans can verify that the model is using real patterns and allows verification that the model is learning sensible patterns, not spurious correlations created by the training data. It also enables debugging because if the model is wrong, seeing what it focused on helps identify where the prediction went astray.

Interpretability for sequences is tricky because sequences have temporal structure. A black-box neural network processes information through many layers, and by the end, the signal is transformed beyond recognition. Traditional linear models are transparent—each coefficient shows the contribution of one variable. But linear models are often too weak for complex sequences. The question is how to get both expressiveness and explainability. Some papers use attention mechanisms, which show which past time steps the model attends to. Others use saliency maps, which highlight which input values most influence the output. A few papers build interpretable architectures from the ground up, sacrificing some accuracy to maintain clarity.

Papers in this theme focus on making sequence models transparent. Some extract rules from neural models—for example, 'if the past three values are increasing and the growth rate accelerates, predict a peak.' Others use methods to identify which past values truly drive the future, rather than just correlating with it. Some combine neural and symbolic approaches: let the neural network be flexible, but require it to produce outputs that are human-interpretable or satisfy domain constraints. The underlying principle is that in high-stakes domains like healthcare or infrastructure, a slightly less accurate but fully explainable model is often preferred to a black-box model with marginal accuracy gains.

Subtheme

Handling messy real-world sequences

Textbook sequences are clean: one value per time step, recorded on a regular schedule, no missing data. Real-world sequences are messy. A patient's heart rate might be recorded every second for one patient and every five minutes for another. A factory sensor might fail for an hour and have a gap. Weather stations measure temperature and humidity and wind, but different stations measure different variables. Medical records mix continuous vitals with categorical diagnoses. Stock data has tick data at irregular times when trades occur, not at fixed intervals. When you have missing values, you need imputation. When sampling is irregular, you need alignment. When data types are mixed, you need encoding.

Each of these adds complexity. Imputation is guessing: you can use the mean, or linear interpolation, or a trained model. But the guess introduces error that propagates forward. Irregular sampling breaks models that assume fixed-rate time steps. Mixed data types mean the model has to learn different representations for numbers, categories, and text in the same sequence. Corruptions introduce noise and outliers. And the model has to learn what is real signal versus what is measurement error. A good sequence model has to handle these realities well, or predictions will be garbage in, garbage out.

Papers tackle these problems by building models that handle missing data natively—for example, methods that skip missing values instead of failing on them. Some do explicit imputation with neural networks trained jointly with the forecasting task. Others learn from irregular samples by encoding timestamps as input, so the model learns that a five-minute gap is different from a one-second gap. Multimodal approaches learn shared representations across different data types. A key insight is that messy data is not a bug to be fixed before modeling; it is part of the problem that the model has to learn to handle. Making models work well with real-world messiness often improves performance more than chasing marginal accuracy gains on clean benchmark data because real deployment always has messiness, so a model optimized for cleanliness will fail when it encounters the actual world.

Subtheme

Adapting models across domains

A model trained on one hospital's patient data often fails on another hospital's data, even if they treat the same diseases. A model trained on one factory's equipment fails on similar equipment at another factory. A model trained on historical weather patterns for 2020 fails in 2024 because the climate has shifted. This is domain adaptation or distribution shift. The core issue is that the input sequences in the new domain are different from training data in systematic ways. The hospital might use different sensors. The factory might have different ambient conditions. The weather patterns have changed due to climate. If you retrain from scratch, you can handle these shifts, but retraining is expensive and requires lots of labeled data in the new domain.

The challenge is knowing what is truly invariant versus what is domain-specific. In healthcare, the biological principles of disease are invariant, but sensor calibration and patient demographics are domain-specific. In manufacturing, the physics of wear is invariant, but equipment models and operating conditions are domain-specific. A smart transfer approach learns the invariant part—say, how disease progresses—and adapts the domain-specific part—sensor offsets or population statistics. The harder case is when the domain shift is subtle. If a hospital's patient population becomes older, or more obese, or sicker, the distribution of vital signs changes in ways that are hard to characterize and adapt to without labeled data.

Papers in this cluster tackle alignment and adaptation. Some use domain adversarial training, which forces the model to learn representations that are useful for prediction but invariant to domain identity. Others use meta-learning: learn to learn in a way that transfers well. Some use unsupervised adaptation, where the model observes unlabeled sequences in the new domain and tunes itself to fit without ground truth. A few use causal models, assuming that the causal structure of a sequence is invariant even if the model's internal weights shift, and then adapt those weights. The underlying insight is that data in the wild is nonstationary—it drifts over time and across contexts—so models that can adapt are more valuable than models that are frozen.
