Routing Channel-Patch Dependencies in Time Series Forecasting with Graph Spectral Decomposition

A plugin that teaches forecasting models to route each time-patch through different frequency channels—smooth-trend experts, wiggle-experts, and spike-experts—based on what each patch actually needs.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Forecasting models face a hard choice: treat each measured variable (like temperature, humidity, pressure) separately and miss their relationships, or mix all variables together and drown in noise. This paper proposes xCPD, a lightweight add-on module that breaks that choice. It splits each patch of the time series into three frequency bands—slow smooth trends, medium wiggles, and fast spikes—then asks: for this specific patch, which variables actually relate to each other, and at which frequency? A dynamic router sends each patch to the right combination of experts, meaning the model learns smooth patterns one way, noise-like patterns another, and sudden jumps a third way.

Zero assumption version

The forecasting dilemma: independent channels or mixed channels?

Imagine you are tracking weather over time—you record temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind. You need to predict the next hour. The naive thing every model tries first is to treat each variable like a separate story: predict temperature from past temperatures, humidity from past humidity, and so on. This works okay for stable slow changes, but it misses the fact that temperature and humidity almost always move together in real air.

So the second approach is to mix them all: feed all variables at once into a single model that learns their relationships. This catches the temperature-humidity link, but it also forces the model to learn noise. On a breezy day, wind and humidity might have a random high-frequency jitter that has nothing to do with each other—it is just sensor flutter. When you jam all channels together, the model has no way to ignore that noise; it learns it anyway, and that wastes capacity.

Neither pure separation nor pure mixing works well. Different relationships show up at different frequencies: seasonal temperature-humidity correlation is slow and stable (low frequency), hourly gusts and sensor noise are fast (high frequency). A single strategy treats everything the same.

The broken assumption. Current forecasting models use one rule for how to handle channels: either ignore them entirely, or always mix them together. Neither works for all patterns.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Project into frequency space

Convert the time series into frequency bands using a shared mathematical lens (graph Fourier basis) so all channels can be compared on equal terms.

Sort patches by their energy

For each short patch of time, measure how much power it has in low-frequency, medium-frequency, and high-frequency bands. Band assignment is automatic and data-driven.

Score each frequency expert

For a given patch, ask: how confident is each expert about this pattern? Compute a confidence score for the low-freq, mid-freq, and high-freq specialists.

Route the patch

Send the patch to the experts with the highest scores. Use a weighted blend: 70% low-freq expert, 20% mid-freq expert, 10% high-freq expert, for example.

Train all experts together

Update all experts simultaneously, with auxiliary losses preventing all experts from being idle or all patches going to one expert. Ensures every expert stays useful.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes the routing path: instead of all patches taking the same route through the model, each patch now gets a custom route based on which frequency band it lives in and which variables actually relate at that frequency. The routing mechanism dynamically selects which subset of channels to interact with for each patch.

the object it moves  =  routing paths per patch
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: frequency-aware channel relevance

measured quantity  =  Confidence score per frequency expert

The paper measures a hidden quantity that was never explicitly talked about in the time series forecasting world before: for each patch and each frequency band, how certain is the model that a particular set of channels are truly related (not just correlated by chance or noise)? This is the routing score that the mixture-of-experts module computes.

The paper estimates this using graph neural network layers on the frequency-domain representations (each channel's projection into frequency space). Each layer refines the confidence scores by combining information from related channels (neighbors in the frequency graph). A low confidence score means: ignore inter-channel interactions for this patch; treat channels independently. A high confidence score means: channel relationships matter for this particular frequency band, so mix them.

Paper reading. The routing score is computed by converting a neural network's scores into probabilities using a standardization function (softmax: turn raw scores into fractions that sum to 1) applied to spectral representations of each channel. It reflects the model's learned confidence that channels interact in a real way—not by chance—at a given frequency for a given time patch.
Why it works

Why it works: separating signal from noise in frequency space

The core insight is that temperature-humidity relationships and wind-noise correlations happen at completely different speeds. Seasonal trends are slow (low frequency); sensor jitter is fast (high frequency). If you mix everything together, the model has no way to tell a true slow relationship from a false fast one. The oversmoothing problem arises because slow and fast patterns interfere.

The paper's solution works in three stages. First, it transforms all variables into a shared frequency coordinate system using graph Fourier basis, a mathematical technique that decomposes the time series by speed rather than time. In this view, all variables can be compared fairly—you can see if temperature and humidity are really linked at the 12-hour timescale (they are) versus the 10-minute timescale (they aren't).

Second, for each patch, the method measures spectral energy—how much of the patch lives in the low, medium, and high-frequency bands. This is a data-driven assignment; no human needs to decide. A patch full of hourly oscillation ends up in the mid-frequency band; a patch with a slow drift ends up in low-frequency.

Third, a mixture-of-experts router dynamically decides which expert to trust. The key: experts are not generic; each one is trained to specialize in one frequency band. The low-frequency expert learns that temperature and humidity move together (seasonal correlation). The high-frequency expert learns to ignore that correlation and focus on independent noise patterns. For a slow patch, the router sends it mostly to the low-frequency expert. For a noisy patch, the router sends it mostly to the high-frequency expert. This way, each frequency band learns its own set of channel relationships, and the oversmoothing vanishes because slow and fast patterns are never forced to share a single set of learned channel weights.

Math after the picture

The routing equation

First in words:

routing_score = softmax(graph_neural_net(spectral_embedding)). Each expert fires with confidence proportional to its score. Final prediction is a weighted average of expert outputs.

Then the compact form:

pred = sum_f softmax(gnn(h_f)) * expert_f(x_f) [where f indexes frequency band, x_f is spectral-domain patch]

Every symbol:

h_fspectral embedding of channel-patch interaction at frequency f
gnn(...)graph neural network that learns to score expert confidence
softmax(...)converts scores into probabilities that sum to 1
expert_f(x_f)prediction from the expert trained on frequency band f
predfinal forecast
A tiny worked example
Patch 1 (slow drift): frequency projection [0.8, 0.15, 0.05] in [low, mid, high]
Neural network scores expert confidence: low=0.7, mid=0.2, high=0.1
Probability conversion: turn scores into fractions [0.58, 0.28, 0.14]
Low-freq expert says: temp will rise 1.2 units
Mid-freq expert says: temp will rise 0.3 units
High-freq expert says: temp will rise 0.1 units
Prediction: 0.58*1.2 + 0.28*0.3 + 0.14*0.1 = 0.696 + 0.084 + 0.014 = 0.794 units
Compare to naive average: (1.2 + 0.3 + 0.1)/3 = 0.533. Routing gives more weight to the confident expert.

The routing scores act as a filter: strong experts pull the prediction toward themselves; weak experts are nearly ignored. This allows frequency-specific patterns to be learned separately without interference, because when the low-frequency expert runs in isolation (high routing score) it learns only slow correlations, and when the high-frequency expert runs in isolation it learns only fast patterns, instead of both experts trying to match all patterns and canceling each other out.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

All experts collapse to one frequency band. If the routing mechanism is untrained or the balance loss is too weak, all patches might end up routing to the low-frequency expert and ignoring the others. The paper fixes this with explicit balance loss: penalize any situation where expert usage is too skewed.
Spectral basis misaligned with the data. The graph Fourier basis is computed from the data structure, but if the underlying channel relationships are time-varying or non-stationary (true in real weather), a single shared basis cannot capture everything. The paper addresses this by computing the basis on moving windows, but this adds complexity.
Frequency bands overlap or are poorly separated. If the spectral energy of a patch is distributed evenly across all frequencies, the model cannot decisively assign it. The routing becomes ambiguous, and the prediction reverts to a muddy average of all experts.
Done right. Train all experts jointly with additional penalties: one that spreads router confidence across experts (so no expert gets ignored) and one that prevents all patches from going to the same expert. Use a model-agnostic plugin design so the routing can be added to any forecasting backbone without retraining. Validate that each expert specializes in its own frequency band by inspecting learned channel dependencies per expert.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple dataset with two variables: X is a slow sine wave (period 24 timesteps) plus random noise; Y is the same slow sine wave. At low frequency, they are highly correlated. Now add independent fast jitter to Y (10-minute noise). At high frequency, they are uncorrelated and the jitter is pure noise.

Train three models: (1) channel-independent (X and Y separate), (2) channel-dependent (X and Y mixed), (3) xCPD with two experts (low-frequency and high-frequency). The demo should show: model 1 fails to predict the slow trend accurately because it ignores the correlation. Model 2 predicts the slow trend okay but wastes capacity on the high-frequency jitter in Y. Model 3 predicts both the slow trend and the high-frequency dynamics without interference—the low-frequency expert learns the true correlation; the high-frequency expert learns to ignore it.

accuracy_slow = ||y_pred_slow - y_true_slow||_2, accuracy_fast = ||y_pred_fast - y_true_fast||_2, combined = accuracy_slow + accuracy_fast

The key moment is when the low-frequency expert's learned weights show X and Y strongly coupled, but the high-frequency expert's weights show them independent—the paper's routing mechanism has split the learning and prevented interference.
