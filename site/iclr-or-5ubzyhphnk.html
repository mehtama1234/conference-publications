An evolutionary perspective on modes of learning in Transformers

Transformers choose between two learning strategies—permanent weight changes or temporary activation tweaks—based on whether the world stays predictable or shifts unpredictably.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Transformers learn in two different ways: by slowly changing their internal numbers (like slowly rewiring a brain), or by quickly adjusting how they think from moment to moment (like adjusting your tone for each person you meet). This paper shows that stable worlds favor the slow rewiring, while unpredictable worlds favor quick adjustments, because committing energy to permanent changes makes sense only if the world stays predictable, whereas reading the current situation pays off when the world keeps shifting. The paper uses ideas from biology—genes change slowly over generations in stable climates (because constant rewiring is wasteful), but bodies adapt quickly within a lifetime in unpredictable climates (because fixed wiring would be wrong half the time)—to explain when each learning path is better.

Zero assumption version

Two learning paths, unclear boundaries

Transformers have always done something puzzling: they learn in two opposite ways at once. The first way is slow—when you show it thousands of examples, it gradually changes its internal numbers (called weights) to get better. The second way is fast—when you give it a new situation in a prompt, it adjusts how it thinks right then, without changing any numbers, by reading the context clues in the sequence of tokens (words or tokens are the pieces a model breaks language into).

A beginner might assume the model should always prefer one strategy: either always drill the numbers into shape, or always stay flexible moment-to-moment. But Transformers do both, and nobody had a clear rule for when to switch. In biology, this is solved: genes change slowly when the world is stable (no need to waste energy rewiring if your grandparents' genes work), but animals stay flexible when the world is chaotic (because fixed wiring would be wrong half the time).

In one sentence. The paper shows that stable worlds make Transformers prefer permanent weight changes, while chaotic worlds make them prefer quick moment-to-moment adjustments.

What the paper changes

Which learning strategy to use

The paper shows that Transformers shift their preference between two paths: changing internal weights over many examples (in-weight learning, the permanent approach) versus reading the current context to adjust thinking on the fly (in-context learning, the temporary approach). This is not a bug—it is a smart choice the model makes.

the object it moves  =  learning strategy choice
The hidden quantity

Environment stability and cue reliability

measured quantity  =  Stability index and cue signal strength

The paper introduces two hidden quantities: how stable the world is (does the same pattern keep working, or does it shift?), and how reliable the context clues are (when you read the current sequence, do those clues actually point to the right answer, or are they misleading noise?). Stability means the world does not change—like a river that always floods in spring. Unreliability means the clues you read right now are noisy or contradictory.

The paper measures these by running controlled experiments: a model learns sinusoids (smooth curves that repeat the same pattern) or Omniglot characters (shapes from a standard test set, each one different from the last). In sinusoid tasks, they vary how much the pattern shifts between attempts. In Omniglot, they vary whether the character identity clues in the prompt are honestly predictive or scrambled. Then they watch: does the model shift from one learning strategy to the other as these knobs turn?

Why it works

Why one strategy beats the other

When the world is stable, one pattern works forever. Slowly grinding your internal numbers to match that pattern pays off because you only have to learn it once, and then you answer every new question using your wired-in knowledge. This is like a bird evolving a beak shape for a specific seed: once the beak is built, catching seeds is automatic. The time cost of learning (a few thousand examples to rewire) is worth it.

When the world flips unpredictably, your wired-in knowledge becomes a liability—it fits the old pattern and betrays you on the new one. Instead, staying flexible and reading the current context clues (the token stream right now) is better, because the clues shift with the world, so you answer correctly without waiting for rewiring time. You do not waste time rewiring; you just squint at the clues and adapt your answer. This is like a chameleon keeping one slow body but adjusting its skin color instantly for each new branch.

The switch happens at a sharp boundary because learning has two costs: the time to acquire the strategy (learning cost) and the penalty when the strategy fails on new cases (mismatch cost). In stable worlds, acquisition cost is small because the pattern never changes; in chaotic worlds, the model is cheaper to stay flexible. When cues are noisy, reading them does not help, because scrambled clues point you the wrong way as often as the right way, so in-context learning fails even in chaotic worlds and the model must rewire instead. The model flips strategies when one cost becomes lower than the other.

Math after the picture

The tradeoff equation

First in words:

Total cost equals learning cost plus mismatch cost

Then the compact form:

total_cost = acq_cost(strategy) + mismatch_cost(strategy, stability, cue_reliability)

Every symbol:

acq_cost(strategy)how many examples to master this strategy (in-weight is slower, in-context is fast)
mismatch_cost(strategy, stability, cue_reliability)penalty when strategy fails on new tasks (in-weight fails if world changes, in-context fails if clues are noisy)
stabilityhow much the task distribution stays the same (1.0 = never changes, 0.0 = complete chaos)
cue_reliabilityfraction of clues that point to the right answer (1.0 = all honest, 0.0 = pure noise)
A tiny worked example
Scenario A (stable world, reliable clues): acq_cost(in-weight) = 1000 examples. mismatch_cost = 0 (world never changes). Total = 1000. acq_cost(in-context) = 100 examples. mismatch_cost = 0 (clues are honest). Total = 100. Winner: in-context learning.
Scenario B (stable world, unreliable clues): acq_cost(in-weight) = 1000. mismatch_cost = 0. Total = 1000. acq_cost(in-context) = 100. mismatch_cost = 500 (noisy clues hurt you often). Total = 600. Winner: in-weight learning rewires despite high cost.
Scenario C (chaotic world, reliable clues): acq_cost(in-weight) = 2000 (must keep updating as world changes). mismatch_cost = 200 (still fails sometimes). Total = 2200. acq_cost(in-context) = 100. mismatch_cost = 50 (clues shift with the world). Total = 150. Clear winner: in-context learning is 15x cheaper.

The model picks in-weight learning when the world is stable enough that rewiring pays for itself (because the permanent knowledge will be right for a long time), and in-context learning when the world is chaotic or clues are trustworthy enough that reading saves time (because the clues are faster and more up-to-date than rewiring).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Noisy clues trap in-context learning. If the context clues are scrambled or misleading, reading them does not help, because scrambled clues point to random answers and waste the model's reading effort. The model confidently gives the wrong answer. In-context learning fails if cue reliability drops below a threshold, but the model may not notice this in time and will keep reading instead of rewiring.
Rare but sudden world shifts break in-weight learning. A model tuned to one pattern will give terrible answers if the world flips once. If a shift is rare but permanent, the model burns many examples relearning instead of switching to in-context mode fast enough.
Intermediate regimes are ambiguous. When stability and cue reliability are both moderate (neither clearly good nor clearly bad), the model can hesitate, switching between strategies and learning poorly from both.
Done right. The model watches both strategies in parallel during early learning, measures their costs, and commits to the winner. This requires the model architecture to support both in-weight and in-context paths naturally, as Transformers do.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a mini Transformer trained on sinusoid regression (a model learns to fit a smooth curve) where you control two knobs: how much the curve's shape changes between batches (stability), and how honest the initial prompt examples are (cue reliability). Train the model and watch which learning path it commits to. Measure in-weight learning by checking if the model's internal numbers drift over time. Measure in-context learning by checking if the model answers better when you give it more context examples at the start. You should see a clear switch: when you turn up stability, in-weight learning takes over; when you add noise to the clues, in-context learning stalls and the model falls back to rewiring.
