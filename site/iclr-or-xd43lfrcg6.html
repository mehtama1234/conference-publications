Detecting AI Videos by Keeping the Hidden Artifacts

AI-generated videos hide clues in tiny pixel details that get erased by resizing; keep the video at full resolution and you catch them.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Every AI video generator leaves fingerprints in the pixels—little glitches, slight timing mismatches, color oddities. Older detection methods shrink and crop videos to smaller sizes, which erases those fingerprints. This paper shows that by keeping videos at their full native resolution and letting a vision model process the whole thing, you catch far more fakes than you can when working from the damaged, downsized version.

The Old Way Throws Away the Evidence

Imagine you are looking at a video to decide if a computer made it or a camera filmed it. The video sits on your hard drive at full detail—thousands of pixels wide, many frames per second. But older detection systems do not look at all that. They first shrink the video down to a tiny size, like 224 by 224 pixels (about the size of a small stamp), and they also might chop off the edges. This shrinking and cropping was done to make detection fast and fit into computer memory.

The problem: shrinking a video crushes the tiny pixel-level details that actually distinguish a fake from real. AI generators have learned to mimic motion, but they leave subtle artifacts—edges that jitter slightly, pixels that do not align perfectly across frames, color shifts that a real camera would not make. All of these are high-frequency glitches, meaning they live in the fine details, not the big shapes. When you downsize the video by 10 times, you lose almost all of those clues. What a beginner might try: "Just use a bigger detection model." But a model trained on crushed videos learns to look for signs that exist only in crushed videos. It cannot detect what the crushing removed.

In one sentence. The old detectors squash videos to stamps and lose the tiny forgery marks in the process.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Input: full-resolution video

Take a video as-is, no shrinking yet. Each frame stays at its native width and height.

Patchify in 3D

Divide the video into tiny overlapping bricks: time slices by pixel blocks. Each brick is 2 frames tall, 14 pixels wide, 14 pixels tall.

Embed each patch

Convert each brick into a list of numbers that a neural network can process. All structure is preserved; no resolution loss.

Vision model reads all patches

A vision model that processes images as patches ingests all the patch number-lists and learns which combinations point to "AI" or "real."

Output: real or fake

Pool all patch embeddings into one score; pass to a binary classifier. One output: is this video genuine or synthetic.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The object this paper moves is the preprocessing step. Instead of downsampling and cropping—shrinking the video to a fixed stamp size—it keeps the video at native resolution. That single change ripples through everything downstream: the model must process more patches, the memory footprint grows, but all the pixel-level forgery artifacts stay intact for the model to learn from.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: high-frequency forgery artifacts

measured quantity  =  Fine-detail authenticity signal

High-frequency forgery artifacts are tiny pixel-level glitches that AI generators create. Unlike the big shapes and colors (which AI has learned to imitate well), these artifacts are the mistakes generators make when stitching patches together, misaligning frames, or blending motion. They are called "high-frequency" because they happen at fine scales (think: individual pixels or small neighborhoods of pixels), not at coarse scales (whole objects, big regions).

The paper measures how many of these artifacts survive the preprocessing step. When you downsample a video by 10x, you throw away roughly 99% of the pixels, so you lose almost all high-frequency signals. When you keep native resolution, every glitch stays visible. The hidden quantity is "how much of the real forgery mark is still present after preprocessing." The method estimates this implicitly: by training the vision model on native-resolution videos, the model learns to pay attention to pixels and patches that carry the most forgery information—and those tend to be the fine details that downsampling erases.

Why it works

Why it works: the artifact preservation chain

The mechanism is straightforward: the fewer steps you take to destroy information, the more information remains. High-frequency forgery artifacts are destroyed by downsampling because downsampling is literally a information-loss operation (you average pixels together and throw away the result). If you skip downsampling, those artifacts stay in the video.

A vision transformer trained on native-resolution videos faces a choice: it can learn patterns that work on shrunk videos (big coarse features only), or it can learn fine-grained patterns that only show up in high-resolution data. Since the training videos are all at native resolution, the model can learn both. It discovers that certain fine-grained pixel-level glitches (like edge jitter, color seams between patches, temporal discontinuities) are highly predictive of AI generation. A model trained only on shrunk videos never sees these glitches—they are already destroyed before training begins—so it cannot possibly learn to detect them.

Second, there is a generalization effect. Real videos filmed by cameras exhibit different patterns of artifacts than videos filmed at a squashed resolution. By training and evaluating on native resolution, you ensure the model learns patterns that actually exist in the wild, rather than patterns that only exist in the preprocessing-damaged version.

Math after the picture

The patchify equation

First in words:

Take the video tensor V (time, height, width, color channels). Extract non-overlapping 3D patches using unfold with patch size (2 frames, 14 pixels, 14 pixels). Embed each patch via a learned linear projection E. The result X is the embedding of all patches.

Then the compact form:

X^(0) = Unfold(V; P_t, P_h, P_w)^T · E

Every symbol:

Vvideo tensor: dimensions (time, height, width, 3 color channels)
P_t, P_h, P_wpatch dimensions: (2, 14, 14) frames by pixels
Unfoldsplit V into non-overlapping 3D patches
Elearned linear embedding matrix: maps patch pixels to embedding numbers
X^(0)the embedded patches ready for transformer processing
A tiny worked example
Suppose a video is 2 frames, 28 pixels wide, 28 pixels tall, 3 color channels.
Patch size is (2, 14, 14), so each patch has 2 * 14 * 14 * 3 = 1176 pixel values.
Unfold splits the 28x28 frames into (28/14) * (28/14) = 4 non-overlapping patches per frame.
With 2 frames and patch size Pt=2, we get 1 time-stack of 4 patches = 4 patches total.
Each patch is projected via E from 1176 dimensions to, say, 768 embedding dimensions.
Result X^(0) is shape (4, 768): 4 patches, each a 768-number embedding.
Compare to the old way: shrink to 224x224 first, then patchify. You lose 28^2 / 224^2 ≈ 99% of pixels before patchifying. Many fine-detail glitches are gone.

By patchifying at native resolution instead of after downsampling, all high-frequency pixel details remain available for the transformer to learn from.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Extreme downsampling breaks it. If the video is aggressively cropped or shrunk during evaluation (downscaling by 10x or more), the method's performance degrades. The paper shows it holds up to moderate downscaling (0.4x scale) but fails under extreme loss. This is expected: once the high-frequency artifacts are erased, no detector can see them.
Computational cost grows with resolution. Processing a 1080p video instead of 224x224 means handling roughly 25x more pixels. The transformer must process more patches, demanding more memory and compute. The paper uses windowed attention (only 114x114 patches per attention block) to keep cost linear in the number of patches, but it is still more expensive than the old method.
Older generators may have different artifact signatures. The paper trains on videos from 15 recent generators. If a fake comes from an older generator that left different forgery marks, the model might not catch it. The high-frequency artifacts of one generator are not the same as another's.
Done right. Use native resolution throughout training and evaluation. Train on a diverse set of recent generators. Ensure the video has not been downsampled before reaching the model. Accept that detection is a continuously evolving arms race, because as generators learn to hide old artifacts, they create new ones, and detectors must retrain on those fresh artifacts to stay ahead.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a side-by-side comparison detector: one branch processes a video at native resolution, the other shrinks it to 224x224 first, then both make a real-vs-fake prediction. Feed it a batch of recent AI-generated videos (from generators the model did not see during training). Measure accuracy on each branch. The high-resolution branch should earn a much higher accuracy score than the shrunk branch—think 85% vs 60%—because the high-resolution version still has the pixel-level glitches intact, while the shrunk version threw them away. Then take one specific fake video, visualize which patches the model paid the most attention to. Those patches should correspond to visible forgery artifacts—jittery edges, color seams, temporal discontinuities—visible in the original but erased after shrinking.
