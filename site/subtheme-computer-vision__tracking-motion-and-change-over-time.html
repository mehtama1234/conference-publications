Tracking motion and change over time

Following objects through video, predicting how scenes will change, and understanding temporal dynamics in visual data.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A static image freezes the world at one moment. But the real world is dynamic — objects move, scenes change, and understanding video requires reasoning about temporal patterns. Can you follow a person through a crowd across multiple video frames, even when they are occluded by other people? Can you predict how a scene will evolve in the next few frames? Can you understand that a sequence of images tells a story? Tracking and temporal understanding are needed for applications like surveillance (which person is which across frames?), sports analysis (tracking players and the ball), robotics (where did the target go?), and autonomous driving (where will the pedestrian be in two seconds?).

The core difficulty is that motion is ambiguous and objects disappear and reappear. In a single frame, you might identify a person. In the next frame, the camera panned, the lighting changed, and the person is now at a different location and appearance. How do you know it is the same person and not someone new? Simple pixel matching fails because appearance changes so much. You need to reason about motion consistency: objects move smoothly, not teleporting. You need to handle occlusion: an object is hidden behind something but might reappear. You need temporal context: the trajectory so far constrains where an object could go next. Longer-term, predicting the future is even harder: it requires understanding physics, intent, and social interaction.

Researchers tackle temporal understanding through several approaches. Some train models to link detections across frames: given boxes in frame t and frame t+1, decide which boxes are the same object. This matching can use appearance features, motion models that extrapolate trajectories, and graph algorithms that solve for consistent tracks. Others use recurrent neural networks or transformers to process video as a sequence, learning temporal patterns implicitly. Some focus on optical flow — estimating the motion of every pixel between consecutive frames — which provides explicit motion information. Others combine detection and tracking in an end-to-end model that jointly reasons about what objects are present and how they move. A few tackle prediction: train models to generate future frames based on past frames, implicitly learning physics and scene dynamics. The goal is to make machines understand video not as a series of independent snapshots, but as a coherent narrative of change over time.

How the work breaks down

Tracking objects through video splits into five directions: single-object tracking that stays locked on one target even through occlusion, multi-object tracking that identifies which person is which across frames in crowded scenes, motion capture that reconstructs human body pose from sparse data, long-term point tracking across entire videos with extreme changes, and video understanding tasks like action prediction and scene reasoning that require temporal context.

Single-object and long-sequence point tracking

Trackers need to stay locked on a target through challenging conditions: occlusion (when objects hide), appearance changes (lighting, viewpoint), and long time spans (hundreds of frames). The core challenge is distinguishing the target from visual clutter, recovering from disappearances, and maintaining identity over extreme transformations.

The satellite video tracking paper solves tracking fast-moving objects in aerial footage by focusing computation only on foreground regions where the target is likely to be, so the model spends resources only on relevant areas instead of scanning the entire image. GOT-Edit addresses the occlusion problem by editing the tracking model online as it processes frames, updating its internal representation of the target when the target reappears, which makes the tracker adapt to changes rather than rigidly comparing pixels. TAPTRv3 extends this to point-level tracking across hundreds of frames by using both spatial context (looking at the neighborhood around where the point was to find similar patches in the next frame) and temporal context (using the trajectory history to predict where the point should go next). RELO takes a different approach: it uses reinforcement learning to train a localization strategy, where the model learns a search pattern that efficiently finds the target, treating tracking as learning where to look next based on reward signals about whether the search was successful.

These papers converge on a shared insight: effective trackers must be both fast and accurate, and the way to achieve this is to focus computation on the regions that matter most, exploit temporal continuity and motion patterns, and adapt or learn smarter search strategies rather than exhaustively comparing everything.

Multi-object tracking and identity consistency

When multiple objects move through a scene, the tracker must solve two problems: detecting each object and then assigning detections across frames to identities so you know which person is which across time. This is harder than single-object tracking because occlusions are frequent, identities can be confused when people pass each other, and the tracker must reason about all objects simultaneously.

PointRePar tackles category-unified 3D tracking, meaning a single model can track different object types (pedestrians, cars, cyclists) at once by parsing the spatial-temporal point relationships between objects, learning that objects of different types interact differently with each other. UniTrack reformulates tracking as a graph problem: each detection is a node, and edges represent whether two detections belong to the same object across time, then a differentiable graph algorithm solves for the best set of edges that creates consistent identities. This approach naturally handles occlusions because the graph algorithm can infer identity even when a detection is missing in a frame.

Both papers push beyond simple appearance matching toward methods that exploit the structure of the problem because doing so improves tracking accuracy. Objects follow smooth trajectories because they have inertia: a person walking in one direction will continue in that direction in the next frame, not teleport randomly. This means predicting where an object will go next based on its past motion is more reliable than searching the entire image naively because most wrong guesses can be ruled out by physics. Interactions between objects are predictable, so reasoning about whether two detections could belong to the same object is easier if you model the spatial relationships: two people standing close together are more likely to be separate objects than overlapping ones. A global optimization across all objects is better than greedy frame-by-frame decisions because a locally optimal match in one frame might create conflicts later—for example, if you match the first person to the wrong detection in frame 1, you might corrupt the identity of a second person in frame 2—whereas solving for consistency across all frames and objects avoids these cascading errors.

Motion reconstruction from sparse data

Sometimes you don't have a complete video; you only have a few scattered frames or sparse keypoints (joint positions) of a person's body, and you need to reconstruct what their full body motion was and what it looked like in the frames you are missing. This is useful for animation, pose correction, and video synthesis.

MotionMAR reconstructs full-body human motion (all joints over time) from sparse observations by auto-regressively predicting motion frame by frame, using the structure of the human skeleton to constrain predictions to physically plausible poses. BigMaQ provides a dataset linking surface-level pose (how the body appears in an image) to 3D skeletal pose across multiple individuals, solving the problem that earlier datasets tracked only sparse joints, not the full textured body surface, so models trained on BigMaQ can work with richer data.

Video understanding with temporal reasoning

Beyond tracking individual objects, video understanding requires reasoning about what is happening: what action is about to occur, what the scene is, or whether a statement about the video is true. These tasks need temporal context because the action or story unfolds over multiple frames.

The Action-Guided paper predicts what action will happen next by learning which past actions led up to the current state, essentially memorizing typical action sequences in a kitchen or workspace. Open-o3-Video grounds video reasoning by explicitly identifying which frames contain the visual evidence for an answer, so when asked "does the person put the apple in the box?" it points to the frames where this happened, enabling the model to scale confidently at test time because it knows which predictions are well-supported.
