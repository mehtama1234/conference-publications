Variational inference & the ELBO

A lower bound that tells you how well a simplified guess about hidden causes matches reality.

In plain terms

Imagine you're trying to understand why something happened, but the real reason is hidden. A doctor sees symptoms but can't directly observe the disease state. A voice-recognition system hears audio but can't see what the person meant to say. A recommendation engine observes clicks but doesn't know the user's true preferences. In all these cases, something observable (the data you see) depends on something unobservable (the hidden cause), and you want to guess what that hidden cause might be.

Here's the problem: calculating the true probability of your data requires averaging over all possible hidden causes—a sum that's usually enormous and impossible to compute. So instead, you create a simpler guess about what the hidden cause might be. This guess is easier to work with, but it's not perfect. The ELBO is a number that measures how good your simplified guess is. It's always smaller than or equal to the true answer, which is why it's called a lower bound. When the ELBO is high, your guess is capturing reality well. When it's low, your guess is missing something important.

The genius move: because the ELBO is always guaranteed to give you a true lower bound, you can use it as a stand-in for the real (impossible to compute) answer. Make your guess better—closer to the truth about what the hidden cause actually is—and the gap between your guess and reality shrinks, which means the ELBO rises. Keep pushing the ELBO up, and you're automatically pushing the real answer up too, because the lower bound never lies. You've turned an impossible problem into a practical one.

The equation
\log p(x)\ge E_q[\log p(x,z)-\log q(z)]
log p(x)The logarithm of the probability that your data actually occurs under the true (but unknown) model.
E_qAn average computed under your simplified guess about what the hidden cause is.
log p(x,z)The logarithm of the probability of both the observed data and a particular value of the hidden cause happening together.
log q(z)The logarithm of the probability of a particular hidden cause under your simplified guess.
≥This shows that the right side is always smaller than or equal to the left side (never bigger).
Worked example
Coin bias guessing
Suppose a coin lands heads 7 times in 10 flips. True prob = 0.7 (unknown).
Your guess (q): the coin is fair, so q(heads) = 0.5.
log p(data) ≈ 3.17 (hard to compute exactly, but it's the truth).
ELBO = E_q[log p(data,Z) - log q(Z)] ≈ 2.81 (easier to compute, always ≤ 3.17).
Gap of 0.36 shows your guess (fair coin) misses some reality.
Why it shows up

The ELBO appears wherever a model must learn hidden structure from observable data. Variational autoencoders use it to learn compressed representations. Bayesian models use it to infer hidden causes. Reinforcement learning agents use it to reason about unobserved states. Generative models use it to approximate which hidden configuration could have produced the data you see. The ELBO is the bridge between an impossible exact calculation and a practical approximate one that actually works.

What ties all these uses together: you can't solve the real problem, so you solve a slightly different problem that's guaranteed to be correct. The ELBO formalizes that trade-off. It says: 'My guess might not be perfect, but this number tells you exactly how far away from perfect it is.' This is why it shows up in 17 papers across representation learning, interpretability, reinforcement learning, and generative models—it's the workhorse for any learning task where hidden structure matters.
