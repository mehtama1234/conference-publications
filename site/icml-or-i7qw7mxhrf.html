Conditional Quantile Adjusted Conformal Prediction for Time Series

A method that builds stable, nested prediction intervals for time series by smoothing out the choppiness in quantile estimation.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When forecasting the future, a honest forecast is a range, not a point. Conformal prediction (a math tool that wraps any model to make honest ranges) works well for data with fixed distributions, but time series are tricky: tomorrow's weather distribution depends on today's season. This paper stops prediction intervals from jumping around and crossing over each other by carefully modeling how the quantile curves should smooth out.

Zero assumption version

Prediction intervals jump around and overlap

When you forecast a stock price or temperature, you want a range, not a single guess — something like 'tomorrow will be 68 to 72 degrees'. Conformal prediction is a math tool that takes any forecasting model and wraps it to give you honest ranges: if you claim 95% of forecasts should fall in your range, then on average 95% actually do.

For time series — data that changes over time, like daily temperatures — the honest range depends on the season and recent volatility. Today's typical range is narrower than winter's range. The problem: when you build prediction intervals step by step, the choppiness in each step's range (caused by randomness in the data you've seen so far) piles up. You get jittery ranges that jump around, and worse, a narrower range at level 90% might sit above a wider range at level 95%, violating common sense. A beginner would just glue together separate conformal intervals at each time step, but that produces these crossing, volatile ranges that are hard to trust.

The root cause. Each conformal interval relies on estimating how extreme recent errors got — the tail of the distribution — which is inherently noisy because tails have few data points.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Gather past errors

For each time step, record how far off the model's guess was (the nonconformity score).

Estimate the tail quantile

Run a standard quantile learner (a model trained to predict how extreme the next error might be) across a grid of quantile levels (e.g., 50th, 80th, 95th percentile).

Fit a smooth curve

Feed those quantile estimates into a Cornish-Fisher curve (a math tool that builds smooth quantile functions from moment estimates) with constraints preventing it from crossing itself.

Build the prediction interval

Use the smooth quantile curve to set the width of the range at each significance level, ensuring nested and monotonic structure.

Deliver honest ranges

The resulting intervals cover the true value at the promised rate and do not jitter or cross — they're stable and trustworthy.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how you build the quantile curve (the function that says 'at this time, the error tail reaches this far'). Instead of taking raw quantile estimates and gluing them together, it fits a smooth parametric curve constrained to never cross itself — ensuring that narrower ranges stay inside wider ones.

the object it moves  =  Quantile curve
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: the nonconformity score's quantile curve

measured quantity  =  Conditional quantile curve

Every conformal method measures how badly its model can miss — the nonconformity score (the distance from the true value to the model's prediction). For a given moment in time, some days the error is small (50th percentile), some days it's larger (90th percentile). The question: how does the shape of this distribution evolve over time? The paper models this as a quantile curve — at each time step, it says 'the 90th percentile error will be this wide, the 95th percentile will be that wide'. That curve is the hidden quantity.

The paper estimates it by training a quantile learner (a standard regression model that predicts percentiles of the error distribution) and then smoothing its output using Cornish-Fisher approximation — a parametric curve built from the mean and spread (moments) of the nonconformity score, with monotonicity constraints to prevent crossing.

Why it works

Why it works: smooth quantiles prevent crossing

The root of the problem is noise. When you estimate the 95th percentile error from just a few past observations, one outlier can swing your estimate wildly. Tomorrow, with different data, the estimate shifts. Conformal intervals built from these choppy estimates jump around and cross — violating the law that a 90% confidence range must sit inside a 95% confidence range.

The fix: model the quantile curve itself. Instead of estimating each quantile independently, fit a smooth parametric curve (a Cornish-Fisher approximation) that says 'the quantiles must vary smoothly over time, following the overall shape of the error distribution.' This curve is parameterized by the conditional moments — the mean and spread of errors at each time step. By construction, a smooth curve is monotonic: if you ask for a higher quantile, you get a wider band. The Cornish-Fisher parameterization adds monotonicity constraints that prevent the curve from twisting back on itself. Now when you build your 90% and 95% intervals, they automatically nest: the 90% range sits inside the 95% range, because both come from the same smooth curve.

The paper proves that this smooth quantile curve, averaged over time, matches the true conditional quantile distribution — meaning your intervals stay honest even in a time-varying world.

Math after the picture

The smooth quantile equation

First in words:

The prediction interval is built by taking the model's guess, then adding and subtracting a margin that comes from a smooth quantile curve. The quantile curve is a Cornish-Fisher function of the mean and variance of the nonconformity score, constrained to be monotonic.

Then the compact form:

q_alpha(t) = mu(t) + z_alpha * sigma(t) * (1 + gamma(t) * (z_alpha^2 - 1) / 6)

Every symbol:

q_alpha(t)The alpha-th quantile (e.g., 95th percentile) of the nonconformity score at time t
mu(t)The mean of past errors up to time t
sigma(t)The standard deviation of past errors up to time t
z_alphaThe standard normal quantile (e.g., 1.96 for the 97.5th percentile of a bell curve)
gamma(t)The skewness (asymmetry) of the error distribution at time t
alphaThe desired confidence level (e.g., 0.9 for 90% coverage)
A tiny worked example
Time step 10: mu(10)=0.5 (mean error), sigma(10)=0.3 (spread of errors), gamma(10)=0.1 (slight skew).
For 95% confidence: z_0.975 = 1.96 (standard table value).
Cornish-Fisher term: 1.96 * 0.3 * (1 + 0.1 * (1.96^2 - 1) / 6) = 1.96 * 0.3 * (1 + 0.1 * 3.16 / 6) = 1.96 * 0.3 * 1.053 = 0.618.
Final margin: 0.5 + 0.618 = 1.118. If the model predicts 100, the 95% interval is [100 - 1.118, 100 + 1.118] = [98.88, 101.12].
Key: the margin is smooth because mu, sigma, gamma are learned via regression and vary smoothly over time.

The Cornish-Fisher curve trades off simplicity (it's a closed-form formula) with accuracy (it captures not just the spread, but also skewness — asymmetry in the error tail).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too little data per quantile level. If you query the base quantile learner at 20 different levels but only have 50 past errors, each level has few examples. The fitted curve can still be noisy. The paper needs enough history to learn the quantile curve reliably; short time series may need gentler constraints to avoid overfitting.
Distribution shift faster than the method adapts. If tomorrow's error distribution is radically different from today's (e.g., a structural break in the data, such as a policy change), the smooth curve lags behind. The method assumes the distribution changes gradually. Sudden shocks will cause undercoverage (the interval too narrow).
Monotonicity constraints too tight. The paper enforces that the quantile curve never crosses itself. If the true distribution has a weird shape (e.g., a bimodal error distribution), the smooth constrained curve may underfit and widen intervals unnecessarily.
When applied well. CQACP shines when you have months or years of time series data with gradual seasonal changes (temperature, demand, financial data). The smooth curve learns the seasonal pattern of risk, and the monotonicity constraint stops jitter.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic time series where the error distribution changes predictably (e.g., smaller errors in summer, larger in winter). Train a simple forecasting model (e.g., linear regression or a moving average). Wrap it with standard sequential conformal prediction and then with CQACP. Measure coverage (do 95% of true values fall in the 95% intervals?), nesting violations (do narrower intervals cross wider ones?), and interval width over the year. Plot the ranges as shaded bands and show that CQACP is stable and nested while naive conformal jumps around.

Coverage = (# of times true value fell in the interval) / (total predictions); Nesting violation rate = (# of times interval_90% wider than interval_95%) / (total pairs); Smoothness = sum of squared differences between consecutive interval widths.

The demo must show three things visually: CQACP's intervals follow the seasonal risk pattern (wider in winter, narrower in summer), they never cross (a 90% band always sits inside the 95% band), and they do not jitter like the naive method does.
