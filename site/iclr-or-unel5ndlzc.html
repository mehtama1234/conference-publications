Arbitrary-Shaped Image Generation via Spherical Neural Field Diffusion

Instead of generating rectangular images at one size, this paper generates a full 3D scene on a sphere, then cuts out any rectangular piece you ask for, at any resolution.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Diffusion models are great at generating images, but they are stuck: once you train a model for rectangular images at a certain size, that is all it can make. This paper asks a different question: what if we built the scene in 3D spherical space instead, like painting the inside of a ball? Then you can slice rectangular windows into that ball at any angle, zoom level, and shape you want — perspective, panoramic, even fisheye — all from one model.

Why rectangular shapes are a trap

Today's image generators work like this: train a diffusion model on a dataset of rectangular photos, and it learns to make new rectangular photos of exactly that size. If you want a different shape — taller, wider, panoramic — you need a completely different model. If you want to show the same scene from different viewpoints or zoom levels, you are stuck.

A beginner might think: just crop the edges or stretch the pixels. But that does not work. Cropping loses information at the edges. Stretching or shrinking makes the content blurry or distorted. The real problem is that the model never learned what a panorama looks like, or how to compose a scene so it makes sense when viewed from different angles.

The core trap: One model, one shape, one size. Flexibility across shapes requires starting over.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Build a 3D scene space

Instead of a flat 2D grid, use a mesh-based sphere in an intermediate representation space.

Step 2: Denoise the full sphere

Run diffusion noise-removal on the entire sphere, with a seam-stitching rule to keep the sphere edges consistent.

Step 3: Query with coordinates

When you want an image, give the model a 3D direction (viewpoint) and resolution; the spherical neural field evaluates that direction.

Step 4: Sample and distort-correct

Sample pixel colors from the sphere; a learned field removes any visual distortion from the mapping.

Step 5: Render the output

Output the rectangular image in whatever shape and zoom you specified.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of generating a flat 2D image, the method generates a complete 3D scene represented on a spherical mesh. This single 3D representation is queried from different angles and resolutions to produce images of any shape.

The hidden quantity

Semantic and spatial consistency across seams

measured quantity  =  sphere-boundary consistency

When you paint a scene on a sphere, the left edge and right edge must match (they are the same spot when you wrap around). The top and bottom must also connect smoothly. If they do not, you see a jarring seam or a discontinuity when you pan across the view. The 3D coordinates must also line up at the edges, so the spatial structure stays consistent.

The paper measures and enforces this consistency using a seam denoising strategy: during the diffusion process, after each noise-removal step, it checks the edges and ensures they align, both in terms of what is painted there (semantic continuity, so a tree stays a tree) and the coordinate mapping (spatial continuity, so the 3D positions match).

Why it works

Why it works: one unified scene, queried flexibly

The method works by splitting the problem into two separate jobs. First, build one complete representation of the entire scene (stored on a sphere). Second, sample from that representation to produce any shape of image you want. By keeping these jobs separate, the model learns the scene once, then renders it from any viewpoint, at any resolution.

During training, the model learns what a coherent 3D scene looks like by denoising the full sphere. This means it learns that adjacent regions must connect smoothly: the left side and right side of a panorama should match, and objects should look the same from different viewing angles. Once trained, when you ask for a specific image (a square crop at a certain zoom), the spherical neural field simply reads off that region of the sphere, using a learned function that maps viewing directions to pixel values. The result has no distortion because the mapping was learned end-to-end from real data, not a rigid mathematical formula.

Math after the picture

The method in one equation

First in words:

Loss equals diffusion prediction error plus a seam consistency penalty; seam penalty is the squared difference between the left edge and the right edge of the sphere, plus the squared difference between top and bottom.

Then the compact form:

L = L_diffusion + lambda * ( ||edge_left - edge_right||^2 + ||edge_top - edge_bottom||^2 )

Every symbol:

Ltotal training loss
L_diffusionhow far the model's prediction is from the true noise
lambdahow much to penalize seam mismatches
edge_left, edge_rightpixel values along the left and right boundaries of the sphere
edge_top, edge_bottompixel values along the top and bottom boundaries
A tiny worked example
Suppose the diffusion loss is 0.5 (the model's noise prediction is somewhat off).
The left edge has values [100, 105, 102], right edge has [110, 100, 105].
The squared difference is (100-110)^2 + (105-100)^2 + (102-105)^2 = 100 + 25 + 9 = 134.
Similarly, top-bottom mismatch is 75.
Total seam penalty: 134 + 75 = 209.
If lambda = 0.001, seam penalty contributes 0.209 to the loss.
Total loss: 0.5 + 0.209 = 0.709.
The model learns to reduce this by denoising better AND making the edges match.

The seam term forces the sphere edges to align, so the full 360-degree scene is internally consistent.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Seam mismatch during denoising. If the seam penalty lambda is too small, the left and right edges drift apart during training, creating a visible discontinuity when the model renders a panorama.
Learned field extrapolation fails outside training range. The spherical neural field is learned from training examples; if you ask for a zoom level or viewpoint far outside the training data, the field may produce blurry or incoherent results.
Equator vs pole singularities. On a sphere, the poles are singular points where all longitudes converge. The model must handle these special cases, or else the content becomes confused or stretched there.
Done right. With strong seam enforcement, training on diverse viewpoints, and careful pole handling, the model learns a coherent 360-degree scene that renders correctly at any angle and resolution.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple diffusion model on a small sphere (e.g. 64x32 latitude-longitude grid). Train it on a dataset of synthetic spherical renders (e.g. 3D rooms rendered from all angles onto a sphere). Then generate a noise sample, run diffusion to get a complete sphere, and render three different crops: a square perspective view, a panoramic (wide rectangle), and a fisheye. Show that all three are visually coherent and free of seams.

Seam consistency = average absolute pixel difference at left-right boundary + average absolute pixel difference at top-bottom boundary; lower is better. Visual coherence = human judges rate whether objects look continuous across a panoramic pan.

The demo must make it impossible to miss that one trained model produces three completely different shapes, all with consistent content and no visible seams.
