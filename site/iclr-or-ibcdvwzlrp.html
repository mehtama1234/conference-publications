ProtoTS: Hierarchical Prototypes for Explainable Time Series Forecasting

ProtoTS explains time series forecasts by finding prototypical patterns at different scales, so you can see exactly which historical examples the model learned from.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Deep learning models can predict future values in time series very accurately, but they act like black boxes—you see a number but not why. ProtoTS solves this by building a library of historical examples at two levels: coarse prototypes that show the big seasonal or trend patterns, and fine prototypes that show small wiggles and exceptions. When predicting, the model finds the closest historical examples and blends them, so the forecast becomes understandable.

Zero assumption version

Black-box forecasts hide the reasoning

Time series forecasting (predicting tomorrow's stock price, power load, or sales) matters in business and medicine. Neural networks get high accuracy, but if the stakes are high—medical alerts, financial trading—you need to know why the model predicted what it did.

A naive approach is to look at which input variables matter most (e.g., this sensor mattered more than that one). But that misses the real story: the model learned patterns that span multiple time steps, mixing several sensors together. A forecast that says tomorrow's load will be 500 megawatts is not answered by 'sensor 3 matters'—you want to know 'this looks like a Tuesday morning in summer with mild weather, and those typically climb to 520 before noon.'

The problem in one sentence. Existing explainable time series models show which inputs matter locally, but they do not show the recurring temporal patterns the model actually learned.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Learn coarse prototypes

Cluster all historical time series sequences into a small number of coarse groups (e.g., 5 patterns that cover 80% of seasonal behavior).

Step 2: Learn fine prototypes per coarse group

Within each coarse pattern, find finer variations (e.g., the Tuesday pattern has a clear-sky version and a rainy version).

Step 3: Denoise the incoming sequence

Take today's sensor readings, smooth out measurement noise while keeping the real signal, so matching to prototypes is clean.

Step 4: Compute similarity to all prototypes

Measure how close today's denoised sequence is to each stored prototype at both the coarse and fine levels.

Step 5: Blend prototypes to forecast

The final prediction is a weighted mix of the prototypes, where the weights come from how similar each prototype is to today.

What the paper changes

The single thing that changes

ProtoTS changes how the model represents what it learned during training. Instead of storing raw learned weights (numbers in a deep neural network), it stores a library of prototypical sequences—real examples or prototypes clustered from the training data. At prediction time, similarity to prototypes replaces the opaque forward pass through many layers.

the object it moves  =  the learned representation: from weights to prototypes
The hidden quantity

Prototype similarity: the measure that drives everything

measured quantity  =  How close the input is to each stored prototype

The hidden quantity is a similarity score: for each stored prototype (at both coarse and fine levels), ProtoTS computes a distance or similarity measure to today's denoised input sequence. This is just a number that says 'today's pattern is 85% similar to that Tuesday prototype' or '20% similar to the holiday prototype.'

The paper measures this similarity by comparing the denoised representations (smoothed sensor readings) to the stored prototypes using a standard distance metric (like Euclidean distance—measuring how far apart two points are in a multi-dimensional space). Lower distance means higher similarity, and the model learns which prototypes matter by seeing which similarities correlate with accurate forecasts on the training data.

Why it works

Why hierarchical prototypes make sense and work

Most real time series have structure at multiple scales. A power grid's daily load has a big seasonal pattern (winter vs. summer) and a weekly pattern (weekday vs. weekend), but also day-to-day noise and anomalies. If you only keep coarse prototypes (one 'winter shape'), you oversmooth and lose forecast accuracy. If you only keep fine prototypes (one for each individual day), you memorize noise and cannot generalize.

ProtoTS solves this by keeping both levels. The coarse prototypes act as a backbone: 'This looks like a winter pattern.' Then the fine prototypes refine that: 'Specifically, it is a clear winter weekday, not a snowy one.' The forecast is not a single prototype, but a weighted blend of the most similar ones at both levels. This works because real forecasting is a mixture—today is never a perfect match to one historical day; it is usually a blend.

The denoising step matters because raw sensor readings have measurement error and random jitter. By smoothing first, the model compares the true underlying pattern in today's data to the underlying patterns in history, not the noise. This makes the prototype matching more reliable because two days that are truly similar will have their similarities obscured by noise if you compare the raw readings directly—smoothing removes that noise, so the genuine similarity scores come through.

Math after the picture

The similarity-weighted forecast

First in words:

forecast equals sum over all prototypes of (similarity weight times prototype forecast) divided by sum of all weights

Then the compact form:

forecast_t = sum_j w_j * proto_j / sum_j w_j, where w_j = exp(-distance_j / temperature)

Every symbol:

forecast_tthe predicted value at time t
proto_jthe learned forecast from prototype j
distance_jhow far the denoised input is from prototype j in the learned space
temperaturea smoothing constant (higher = softer blending)
w_jthe final weight of prototype j (higher distance = lower weight)
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have 3 coarse prototypes with distances 0.2, 0.5, 1.0 from today, and a temperature of 1.0.
Weights: w1 = exp(-0.2/1.0) = exp(-0.2) ≈ 0.82, w2 = exp(-0.5) ≈ 0.61, w3 = exp(-1.0) ≈ 0.37.
Sum of weights: 0.82 + 0.61 + 0.37 = 1.80.
Suppose the prototypes predict 100, 95, 80 respectively.
Forecast = (0.82*100 + 0.61*95 + 0.37*80) / 1.80 = (82 + 57.95 + 29.6) / 1.80 = 169.55 / 1.80 ≈ 94.2.
The closest prototype (100) pulls the forecast up; the distant one (80) has little pull.

The similarity-weighted blend means close historical patterns dominate the forecast, and distant ones barely influence it, creating an explainable prediction you can show by saying 'this looks most like this historical day.'

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Insufficient prototypes make the forecast rigid. If you keep only 3 coarse prototypes for a time series with 10 natural patterns, every forecast becomes a blend of those same 3, and many forecasts will be inaccurate or will have the wrong explanation attached.
Too many fine prototypes overfit to training noise. If you keep 100 fine prototypes for every coarse prototype, the model starts remembering individual training examples instead of learning generalizable patterns, and the explanations become 'this is like this one specific day' rather than 'this is like this class of days.'
Denoising can remove real signal if tuned wrong. If the smoothing is too aggressive, you erase real short-term oscillations (like a sudden demand spike) and the forecast misses important events.
Done right. Choose the prototype count by cross-validation (trying different numbers on held-out data and picking the one with best accuracy and clear explanations). Use domain knowledge to decide how smooth the denoising should be—ask experts how much short-term noise they expect in a typical reading.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small forecasting task (e.g., predict the next 7 days of a public dataset like LOF power consumption or electricity prices). Train ProtoTS and a black-box neural network on the same data. On a held-out test set, (1) measure how close each forecast is to the actual value, (2) for each test forecast, retrieve and display the top 5 most similar prototypes in the training set, and (3) show that those 5 historical examples are visually recognizable as similar to today by eye.

forecast_error = mean(abs(predicted_value - actual_value)) and explanation_completeness = fraction of test examples where a domain expert recognizes the top-5 prototypes as sensible historical matches

The demo must make it impossible to miss that the forecast is not a mysterious number but a blend of a few recognizable historical patterns, all of which you can inspect and even edit by hand to steer the model.
