Residual Context Diffusion Language Models

Diffusion models that decode many words at once waste computation by throwing away uncertain words, but recycling their hidden information fixes that.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Most language models guess one word at a time. Diffusion models try to guess many words all at once, which finishes in fewer steps because multiple positions are refined in parallel, but they throw away the uncertain guesses instead of learning from them. This paper shows that the discarded guesses still contain useful context about what words should come next, because even uncertain predictions reveal patterns about neighboring words and the overall meaning, and proposes a way to recycle that context into the next refinement step, making the model faster and more accurate without adding much extra computation.

Zero assumption version

Throwing away information to go faster

Normal language models (like ChatGPT) generate text one word at a time. A newer approach called diffusion language models works differently: they start with noise and refine many words at once in each step, like a blurry image gradually sharpening. This lets them finish in fewer steps because they are working on multiple words in parallel.

But these models have a flaw. At each step, the model is more confident about some words and less confident about others. The standard approach throws away the low-confidence words (the remasking trick), keeping only the confident ones for the next step. The low-confidence words are discarded completely, wasting the computation and the contextual information they contained.

The waste: discarded words still know something useful about the context, but that knowledge is thrown out. Recycle the discarded words by converting their hidden information into residuals (adjustments) that feed into the next decoding step.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Generate predictions for many word positions at once

Diffusion model produces hidden information (a vector, just a list of numbers) for each position.

Step 2: Sort words by confidence

Model judges which predictions are strong and which are weak.

Step 3: Recycle weak words into adjustments

Instead of discarding weak predictions, convert their hidden information into residuals (small numerical adjustments).

Step 4: Inject residuals into the next step

Feed the adjustments into the model as additional context for the next denoising iteration.

Step 5: Train efficiently with decoupled stages

Use two separate training phases to avoid running out of memory during learning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: discarded word information

The paper changes one key thing: instead of throwing away the hidden numerical patterns from low-confidence predictions, it converts them into residual adjustments (small numerical corrections) and passes those adjustments to the next refinement step. This is a concrete, mechanical change to how the model recycles its intermediate computations.

the object it moves  =  discarded word representations
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: contextual residuals

measured quantity  =  contextual residuals (recycled adjustment signals)

A contextual residual is the useful information hidden inside a low-confidence word that would normally be thrown away. It is not the word itself but the learned numerical pattern (vector) that the model produced while thinking about that word. Instead of deleting it, the paper converts that pattern into a small adjustment signal.

The paper measures and uses this by taking the hidden vectors of discarded words, passing them through a lightweight learned module, and adding the resulting adjustments back into the model's state before the next denoising step. This lets subsequent steps benefit from the context that the discarded words had already started to encode.

Why it works

Why it works: recycling computation preserves continuity

Normally, discarding a word means its hidden information vanishes. The next step of refinement has no memory of it. But that discarded word had already begun to encode useful context—what words came before, what makes sense in this situation. By converting the discarded word's hidden representation into a residual adjustment, the paper preserves that partial understanding and smuggles it forward.

The next refinement step receives not just the kept words and fresh noise, but also these contextual hints. Because the hints are small adjustments rather than full new words, they act as guides without overwhelming the model's own predictions. The model learns to blend the carried-forward context with its fresh reasoning, making better decisions overall. This is efficient because the residuals are just numbers, which cost far less computation than recomputing the context from scratch, and the training uses two separate stages to avoid memory overload.

The math: residual injection

First in words:

residual_adjustment = learned_transform(hidden_vector_of_discarded_token) + next_step_input

Then the compact form:

h_next = h_next + W * h_discard

Every symbol:

h_nexthidden state for the next denoising step
h_discardhidden representation of the token being discarded
Wlearned weight matrix that transforms discarded vectors to residuals
+the residual is added as an adjustment, not a replacement
A tiny worked example
Discarded token has hidden vector: [0.3, -0.2, 0.5]
Weight matrix W transforms it: W * [0.3, -0.2, 0.5] = [0.1, -0.05, 0.15]
Next step input: [2.0, 1.5, 1.0]
After residual injection: [2.0 + 0.1, 1.5 - 0.05, 1.0 + 0.15] = [2.1, 1.45, 1.15]
The model's next predictions are nudged by the contextual hint.

The residual is a small adjustment added to the next step's input, carrying forward the discarded token's contextual signal without replacing what the model itself predicts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Residuals can drift too far from reality. If the learned transformation W is not trained correctly, the injected residuals can mislead the next step instead of guiding it. This requires careful tuning of the two-stage training pipeline.
Memory still required during initial training. The decoupled two-stage approach helps reduce memory later, but training the transformation layer initially still demands significant graphics-card memory because both the model and the learned transform must fit together before they can be split apart.
Residuals may not transfer across different model sizes or architectures. A transformation trained on one model size may not work well when applied to a larger or differently-structured diffusion model.
Done right: residuals act as gentle contextual nudges. When the learned transformation is trained properly on realistic data, the residuals improve both speed and accuracy because they carry true contextual information forward without overriding or confusing the model's own predictions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small diffusion language model that decodes 8 words at each step. Implement standard remasking (discard low-confidence words) and compare it to RCD (convert discarded words to residuals and inject them). On a simple task like completing sentences or solving arithmetic problems, measure accuracy and count the total number of denoising steps needed to reach a target quality.

improvement = (accuracy_with_RCD - accuracy_baseline) / accuracy_baseline * 100%, speed_gain = steps_baseline / steps_with_RCD

The demo must show that recycled residuals improve accuracy on the same number of steps, or reach baseline accuracy in fewer steps, proving that the discarded words genuinely carried forward useful context, because if residuals were just noise they would not consistently steer the model toward better predictions.
