Seeing Through Deception: Uncovering Misleading Creator Intent in Multimodal News

A dataset of 12,000 image-caption pairs explicitly labeled with creator intent forces vision-language models to learn deception detection beyond shallow tricks like polish or consistency.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

When you see a news photo paired with a headline, you naturally ask: did the creator pick this photo to mislead me? Vision-language models (the systems that reason about images and text together) mostly fail at this question. They get distracted by surface tricks like nice image quality, matching text, or a professional look—missing the actual creator's goal. This paper builds a dataset where each fake news example is labeled with what the creator was trying to do (exaggerate, reframe a photo, hide something). Training models on this intent-labeled data teaches them to reason about hidden goals, not just spot visual consistency.

Zero assumption version

Models fall for shallow tricks in fake news

Misinformation spreads through images and captions that look real. Spotting it requires understanding what the creator actually wanted the viewer to believe, but vision-language models (systems that read images and text together) mostly ignore this. Instead, they focus on surface cues: is the photo high quality? Does the text match the image? Does it sound professional? A creator can exploit these shortcuts—polishing a deceptive image, writing fluent text around a misleading photo, or making the whole thing sound authoritative.

A beginner might naively assume models see through deception by checking facts. But facts are hard to verify instantly. Instead, current models just check: does it LOOK believable? This fails because real deception is often designed to look as believable as real news. The paper shows that adding hints like 'be skeptical' flips model predictions entirely—the model is not reasoning about intent; it is just responding to the prompt's mood.

The fix. Create a dataset where each fake news example is labeled with the creator's actual goal (exaggerate, hide, reframe, etc.). Train models on intent labels, not just misleading-or-not labels.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Pick 2,000 real news articles as ground truth.

Start with trustworthy, factual reference content.

Define six creator intentions (desire + execution).

Exaggerate, hide, reframe, sensationalize, etc., with concrete tactics.

Generate 6 variants per article: two for each intent class.

Use FLUX image-generation and prompts to create believable fakes aligned to each intent.

Label each variant with its intent goal.

Explicitly tag what the creator is trying to make viewers believe.

Train models on intent labels, measure transfer to real misinformation.

LLaVA and Qwen models trained on DeceptionDecoded then tested on three real MMD benchmarks.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard misinformation datasets label each image-caption pair as misleading or not. DeceptionDecoded goes deeper: it labels the creator's intention—what goal or tactic the creator used. This shift from binary classification to intent reasoning is the paper's move.

the object it moves  =  Intent labels on fake-news data
The hidden quantity

The creator's intent signal

measured quantity  =  Creator communicative goal

Every piece of misinformation has a hidden layer: what did the creator want viewers to think or feel? Was it to exaggerate a real event? Hide a fact? Reframe a photo's context? Standard datasets ignore this and just mark true-or-false. This paper measures intent explicitly—the creator's desired influence and the execution tactic they used to achieve it.

The paper estimates intent by prompting GPT-4o to describe the manipulation, then synthesizing images with FLUX to match that intent. Humans then verify the intent-image alignment and inter-rater agreement. This creates a direct, labeled measurement of creator goal at scale.

Why it works

Why training on intent defeats surface tricks

Models like LLaVA learn patterns from training data. If you only show them 'misleading-or-not' labels, they learn surface features: high-quality images feel true, bad grammar feels false, matching captions reinforce each other. A polished fake photo with fluent text looks 'true' by these rules.

DeceptionDecoded breaks this by annotating the creator's actual goal. A polished fake is labeled 'this is trying to exaggerate a real event.' Now the model learns a new pattern: polish combined with a narrative mismatch signals intent-driven deception, not truth. It stops trusting surface consistency and instead asks what the creator wanted. The model learns to reason about hidden goals, not just surface cues.

Training on 6,000 intent-labeled examples then transfers: when the model sees a real-world fake on a new benchmark, it recognizes intent-patterns it learned, not just surface authenticity. This is why fine-tuned LLaVA and Qwen show large F1 improvements on three independent real-misinformation datasets—they have learned intent-reasoning that generalizes.

Math after the picture

Predicting intent from image and caption

First in words:

Loss = cross_entropy(predicted_intent, true_intent) + lambda * KL_divergence(predicted_distribution, calibration_prior)

Then the compact form:

Loss = -sum_i true_intent_i * log(predicted_intent_i) + lambda * sum_i predicted_i * log(predicted_i / prior_i)

Every symbol:

true_intent_iwhether the i-th intent category is true for this example
predicted_intent_imodel's probability that intent i is present
lambdahow much to penalize overconfident, miscalibrated predictions
prior_ithe base rate of intent i in the dataset
A tiny worked example
Suppose true intent is 'exaggerate' (label = 1), and the model predicts 0.8 for exaggerate, 0.15 for hide, 0.05 for reframe.
Cross-entropy term: -1 * log(0.8) - 0 * log(0.15) - 0 * log(0.05) = -log(0.8) = 0.223 (small penalty because model got it mostly right).
Now suppose model predicts 0.99 for exaggerate (overconfident) and prior is 0.3. KL term: 0.99 * log(0.99/0.3) = 0.99 * log(3.3) = 0.99 * 1.19 = 1.18 (penalizes overconfidence, pushes model toward prior belief).
Total loss with lambda=0.1: 0.223 + 0.1 * 1.18 = 0.341. The KL term keeps predictions honest, not extreme.

The first term penalizes wrong intent predictions; the second term penalizes overconfidence and pushes models toward the natural pattern of how often each intent appears in the data.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Intent labels may be ambiguous in real-world misinformation. DeceptionDecoded is synthetically generated with clean intent labels. Real fake news often mixes multiple intents or is deliberately ambiguous. A model trained only on single-intent examples may struggle with messy real-world cases.
FLUX-generated images may have artifacts that leak synthetic data. If the model learns to detect 'this image was AI-generated' as a shortcut, it is still using surface cues, not intent reasoning. The paper addresses this by requiring high inter-rater agreement and testing on real-world examples, but artifact bias is a real risk.
Prompt injection can still fool the model. The paper shows that adding 'be skeptical' or 'trust this' hints changes model predictions. Even after fine-tuning on intent, a model can be manipulated by high-level framing cues in the question itself.
Model learns intent reasoning, not perfect detection. Even with intent labels, misinformation detection is not 100% solvable. The model learns to reason about goals, which is better than surface cues, but deceptive creators can still evolve their tactics faster than data collection can track them, because the pool of new tactics is larger than the pool of new data each month.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple UI that shows an image-caption pair and asks: what did the creator want viewers to believe? Train a small LLaVA model (7B parameters) on 1,000 DeceptionDecoded intent-labeled examples. Then test it on five held-out synthetic fakes (with known intent ground truth) and five real-world misinformation examples from MMFakeBench. Show side-by-side: (1) the predicted intent, (2) the model's confidence, (3) whether it matches ground truth, (4) the image and caption. A good demo reveals whether the model is reasoning about goals or just spotting polish and matching.

Intent-reasoning success = accuracy_on_known_intent + (1 - avg_confidence_on_adversarial) + transfer_to_MMFakeBench

The demo should make it impossible to miss that the model is reasoning about creator goals, not just visual polish. A polished fake should be flagged if the creator's intent is deceptive; a grainy real photo should pass if the creator's intent is truthful.
