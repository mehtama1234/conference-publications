Imaging and reconstructing hidden structures

Using AI to recover sharp images from blurry scans or partial data, revealing internal structures in microscopy, medical imaging, and cryo-EM without expensive higher-resolution equipment.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A microscope image is always fuzzy. A medical scan — CT, MRI, X-ray — is always noisy and low resolution. Cryo-EM (a technique to see proteins) produces images so grainy they look like abstract art. This is not a failure — it is the cost of using safe radiation doses or capturing fleeting biological moments. But the loss of detail costs researchers: they miss tiny features, misdiagnose diseases, or cannot see exactly where a drug binds to its target.

Traditional approaches sharpen images by assuming specific models: assume the image is a blurred version of a sharp image, then deblur it using math. But biological structures are so varied that no single mathematical model works well. Cryo-EM images of proteins are determined by how they were randomly frozen and angled in the microscope, so the same protein looks different in each image. A tumor in a scan has texture and shape that varies from person to person.

This cluster uses neural networks to learn what sharp biological images look like by training on pairs: start with high-quality reference data (expensive, rare) and lower-quality versions (the actual scans we want to improve), and train a network to map from fuzzy to sharp. The challenge is generalizing: images from one microscope are different from another, data from one disease looks different from another. So papers here explore transfer learning (train once on a reference dataset, adapt quickly to new data), uncertainty quantification (the network should admit when it is guessing), and physics-informed networks (build in what we know about how imaging works). The payoff is microscopy and medical imaging that is clearer and cheaper because doctors can see tiny features without higher-end hardware, speeding up diagnosis and discovery because clearer images reveal disease earlier.

How the work breaks down

This cluster splits into four natural directions: handling grainy cryo-EM protein images to see atomic structure, sharpening medical scans (CT and MRI) from sparse or noisy data, recovering sharp images from brain signals or eye movements, and reading tiny biological features from microscopy and cellular imaging. Each direction faces the same core problem: the measurement is fuzzy or incomplete, so networks learn to fill in missing detail by training on reference data.

Cryo-EM: From Grainy Particles to Atomic Models

Cryo-EM produces images so grainy they look random. A protein is frozen in ice, blasted with electrons, and photographed from different angles, but the image each angle captures depends on how the protein happened to be oriented at that frozen moment. Researchers need to recover the actual 3D atomic structure from thousands of these grainy 2D photos. Three papers here tackle different parts of this pipeline: building the atomic model from the density map, handling the raw particles, and making the approach work at scale.

CryoNet.Refine solves the last step: given a rough atomic model (the skeleton of where atoms go) and a cryo-EM density map (the grainy 3D image), it refines the model so atoms sit exactly in the peaks of the density. Most methods assume the density is already sharp; CryoNet uses a diffusion model (a network trained to denoise images step by step) to sharpen the density first, then place atoms more accurately. It works on proteins, RNA, and large assemblies. CryoACE goes earlier in the pipeline: instead of waiting for a clear model, it builds the atomic structure atom-by-atom directly from the noisy density map, and for the first time shows that networks can capture how proteins actually move and bend in the ice, not just their still average. CryoLVM takes a different path: it trains a large vision model on thousands of cryo-EM images to learn what cryo-EM densities look like, then applies that knowledge to sharpen grainy maps, fill in gaps, and restore parts of the image that are missing. CryoSplat uses Gaussian splatting (representing 3D space as overlapping blobs instead of a grid) to reconstruct the full 3D volume directly from raw particle images, skipping the usual expensive alignment step. Together, these papers show that each bottleneck in cryo-EM—sharpening, atomic placement, handling particles, and reconstructing 3D—can be attacked with the right neural network, and each attack cuts cost or time.

The work spreads across the pipeline because cryo-EM is a classic ill-posed problem: many different 3D structures could produce the same grainy images you observe, so you need prior knowledge (the density should be smooth, atoms should sit in known shapes, proteins move only slightly) baked into the network to pick the right answer. These papers differ in where they inject that prior: diffusion for sharpening, symmetry constraints for atoms (atoms belong in regular lattices with repeating patterns), vision pre-training for transfer, and direct 3D rendering for particles.

Medical Scans: Fewer Measurements, Sharper Images

CT and MRI scans are expensive and noisy. A CT scanner shoots X-rays through the patient and measures what comes out; fewer X-rays means less radiation (safer) but noisier images because each X-ray carries information, so fewer signals leave more ambiguity about what is inside. An MRI coil measures magnetic signals at different times; collecting fewer measurements scans faster (which matters for patients who cannot hold still or are critically ill) but leaves gaps in the data because the reconstruction algorithm assumes it has measurements at regular intervals, and gaps mean the algorithm cannot fill in parts of the image reliably. Doctors want sharp images with low dose and quick scans. The challenge: images from one scanner are different from another, patients' anatomy varies wildly, and the reconstruction math is non-obvious. Five papers here show different ways to use neural networks to invert the measurement process and produce sharp images from limited data.

Foundation VAE trains a base model on a large dataset of CT scans, learning what normal anatomy looks like. It can then be fine-tuned or used as-is to reconstruct cleaner CTs from raw measurements, and it also lets you generate new synthetic scans for data augmentation or testing. NAB handles sparse-view CT specifically (when you have only a few X-ray angles instead of the full circle) by learning to guess which regions need smoothness and which allow sharp boundaries, adapting to different scanner geometries on the fly. Principled Confidence Estimation solves an important practical problem: when a neural network reconstructs a CT, how do you know which parts it hallucinated versus which parts are really there? This paper shows how to wrap uncertainty estimates around the reconstruction (so a doctor sees confidence regions, not just guesses) and detect when the network is making things up. IPOD (the MRI paper) eliminates the need to train on high-quality reference scans from the same scanner; instead it learns from any MRI data and adapts in seconds to a new protocol or anatomy, achieving high-fidelity MRI without collecting expensive gold-standard training data. Plug-and-Play Diffusion Meets ADMM combines two optimization ideas: diffusion models (which gradually denoise) and ADMM (an algorithm for solving inverse problems), balancing bias (over-smoothing) and hallucination to reconstruct CT and MRI with fewer artifacts and faster convergence. Together, these papers reduce the tension between dose/speed and image quality by letting networks learn to invert the physics of measurement rather than applying hand-tuned filters.

The unifying thread: each paper bakes in physics (the measurement model, smoothness priors, or convergence algorithms) to steer the network away from hallucinations, and most explore transfer learning (train once on a large cohort, adapt to a new scanner or patient group) because medical data is scarce.

Brain Signals to Images and Behavior: Decoding from Sparse Electrical Readings

The brain speaks through electricity: EEG (scalp electrodes), fMRI (blood flow in the brain), and sEEG (electrodes placed on the brain during surgery) each measure a different signal, but all are noisy and limited in detail. Neuroscientists and clinicians want to know: what is the person seeing, intending, or feeling? And can we read that intent to help people who cannot move, because if we decode intent from brain signals, paralyzed patients could control a cursor or synthesizer without moving their body. The challenge: the signal is fragmentary (EEG has only a few electrodes, fMRI has low time resolution), the person's brain wiring is unique, and signals from one person rarely transfer to another because everyone's brain layout is slightly different and their electrodes are in slightly different spots. Four papers here show how to reconstruct images or behavior from fragmentary neural recordings.

Assembling the Mind's Mosaic learns to decode semantic intent from EEG—it reads scalp electricity and infers what word the person is trying to speak. It works across languages and on clinical recordings from surgery, handling the variety in electrode placement and individual brain wiring by learning a shared semantic space (a common language neurons and brain signals both speak). MindPilot goes the other direction: it sends visual stimuli (images flashing on a screen) while recording EEG, and learns which images produce which brain patterns, so it can find the best image to guide emotional regulation or attention. Brain-IT reconstructs the actual images a person saw from their fMRI signal—it maps blood flow patterns in visual cortex back to pixel space. This is hard because fMRI is blurry and slow (one measurement every 1–2 seconds) while vision is sharp and fast, so the network must learn that brain activity patterns encode high-level features (what object?) rather than exact pixels. MindHier does the same for fMRI but uses hierarchical reconstruction mimicking human vision: it first guesses rough scene structure (is it indoors or outdoors?) then fills in objects, which is both faster (because it makes fewer guesses, not trying to solve the whole image at once) and more semantically faithful than diffusion-based image generation (because human brains organize vision the same way, so a network trained to mimic that hierarchy learns a better prior). Together, these papers show that the same principle works across different brain signals and different tasks: learn a shared space where neural signals and external states (images, words, intentions) live nearby, then use that space to translate between them.

The common thread: each paper exploits the structure of the data (images have visual hierarchy, languages have shared concepts across speakers, brain signals from nearby electrodes correlate) to handle the sparsity and noise. None of these tasks is possible without transfer learning because a single person's data is too small to train a network from scratch.

Microscopy and Cell Imaging: Seeing Subcellular Detail Without Expensive Hardware

Microscopy images are always blurry or dim. A standard microscope cannot resolve features smaller than about half the wavelength of light (roughly 200 nanometers); to see smaller features (viruses, protein complexes, organelles), you need expensive super-resolution microscopy or high-end electron microscopy. Fluorescence microscopy has another problem: it fades, photons are rare, and the signal is buried in noise. Researchers want the detail of high-end microscopy without the cost or complexity. Four papers show how neural networks can squeeze more detail from the images you already have.

Optimal Transport Unlocks End-to-End Learning for Single-Molecule Localization solves an old problem in new way. When fluorescent molecules emit photons, the detector gets a dim, blurry cloud; traditionally you fit a Gaussian peak and hope the center is the true location, but when many molecules are close together, this fails. This paper trains a network end-to-end using optimal transport (a math framework for comparing blurry distributions), which automatically learns the shape of the blur and solves for the positions of all molecules at once, beating traditional methods especially in crowded scenes. Recover Cell Tensor sharpens fluorescence microscopy by treating the image as a sparse tensor (a 3D matrix with most entries zero) and using a diffusion model to fill in plausible values, recovering signal that photon noise had buried. PETRI learns unified embeddings of cells across different imaging modalities (two cells measured by different techniques should be similar) and in doing so, reconstructs the missing parts of each modality, so a cell measured only by technique A can borrow information from cells measured by technique B. AutoMat tackles an inverse problem from materials science: given a noisy electron microscopy image of a crystal lattice, recover the atomic structure of the material. It combines physics (the image is a projection of the 3D lattice) with learned features, letting an agent reason about which lattice fits the image best. Together, these papers show that microscopy bottlenecks—resolution limits, photon noise, missing modalities, and crystal structure recovery—can all be loosened by having networks learn what biological and material structures look like.

Disease Screening and Interpretation: Making Diagnosis Clear and Trustworthy

Hospitals collect images and brain scans by the million. Radiologists and neurologists must interpret them, spotting early signs of disease. But human interpretation is slow, variable, and sometimes wrong. Networks can screen images fast and consistently, but doctors need to trust the diagnosis—they want to see which part of the image triggered the alert and understand why the network thinks a disease is there. The challenge is balancing speed (screen all images fast) with interpretability (show the evidence) and generalization (a model trained on one scanner or patient population often fails on another).

ADHD Disease Detection uses long-term patterns in brain function (how different brain regions talk to each other over many minutes) and memory graphs to detect ADHD in kids. Unlike methods that look at one moment in time, it captures how brain networks organize over time, improving detection on standard benchmarks. Evidential Reasoning Advances Interpretable Real-World Disease Screening makes diagnosis interpretable by reasoning over regions of abnormality: instead of a black-box score, it retrieves which parts of the scan look abnormal and builds an evidence chain. Med-Scout fixes a blind spot in vision-language models used for medical imaging: standard models often miss geometric detail (where exactly in the scan is the finding?) and focus instead on semantic labels. By training with reinforcement learning, Med-Scout learns to attend to spatial position and shape, beating off-the-shelf models by 40% on medical tasks. Glance and Focus Reinforcement for Pan-Cancer Screening applies reinforcement learning to screen for any cancer type from histology images (tissue slides): the network learns a strategy (glance at the slide overview, then focus on suspicious regions) that finds cancers faster (because it does not waste time examining clearly normal tissue) and with fewer false alarms than one-pass screening (because the zoom step lets it see cellular detail before deciding). Together, these papers recognize that medical AI must be not just accurate but interpretable and efficient—doctors are the ones who act on the network's alert, so the network must show its work.
