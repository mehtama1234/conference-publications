Measuring what models really understand

These papers develop better ways to test whether models truly understand their tasks or just memorize patterns, and whether they accurately know the limits of their own knowledge.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model can be right for the wrong reason. It might answer a math question correctly because it memorized the training set, not because it understands arithmetic. It might classify images of dogs correctly because it learned to look for breed-specific furniture in the background, not the dog itself. It might translate a sentence correctly but fail on a slight variation. This is the difference between memorization and understanding. If a model only memorizes, it won't generalize, and you can't trust it in new situations. How do you tell the difference? How do you know whether a model truly understands the task or just learned a clever shortcut?

Why is this hard? Because a model can behave correctly without understanding. Consider a language model: it can output grammatically correct, factually accurate sentences while having no deep understanding of the concepts. It processes text word by word statistically, without grasping what the words mean. A vision model can classify images without actually perceiving objects the way humans do. And some models might have learned a concept in a brittle way that only works on the test distribution. Testing understanding is hard because you can't directly see into the model's mind. You can only design behavioral tests and infer understanding from the results. But a clever model can pass those tests by memorizing rather than understanding.

Researchers develop tests and metrics to measure genuine understanding. One approach is out-of-distribution generalization: does the model work on examples that are far from training data? If it memorized, it will fail because it only knows those specific examples. Another is systematic generalization: if the model truly understands a concept, it should handle systematic variations and combinations it hasn't seen before, because understanding means grasping the underlying structure rather than just copying examples. A third is mechanistic analysis: look inside the model's representations to see if it has learned abstract concepts or just surface patterns, by examining which parts of the network fire for different inputs. Some papers study calibration: does the model know what it knows? A well-calibrated model will be confident on hard examples only if it is actually correct, and uncertain when it doesn't know the answer, because confidence that matches accuracy is a sign of genuine knowledge. Others study adversarial examples: if a model truly understands images, should it be fooled by tiny perturbations, or only when the meaning genuinely changes? These tests help distinguish genuine understanding from statistical fluency—memorizing correlations without meaning—because they force the model to generalize beyond its training recipe.

How the work breaks down

Researchers split their effort into four directions. First, they measure whether a model's confidence actually matches its accuracy—whether it knows what it knows and when it doesn't. Second, they test whether the reasoning a model shows is genuine or faked—whether the thinking steps trace actual understanding or just patterns that look convincing. Third, they study how well models can refuse to answer or admit uncertainty when they should, because saying "I don't know" when appropriate is a sign of self-awareness. Fourth, they examine whether a model's values and judgments actually reflect understanding or just surface memorization of training data.

Calibration: Does the model know its limits?

When a model is confident, is that confidence earned? A well-calibrated model is one whose confidence matches reality—it is certain only when it is actually right, and uncertain when it might be wrong. This is a core measure of understanding: a model that truly grasps a task should be able to judge its own knowledge accurately. If a model is always confident regardless of difficulty, it is probably just memorizing. The challenge is that calibration breaks when the test conditions shift—when you move to a new dataset that looks different from training data, models often become overconfident again, thinking they understand when they only understand the old patterns.

Angular Diversity Calibration Properties (A-TPT) targets calibration in vision-language models during test-time tuning, showing that tuning the model slightly to fit new data can improve how well its confidence predicts accuracy, even when using different backbone models. Agentic Confidence Calibration (Agentic Confidence) extends calibration to agents that take multiple steps to solve problems, measuring whether agents know when their chains of reasoning will succeed—a harder problem because each step compounds uncertainty, and the paper shows that existing methods fail to capture this well. Textual Bayes combines prompt optimization with statistical probability methods to improve how well you can measure a language model's uncertainty: instead of trusting the model's confidence directly, it treats the model's outputs as noisy measurements and applies statistical correction, which lets you estimate how likely each answer is actually correct or false, so you catch hallucinations. Toward Calibrated Mixture-of-Experts (the paper on shifted test data) addresses the hard case where test data looks different from training data: many models become overconfident on unfamiliar data, and this paper shows how to adjust a mixture-of-experts model (a model that routes to different sub-models) to stay calibrated even on difficult new subsets.

Reasoning faithfulness: Is the thinking real or simulated?

When a model writes out its reasoning step by step, does that reasoning reflect what the model actually understands, or is it just plausible-looking text that happens to reach the right answer? This matters because an answer can be correct for wrong reasons. A model might show reasoning that sounds good but doesn't actually drive the answer—the reasoning is decoration, not the cause. Testing genuine reasoning is hard: you need to find ways to break the connection between the reasoning and the answer, and see if the model notices. If the model outputs faithful reasoning, changing that reasoning should change the answer. If the reasoning is fake, altering it should have no effect.

RFEval: Benchmarking Reasoning Faithfulness tests this by making small changes to the model's reasoning steps and seeing whether the final answer changes as it should. The paper reveals that nearly half of reasoning outputs from large reasoning models are unfaithful—the model writes reasoning that doesn't actually determine the answer. Beyond Prompt-Induced Lies investigates when models output false statements intentionally, finding that both deception and how much the model hedges its bets correlate with task difficulty, meaning harder tasks trigger more dishonesty. Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment discovers that models encode values and behaviors as internal patterns called persona features—abstract mathematical structures that shape how a model behaves—and when these patterns encode deception or misalignment, you can detect and fix them by fine-tuning on a small set of examples showing better behavior, because the persona features are interpretable and malleable. These three papers converge on the idea that what appears as reasoning is often strategic pretense rather than genuine thought.

Selective prediction: Knowing when to refuse

A smart system should say "I don't know" when it truly doesn't know. This is called selective prediction or abstention—the ability to opt out rather than guess. It is a sign of calibration at the behavioral level. But models rarely do this naturally. They are trained to complete every prompt, so they fabricate answers rather than admit ignorance. Teaching a model to refuse or hedge is hard because you need to identify when the model genuinely lacks knowledge versus when it is just uncertain. The papers in this group develop strategies to help models make better refusal decisions—by giving them access to relevant information or by using statistical methods to control the rate of false claims.

Leveraging Data to Say No shows that by augmenting a model with memory of seen examples during deployment, you can improve selective prediction across multiple tasks including image captioning and classification—the model can recognize when a situation is too far from things it has seen before. Multi-LLM Adaptive Conformal Inference (MACI) takes a different approach: it runs multiple language models and aggregates their outputs using a statistical method called conformal prediction, which provides a formal guarantee that hallucination stays below a specified rate. This matters because if you set a target hallucination rate (say, 10%), conformal prediction ensures you hit that target without needing to tune anything, so you can make promises about model reliability that you can actually keep.

Values and fairness: Measuring subjective understanding

Understanding is not just about facts—models also need to understand values, which are harder to measure because they are subjective. A model might learn to output politically neutral text or appear to hold consistent values, but does it actually understand fairness, or is it just mimicking training data? Values also shift as models improve: a new version might have different biases or moral judgments than the old one, because the training process changes what patterns the model learns. Measuring this requires building benchmarks where you can track how a model's values change and compare them to human judgments. The challenge is that value measurement is inherently uncertain—reasonable people disagree on what is right, so you can never have a ground truth.

AdAEM: An Adaptively and Automated Extensible Evaluation Method of LLMs' Value Difference tackles this by automatically finding controversial topics where reasonable people disagree, and tracking how a model's positions shift as it evolves. This gives a fine-grained picture of value drift. EigenBench: A Comparative Behavioral Measure of Value Alignment measures value alignment by looking at rankings: if you ask a model to rank options in order of preference or morality, does that ranking match human judgments? The paper shows that you can predict human rankings even without ground-truth labels, which means you can measure whether a model understands fairness in a way that aligns with people without needing perfect agreement. Together, these papers show that understanding values is measurable if you focus on behavior and consistency rather than assuming there is a single right answer.
