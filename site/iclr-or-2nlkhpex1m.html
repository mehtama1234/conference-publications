Adaptive Visual Token Pruning in Large Vision-Language Models

Decide which parts of an image are safe to ignore based on whether the model is actually looking at them or whether they are unique.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Large models that read both images and text process every pixel region separately, which burns a lot of computation. The paper asks a practical question: which image regions can we safely throw away without hurting the answer? Two different strategies work best in different situations. On simple images with one obvious subject, the model naturally looks at the right regions and you can rely on where its gaze goes. On complex images with lots of detail scattered around, you need a different rule: keep regions that add new information (not just copies of what you already saw).

Zero assumption version

Why throw away image regions?

Large models that read both images and text (called vision-language models) take an image and break it into dozens or hundreds of small tiles or regions, which the model processes separately. Processing every single tile takes time and memory. A wide vista might show a vast sky that looks the same in every region. Throwing away 20 redundant sky regions and keeping just one would save a lot of work. But which regions are truly redundant and which ones matter? A naive approach is to throw away regions based on simple heuristics like size or motion, which loses useful detail. Others throw away based on where the model is looking, which works when the model knows what to focus on. But on complicated photos with lots of stuff happening in different places, the model does not concentrate its gaze anywhere, and throwing away regions the model ignores might discard something important.

The paper's move in one sentence. Look at two different signals (where the model is looking and whether a region is adding new information) and pick the right one for the image at hand.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Break image into tiles

The model receives an image split into dozens of small overlapping regions.

Measure two signals

For each region, compute where the model is looking (attention score) and how different it is from other regions (diversity or effective rank).

Choose the strategy

Check if the image is simple (one subject, concentrated attention) or complex (scattered features).

Keep the best regions

Simple image: keep regions with high attention. Complex image: keep regions that add unique information.

Run with fewer regions

Process the selected regions through the rest of the model, saving time.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the rule that decides which image regions to keep and which to throw away. Instead of using a fixed rule (always use where the model is looking, or always use diversity), the rule now depends on the image itself.

the object it moves  =  the pruning rule
The hidden quantity

Two measures of what to keep

measured quantity  =  focus concentration and uniqueness score

The paper measures two different properties of the image. The first is how concentrated the model's gaze is. If the model stares at just one region (like a photo of a single portrait), the gaze is concentrated. If the model glances everywhere equally (like a busy street scene), the gaze is scattered. The second is a uniqueness score that says how much repetition is in the image. If many regions look the same (a wall texture), the score is low. If every region looks different (a crowded marketplace), the score is high. The paper discovers that concentrated-gaze images work well with gaze-based pruning because the model has actually learned to focus on what matters, so its gaze is a reliable guide to which regions to keep. Scattered-gaze images need diversity-based pruning instead, because the model is not concentrating anywhere, so hunting for unique information is safer than trusting a scattered gaze to guide pruning.

Why it works

Why image-aware pruning works

When an image is simple — one person's face, a single object in focus — the model's attention naturally concentrates on the relevant regions. Throwing away the regions the model is not looking at makes sense, because the model has learned what matters. This is the attention-based pruning strategy. But when an image is complex — a crowd, a nature scene with foreground and background, a desk full of objects — the model's attention spreads out thinly across many regions because many parts genuinely matter. In this case, throwing away regions just because the model is not looking at them is dangerous; you might discard something unique. Instead, diversity-based pruning keeps regions that add new information. If regions 5 and 6 show the same red brick wall, keeping both wastes budget. Keeping one brick region and one crowd region preserves the actual variety. The paper's move is to measure image complexity (how concentrated is the model's focus) and pick the right strategy. On simple images, trust attention. On complex images, hunt for diversity. This is why it works: it never wastes the pruning budget on the wrong signal.

Math after the picture

Scoring regions

First in words:

attention score times diversity bonus, or pure diversity, depending on image class

Then the compact form:

if image_complex: kept = argmax_k(erank[k]) else: kept = argmax_k(attention[k])

Every symbol:

erank[k]the effective rank (uniqueness score) of region k, higher is more unique
attention[k]the attention score of region k from the transformer, higher means the model is looking there
image_complexa boolean: true if image has high attention entropy, false if concentrated
argmax_k(...)select the top regions by the score inside
A tiny worked example
Image 1 (simple): portrait of a face. Attention concentrated on eyes and mouth (scores 8, 7, 1, 1, 1, 1, ...). Effective rank low (background is all blur). Strategy: use attention. Keep regions with scores 8 and 7. Process 2 out of 12 regions.
Image 2 (complex): busy market. Attention spread thin (scores 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, ...). Effective rank high (vendors, fruit, signs all different). Strategy: use diversity. Rank regions by erank (fruit stall=8, sign=7, vendor=6, vendor duplicate=3, ...). Keep top 6 by uniqueness. Process 6 out of 12 regions.
Both choices use roughly half the original regions, but the first uses attention (because simple) and the second uses diversity (because complex). Same pruning budget, right signal.

The equation is not a formula but a choice rule. On simple images, ride the model's gaze. On complex images, hunt for unique regions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Misclassifying image complexity. If the attention-entropy threshold is set wrong, a moderately complex image is pruned using attention (wrong) and loses quality.
Effective rank does not catch all kinds of redundancy. A scene with repeating patterns (tiles, rows of chairs) may look diverse in the raw pixel features but have semantic redundancy the diversity measure misses.
Hallucinations still happen with diversity pruning. Even after filtering for unique regions, the model can invent details about regions it never actually processed, especially if the removed regions were critical context.
Done right. Tune the image-complexity threshold on a balanced set of simple and complex images (not just benchmarks), and validate that diversity scores actually capture human-perceived importance before deploying.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple vision-language model on a small dataset (CIFAR or a curated set of photos with varying complexity). For each image, compute attention entropy and effective rank. Classify the image as simple or complex using the paper's threshold. Apply attention-based pruning on simple images and diversity-based pruning on complex images. Measure final accuracy and hallucination rate (whether the model invents details about missing regions) for images in each category.

overall_accuracy = (simple_acc * num_simple + complex_acc * num_complex) / total_images; hallucination_rate = false_details_reported / total_queries

The demo must show that simple images pruned by attention and complex images pruned by diversity each beat the alternative strategy on their home turf.
