Reading out what models secretly think

Models encode information in their internal activations—patterns they notice, knowledge they have, reasoning they do. These papers translate that internal code into human language.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Inside a model's computation, information is encoded in a numerical format that computers can process but humans cannot read. The numbers in a hidden layer don't look like English words or ideas. But they must encode something — patterns the model noticed, knowledge it extracted from training data, hypotheses about the input. Some researchers use other neural networks to translate this internal code into human language. You can take a specific internal state, pass it to a separate decoder network (a small model trained to output descriptions), and ask it to generate a sentence describing what that state represents.

This is helpful because it lets a language model help us understand another language model. But it's also risky: the decoder itself might hallucinate. It might describe features that don't really exist or get the meaning subtly wrong. Researchers validate by checking whether the decoded descriptions actually predict the original model's behavior: if the decoded description is accurate, the original model should act consistently with it. Some papers also use prompting: activating the model to 'think aloud' about what it's computing at each step. The challenge is making sure the translation is faithful, not just plausible-sounding.

How the work breaks down

Understanding what hidden inside a model's computations splits naturally into five directions: discovering what beliefs and concepts the model has learned to recognize, reading out those internal patterns into human language, steering the model by changing the numbers inside its hidden layers, analyzing how information is organized in space within the model's hidden layers, and finally checking whether our interpretations are faithful—that they actually explain the model's behavior.

Translating internal patterns into language

When a model processes information, it stores knowledge inside its hidden layers as patterns of numbers. But those numbers don't look like words or concepts humans can read. The core challenge is: how do you turn a numerical pattern into a sentence that explains what the model actually knows?

Belief Dynamics Reveal the Dual Nature of In-Context Learning and Steering (the process of changing internal numbers to control behavior) tackles this by tracking what the model 'believes' as it processes text—by watching how beliefs shift in the hidden layers, it can predict not just what the model will do, but also how those shifts change its behavior. This means the internal states actually encode real knowledge. AI Engram: In Search of Memory Traces in Artificial Intelligence goes further: it shows that learned knowledge leaves traces you can modify by changing the numbers directly in the hidden layers. Researchers can add or remove facts the model knows, confirming that the internal representation stores retrievable knowledge. The Deleuzian Representation Hypothesis takes a different approach: discovering what concepts emerge naturally when you train networks on raw data without labels. These three papers converge on the same insight—internal patterns correspond to real knowledge and beliefs the model has learned.

Finding concepts and error sources in models

Models sometimes fail in predictable ways, or rely on spurious patterns instead of the features that should matter. To fix these problems, you first need to identify which concepts the model uses and which ones cause errors. This means discovering what features matter and where the model goes wrong.

CB-SLICE: Concept-Based Interpretable Error Slice Discovery finds error patterns by uncovering the concepts that cause failures. When a model misclassifies images or makes reasoning mistakes, those errors cluster around certain learned patterns—by naming the concepts, you can see exactly which ones are broken. Debugging Concept Bottleneck Models through Removal and Retraining uses a different angle: it removes suspected bad concepts and retrains the model to check whether performance improves. If removing one concept fixes errors without breaking other behaviors, you've found a real cause because only the guilty concept could cause exactly that effect. Learning Concept Bottleneck Models from Mechanistic Explanations builds interpretability into the architecture from the start by training the model to predict explicit intermediate concepts—mechanical explanations of its reasoning—which automatically makes errors traceable to which concept predictions were wrong. Mixture of Concept Bottleneck Experts extends this by letting different expert networks use different numbers of concepts, showing that you can dial the accuracy-interpretability trade-off by controlling how many intermediate steps the model must explain. Together, these four papers show that error analysis works best when you force the model to label its own reasoning.

Mapping the structure of what models know

A model's knowledge isn't stored randomly—it's organized in space. Where concepts are positioned matters because it reveals why models fail or succeed. When concepts point in the same direction, retrieval works. When they overlap or clash, errors happen. The spatial arrangement inside the model's hidden layers—where information sits relative to other information—shapes everything the model can do.

Cross-Modal Redundancy and the Structure of Vision-Language Representations shows that in models trained on both images and text, the internal space has two kinds of patterns: some parts are shared between modalities (carrying the connection between image and text), while other parts are unique to images or text (explaining why the modalities don't align perfectly). By restricting operations to the shared parts, retrieval improves. Navigating the Hidden Space Dynamics of Neural Models maps the entire space by treating how information flows—different regions inside the model's hidden layers correspond to learning new facts, memorizing, or spotting examples it's never seen. This works because mapping how information flows through different regions reveals what knowledge is stored in each, so you can extract it without needing to present examples. How Representation Failures Shape Vision Language Models quantifies the overlap between different concept patterns: when concepts point in clashing directions, models make mistakes. You can predict errors just from knowing where concepts sit. How Models Develop Factored Representations shows that when data has independent factors, models separate them into independent dimensions, but when factors depend on each other, models use more dimensions—they sacrifice efficiency to preserve accuracy. The Structure and Topology of Circuits: the Manifolds of Modular Addition proves that different architectures solve the same problem with equivalent internal organization, showing the structure is fundamental, not accidental. The Shape of Adversarial Influence: Characterizing Models with Persistent Homology uses mathematical tools to reveal that hidden spaces consistently compress many varied features into fewer dominant ones, which explains why some attacks work: they target those compressed regions. These papers agree: where things sit in space determines behavior.

Steering models by changing internal numbers

If you can read the model's internal state, can you control it? By modifying activations—the numbers flowing through the network—you can steer the model's behavior without retraining. The challenge is finding which activations to modify and how much they need to change to produce the behavior you want.

Old Habits Die Hard: How Conversational History Geometrically Traps LLMs reveals why steering is necessary: models get stuck in spatial traps where past context confines their future behavior. They can't escape old patterns even when asked. Steer Like the LLM: Activation Steering that Mimics Prompting shows you can escape that trap by modifying the hidden layer numbers (the internal values that flow through the computation)—the effect mimics natural prompting but with more control and clarity. Steer Where It Matters: Token-Level Visual-Sensitivity Steering for LVLMs Hallucination Mitigation goes deeper by identifying exactly which tokens need steering. Hallucinations come from specific tokens making wrong choices, and by steering only those tokens, the method fixes errors without scrambling other capabilities. Steering at the Source: Style Modulation Heads for Personality Control finds the precise internal circuits responsible for personality or style, then modifies them directly. This means you can change behavior reliably without breaking other behaviors. Collectively, these papers show that steering works best when you understand what's constraining the model and intervene at the right level—sometimes per-token, sometimes per-layer, but always informed by what the model's structure actually is.

Checking when interpretations are true

The deepest risk in reading models is hallucination—not the model hallucinating, but the person interpreting it hallucinating about what the model does. A description of an internal state might sound plausible but be wrong. You need methods to verify that an interpretation works: that it actually predicts behavior, not just sounds good. Different papers tackle validation from different angles.

A Geometric Analysis of Small-sized Language Model Hallucinations classifies when models confabulate, releasing a dataset that lets researchers study what patterns mark false outputs. Adaptive Concept Discovery for Interpretable Few-Shot Text Classification validates concepts by checking whether they work on new domains and whether humans agree with them. Bridging the Knowledge-Prediction Gap in LLMs on Multiple-Choice Questions exposes a subtle error: models sometimes know facts internally but can't output correct answers. By finding this gap and fixing it, the paper shows that internal knowledge and external behavior must be checked separately. Characterizing and Mitigating Reasoning Drift in Large Language Models catches a different failure: models drift from valid reasoning toward whatever fits the training data. By validating what reasoning is truly principled, the paper ensures interpretations correspond to real logic, not just memorization. Escaping Low-Rank Traps: Interpretable Visual Concept Learning via Implicit Vector Quantization prevents the interpreter itself from collapsing—it forces concepts to be diverse so you can't explain everything with just a few dominant patterns. Interpretable Self-Supervised Learning via Representer Landmarks and Nyström Approximation audits what unlabeled learning algorithms actually learn, revealing that they can exploit demographic shortcuts—showing that interpretability serves safety. ForensicConcept: Transferable Forensic Concepts for AIGI Detection transfers interpretations across different fake-detection models, confirming that concepts are real if they transfer rather than being specific to one architecture. LLM Self-Recognition: Steering and Retrieving Activation Signatures shows you can identify and steer a model by its internal signature without corrupting its outputs—the interpretation is valid if it works without changing what the model says. Imagination Helps Visual Reasoning, But Not Yet in Latent Space tests a key assumption: that reasoning happens best in hidden layers. By comparing explicit reasoning against hidden reasoning, it shows that interpretations must match where computation actually happens. Together these papers enforce the discipline: an interpretation is valid only if it predicts new behavior, transfers cleanly, and survives adversarial testing.
