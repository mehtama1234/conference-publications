Large-scale Uncertainty Quantification for Latent Variable Models Using Subsampling Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Analyze noisy Langevin updates and intermittent Gibbs refreshes together, then tune their step sizes and refresh rates so the resulting uncertainty estimates track a valid large-sample limit.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Models with hidden variables contain global parameters, which are shared numbers affecting all data points, and local hidden variables attached to individual observations. SGLD-Gibbs scales approximate Bayesian updating to large data by updating global parameters with noisy derivative-based steps and updating only some local variables through Gibbs sampling, which means drawing directly from a conditional probability law. It is fast, but it is hard to know whether its uncertainty estimates are trustworthy because step sizes, batch sizes, and local-refresh schedules interact in complicated ways. This paper studies the joint scaling limit of those interacting updates and converts that limit into tuning guidance.

Zero assumption version

Fast approximate Bayesian updates do not automatically give valid uncertainty

In a model with hidden variables, global parameters govern the whole population while local hidden variables explain individual examples. Exact Markov chain Monte Carlo is often too slow because every iteration may require touching every local variable.

SGLD-Gibbs cuts that cost by using random mini-batch derivatives for the global parameters and by refreshing only subsets of local variables. That is computationally attractive, but the resulting chain is not an obvious approximation of any simple target. If hyperparameters are set poorly, the method can return narrow or distorted uncertainty intervals even when point estimates look reasonable.

The paper asks a calibration question: under what scaling of step size, time, and local refresh rate does the algorithm converge to a limit that still reflects the uncertainty carried by both global and local randomness after conditioning on the data?

In one sentence. Study the joint large-sample limit of noisy global updates and sporadic local updates, then choose algorithm settings that keep that limit faithful to uncertainty after conditioning on the observed data.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how SGLD-Gibbs is tuned. Instead of picking step sizes and update rates heuristically, it derives them from a limit theorem describing the joint behavior of global and local updates.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: latent-refresh noise contribution

measured quantity  =  latent-refresh noise contribution

The uncertainty of the global parameters does not come only from derivative-estimate noise. It also comes from randomness in the local latent variables, which are updated intermittently. That contribution is hidden because the global-parameter path only feels it indirectly through changing sufficient statistics and conditional expectations.

The paper makes this contribution explicit by showing that individual local variables converge to jump processes in the scaling limit. Those jumps feed into the effective stationary probability law of the global parameters. Once that interaction is visible, one can tune the algorithm so local-refresh noise is neither ignored nor exaggerated.

Why it works

Why the jump-diffusion limit gives tuning guidance

A tuning rule is useful only if it connects algorithm settings to the stationary uncertainty eventually produced by the chain. The limit theorem provides exactly that connection. It says that after appropriate rescaling, the global parameters behave as a diffusion, which is a continuous random motion with drift and variance, while local hidden variables behave like jump processes because they only move at refresh times.

That decomposition matters because different hyperparameters control different pieces. The global step size and mini-batch scale control diffusion variance and bias. The local refresh schedule controls how much latent-state randomness is injected into the global dynamics. When the schedule is too slow, the diffusion part is driven by stale conditional structure. When it is too fast relative to the step size, computation is wasted and the approximation enters a different regime than intended.

By identifying the joint limit, the paper can state scaling relations under which the stationary spread of global parameters reflects the right balance of derivative-estimate noise and latent-update noise. That is why the tuning guidance targets uncertainty quality directly rather than only optimization speed.

A tiny worked example
Suppose the global step size is small, but local variables are refreshed only once every many iterations.
Then the diffusion part of the global update is driven by conditional summaries that stay stale for long stretches.
In the scaling limit, the local jump intensity is too low, so latent uncertainty barely enters the stationary spread of the global parameters.
Now increase the refresh rate while keeping the step size on the matched scale suggested by the theory.
The local jump contribution becomes visible in the global limit, and credible intervals for global parameters widen toward the correct uncertainty level.

The theorem is not only descriptive. It tells which algorithm settings place the approximation in a regime where uncertainty from local latent variables is still represented in the global stationary law.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Finite-sample behavior may differ from the limit. The scaling theory describes large-sample behavior. On moderate datasets, the asymptotic regime may not be fully active, so the recommended tuning can still need adjustment based on observed calibration error.
Conditional Gibbs updates can be expensive or approximate. The tuning guidance assumes that local refreshes follow the intended conditional law. If those refreshes are themselves approximated poorly, the jump contribution identified by the theory is misrepresented.
Model misspecification can hide calibration gains. Even with correct scaling, uncertainty from the algorithm reflects the assumed latent variable model. If that model is badly wrong, credible intervals can remain misleading because they are calibrated to the wrong statistical structure.
Done right. The guidance is most useful when the latent conditional updates are trustworthy, the data are large enough for the scaling regime to be relevant, and calibration is evaluated against held-out uncertainty targets rather than only point estimates.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Use a model with hidden variables such as a topic model or mixture model with many local assignments. Run SGLD-Gibbs under several step-size and local-refresh schedules, including one schedule matched to the paper's scaling guidance. Measure parameter root-mean-square error, observed coverage of credible intervals for a few global parameters, and runtime per effective sample. The theory-guided schedule should produce interval coverage closer to nominal because the local-refresh randomness enters the global chain on the intended scale.

coverage = parameters_whose_true_value_lies_in_interval / total_parameters_checked; rmse = square_root(average((estimate - truth)^2))

The demo should show that scalable approximate Bayesian updating is not only about reaching a good point estimate. The interaction between global diffusion noise and local jump noise determines whether the reported uncertainty is believable.
