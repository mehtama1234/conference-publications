Rejuvenating Cross-Entropy Loss in Knowledge Distillation for Recommender Systems

Cross-Entropy loss in recommendation distillation has a hidden mismatch between what theory guarantees and what practice needs, fixed by splitting items and carefully reweighting them.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

In a recommender system, you want to teach a small model to imitate a large one by learning to rank items the same way. The standard method uses Cross-Entropy loss on a sampled subset of items, but the paper reveals a hidden gap: the theory only works if your sample includes the small model's favorite items, yet you actually want to learn from the big model's favorites. This paper bridges that gap by splitting items into two groups and reweighting them to match what the theory requires.

Zero assumption version

Why standard Cross-Entropy fails here

When you train a small model to mimic a large one on ranking, you can only afford to check a handful of items each time—the rest are unknown. You pick a subset and apply Cross-Entropy loss, a standard cost function that penalizes wrong rank order.

The theory says: if you minimize this loss on your subset, you automatically improve the ranking quality (measured by NDCG, a metric for how well items are ordered), because the loss punishes wrong orderings and pushing those penalties down forces better rankings. But there is a hidden catch. This promise only holds if your subset happens to include the small model's own top picks. Your real goal, though, is to learn from the large model's top picks. Those are two different sets, and the paper shows the gap is often huge.

The core problem. Cross-Entropy loss has a built-in assumption that breaks when your sample contains the teacher's favorites instead of the student's.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Look at teacher's top items

Take the large model's favorite items you are distilling from.

Ask: does student already rank these high?

Split into group 1 (student ranks high) and group 2 (student ranks low).

Group 1: standard training

For items both models agree on, use normal Cross-Entropy loss.

Group 2: clever sampling

For items where they disagree, sample smartly to make student and teacher temporarily agree, then train.

Combine the two losses with adaptive weighting

Blend both groups' contributions so ranking quality improves overall, because one group may learn faster than the other, and letting each contribute proportionally to its confidence keeps the model stable.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how you divide and weight items when you apply Cross-Entropy loss. Instead of treating all items the same, it splits the teacher's favorite items into two groups based on whether the student already ranks them high, then reweights them to satisfy the hidden assumption the theory needs.

the object it moves  =  item grouping and loss weighting
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: whether the theory assumption holds

measured quantity  =  Closure assumption satisfaction

The theory has a silent requirement called the closure assumption: it only guarantees that Cross-Entropy loss improves ranking quality if your sample contains the student's top items, because the learning signal (which way to nudge the weights) is strongest for items the model is uncertain about, and those are exactly the student's top-ranked items where small changes matter most. The paper measures whether this is true for each subset of items, and introduces a quantity that tracks how much the student and teacher disagree on their favorites.

For group 1 (both agree), the assumption is satisfied naturally. For group 2 (they disagree), the paper uses a sampling trick to temporarily make the student's top picks on that subset overlap with the teacher's, forcing the assumption to hold. The adaptive weight for each group is then set based on how confident we are in each part of the theory.

Why it works

Why it works: matching theory to reality

The core mechanism is simple: the theory says it works under one condition (the subset contains the student's top items), but your real goal requires the opposite (use the teacher's top items). The solution is to split the teacher's items into two groups and handle each differently.

For group 1 (items ranked high by both), the condition is naturally met, so standard Cross-Entropy loss applies the theory correctly and training improves ranking, because the loss gradients are now computed on items the model cares most about (its own top picks), where tiny improvements have big effects on final ordering.

For group 2 (items the student ranks low), the condition is violated. The paper uses a sampling strategy where, during training on group 2, it intentionally samples items to highlight the teacher's preferences in a way that makes the student's top picks on that sample overlap with the teacher's top picks. This is a temporary alignment just for training. By making this tactical adjustment, the theory's assumption becomes satisfied for group 2 as well.

Finally, the paper learns an adaptive weight for each group during training: if one group is contributing well to the overall ranking quality, it gets more weight; if the other is struggling, less. This way both groups cooperate to improve the model's ranking ability, because the group with lower loss variance and more consistent gradients will naturally gain weight and guide the model's learning, while noisy contributions stay suppressed.

The loss equation

First in words:

Total loss is the adaptive blend of Cross-Entropy on group 1 and Cross-Entropy on group 2, where group 2 is computed on a sampled subset designed to satisfy the closure assumption.

Then the compact form:

L_total = alpha * L_CE(group1) + (1 - alpha) * L_CE_sampled(group2)

Every symbol:

L_totalthe total cost to minimize
L_CE(group1)Cross-Entropy loss on items both models rank high
L_CE_sampled(group2)Cross-Entropy loss on group 2, computed on a special sample
alphathe adaptive weight between 0 and 1, learned during training
A tiny worked example
Suppose group 1 contributes loss 0.5 and group 2 contributes loss 0.3.
Early in training, alpha starts at 0.5 (both groups weighted equally).
After some steps, group 1 improves ranking faster (loss trend down), group 2 is noisier.
Alpha learns to increase to 0.7, so group 1 gets more influence: L_total = 0.7 * 0.4 + 0.3 * 0.3 = 0.28 + 0.09 = 0.37.
This guides the model to trust the clearer signal and treat disagreement items more carefully.

Alpha adapts during training to tell the model which group is more reliable, keeping both contributions aligned with the theory, because a single fixed weight would waste effort on whichever group is noisier, while learning alpha lets the model discover the optimal balance.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Large disagreement between models. If the student and teacher's top items overlap very little, group 2 becomes huge and sampling becomes noisy. The method still works but needs more training steps.
Alpha learning is unstable. If alpha oscillates wildly, the model cannot decide which group to trust, and ranking may not improve smoothly, because the weights keep flip-flopping between favoring group 1 and group 2, causing the gradients to contradict each other step by step. Careful learning rate tuning helps, because a smaller step size dampens these oscillations and lets alpha drift slowly toward the true optimum.
Sampling strategy does not align preferences. If the sampled subset for group 2 does not actually make the student's top picks match the teacher's, the theory assumption is still violated and improvements may be small.
Works well when theory is satisfied. When the sampled subsets genuinely satisfy the closure assumption, the theory guarantees ranking quality improves, because the gradient flow now points reliably toward better rankings, and the method shows consistent gains across datasets because this guarantee is dataset-agnostic.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple recommender: a teacher model that ranks 100 items, a student model that learns to copy it on a budget (sample only 10 items per step). Run three setups: (1) standard Cross-Entropy on 10 random items, (2) standard CE on 10 teacher-top items, (3) RCE-KD that splits and reweights. Measure ranking quality by NDCG (how well ranked items match the teacher's order, from 0 to 1). Plot NDCG over training steps for all three.

NDCG = (sum of gains at each position) / (sum of gains in ideal order)

The demo must show: standard CE (setup 1 and 2) plateaus or drifts, but RCE-KD (setup 3) climbs and stays high, proving the split-and-reweight strategy works.
