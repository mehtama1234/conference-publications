Into the Rabbit Hull: Minkowski Geometry in DINOv2

A vision model's learned concepts live in convex mixtures around archetypal landmarks, not unbounded floating directions.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

DINOv2 is a vision model that powers robots and image segmentation, but until now we did not know how it actually stores ideas. This paper opens it up by learning 32,000 visual concepts and discovering they cluster around archetypal landmarks (like a prototypical rabbit or brown color) rather than spreading out in all directions. What makes this different: the model mixes a handful of archetypes together, the way you might blend a rabbit plus brown plus fluffy to spot brown fluffy rabbits.

Zero assumption version

We cannot see inside a vision model

Modern vision models like DINOv2 do amazing things: they guide robots, segment images, spot objects. But we have no idea what they are actually thinking. When you look at a pixel, the model has some internal representation (a list of numbers) that holds its understanding. What concepts does it use? Are they simple (like shapes, colors) or tangled together?

A beginner might think: just look at the numbers. But with millions of numbers and no labels, that is impossible. You might try to find the most important numbers (sparse coding: only a few matter at a time). But the paper discovered that is incomplete—the model actually blends things together constantly, like mixing paints. You need a new lens: the model does not compute with individual directions; instead it picks a few archetypal examples (a prototypical rabbit, a prototypical brown, a prototypical fluffy) and blends them together to make sense of everything.

The puzzle. Vision models store concepts around archetypal landmarks, not as independent directions that float freely in space.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the assumption that concepts are free-floating directions with a new assumption: concepts live in bounded regions defined by archetypal landmarks. Instead of a neuron storing any value, the model uses only blends of a small set of archetypal examples. That single assumption shift changes how we interpret every number the model outputs.

the object it moves  =  how concepts are arranged in space
The hidden quantity

Archetype membership: the key hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  convex mixture coefficients

The hidden quantity is: for each small patch of the image, what fraction comes from each archetype? This is a blend percentage: a list of percentages that add to 100%, like 40% rabbit, 35% brown, 25% fluffy. The paper estimates this by training sparse autoencoders (neural networks that learn to find and name patterns in the model's internal numbers) and reading the mixing weights directly from attention heads (the model's pathways for combining information).

Why this matters: in the old view, a small patch was just a list of numbers with no clear meaning. In the new view, a small patch is a recipe: these archetypal ingredients in these proportions. That recipe is readable—you can see exactly which archetypes the model chose.

Why it works

Why this works: how archetypes anchor the model

The vision model uses attention heads to combine information. Each attention head takes a set of archetypal value vectors (learned during training) and picks a weighted average of them for each patch. When you add multiple heads together, you get a sum of multiple weighted averages—which mathematically is a convex mixture of a larger set of archetypes. The model is not free to put information anywhere; it is forced to express everything as a blend of learned landmarks.

Why does the model use archetypes? Because attention naturally creates them. When the model learns to solve tasks (classify images, segment objects, predict depth), it discovers that blending a few prototypical patterns is more efficient than using unbounded directions because a small set of reusable pieces covers all the situations it will face faster than learning a unique direction for each situation. The math of attention (softmax over values) naturally creates convex regions, which means the model can only compute weighted averages—not arbitrary sums that go outside the range of its inputs, which forces it to create bounded clusters of archetypes so the model learns to pack archetypes tightly and reuse them. This is not a constraint someone imposed; it is an emergent property of how the model trains.

Math after the picture

The Minkowski Representation Hypothesis, in numbers

First in words:

A token is a convex combination of archetypes. The token representation equals a sum of mixture coefficients times archetype vectors.

Then the compact form:

token = sum_h (sum_i c_hi * archetype_i) where c_hi are convex weights (sum to 1), h indexes attention heads, i indexes archetypes

Every symbol:

tokenthe model's internal understanding of one image patch
c_hihow much archetype i contributes in attention head h (between 0 and 1)
archetype_ia learned prototype pattern, like brown or fluffy
sum_hadd up contributions from all attention heads
sum_iadd up all archetype contributions within a head
A tiny worked example
Suppose attention head 1 blends: 60% rabbit + 30% brown + 10% grass = 0.6*rabbit_vector + 0.3*brown_vector + 0.1*grass_vector
Attention head 2 separately blends: 50% fluffy + 40% white + 10% grass = 0.5*fluffy_vector + 0.4*white_vector + 0.1*grass_vector
The final token is the sum of these two: (0.6*rabbit + 0.3*brown + 0.1*grass) + (0.5*fluffy + 0.4*white + 0.1*grass)
Each coefficient stays between 0 and 1 within its head, enforcing a convex region—no unbounded directions.

This structure means tokens live in a bounded Minkowski sum of convex cones, not an unbounded vector space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Archetypes are not unique. Different sparse autoencoders may learn different sets of archetypes. The paper shows this is expected: the factorization is non-unique without access to attention weights. Steer toward models with clear archetypal structure.
Some concepts may not cluster tightly. If a concept is used in only one way, it may not form a tight archetype cluster. The tighter the cluster, the more reliable the interpretation becomes because a tight cluster means the concept is always used the same way, so reading the recipe is accurate, whereas a loose cluster means the model uses that concept in many different contexts and the single archetype may not capture all of them.
Positional information complicates reading. Early layers encode position densely; later layers compress position to a 2D sheet. Interpretability is clearest in middle layers, where position is decodable but not dominant.
Done right: multihead interpretation. By reading attention weights directly from multiple heads, the paper resolves ambiguity and shows the exact archetype recipe the model computed.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a sparse autoencoder on DINOv2 activations (take 1 million ImageNet images, extract patch tokens from one layer, learn 32,000 concept atoms). Then, for a held-out test image: (1) Get the learned archetype vectors. (2) For each patch token in that image, compute the convex mixture coefficients by solving a least-squares problem with the constraint that all coefficients sum to 1 and stay non-negative. (3) For each patch, plot the top 5 archetypes and their percentages. (4) Verify that the reconstruction loss (using only the top archetypes) is low—archetypes account for most of the variation. (5) Show that tokens near each other in the image have similar archetype recipes, proving the representation is smooth and interpretable.

reconstruction_error = mean(||token - (c_1*archetype_1 + c_2*archetype_2 + ... + c_k*archetype_k)||^2) using top-k archetypes

The demo must show: archetypes are reused consistently across the image, a small number of archetypes (10-20) reconstructs most tokens correctly, and tokens at similar locations have nearly identical archetype recipes.
