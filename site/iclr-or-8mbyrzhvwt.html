CounselBench: Expert Evaluation of LLMs in Mental Health

A benchmark that makes mental-health AI errors visible by having real therapists rate LLM answers and crafting adversarial questions to trigger specific failure modes.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Mental health advice is different from medical facts — it is messy, personal, and requires judgment about what a real person needs at this moment. LLMs can sound confident even when they are harmful, and simple tests miss the real problems. This paper fixes that by recruiting 100 licensed therapists to rate LLM answers on 2,000 real patient questions and then inventing tricky questions designed to break each model's weaknesses.

Why rating mental health answers is hard

Most benchmarks test medical facts — 'What is the treatment for diabetes?' — where there is a right answer. Mental health is not like that. A person asks 'My partner ignores me when I am sad' and a helpful answer must weigh many things at once: is this person in danger, what do they actually need to hear, have they tried talking first, do they have support. An LLM can generate something that sounds kind but misses the person entirely or even gives advice that a real therapist would never give. The problem is invisible until someone trained to spot it reads carefully.

Today, we rate LLMs on multiple-choice tests or fact retrieval. That leaves open-ended mental health untouched. A simple metric like 'did it sound nice?' misses safety red flags (telling someone to 'just think positive' when they need medical care), overgeneralization (applying one person's story to all people), and lack of personalization (giving cookie-cutter advice that ignores what the person actually said). Even worse, other LLMs judge LLM answers and consistently overestimate quality — they miss exactly the problems a human therapist catches.

The move. Recruit 100 real therapists to rate LLM answers on authentic patient questions, then craft adversarial questions that deliberately trigger known failure modes to catch them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect 2,000 real questions

Take patient questions from a public forum (CounselChat) that cover 20 mental health topics.

Ask four models to answer

GPT-4, LLaMA 3, Gemini, and a human therapist each answer the same question.

Have 100 therapists rate

Each therapist rates each answer on six clinical dimensions (safety, consistency, personalization, clarity, empathy, relevance) and marks specific problems with quotes.

Find what breaks each model

Analyze which errors appear most often for each model (overgeneralization, unsafe advice, cookie-cutter response).

Write adversarial questions

Therapists author new questions specifically designed to trigger those known weaknesses, then rate all 9 LLMs on them.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts the object of measurement from multiple-choice facts to open-ended response quality. Instead of asking 'does the model know this fact?', it asks 'did this answer actually help a real person and avoid harm?' — because mental health is about judgment in context, not just recalling information. The evaluation framework (the six dimensions) is the new object — a rubric that captures what therapists actually care about: not just being correct, but being safe, personalized, and honest about limitations.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Response safety and clinical fitness

measured quantity  =  Clinically grounded response fitness

The paper measures something that no benchmark before has isolated: whether an LLM-generated answer is clinically safe and fits the person's actual needs. This is not just accuracy. It is a blend of six dimensions: (1) safety (does it avoid giving dangerous medical advice?), (2) consistency (does it contradict itself or medical knowledge?), (3) personalization (does it treat this person as an individual or give cookie-cutter advice?), (4) clarity (is the advice actually understandable?), (5) empathy (does it acknowledge the person's feelings?), (6) relevance (does it address what they actually asked?). A response can score high on consistency (it is factually true) but low on personalization (it ignores the person's specific situation). The paper estimates this by having five licensed therapists independently rate each response on each dimension using a 1-5 scale, then averaging.

The hidden quantity becomes visible through span-level annotation — therapists mark the exact phrases that fail. For example, a response marked 'unsafe' has the risky sentence highlighted. This creates a precise record of what went wrong and where, not just a number.

Why it works

Why this catches what other benchmarks miss

Traditional benchmarks measure whether a model knows facts. This benchmark measures whether a model is safe and useful in a complex, real-world task. The insight is that you cannot detect this from the model's internal behavior — you have to ask real judges. And you cannot just ask any judge; the judge must understand what makes a mental health answer dangerous or unhelpful. A non-expert might think 'tell the person to meditate' is good advice; a therapist knows it is often premature and dismissive. By recruiting licensed practitioners and giving them a shared rubric, the paper creates a 'ground truth' about response quality that a test set with one right answer cannot capture.

The second mechanism is adversarial: once you know how models fail (e.g., 'LLaMA gives unsafe medical advice,' 'Gemini overgeneralizes'), you can write new questions specifically crafted to trigger that failure in all models at once. This is a stress test. It forces models into their known weaknesses and reveals which ones are mode-specific fragilities (all models fail on this question) versus model-specific ones (only LLaMA fails). This makes the failure modes systematic and comparable, not random noise, because you control exactly what the question asks and measure the exact error that results — no randomness in which questions the model sees or how it interprets them.

The scoring formula

First in words:

For each response and each dimension, the score is the average of the five independent therapist ratings.

Then the compact form:

dimension_score = (r1 + r2 + r3 + r4 + r5) / 5

Every symbol:

dimension_scoreaverage rating for one dimension (e.g., safety) on one response
r1...r5individual ratings from five therapists, each from 1 (bad) to 5 (good)
(r1 + r2 + r3 + r4 + r5) / 5the mean of the five independent judgments
A tiny worked example
Response from GPT-4 to 'I am always anxious' on the Safety dimension:
Therapist 1 rates it 4 (mostly safe, one minor overstatement).
Therapist 2 rates it 5 (no risks detected).
Therapist 3 rates it 4 (safe, but misses one nuance).
Therapist 4 rates it 5 (completely safe).
Therapist 5 rates it 3 (one phrase could encourage self-diagnosis without medical consultation).
Safety score = (4 + 5 + 4 + 5 + 3) / 5 = 21 / 5 = 4.2
For Personalization, same response:
Therapist 1 rates it 1 (completely generic, ignores the person's situation).
Therapist 2 rates it 2 (applies to many people, not just this one).
Therapist 3 rates it 1 (cookie-cutter).
Therapist 4 rates it 2 (some acknowledgment of context).
Therapist 5 rates it 1 (treats all anxiety as the same).
Personalization score = (1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 1) / 5 = 7 / 5 = 1.4
The response is safe but impersonal. A different model might score 3.0 on Safety but 3.8 on Personalization — different strengths and weaknesses.

By averaging multiple independent experts on the same response, random disagreements wash out and systematic patterns (this response is unsafe, this one is generic) become visible.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

LLM judges are unreliable. When the paper asked GPT-4 to rate other LLM responses, GPT-4 overestimated how good they were. It gave near-perfect scores to responses that human therapists flagged as unsafe or impersonal. The paper found that LLM judges uniformly assigned the lowest safety scores even to harmful content. This is a real problem because it means you cannot use a cheaper LLM-as-judge to run the benchmark at scale — you need humans.
Adversarial questions test edge cases, not typical performance. The adversarial component (CounselBench-Adv) is specifically designed to make models fail. It reveals weaknesses but does not represent how the model behaves on most patient questions. A model could pass the regular benchmark and still fail the adversarial one, or vice versa.
Therapist disagreement on edge cases. For some responses (e.g., advice that is clinically true but blunt), therapists gave very different ratings. Averaging five raters smooths this by washing out individual preference, but the underlying ambiguity remains — the benchmark cannot fully resolve whether harsh-but-honest is better or worse than gentle-but-vague because this is a genuine open question in practice (therapists truly disagree) and the benchmark can only measure what the average view is.
Done right. The benchmark is done right by anchoring to clinically trained raters, enforcing credential verification, giving raters a shared rubric, collecting span-level annotations (not just scores), and separating evaluation-set performance from adversarial failure-mode testing.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should take a new patient question (one not in the benchmark) and ask two LLMs to answer it. Then ask two mock raters (using the six-dimension rubric) to grade each response on each dimension. The output should show: which model scored higher on which dimensions, where the raters disagreed, and which specific phrases prompted the lowest scores. The demo should make it clear that this is not a single 'accuracy' number — it is a profile of strengths (this model is empathetic) and weaknesses (this model is unsafe). If you then use an adversarial question (e.g., a question designed to make models give medical advice without disclaimers), the demo should show both models' failure modes clearly.

quality_profile = [safety_score, consistency_score, personalization_score, clarity_score, empathy_score, relevance_score]; model_A_quality_profile vs model_B_quality_profile

The impossible-to-miss takeaway is that LLMs score very differently across dimensions — one model can be safe but impersonal, another personable but risky — and a single number cannot capture what matters for mental health.
