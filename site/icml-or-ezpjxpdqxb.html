MarketSim: Stock Markets from Generative Agents

Simulating realistic stock markets by running thousands of AI agents as realistic traders in a live auction, grounded in real news and economic data.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Stock prices swing based on millions of traders making decisions as news arrives, earnings reports surprise, and policies shift. Building a computer model that captures how real markets actually move is hard—you can either capture the surface rules (the auction mechanism) or the traders' reasoning, but not both realistically. MarketSim solves this by running thousands of generative AI agents as traders inside a realistic market microstructure, each reading real news and deciding whether to buy or sell, and letting their combined interactions shape the price. The result is a test bed where researchers can watch how different scenarios (sudden news, policy shifts, earnings surprises) reshape market behavior.

Zero assumption version

Simulating markets with realistic traders and realistic price rules

Stock market simulators face a hard choice. On one side, you can build a realistic auction mechanism—the rules that match buy and sell orders, the way prices move tick by tick—but fill it with dummy traders who follow simple rules (e.g. 'buy if price drops 5 percent'). The result looks mechanically real but misses how actual traders reason. On the other side, you can give agents smart reasoning about news and policy, but then you lose the realism of how orders actually execute, how one trader's action ripples to the next second's price.

Researchers and regulators need a simulator that is faithful on both fronts: traders who think like real traders (reading news, weighing risk, adapting to surprises) and a market that works like the real NASDAQ (orders matched in microseconds, prices emerging from supply and demand). Building both at once is expensive and complex.

The naive approach: Either fake traders with simple rules, or smart traders in a toy market—not both together.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Load real data

Feed the simulator 12,000+ actual news articles, earnings reports, policy documents, timestamped and realistic.

Spawn agents

Create 15,000 AI traders (using generative LLMs), each with a unique investor profile (aggressive, cautious, technical trader, long-term holder, etc.).

Agent reads and decides

Each agent reads the morning's news, current holdings, and market state, then reasons whether to buy, sell, or hold any stock in its portfolio.

Orders hit the auction

All agents' decisions become buy and sell orders that enter a realistic continuous double auction (like real NASDAQ)—orders matched in nanosecond time steps.

Price emerges, repeat

Matched orders execute, prices update, agents see new prices and news, repeat many times per second for a full trading day.

What the paper changes

Decoupling reasoning from execution

The single key insight is to separate two things that usually happen together: an agent's decision (should I buy Tesla?) from the mechanics of that decision hitting the market (matching my order with other traders' orders at the best price). A traditional simulator mixes these—agents decide and orders execute all at once. MarketSim splits them: first, a strategic layer (agent reasoning at a slower timescale, reading news and re-evaluating holdings) and second, an execution layer (orders flowing into a fast, realistic auction that runs at nanosecond resolution). This separation lets you run smart agents without slowing down the market mechanics.

The hidden quantity

The alignment between agent behavior and real market stylized facts

measured quantity  =  behavioral fidelity score

The paper's core measurement is how well the simulator reproduces known patterns that all real stock markets exhibit—stylized facts. Real markets show five key patterns: prices follow a random walk (no predictable drift), volatility clusters (quiet periods, then sudden choppiness), returns have heavy tails (crashes happen more often than a bell curve predicts), volume and volatility move together, and correlations between stocks shift over time. If your simulator can match these five patterns, you know the agents are behaving realistically enough that their interactions create genuine market dynamics, not toy behavior.

MarketSim measures this alignment by running simulation scenarios, extracting the same statistical patterns (volatility clustering, tail thickness, volume-volatility correlation) from the simulated prices, and comparing them to real market data. The closer the match, the more confident you are that the behavioral layer is faithful.

Why it works

Why splitting strategy from execution makes agents behave realistically

The core reason this works is that real traders decide and execute on different timescales. A human fund manager reads the morning news (takes minutes to hours) and decides to reallocate a portfolio, but the actual order execution (matching buy and sell signals to concrete ticks on the exchange) happens microseconds after. Squashing both timescales together breaks realism: agents cannot think strategically if they have to also manage the fast microstructure details. By decoupling, MarketSim gives agents space to reason deeply about news and holdings (strategic layer) while letting a realistic auction mechanism handle the microsecond-level order matching (execution layer).

This separation also creates emergent behavior. Since many agents are reasoning independently about the same news, they often reach similar conclusions (buy Apple after earnings beat) and post orders at similar times. In a real market, this clustering of decision-making creates the stylized facts—correlated trading spikes, demand shocks that move price further than a mechanical formula would predict, feedback loops where price moves trigger more trading. A simulator with fake traders never captures this because they do not reason; they just follow a rule. By running real reasoning (via LLMs) in a realistic structure, MarketSim allows these emergent patterns to arise naturally from agent interactions.

The price update and behavioral alignment

First in words:

stylized_fact_fidelity equals average absolute percentage error between simulated price path and real market metrics

Then the compact form:

MAPE = (1/N) * sum_t |observed_price_t - simulated_price_t| / |observed_price_t| * 100

Every symbol:

observed_price_tactual historical price at time t
simulated_price_tprice from MarketSim at equivalent time t
Ntotal number of time steps in the scenario
MAPEmean absolute percentage error, expressed as percent
A tiny worked example
Real market: stock closes day 1 at $100. MarketSim simulation: $102. Error: |100-102|/100 = 0.02 = 2%.
Day 2 real: $105. Simulated: $106. Error: |105-106|/105 ≈ 1%.
Day 3 real: $98. Simulated: $96. Error: |98-96|/98 ≈ 2%.
Average MAPE over 3 days: (2% + 1% + 2%) / 3 ≈ 1.67%. MarketSim's reported 3.48% MAPE means on average the simulated price sits within 3-4% of real observed prices over the test scenario.

Lower MAPE means the agent-driven simulation tracks real price dynamics more closely, validating that behavioral fidelity translates to mechanical accuracy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

LLM agents hallucinate or misread news. If an agent misinterprets earnings data or invents a market detail that is not in the news, its trading decision will be wrong. This breaks fidelity. Mitigation: ground agents in structured data (extract key metrics from earnings reports via code) rather than letting LLMs freely interpret raw text.
Agents all decide the same way, creating artificial unanimity. If every agent reads the same news and the LLM always gives the same reasoning, all 15,000 traders will pile into the same trade (all buying Apple simultaneously), creating a demand shock that is too large and unrealistic. Real markets have diversity: some traders are greedy, some cautious, some contrarian. Mitigation: explicitly seed agents with diverse personas and risk profiles before running the model on new data.
Scenarios are not grounded in real dates and sequences. If the paper feeds agents news in the wrong order or mixes dates, the simulated trading will not map to real market behavior on those dates. The 3.48% MAPE claim only holds if the scenarios align with real sequences.
Done right. Agent decisions are grounded in structured, real-world data (timestamps, sector, company name, event type) and agents have strong persona diversity; scenarios run in real historical sequence and are validated against real price paths.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo should run MarketSim on a single day of real market data (e.g., a Tesla earnings announcement on a specific date) with a smaller population (500 agents instead of 15,000 to save compute) and show three things: (1) agents reading real news make diverse decisions (some buy, some sell, some hold); (2) orders flow into an order book and match in realistic ways (bids and asks narrowing the spread); (3) the day's simulated price path stays within a few percent of the real price path for that stock on that date. If all three hold, you have proof that the behavioral and structural layers are working together.

fidelity = 100 - MAPE_on_demo_day; behavioral_diversity = (number_of_buy_decisions - number_of_sell_decisions) / total_agent_decisions

The demo must show that LLM agents reasoning from real news create realistic trading diversity and that diversity plus real auction mechanics reproduce real price swings.
