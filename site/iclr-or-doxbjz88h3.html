Designing Experiments to Tell What Neurons Actually Encode

Use information theory to find the exact tasks that reveal whether neurons encode raw sensory patterns or knowledge-adjusted patterns.

Brains solve problems under uncertainty, combining what they see with what they already know. Two competing theories disagree on how neurons store this knowledge: one says they store raw sensory patterns only, the other says they store the combination. This paper shows how to design experiments that definitively prove which theory is right.

Zero assumption version

How do neurons store uncertainty?

Imagine you hear a faint noise in the dark. Your brain combines two things: what you actually hear right now (the raw data) plus what you already know about the world (a faint noise is more likely a car horn than a chainsaw). Two competing theories explain how neurons store this knowledge. The raw-pattern theory says neurons write down only the sensory data, like a camera recording what you hear. The knowledge-adjusted theory says neurons write down the final guess that combines data with what you know, like a weatherman combining raw measurements with historical patterns.

A naive experiment would play sounds and ask subjects to guess what made them. But both theories would pass this test equally well, because both make humans answer correctly. The real problem is that no one knows how to design an experiment where the two theories predict different answers. The paper solves this: it finds the exact task design that makes each theory wrong in a distinctive way.

In one sentence. Find the specific tasks where raw-pattern-coding and knowledge-adjusted-pattern-coding neurons give contradictory answers.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Set up two competing hypotheses

Likelihood encoding (raw sensory patterns only) versus posterior encoding (sensory plus prior knowledge).

Build a mismatch test

Design neural network decoders that look for one kind of information and measure error when each decoder tries to read the wrong thing.

Calculate the information gap

Compute the expected difference in decoder error when likelihood decoder reads likelihood-coded neurons versus when it reads posterior-coded neurons, using KL divergence math.

Search for maximum gap

Vary the task parameters (prior odds, stimulus distribution, contrast level) to find the task design that widens the gap most.

Run the optimal experiment

Collect actual neural data under that task design; which decoder wins shows which coding hypothesis the real neurons use.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the task stimulus distribution—the mix and probability of sensory patterns subjects see during an experiment. Instead of using a generic, neutral task that both theories pass equally, it finds the exact distribution that creates a gap where each theory predicts different neural responses. A standard experiment might show orientation gratings equally often; this paper reweights them so that some orientations become rare in one context and common in another, forcing the learned world-knowledge (prior, what the brain already knew before the task started) to push harder against the sensory data.

the object it moves  =  task stimulus distribution
The hidden quantity

The information gap—measuring mismatch between theories

measured quantity  =  expected decoder performance difference

The information gap is the expected performance gap when you try to read the wrong kind of information from neurons. If a population actually encodes likelihood functions (raw sensory patterns), a decoder designed to read likelihood will do well, but a decoder looking for posterior information will fail with high error. The gap is the difference in their errors. Measuring it tells you which hypothesis the neurons are actually using.

The paper estimates this gap by computing how much a task-marginalized surrogate posterior (what the true posterior looks like when you average over all possible tasks) differs from the true posterior. That difference, measured as KL divergence (a distance metric between two probability distributions), predicts decoder performance differences across all task settings.

Why it works

Why it works: the mismatch forces the truth out

The mechanism is simple: if neurons truly encode likelihood (raw patterns), then asking them a posterior question fails. The decoder looking for posterior information gets the wrong thing and suffers high error. The reverse happens with posterior-coding neurons. By designing the task so the two theories disagree maximally, you guarantee that actual neural data will show which decoder wins, proving which coding scheme the brain uses.

The paper optimizes by varying two knobs. First, it changes the prior knowledge (context): maybe in one half of the experiment, the world tends to have high-contrast stimuli, and in the other half, low-contrast. Neurons that truly encode the posterior will fire differently in each context (because prior changes), but likelihood-coding neurons stay the same. Second, it shifts the stimulus distribution within each context. Some orientations become rarer in context A, commoner in context B. This forces the prior to push against the sensory data more aggressively. Together, these moves widen the gap between what likelihood and posterior decoders predict, making the experiment maximally informative.

Math after the picture

The information gap equation

First in words:

Information gap is the expected cross-entropy loss difference between the optimal posterior decoder and the optimal likelihood decoder, derived from the KL divergence between the true posterior and a task-averaged surrogate posterior.

Then the compact form:

Delta_info_L = E[CE_loss(g_P, r_L)] - E[CE_loss(g_L, r_L)] = E[KL(p_true | p_marginal)]

Every symbol:

Delta_info_Lthe information gap for likelihood-coding hypothesis
CE_loss(g_P, r_L)cross-entropy loss when posterior decoder g_P reads likelihood-coded neurons r_L
CE_loss(g_L, r_L)cross-entropy loss when likelihood decoder g_L reads likelihood-coded neurons r_L
KL(p_true | p_marginal)KL divergence between true posterior and task-marginalized surrogate posterior
E[·]expected value averaged over all trials
A tiny worked example
Imagine a likelihood-coding neuron population with true likelihood encoding.
A likelihood decoder tries to extract orientation: 100 trials, error = 0.2 (good match).
A posterior decoder tries to extract orientation adjusted by prior: 100 trials, error = 1.8 (bad mismatch).
Information gap = 1.8 - 0.2 = 1.6 (strong signal that neurons encode likelihood).
Now test a posterior-coding population: likelihood decoder error = 1.9, posterior decoder error = 0.3.
Information gap = 0.3 - 1.9 = -1.6 (strong signal in opposite direction, proving posterior coding).
The task that achieves this ±1.6 gap is the one the paper finds by maximizing KL divergence.

The KL divergence between true and marginal posteriors tells you how hard the prior pulls against the sensory data; larger divergence means larger gap, so maximizing divergence finds the task design with maximum experiment discriminative power.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Decoders are too weak If the neural network decoders are poorly trained or under-parameterized, they fail to extract either kind of information well, washing out the gap. You must fit the decoders carefully and validate that they achieve near-optimal performance on synthetic ground-truth data before running the actual experiment.
Task design is not realized in the lab The optimal task distribution is calculated mathematically, but in a real experiment the context priors or stimulus distributions might drift or not match the theory. If the prior is weaker than designed, the gap shrinks and the experiment loses power.
Neurons encode a mixture of both Real neural populations might partly encode likelihood and partly encode posterior. The information gap still works, but the gap becomes smaller and the experiment needs more trials to reach a conclusion.
Stimulus contrast is mismatched The paper shows that optimal task design depends on stimulus contrast level. If the lab uses a different contrast than the theory predicts, the gap may not reach its theoretical maximum and the experiment power degrades.
Done right Carefully calibrate decoders on synthetic data; measure and match the actual prior odds and stimulus distribution in the behavioral task to the mathematical design; scale contrast to match the theory; collect enough trials to resolve the gap signal reliably.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

The demo should simulate a two-context orientation discrimination task with either likelihood-coding neurons or posterior-coding neurons (ground truth). Then fit two decoders: one trained to extract orientation from the neural responses, and one trained to extract orientation adjusted by the context prior. The demo sweeps the context frequencies and stimulus distributions, computing the information gap at each step, and finds the parameters that maximize it. Finally, it measures decoder performance on held-out neural data and shows that the decoder matching the true encoding wins by the predicted amount.

information gap = cross-entropy loss of mismatched decoder - cross-entropy loss of matched decoder

The core claim is this: when you run the optimized task, the decoder matching the true neural encoding outperforms the other by the predicted gap, because the task design amplifies the coding difference so that one decoder's assumptions match the neural firing patterns while the other's do not. If you reverse the ground truth from likelihood to posterior coding, the winner flips and the gap sign reverses, proving the method discriminates.
