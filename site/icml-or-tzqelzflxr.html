The sum-of-exponentials bottleneck

Many real optimization problems need to reason about multiple possibilities at once. For instance, deciding how to transport goods from warehouses to stores, or building a model that hedges against many possible futures. These problems use a mathematical tool called LogSumExp—it takes the exponential of each option, sums them all, and takes the logarithm.

The problem: when you have thousands or infinitely many options, computing the gradient (which way to nudge the answer to improve) requires touching every single term, because each term contributes a piece to the total signal. That is impossibly slow. So people resort to approximations that throw information away, making the final answer worse.

In one sentence. The paper swaps in a new formula that keeps the original properties but only touches a fraction of the terms.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the LogSumExp formula with a gentler version that achieves the same goal but uses fewer calculations. Specifically, it modifies the KL divergence (a measure of how different two probability patterns are) by adding safeguards so that the gradient (the nudge signal) stays clean and fast even with massive numbers of options.

the object it moves  =  LogSumExp approximation
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: how many terms to touch per update

measured quantity  =  Term-count per update step

Every time the optimizer wants to nudge a parameter, it computes a gradient—a signal showing which direction improves the answer. With the original LogSumExp, that gradient must touch every exponential term because the mathematical formula includes all of them. With the substitute formula, a random (randomly sampled) gradient can touch only a small subset and still give a good signal, because the dropped terms were negligible contributors.

The paper measures how many term-touches are needed per gradient step. The original method: all N terms per step. The substitute method: roughly sqrt(N) or a constant number, depending on the problem.

Why it works

Why it works: trading off scale and stability

The original LogSumExp is defined by taking each option's score, applying exponential, summing them all, and taking logarithm. When the scores are large, computing each exponential overflows. When there are millions of terms, it is just too slow.

The paper's Safe KL divergence cuts out the long tail. It says: large scores (hot terms, important options) matter; tiny scores (free terms, negligible options) can be ignored without breaking the math. By summing only the important terms and adjusting for the ones left out, the formula stays convex (no hidden valleys) and smooth (no sharp kinks). This means random (random-sample based) gradient descent still converges reliably even though it is only touching a fraction of the data.

The reason it works: the neglected terms were already contributing almost nothing to the gradient anyway. Dropping them does not lose information because in the tail of an exponential pattern, each added term adds less signal; it just saves CPU. The safeguards ensure that the solver still points in the right direction because they preserve the mathematical properties that make gradients reliable.

The Safe KL divergence formula

First in words:

The LogSumExp is approximated by summing exponentials only above a safety threshold, then adding a correction term that accounts for the dropped mass.

Then the compact form:

Safe-LogSumExp(x) = log(sum_i exp(x_i * I[x_i > threshold]) + epsilon * exp(threshold))

Every symbol:

x_ithe score or weight of option i
I[x_i > threshold]an indicator: 1 if x_i exceeds the threshold, 0 otherwise
thresholdthe minimum score to include in the sum
epsilona small adjustment for the dropped-out mass
Safe-LogSumExp(x)the approximate value, fast to compute
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 5 options with scores: x_1=10, x_2=8, x_3=3, x_4=2, x_5=1.
Original formula: log(exp(10) + exp(8) + exp(3) + exp(2) + exp(1)) = log(22026 + 2981 + 20 + 7 + 2) ≈ 10.02.
Set threshold=5 (only big scores matter). Safe KL includes only x_1 and x_2.
Safe-LogSumExp ≈ log(exp(10) + exp(8) + correction) ≈ log(22026 + 2981 + 29) ≈ 10.00.
The results are almost identical (10.02 vs 10.00) but computation is 3x faster: only 2 exponentials instead of 5.

The safety threshold ensures we drop only negligible terms while keeping all the signal.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Threshold set too high. If you drop too many terms, the gradient becomes too noisy and the solver converges slowly or stops.
Correction factor forgotten. If you drop terms without adjusting epsilon, the formula loses its smooth convex shape and the solver hits kinks.
Problem structure mismatch. Some problems have uniform option weights (no clear high/low split). The Safe KL is less of a win there.
Done right. With a data-driven threshold and careful correction, the method scales well to thousands of options while preserving convergence guarantees.
