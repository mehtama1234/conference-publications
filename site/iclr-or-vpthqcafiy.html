Removing LayerNorm and Why Models Still Work

You can delete all the normalization layers from a language model and it still answers questions nearly as well, which means those layers do not play a necessary role in the task.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Large language models use layers called LayerNorm to keep internal numbers from growing too wild during training. But once the model is finished, nobody knew if these layers still mattered for answering questions. This paper removes every normalization layer from GPT-2 and fine-tunes the model briefly on a tiny dataset. The result: the model keeps working, barely losing accuracy, which suggests LayerNorm was a training crutch, not a necessary part of the final machine.

Zero assumption version

A tool for training that stays in the final machine

LayerNorm is a mathematical tool that rescales numbers inside a model during training. Think of it like a governor on an engine: it keeps intermediate values from ballooning and exploding. When you build a neural network without it, training becomes shaky and unstable—the model's internal state can wild-swing and never settle. So engineers always add LayerNorm.

But here is the puzzle: when the model finishes training and you ask it a real question (which is called inference—when the finished model answers a new question), LayerNorm is just sitting there, still doing its rescaling job. Nobody had actually tested whether it was still needed. Maybe the weights had learned to work without it by then. Or maybe it is genuinely preventing disaster on every forward pass. The problem for researchers trying to understand how these models work is that LayerNorm adds extra complexity and nonlinearity, making it harder to trace what each piece of the model is actually doing. It is a roadblock to mechanistic interpretability—the goal of opening the model's hood and seeing exactly how it reasons.

In one sentence. The paper removes all LayerNorm layers from GPT-2, fine-tunes briefly on a sliver of data, and finds the model still works—showing LayerNorm is not essential at inference time.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Replace each LayerNorm

Swap out each LayerNorm layer with a fake version (FakeLN) initialized to mimic it, using a fixed rescale factor.

Step 2: Remove LayerNorms one by one

Delete one FakeLN at a time, starting from the middle of the model, and immediately fine-tune on a tiny dataset (a fraction of original training data) for a few steps.

Step 3: Stabilize the numbers

Add an auxiliary loss that gently penalizes wild swings in value magnitudes across all word positions, keeping internal values smooth.

Step 4: Repeat systematically

Proceed layer by layer, always fine-tuning after each deletion, until all LayerNorms are gone.

Step 5: Test the interpretability payoff

Now test whether methods to understand the model (like attribution patching and tracing direct effects) work more cleanly on the LayerNorm-free version.

What the paper changes

The single thing the paper changes

LayerNorm layers. The paper deletes all of them and replaces them with a simpler linear transformation (FakeLN) that stays frozen.

the object it moves  =  LayerNorm layers
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: activation scale consistency across positions

measured quantity  =  magnitude stability (how evenly scaled are values across positions)

In a language model, each token (a piece of text like a word or punctuation) gets processed by multiple layers. At each layer, the numbers attached to that token (called activations or hidden states) can vary wildly in size. LayerNorm forces these sizes to stay in a predictable band. When you remove LayerNorm, the model's internal numbers no longer have that enforced consistency.

The paper measures this by tracking the L2 norm (roughly, the size or length of the internal number-list) for each token position. Normally, in models with LayerNorm, the very first token in a sequence has an abnormally large norm—sometimes 10 times bigger than later tokens. This weird spike is a symptom of LayerNorm constantly rescaling things. The paper shows that removing LayerNorm eliminates this spike; all tokens end up with much more uniform, moderate sizes. That consistency is the hidden quantity the model learns to maintain all by itself, even without explicit normalization.

Why it works

Why the paper's method works: learning to self-regulate

When you simply delete LayerNorm, the model collapses because the training weights were wired to expect that rescaling to happen. Internal values spiral and computation breaks. The paper sidesteps this by replacing each LayerNorm with FakeLN—a frozen linear rescale that starts out mimicking the original LayerNorm. The model sees familiar rescaling at first.

Then, layer by layer, the paper deletes FakeLN and immediately fine-tunes on a small dataset. During fine-tuning, the model's weights adjust slightly to compensate. The auxiliary loss helps by penalizing any wildly uneven norms across token positions, gently steering the model toward self-regulating activations. Over many small tweaks across all layers, the model discovers that it can keep its internal numbers in a steady band without the explicit normalization—it has learned a natural equilibrium.

Why does this work? Because the original training process already made the model stable enough. LayerNorm was added for stability during training because the learning process (the incremental adjustments to weights, guided by comparing predictions to real answers) was noisy and erratic. But at inference time, when you are just running the forward computation deterministically, that instability is not happening. The model can actually function without the safety net, and with a tiny bit of fine-tuning, it adapts to do exactly that.

Math after the picture

The equation: a penalty on uneven activation sizes

First in words:

The auxiliary loss is: for the final LayerNorm, measure the standard deviation (spread) of magnitudes across all token positions, excluding the first position, and penalize if that spread is too large.

Then the compact form:

L_aux = lambda * ||sigma(positions) - sigma_avg||^2

Every symbol:

sigma(positions)the standard deviation of activation magnitudes at each position
sigma_avgthe average standard deviation across positions (excluding first and end tokens)
lambdaa weight controlling how strongly to push for consistent magnitudes
L_auxthe penalty added to the training loss
A tiny worked example
Position 1 (first token): activation norm = 600
Position 2 (next token): activation norm = 320
Position 3 (next token): activation norm = 310
Spread = standard deviation of these = roughly 150 (big difference between first and rest)
Target average spread for normal positions (pos 2-3) = roughly 5 (very consistent)
Penalty = 10 * (150 - 5)^2 = 10 * 21,025 = 210,250 (large penalty pushes model to flatten the spread)
After a few training steps with this penalty, positions 2-3 might grow toward 400, and position 1 shrinks toward 350 (all more equal).
Now spread = roughly 25, penalty = 10 * (25 - 5)^2 = 4,000 (much smaller, model learns to balance)

The auxiliary loss nudges the model to keep activation sizes roughly equal across all token positions, which is what LayerNorm did implicitly; the model learns to self-regulate instead of relying on explicit rescaling.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Removing LayerNorm all at once causes immediate collapse. Deleting LayerNorm without replacement or fine-tuning breaks the model catastrophically. You must start with FakeLN and remove incrementally.
Removing in the wrong order destabilizes training. The paper finds that removing MLP LayerNorms first (the middle of the model) works better than other orders, because they interact less with the mechanism that routes information between positions (the multi-head mechanism that lets each word see which other words matter). Removing those early causes larger loss spikes.
Skipping the auxiliary loss causes wild activation swings. Without the norm-stability penalty, some training runs spiral into unrecoverable high loss. The auxiliary loss is not optional; it is essential for stable fine-tuning.
Fine-tuning on too little data does not adapt weights enough. The paper shows the amount of fine-tuning data needed grows sublinearly with model size, but it is never zero. Without enough adaptation steps, the model keeps making LayerNorm-dependent decisions.
Done right. Methodically remove LayerNorms one at a time, always fine-tune immediately after, use the auxiliary loss to steer toward norm consistency, and validate that loss increases by only a small margin (under 0.05 units of prediction error, scaled for GPT-2 XL).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small GPT-2 model from scratch. Train it on a toy dataset (like a subset of WikiText). Remove all LayerNorm layers using the paper's protocol (replace, remove one by one, fine-tune each step, use the auxiliary loss). Measure the validation loss before and after. Then run the model on a simple prompt like a news headline start, and show that the next-token predictions are nearly identical between the original and LayerNorm-free versions. The demo should show (a) the loss increase over the course of LayerNorm removal (a graph of loss per removal step), (b) a side-by-side table of token probabilities for the same prompt, and (c) the L2 norm of activations across token positions (visualizing that first-token inflation vanishes).

loss_increase = loss_final - loss_original; token_prediction_match = fraction of top-5 next tokens that match between original and LayerNorm-free

The demo must make impossible to miss that removing LayerNorm does not break the model; it merely shifts how activations regulate themselves, and predictions stay nearly identical.
