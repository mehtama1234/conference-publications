Incorporating Expert Priors into Bayesian Optimization via Dynamic Mean Decay

Gradually fade out expert hints so a search algorithm escapes misleading hints and finds the true best setting.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Bayesian optimization is a method for finding the best setting (like the best hyperparameters for training) by learning which regions of the search space are promising. When domain experts have hints about where to look first, existing methods either rely too heavily on those hints and fail if they are wrong, or ignore them entirely. DynMeanBO uses a dynamic decay mechanism that starts by believing the expert hints strongly, then gradually forgets them and returns to unguided search, allowing the algorithm to escape bad hints and still gain speed when hints are correct.

Zero assumption version

Expert hints can mislead an algorithm

In black-box optimization, you want to find the setting that makes your objective function (e.g., test accuracy, cost efficiency) as high or low as possible, but you can only evaluate a few settings because each evaluation is expensive. Bayesian optimization solves this by building a statistical model of the function as it learns, then uses that model to pick the next setting to try.

When you have a domain expert (someone who knows the problem), they often have hunches about where good settings lie. A naive approach is to tell the algorithm I trust these regions most and search there first. But if the expert is wrong, this fails: the algorithm gets locked into searching only bad regions and misses the true best setting.

Existing methods for using expert knowledge either bake it in so rigidly that a wrong hint ruins the search, or they treat the hint as optional and never really use it. There is no middle ground that says: use the hint to start faster, but gradually stop trusting it if it seems wrong. DynMeanBO fills that gap by letting the hint guide early iterations (when speed matters most) but fading it away as the algorithm learns from data, so late-stage exploration is not hijacked by old assumptions.

In one sentence. Use expert hints to speed up search early because early iterations set the tone for where the algorithm looks, then fade the hints away so the algorithm explores freely if they are misleading and can escape local traps.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the Gaussian process mean function, the statistical model's belief about the average function value across the search space. Instead of starting with a flat neutral mean, the paper starts with a learned belief about where the expert thinks good settings are, then multiplies that contribution by a decay factor that shrinks over time.

the object it moves  =  Gaussian process mean function
The hidden quantity

The decay rate: how fast to forget the hint

measured quantity  =  Expert hint influence decay schedule

The hidden quantity the paper introduces is a schedule for how much to trust the expert hint at each step. Early on, the schedule says strongly prefer the expert region; later, it says barely remember it exists. The schedule is a single number (call it a multiplier) that starts near 1 and shrinks toward 0.

The paper estimates this decay implicitly through the algorithm's design: they choose a decay mechanism (e.g., exponential decay with iteration number) that ensures the hint gradually loses power without ever permanently locking the algorithm into wrong regions. The exact decay rate determines the trade-off between gaining early speed from the hint and retaining broad exploration ability.

Why it works

Why decay lets the algorithm escape bad hints and gain speed

The mechanism works in two phases. Phase 1 (early iterations): the expert hint is strong, so the Gaussian process mean pulls the algorithm toward the expert region. This accelerates convergence when the expert is right, because the algorithm spends fewer wasted iterations in obviously bad regions. Phase 2 (late iterations): the decay multiplier shrinks, so the expert hint influence fades. By iteration 100 or 1000 (depending on the decay schedule), the hint is nearly gone, and the algorithm behaves like standard Bayesian optimization: it explores freely across the entire search space based on what it has learned, not on the possibly-wrong hint.

Why does this work? If the expert hint points to a local optimum or a completely wrong region, Phase 1 wastes some evaluations there, but Phase 2's exploration eventually escapes and finds the true global optimum. The algorithm is not locked in by the hint because the hint's voice gets smaller and smaller. Meanwhile, if the expert is right, Phase 1 gains a speed boost because those early iterations confirm the expert's hunch, allowing the algorithm to focus nearby and find the best setting faster.

Math after the picture

The decay equation

First in words:

The mean function is the expert prior scaled by a decay weight, plus a learned residual. The decay weight shrinks with iteration number.

Then the compact form:

m(x) = decay(t) * m_expert(x) + m_learned(x)

Every symbol:

m(x)predicted average value at setting x
decay(t)decay schedule that shrinks from 1.0 to 0.0 as iteration t increases
m_expert(x)expert hint: high where the expert thinks good settings are, low elsewhere
m_learned(x)residual learned from evaluations: the model's own belief after seeing data
A tiny worked example
Suppose decay(t) follows exponential decay: decay(t) = exp(-t / tau), where tau = 10 is a time constant.
At iteration t=0: decay(0) = 1.0. Expert hint contributes at full strength.
At iteration t=10: decay(10) = exp(-10/10) = 0.368. Expert hint contributes at 37% strength.
At iteration t=50: decay(50) = exp(-50/10) = 0.0067. Expert hint contributes at less than 1% strength; algorithm is nearly unguided.
If expert prior is 100 at a region and learned model adds 20, total mean = 1.0 * 100 + 20 = 120 early on, but 0.0067 * 100 + 20 = 20.67 late on (learned model dominates).

The decay factor shrinks the expert's voice from loud to silent, while the learned model gradually takes over as the algorithm discovers better regions on its own, because the model's predictions improve with each new evaluation data point.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Decay schedule mismatch If the decay schedule is too slow, the hint lingers and misleads for too long. If it is too fast, the algorithm ignores the hint before it can provide a speed boost, because the hint only saves time if it steers evaluations early when most choices remain unmade. Tuning the schedule requires some problem knowledge.
Expert hint is completely wrong If the expert points to a region far from any good setting, the early iterations waste budget. Decay mitigates this, but does not eliminate it entirely. A very wrong hint still costs some evaluations.
High-dimensional spaces Expert intuition often breaks down in high dimensions (like tuning 50+ hyperparameters). A 50-dimensional hint is often less reliable than in low dimensions because humans think in simple local patterns that rarely survive in complex high-dimensional spaces, so decay must be faster to avoid overfitting to the hint.
Done right Tune the decay time constant and the strength of the expert hint using a small validation set or historical data. Set decay to release the hint gradually over the first 20-50 percent of your evaluation budget, reserving the rest for free exploration.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A convincing demo picks a real black-box function (e.g., a hyperparameter optimization task on a small neural network, or a synthetic benchmark function with a known global optimum). Define a plausible expert prior that is either correct or biased in a controlled way. Run three algorithms: (1) standard Bayesian optimization with no hint, (2) full Bayesian optimization with the expert hint baked in permanently (the naive approach), and (3) DynMeanBO with dynamic decay. Measure convergence speed (iterations to reach a target accuracy) and final best value. The demo should show that DynMeanBO is as fast as the hint-aware method when the hint is correct (because both exploit the hint early), but outperforms it when the hint is wrong or biased (because decay lets DynMeanBO escape the bad hint while the naive method stays trapped).

convergence_speed = iteration_count_to_reach_target, robustness = final_best_value_with_wrong_hint / final_best_value_with_correct_hint

The demo must make clear that the algorithm gains speed when experts are right, loses almost nothing when experts are wrong, and gradually forgets bad hints rather than locking into them.
