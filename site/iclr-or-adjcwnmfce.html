Constrained Diffusion via Split Sampling

When a generative model needs to produce outputs that obey strict physical laws, split the sampling into two pieces that enforce the constraints one step at a time.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Generative models (trained models that can dream up new outputs by sampling) are essential tools for science, but they have a dangerous blind spot: they can dream up outputs that violate basic physical laws like energy conservation. This paper solves that by adding constraint-satisfaction built into the sampling process itself, so the model explores only physically plausible outputs.

Zero assumption version

Generative models dream up physically impossible worlds

Imagine you build a model that learns to generate realistic weather patterns or fluid flows by watching real data. The model learns to produce new scenarios that look convincing. But when physicists check the generated outputs, they find violations: a generated weather pattern that gains energy from nowhere, or a fluid flow that creates matter out of thin air. These are invisible to the model because it was trained only on what realistic data looks like, not on the deeper constraints that make reality possible.

The naive fix is to add a soft penalty: tell the model 'outputs that violate energy conservation get a small punishment during training.' But soft penalties do not enforce—they just nudge. A clever optimizer can still find outputs that satisfy the constraint just barely enough to minimize the penalty while being physically nonsensical. For applications like hurricane forecasting or molecular design, 'probably okay' is not acceptable. You need a hard guarantee that every generated sample obeys the laws of physics.

The paper's move in one sentence. Split the sampling into two alternating steps: a generative step that explores plausible outputs, and a hard constraint step that snaps the candidate back onto the physically valid set.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: the sampling trajectory

In the standard sampling algorithm, the model takes random steps guided only by the learned score (the model's guess at which direction is good). In this paper, every step is interrupted by a hard constraint operation: after each generative move, the algorithm projects the candidate onto the constraint boundary. This one change—inserting a constraint projection into each step—is what forces every final sample to be physically valid.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: distance from the constraint surface

measured quantity  =  constraint violation distance

The paper measures how far a candidate sample sits from the nearest point on the physically valid surface. This distance is zero for any valid sample and positive for violations. Unlike a soft penalty that assigns a cost, this is a geometric measurement: how many steps would you need to take to reach a safe region?

The algorithm uses this distance implicitly every step: it projects by finding the nearest point on the constraint set, which is equivalent to minimizing this distance. The paper proves mathematically that even though the sampling takes random steps, this repeated projection drives the violation distance toward zero over time.

Plain reading. Think of the constraint as a thin wall in a high-dimensional space. The algorithm measures distance to that wall. Each projection step is just 'find the closest point on the wall and go there.' Repeat enough times and you stay on the wall.
Paper reading. The constraint set C is a bounded measurable region in d-dimensional space. The algorithm solves a primal-dual optimization where the dual variable enforces the constraint via Lagrange multipliers. The projection operator is the computational core: minimizing distance to C.
Why it works

Why it works: the two-step dance

The central observation is that constraint enforcement and exploration can be separated into two tasks. Naive approaches try to do both simultaneously: walk toward plausible regions while staying valid. This creates a conflict—the model wants to go one way, the constraints want to go another, and the compromise is neither credible nor valid.

The paper's solution splits the problem: (1) Let the model take a full exploratory step as if constraints did not exist. This preserves the model's learned knowledge about what plausible outputs look like. (2) Then immediately project: find the nearest valid output, which is a well-understood optimization problem with fast algorithms. By alternating these two simple operations, the algorithm satisfies both goals without compromise. The projection is not a nudge or a suggestion; it is a deterministic jump to the valid set.

Mathematically, this works because of a classical result in optimization: alternating between two operations—one that explores, one that constrains—converges to a solution that is both plausible (according to the model) and valid (according to physics). The more steps you alternate, the tighter the solution becomes. The paper proves that this convergence is guaranteed even for non-convex constraints (like airflow equations) where most other methods fail.

A tiny worked example
Suppose we are generating a temperature field. Constraint: total heat must equal 1000 (conservation).
Current candidate has total heat 1100 (violates by 100).
Model suggests moving by +20 heat (exploring a warmer region).
Model-guided move alone: 1100 + 20 = 1120 (worse violation).
Projection: find nearest valid point to 1120 with total heat 1000.
Result: scale all values down so total becomes exactly 1000.
After projection: 1000 (zero violation, and as close to model's suggestion as possible).

The projection ensures exact satisfaction; the model term ensures exploration stays grounded in learned plausibility.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Expensive projection for complex constraints. Finding the nearest point on the constraint surface requires solving an optimization problem at every step. For high-dimensional systems or complicated physics, this can be slow. The paper mentions using augmented Lagrangian methods, which are fast but still add overhead.
Non-convex constraints are harder than convex ones. For linear constraints (like a simple balance equation), projection is fast and exact. For nonlinear constraints (like a complex fluid dynamics model), the projection may be approximate or require numerical solvers. The algorithm still works, but the guarantee weakens slightly.
Exploration can get trapped in a valid region. If the constraint surface has multiple disconnected pieces (like several separate valid scenarios), the algorithm may explore only one region. Soft penalties sometimes had an advantage here because they encouraged wandering across violated regions to find new modes.
Done right: step-by-step convergence proof. The paper provides a mathematical proof that alternation converges to sampling correctly from valid points only. This guarantee holds even for non-convex constraints, which is a significant theoretical achievement.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small diffusion model trained on 2D cloud-like shapes, then add a constraint: all generated shapes must have total mass (summed pixel brightness) between 90 and 110. Implement three methods: (1) unconstrained sampling, (2) soft penalty, (3) split alternating sampling. For each, generate 100 samples. Measure: How many samples violate the mass constraint? How realistic do the constrained samples look compared to unconstrained ones? Compute the average mass for each method and its standard deviation. The soft penalty should have many violations; the split sampling should have zero violations while looking nearly as realistic as unconstrained samples.

constraint_violation_rate = (number of samples with mass outside 90-110) / 100

The demo makes it impossible to miss why the trade-off works: soft penalties leak violations, while hard projection guarantees satisfaction because it snaps to the nearest valid point, which preserves realism better than a smooth penalty that compromises everywhere.
