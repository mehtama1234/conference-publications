MINT: Neuro-Symbolic Planning with Optimal Queries

An AI system learns which questions to ask humans about unknown objects and goals so planning succeeds faster.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When an AI tries to plan in the real world, it often hits unknown information — objects it has never seen, human goals it has not been told. Instead of guessing, an AI can ask. This paper builds a system that learns to ask the right questions at the right time so it needs the fewest human answers while still reaching expert-level planning success.

Zero assumption version

Planning with unknowns breaks quickly

In the real world, an AI planning agent does not know everything: What objects are in the room? What does the human want? A naive planner assumes fixed answers and proceeds. The moment something unexpected arrives — an unknown object, a goal not mentioned — the plan falls apart.

A beginner's fix: ask the human everything upfront. But that is wasteful; the human ends up answering dozens of questions, many irrelevant. The real challenge: figure out which specific questions to ask, in which order, so that with the fewest answers the planner gets enough certainty to act.

The method in one sentence. Build a tree of possible futures, predict how uncertain each future is, and learn to ask the human the questions that would shrink the biggest uncertainties.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Lay out possible futures

For each unknown thing (object type, goal), build branches where it could be A or B or C.

Simulate planning on each branch

A neural planner (a learned model) predicts success odds for each possible future.

Measure remaining uncertainty

Look at all branches; how different are their success odds? High spread means high uncertainty.

Rank questions by impact

For each possible question, ask: if we ask this, which branches merge closer (shrinking spread)?

Get human answers one by one

Ask the human the top-impact question; update branches; repeat until plan is certain enough.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: outcome uncertainty

measured quantity  =  outcome uncertainty in planning

This is the size of the disagreement in success odds across all possible futures. If the neural planner predicts success at 92%, 75%, and 88% on three possible futures, the uncertainty is high (values spread across a wide range). If it predicts 89%, 87%, and 90%, the uncertainty is low (clustered tight). The paper measures this by looking at how spread out the predicted success odds are across all branches.

Why it works

Why it works: high-impact questions first

The core insight: some questions matter more than others because they split the futures differently. Asking whether the object is a ball or a cup might collapse many branches into one outcome (success). Asking the exact color of the ball might not matter — many color versions still lead to success. By measuring which question would shrink outcome uncertainty the most, MINT picks the one that answers get the most planning mileage.

The mechanism has three parts. First, the symbolic tree lists every possible combination of unknowns (object A or B or C, goal X or Y, etc.). Second, a neural planning policy (trained on past planning problems) runs on each branch and predicts: if the unknowns were these, would this plan succeed? That gives a success odds curve across branches. Third, MINT measures the spread of that curve. A question that splits the branches so they all converge to high success (or all to failure) is high-impact — answering it removes the confusion. A question that leaves spread unchanged is low-impact — it partitions the branches but does not align them. MINT ranks by impact and asks the human questions in that order, re-measuring spread after each answer.

Math after the picture

The formula: minimizing uncertainty

First in words:

Outcome uncertainty is the spread of the planner's success predictions across all possible futures. A question is worth asking if it shrinks this spread the most.

Then the compact form:

U(world) = Var(P(success | world)) across all unknown combinations Importance(question) = U(current) - Avg(U(after answering question))

Every symbol:

U(world)outcome uncertainty measure (variance) in a world state
P(success | world)the planning policy's predicted success probability given this world
Var(...)variance: how spread out the numbers are
Importance(question)how much a question reduces uncertainty
A tiny worked example
Suppose the neural planner predicts success on four possible futures (the four unknown combinations) at odds of 92%, 75%, 88%, 81%. The average is 84%, and the spread is wide (92 is 8 points up, 75 is 9 points down).
Variance = (8^2 + (-9)^2 + 4^2 + (-3)^2) / 4 = (64 + 81 + 16 + 9) / 4 = 42.5
Now suppose asking 'what is the object?' would split into two groups: if it is A, all four futures predict 90% ± 2%. If it is B, all four predict 70% ± 1%. Asking narrows variance in both groups to ~2.5, so Importance(question) ≈ 42.5 - 2.5 = 40 points of uncertainty removed.
Another question 'what color is it?' splits into three groups: one predicts 85% ± 10%, another 82% ± 11%, another 87% ± 9%. Average variance stays ~100. So Importance(color) ≈ 0 — not worth asking.
MINT picks the first question (importance 40) before the second.

A question matters if it groups the branches into clusters with tighter agreement, shrinking the overall spread.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Neural planner is poorly trained. If the planning policy has not seen enough past problems, it makes bad predictions of success odds. Its uncertainty estimates are noise, not signal. Outcome spread becomes meaningless, and MINT asks the wrong questions.
Unknown unknowns. If an unknown is so rare the symbolic tree does not include it as a possibility, MINT cannot ask about it. The planner hits a surprise not in the tree and fails.
Human answers are unreliable. If the human lies or guesses, the updated tree is wrong. MINT still shrinks variance, but the branches are misaligned, and the plan fails anyway.
Done right. Train the neural planner on diverse past planning problems; build the symbolic tree to cover known unknowns; trust that the human answers are honest; monitor plan success in the field and retrain the planner if failure rates climb.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulated world with three tasks: clear a table (objects could be dishes, toys, or trash), organize a shelf (goal might be by color, by height, or by value), and move objects between rooms (path unknowns: locked doors, stairs, furniture). Create a planning policy (trained on a few hundred simulated planning attempts). For each task, run MINT: have it choose which two questions to ask a human (simulated, giving correct answers), then plan. Measure and compare: questions asked, success rate, and human time spent.

Efficiency ratio = Plan success rate / Questions asked. Higher is better.

MINT should achieve >85% success with ≤3 questions per task; a naive baseline asking 10 questions should also hit >85% but waste 7 questions.
