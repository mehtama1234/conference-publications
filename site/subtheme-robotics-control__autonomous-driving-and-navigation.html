Autonomous driving and navigation

Papers solving the problem of making vehicles and robots navigate safely through complex, changing environments using perception and planning—the challenge is handling occlusion, uncertainty, and multi-agent scenarios.

What this is

Autonomous vehicles have to make split-second decisions in complex environments. A car is driving down a street and sees a pedestrian stepping between parked cars. It has to predict whether the person will continue into the street, estimate how much time it has to react, and decide to brake or swerve. A delivery robot navigating a warehouse has to move around people, obstacles, and other robots while carrying fragile packages. These are all navigation problems: how do you sense the world, predict how it will change, and decide where to move next?

The core difficulty is uncertainty and partial information. The robot's sensors do not see everything—some things are blocked (occluded). People and other objects behave unpredictably. The robot has to make a decision now, but the consequences play out over the next few seconds, and those consequences depend on the behavior of other agents in the world. Predicting whether a person will step into traffic requires modeling human intent, which is not visible. Planning a path for a car is easy in empty space; the complexity comes from other agents, uncertain obstacles, and the need to make decisions in real time. Also, any mistake can be costly—hitting a person or another vehicle is not an acceptable failure mode.

Papers tackle this by combining perception (seeing the world), prediction (guessing what others will do), and planning (choosing a safe trajectory). Some use neural networks to learn end-to-end: camera images map directly to steering and acceleration commands, trained on human driving data. Some build explicit models of the world—tracking where other cars and people are, predicting their future positions—and then use planning algorithms to find a safe path. Others combine the two: use neural networks to extract features from the scene, feed those to a planning algorithm that is aware of the robot's constraints (can only turn so fast, takes time to brake). Recent work emphasizes multi-agent reasoning—modeling not just one other agent, but the interaction between many agents—because traffic is a game where everyone is making decisions based on what they expect others to do.

How the work breaks down

The work here splits into five clear directions. First: end-to-end learning, where neural networks learn to drive directly from camera images. Second: prediction and planning, where the system builds a model of the world and what other agents will do next, then plots a safe path. Third: multi-agent reasoning, where algorithms model how different vehicles and pedestrians interact and influence each other's decisions. Fourth: vision-language navigation, where robots follow natural language instructions while navigating real spaces. Fifth: special challenges like temporal attacks, domain generalization, and continual learning as the robot encounters new environments.

End-to-End Autonomous Driving with Learning

End-to-end learning for driving works by training neural networks on real or simulated driving data, so the network learns to map what the camera sees directly to steering and acceleration commands. The core challenge is that a single trajectory (the human's path through traffic) does not fully capture all the valid ways to drive the same scene. A car could brake hard, accelerate gently, or switch lanes—all are correct if they are safe. Learning from only one "right answer" per situation limits what the network can learn.

VADv2 solves this by using probabilistic planning—instead of predicting a single trajectory, it predicts many possible safe futures. This lets it capture many valid driving behaviors at once. On the closed-loop benchmark CARLA Town05, VADv2 beats earlier methods because it is not locked into one learned path; it can handle new scenarios by reasoning about what is actually safe in this moment. DriveMamba takes a different angle: it uses a state-space model, a type of neural network that is very fast and can track long sequences of information (what happened over the last 5 seconds of driving). It works on both nuScenes and Bench2Drive, datasets where autonomous cars are tested in diverse real-world scenarios. RoCA addresses the hardest part of end-to-end driving: making the skill work across different cities. A model trained on one city's driving patterns often fails in a different city because the roads look different, traffic behaves differently, weather is different. RoCA learns city-independent features so that the learned driving skill transfers across cities without expensive retraining.

Prediction and Trajectory Planning

Planning a safe trajectory for an autonomous vehicle requires knowing not just where obstacles are right now, but where they will be in the next few seconds. Simply avoiding what is in front of you today is not enough; you must predict where pedestrians and other vehicles are heading. Many planning methods split this into two stages: first predict where the world will be in future moments, then choose a path through that predicted world.

OccDriver uses future occupancy—a prediction of which parts of the scene will be occupied by objects (cars, pedestrians, trees) in the next few seconds. Instead of predicting exact positions of each vehicle, occupancy asks "will this patch of road be blocked or free?" This is actually simpler and works better in real closed-loop driving because it handles uncertainty well. On nuPlan (the benchmark for closed-loop autonomous driving), OccDriver beats earlier methods because occupancy naturally captures "this might be blocked by something I haven't fully seen yet." UrbanVerse tackles the sim-to-real gap: simulators are useful for testing (cheaper and safer than real cars), but a policy trained in simulation often fails in real streets because the simulated world does not look like the real world. UrbanVerse builds simulation environments from real city-tour videos, so the simulated scenes look like actual streets in Bangkok, Tokyo, São Paulo, and 21 other cities. A robot trained in this realistic simulation transfers to real-world navigation with a 30% success boost because visual features from real cities are more consistent with test environments. BridgeDrive uses diffusion models (the same type that generate images) to plan trajectories. It works by starting with a random noisy path and gradually refining it into a safe trajectory that avoids obstacles. On Bench2Drive, it beats earlier trajectory planners by 7.72% because diffusion naturally handles the uncertainty of the driving task—there are many valid ways to navigate the same scenario. NOMAD learns from many trajectories collected over time (lifelong learning) by keeping memory of past good behaviors. When the robot encounters a new scenario in the long tail of rare events (like a seven-car pileup on a highway), it can recall similar past situations and adapt its planning accordingly.

Collaborative Perception and Multi-Agent Reasoning

Autonomous vehicles often cannot see everything on their own. A parked car blocks the view of what is behind it. In dense urban streets, a truck prevents nearby cars from seeing pedestrians crossing ahead. One solution: vehicles share sensor data with each other wirelessly, so each car can "see" obstacles that its neighbors see. The challenge is aligning what one car perceives with what another car perceives, because they are in different positions and their cameras have different angles.

GT-Space solves this by using a shared feature space—a common language that all vehicles use to describe what they see. When two cars share sensor data, GT-Space aligns their features so that both are describing the same object in the same terms. On realistic datasets with vehicle-to-vehicle communication (OPV2V, V2XSet, and RCooper which is real-world), GT-Space detects obstacles more accurately than baselines because aligned perception means less confusion about where things actually are. Motion Planning in Compressed Representation Spaces enables closed-loop planning on large multi-agent scenarios from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset—datasets with many cars, trucks, and pedestrians interacting at once—because compressing the scenario into a dense code removes unnecessary detail. Instead of planning separately for each agent, it learns to compress the full multi-agent scenario into a dense code, then plans using that compressed representation. This works on 100s of agents and is 100x faster than methods that track each agent separately because tracking is expensive and compression avoids redundant computation. Pairwise is Not Enough (HMAGAT) recognizes that traffic is not just pairwise interactions—it is not just "car A decides based on car B." In reality, many vehicles influence each other simultaneously. A self-driving car has to reason: "Car to my left is slowing down because it sees a pedestrian crossing ahead; I should anticipate that and start braking too." HMAGAT uses hypergraph neural networks, which model many-to-many relationships all at once. It learns this on 100x less training data than competitors and with only 1M parameters (tiny by modern standards) because the hypergraph structure is an efficient way to express multi-agent reasoning.

Vision-Language Navigation

Vision-language navigation is the problem of a robot following natural language instructions while moving through a real physical space. A human might say "go to the kitchen, turn left at the living room doorway, then find the refrigerator." The robot has to understand the language, recognize places in the real world that match the description, and navigate there. This is hard because language is ambiguous ("turn left" depends on which way the robot is facing), visual scenes have things blocking the view and lighting changes, and the robot has to find its own way without a map.

JanusVLN separates two things: meaning (what the instruction means) and location (where things are). A key insight is that the robot needs long-term memory of the path it has taken and the places it has seen, but storing full video is expensive. JanusVLN uses compact compressed codes that describe past locations without storing raw images. On standard benchmarks, it improves success rates by 10.5% to 35.5% because the robot can remember the shape of the space it is navigating and refer back to earlier observations. All-day Multi-scenes Lifelong VLN handles a harder version: the robot has to keep learning and adapting as it navigates multiple different environments (home, office, garden, store) over a long period. It uses Tucker Adaptation, a method to learn task-specific features efficiently without forgetting how to navigate previous environments. Instruction Decomposition and Action Alignment breaks down complex instructions into simpler sub-goals. If told "go find the red chair in the living room," the system first parses this into "(1) enter the living room, (2) find something red, (3) it should be a chair." Each sub-goal is easier to execute than the full instruction. The dataset includes 160K labeled images aligned with instruction steps, so the robot learns which visual features correspond to which instruction words. OmniNav handles different navigation modes—sometimes the human gives an instruction, sometimes the robot is just supposed to explore a new space. A single architecture handles all these by predicting waypoints (intermediate goals) with high precision. It runs at 5 Hz on real robots, fast enough to navigate smoothly without lag.

Staying Safe and Adapting to New Conditions

As autonomous systems move into real deployments, they face conditions that training did not prepare them for. Adversarial attackers might deliberately misalign sensor data (making LiDAR timestamps drift relative to camera timestamps). Environments change with seasons (snow on the road looks different than pavement). A robot trained in one building must adapt quickly when deployed in a new building. These adaptation challenges are critical because failures are costly.

Detecting Temporal Misalignment Attacks studies a specific attack: an adversary slightly shifts the timestamps of sensor data so that the autonomous car fuses outdated information. This could cause the car to see a pedestrian where there is none or miss a real obstacle. AION (a detection system) learns to spot these attacks by recognizing that combining camera and LiDAR data produces unusual outputs when data is misaligned. On KITTI and nuScenes, it detects the attack with very high accuracy (AUROC 0.94+), catching the attack before it causes bad decisions. Continual Vision-and-Language Navigation via Mixture of Macro and Micro Experts addresses a central problem in lifelong learning: when a robot learns a new environment, it should not forget how to navigate old environments. M3E uses two types of experts—macro (large scale, capturing coarse strategies) and micro (fine details, capturing specific features). New environments get adapted experts that combine memory of the old experts with new training, so the robot retains old skills while learning new ones. Fine-tuning on just 10% of new data outperforms full retraining because the old expert's memory preserves what was already learned. Mitigating Error Accumulation uses a memory-augmented Kalman filter—a classical filtering technique from robotics that has been enhanced with neural network memory. As a robot navigates and accumulates small errors, these errors compound over time. The Kalman filter is designed to correct this drift. Adding neural memory lets it learn task-specific patterns in how and where errors accumulate, so with just 10% of training data for fine-tuning, it regulates drift far better than methods that only use filtering because neural memory captures the real error patterns this particular robot experiences. Plan-R1 treats planning as language modeling—a very different approach. Instead of explicitly computing safe trajectories, it treats each trajectory as a sequence (like a sentence) and learns which sequences are safe and which are not. It discards undesirable patterns learned from data and aligns planning with safety constraints, so the robot maintains human-like, predictable behavior on nuPlan.
