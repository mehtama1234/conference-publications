SOSBench: Safety in Science

A benchmark that catches how often language models help with dangerous science, even when they have been trained to refuse.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Large language models are becoming experts at science and reasoning. But if they are trained to say no to harm, do they actually refuse? SOSBench is a test that asks dangerous science questions from real regulations across six domains: chemistry, biology, medicine, pharmacology, physics, and psychology. The trick: instead of simply asking for bomb recipes, it builds realistic, knowledge-heavy requests that mimic real misuse. The paper finds that frontier models fail alarmingly often, disclosing dangerous content in 50-85% of cases, proving that current safety training does not keep pace with dangerous capability.

Zero assumption version

Why naive safety benchmarks miss real threats

Today, most safety benchmarks ask models simple questions: tell me how to build a bomb, explain how to make poison, classify whether this instruction is harmful. These are low-risk because they require almost no scientific knowledge. A model can refuse them just by pattern-matching: no special knowledge needed.

But real dangers are knowledge-intensive. A skilled chemist asking how to synthesize an explosive from legitimate precursors, using technical jargon and embedded legitimate uses, is much harder to refuse safely. Existing benchmarks do not test that. So a model trained to be safe can still disclose dangerous science because the training never saw these harder, realistic scenarios.

The test that catches real knowledge misuse. SOSBench asks realistic, knowledge-heavy questions across science domains, seeded from actual regulations, and evolved to elicit dangerous capability.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Collect seeds from regulations

Extract high-hazard terms (e.g., chemical names, procedures) from real government safety codes and laws.

Step 2: Expand synonyms and forms

For each seed term (TNT), find all its variants: abbreviations, formulas (C7H5N3O6), trade names, colloquial names.

Step 3: Mutate instruction templates

Take generic instruction patterns (like chain-of-thought reasoning requests) and swap in the seed terms to create initial prompts.

Step 4: Evolve via surrogate models

Run prompts through multiple models with weak safety filters, keep only those that trigger harmful responses, and iteratively refine language to maximize knowledge-heavy framing while maintaining harm.

Step 5: Score coverage and finalize

Ensure every dangerous term and every domain are equally represented in the final 3,000-prompt benchmark, then test frontier models.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper replaces simple, low-knowledge safety prompts with knowledge-intensive, regulation-grounded ones. Each prompt is a realistic misuse scenario that requires domain expertise to answer safely.

the object it moves  =  Prompt realism and knowledge intensity
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Coverage-driven harm elicitation

measured quantity  =  How often a model discloses dangerous knowledge

The paper measures a hidden quantity: for each dangerous term (a chemical, a procedure), how many models—when given a hard, realistic prompt—actually disclose harmful content? This is coverage. A term is fully covered when all surrogate models fail to refuse it. The algorithm then focuses mutations on under-covered terms to ensure balanced, realistic danger across the entire domain.

This is the heartbeat of the method. The benchmark does not just count harmful responses; it ensures every high-risk concept is tested in realistic, knowledge-heavy language. That coverage forces the final 3,000 prompts to be authentically dangerous and not just superficial.

Why it works

Why it works: Evolution locks in realistic knowledge-heavy scenarios

The paper's trick is a dual loop. First, it starts with seeds—codified hazards from real regulations (government-mandated dangerous terms). These seeds are legitimate and grounded, not invented. Second, it evolves them: mutate the language, test with multiple models, keep only the prompts that trigger harmful responses from at least some models, then focus evolution on the gaps (the under-covered terms that have not yet elicited harm from all surrogates).

Why does this work? Because evolution by model feedback is fast and smart. Each mutation that triggers a harmful response teaches the pipeline what language patterns fool safety. By the time you pick the final 3,000 prompts, they are authentic, hard, and representative. They are not artificially evil; they are what real misusers would craft. Then when you test frontier models, you catch real safety failures that simple benchmarks miss.

How to measure coverage and balance

First in words:

coverage of term t equals the maximum harmfulness score across all prompts containing that term; keep evolving until all terms are fully covered (all surrogates fail to refuse)

Then the compact form:

c(t) = max{s(p) : t in term(p)}; iterate until c(t) = C for all high-risk terms

Every symbol:

c(t)coverage level of term t (how many models failed on prompts with t)
s(p)harmfulness score of prompt p (count of surrogates that produced harm)
Ctotal number of surrogate models used for validation
ta high-risk term (chemical, procedure, concept)
pa generated prompt
A tiny worked example: Coverage-driven selection
Term: TNT synthesis. After evolution, 3 out of 4 surrogates failed to refuse. Coverage c(TNT) = 3, not yet 4.
Term: Aspirin synthesis. All 4 surrogates refused, even the hard prompts. Coverage c(Aspirin) = 0 (not harmful enough).
Next round: prioritize mutating TNT variants (increase c(TNT) toward 4), ignore Aspirin.
After refinement, TNT prompt v2 now fools all 4 models. Coverage c(TNT) = 4. Term is fully covered; sample it into the final dataset.

The algorithm runs iteratively: mutate prompts with low coverage, re-test, and only lock in prompts when they consistently elicit harm across multiple models.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Surrogate models are too weak. If your pool of surrogate models has poor safety alignment, the benchmark will not evolve toward truly hard prompts—it will just find adversarial tricks. You need a real safety-trained surrogate to measure against.
Coverage heuristic misses emergent risks. The algorithm focuses on terms that fool existing models. It may miss entirely new harm pathways that no model has seen. The benchmark is reactive, not predictive.
Regulation seeds are not universal. Different countries have different hazard lists. A benchmark seeded from US regulations may miss high-risk domains important in other legal contexts.
Done right: Diverse, grounded seeds plus iterative evolution. Start with multiple regulatory sources, use a diverse set of surrogates, and manually inspect the final prompts to ensure they represent realistic misuse scenarios.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a mini SOSBench on one domain (e.g., simple organic chemistry synthesis). Collect 10 regulations-derived seed terms. Expand each to 5 variant forms. Manually write 5 instruction templates. Use a model with weak safety (or no safety) to filter: keep only prompts that trigger unsafe responses. Then test a frontier model (GPT-4, Claude, Deepseek) on these 50 prompts and count how many it refuses. Finally, run the same frontier model on the same terms in simple, obvious language (e.g., "How do I make TNT?") and count refusals there. The gap is the knowledge-intensity effect.

Refusal gap = simple_prompts_refused - knowledge_heavy_prompts_refused; positive means knowledge framing defeats safety

If the gap is at least 20 percentage points, the demo proves that realistic, knowledge-heavy framing defeats safety training, and benchmark evolution is necessary to catch those failures.
