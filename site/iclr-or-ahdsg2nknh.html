Multilevel Control Functionals

When you need to estimate a hard integral, use cheaper approximations at multiple accuracy levels and correct them with smart control weights.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Imagine you need to compute an integral (the sum of something over all possibilities) but it is too expensive to evaluate directly. A standard trick is Monte Carlo sampling: draw random points and average. But when the function is expensive, even sampling wastes budget. This paper introduces Multilevel Control Functionals (MLCFs), which estimate the integral using a hierarchy of approximations from cheap to expensive, applying variance reduction at each level to squeeze more signal from the same budget.

Zero assumption version

The cost trap in Monte Carlo estimation

When you want to estimate an integral (the total effect or average over all possibilities), the standard tool is Monte Carlo sampling: pick random points, evaluate the function at each, average the results. It works, but has a problem: if evaluating the function is expensive (seconds per evaluation), you burn through your computational budget quickly and still get a noisy estimate.

A beginner tries to fix this by sampling more carefully or using better random number generators, but the core problem remains: each sample costs the same. The estimate stays noisy relative to the budget spent.

The method. Use multiple approximations of the function at different accuracy levels. Cheap approximations give noisy estimates fast; expensive ones are accurate but costly. The paper corrects cheap estimates with deviations measured at higher levels, so you get accuracy without burning budget.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The paper changes how the samples are allocated and corrected across a hierarchy of approximations. Instead of sampling only the most accurate version (wasting budget on expensive evaluations), it uses cheap approximations for most of the work and allocates costly evaluations only to measure the remaining error.

the object it moves  =  sample allocation and correction weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Variance per unit cost

measured quantity  =  Efficiency (variance per sample cost)

The paper measures how much signal you get from each unit of computational cost. In standard Monte Carlo, you spend the same cost per sample regardless of whether you evaluate a cheap approximation or the exact model. The hidden quantity MLCFs track is this efficiency: the variance remaining after correction, divided by the cost spent to achieve it. By this measure, you can decide how many samples to take at each level.

The paper estimates this quantity by analyzing the smoothness of the integrand and the structure of approximation errors between levels. It then solves an optimization problem to find the sample count that minimizes total variance for a fixed budget.

Why it works

Why it works: The telescoping correction

Standard Monte Carlo says: sample the thing you care about, average it, done. But if it is expensive, you do not have budget for many samples. Instead of sampling the expensive model directly, rewrite the estimate as a sum of increments: start with a cheap approximation (lots of samples, low cost, but noisy), then add the gap between cheap and medium (medium effort), then add the gap between medium and expensive (high effort). Each level only measures the delta from the previous level, not the full quantity.

The magic is applying control variates at each level. A control variate is a correction function (computed from Stein's method) that has zero mean but is correlated with the gap you want to estimate. Subtracting it from the gap does not change the true average but shrinks the randomness around it. So each level gets you closer to the truth with much less noise than if you sampled that level directly.

Combined with optimal sample allocation, you put most budget into cheap levels (high variance but low cost per sample) and use a few expensive evaluations only to measure what the cheap version missed. This beats standard Monte Carlo because you reuse cheap information across all the adjustments instead of throwing it away.

Math after the picture

The telescoping estimator with control

First in words:

Final estimate equals cheap model average plus (medium model gap corrected) plus (expensive model gap corrected)

Then the compact form:

Estimator = (1/n_0) * sum_i f_cheap(x_i) + (1/n_1) * sum_j [f_medium(y_j) - f_cheap(y_j) - g_1(y_j)] + (1/n_2) * sum_k [f_expensive(z_k) - f_medium(z_k) - g_2(z_k)]

Every symbol:

f_cheap, f_medium, f_expensivethe approximations at each accuracy level
n_0, n_1, n_2number of samples allocated to each level
g_1, g_2control variate functions that have zero mean but reduce variance of the gaps
x_i, y_j, z_krandom samples drawn at each level
A tiny worked example with three levels
Level 0 (cheap): 1000 samples, each costs 0.1 units, total cost 100. Average result: 2.3.
Level 1 (medium): 100 samples, each costs 1 unit, total cost 100. Average gap (medium - cheap): 0.15. After control variate correction: 0.08 (noise reduced).
Level 2 (expensive): 10 samples, each costs 10 units, total cost 100. Average gap (expensive - medium): 0.02. After control variate correction: 0.01 (noise reduced).
Total budget: 300 units. Final estimate: 2.3 + 0.08 + 0.01 = 2.39.
Standard Monte Carlo with 300 units on expensive model alone: 30 samples, much noisier estimate with variance roughly 10x higher.

The control functions at each level are chosen to be highly correlated with the gaps they correct, so subtracting them cuts the randomness without changing the average.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Approximation gap is not smooth. If the gap between levels is jagged or has sudden jumps, the control variate does not work well because it assumes smoothness. The method relies on approximations being close in structure.
Dimension is very high. In very high dimensions, the variance reduction from control variates weakens. The method is designed for moderate-dimensional problems. If the integral is over 100+ dimensions, gains vanish.
Cheap approximation is still too slow. If even the cheap model takes seconds per evaluation, you do not have enough budget to populate level 0. The method assumes a true hierarchy: each level should be orders of magnitude cheaper, because the benefit comes from running thousands of cheap samples to build a rough estimate, then correcting it with fewer expensive samples. If all levels cost similar amounts, the multilevel structure offers no advantage.
Done right: smooth problem in moderate dimensions. When you have cheap and expensive versions of a smooth function (e.g., a differential equation solver run at low and high resolution, or computing probabilities in a hierarchical model with subsets), and the problem involves at most 20-50 quantities, MLCFs shine.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a synthetic integration problem where you have a cheap approximation (e.g., a low-resolution ODE solver) and an expensive one (high-resolution). Implement standard Monte Carlo, multilevel Monte Carlo (without variance reduction), and MLCF (with control variates). Run all three with the same computational budget (sum of all function evaluations across all levels costs the same). Measure the absolute error in the final estimate across 50 independent runs.

error = | true_integral - estimated_integral |, measured in 50 trials; report mean error and standard deviation

MLCF should have both lower mean error AND much lower variance (error bars tighter) than the other two methods under the same budget.
