Zero assumption version

Modeling dynamic economies with changing action sets

Economists study markets where people buy, sell, and trade over time under uncertainty (weather, health, job loss). The standard tool, called general equilibrium theory, asks: what prices balance supply and demand so everyone is happy with their choice? But this breaks when time and uncertainty matter together. In real life, if I go bankrupt today, tomorrow I can no longer buy anything—my action set shrinks. Standard equilibrium theory ignores this: it assumes my options today do not change depending on what you do.

A beginner might try to extend equilibrium theory by just adding time, treating each year as a separate market. This fails because it misses how today's bankruptcy ripples forward: if I know I might have no buying power tomorrow, I save differently today. The real problem is that the paper needs a single framework that captures time, uncertainty, and the fact that one player's action shapes another player's choices—all at once.

The core puzzle. How do you prove equilibrium exists when players' available actions depend on what other players do?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper redefines the core object: instead of a static market where everyone's actions are independent, it rewrites the game so that one player's choice directly determines which actions the other player can take. In game-theory terms, the 'action set' becomes a function of other players' moves.

the object it moves  =  the action set
The hidden quantity

Recursive Radner Equilibrium

measured quantity  =  equilibrium price sequence

An equilibrium is a sequence of prices over infinite time such that, at each moment, every player is making the best choice given the prices they see and the randomness ahead. The key hidden quantity is the price function itself—a rule that, given the current wealth and holdings, returns the price.

The paper constructs this function by solving a Markov pseudo-game. The function must satisfy a recursive condition: the price today must be consistent with optimal choices today and equilibrium prices tomorrow. This is called a 'fixed point'—a price path that, when players optimize given that path, produces the path itself.

Why it works

Why it works: three moves

The paper makes three moves. First, it reformulates the economic problem as a game: instead of asking 'what price balances supply and demand,' it asks 'what price-setting strategy is a best response when other players use that strategy too?' This is a fixed-point problem.

Second, it rewrites the action space so that when player A's wealth drops (because they made a bad trade), player B automatically faces fewer options. This is done by making each player's action set a function of the state (wealth, holdings). The paper then proves that in this reformulated game, an equilibrium must exist—a price and quantity sequence nobody wants to deviate from.

Third, the paper shows this fixed point can be found fast. It constructs a concave optimization problem whose solution IS the equilibrium. A first-order method (gradient descent on prices) converges to equilibrium in polynomial time, meaning the algorithm scales to realistic economies. The neural network learns the price function by playing against itself, like an adversary learning to set prices and a player learning to trade optimally.

Math after the picture

The equilibrium recursion

First in words:

price today equals a function of current state; when players optimize under that price, the market clears and produces tomorrow's state, which matches tomorrow's price function

Then the compact form:

P(s) = argmax_p [lambda * (D(p,s) - S(p,s))^2 + V(s,p)]

Every symbol:

sthe state: wealth and holdings of all players
P(s)the equilibrium price function at state s
D(p,s)total demand when price is p and state is s
S(p,s)total supply when price is p and state is s
lambdahow strongly we penalize excess supply or demand
V(s,p)continuation value: expected future payoff under price p
A tiny worked example
Imagine two traders, each with 10 units of wheat. State s = (wealth_A=100, wealth_B=100).
Trader A wants to buy if price < 5 per unit (expects rain, higher value tomorrow). Demand D(4,s) = 5 units.
Trader B is pessimistic, wants to sell. Supply S(4,s) = 8 units. Excess supply = 3.
Algorithm raises price to 6, which shifts demand down to 2 and supply to 5. Still unbalanced.
At price 7: demand = 0, supply = 3. Overshoot. Try price 5.5: demand = 2, supply = 4.
After a few iterations, price converges to 5.2. At this price, demand = supply, state clears.

The price adjusts until the amount buyers want at that price exactly equals the amount sellers want to give up, and this balance holds at every future state.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Random shocks break determinism. If random events (weather, job loss) are too extreme, the state space explodes and the algorithm becomes slow. The paper assumes shocks are bounded.
Incomplete markets create multiple equilibria. If traders cannot insure against all risks (no derivative contracts), there may be many equilibria, not one. The paper assumes sufficient market structure.
Neural network approximation introduces error. When a network learns the price function, it may overshoot and violate constraints (e.g., prices go negative). The paper uses a constrained adversarial training scheme.
Done right. When state spaces are tractable and markets are complete, the algorithm provably finds equilibrium in polynomial time.
