A paper that turns finding fair prices in a random economy into solving a game between two players, then solves it fast using neural networks.

In a real economy, people trade goods over time, prices change, and future uncertainty shapes choices today. Economists want to predict when such economies reach a balance (called an equilibrium) where no one can improve by switching trades. This paper takes that problem and recasts it as a two-player game: one player sets policies, the other finds the best response. By solving this game, we find the equilibrium and can use a neural network to compute it quickly.

Zero assumption version

The stuck problem: prices in uncertain, changing economies

In a real economy, people have different goods and different needs. They trade over time. The future is uncertain—next year's prices, next year's needs, all unknown. Economists call this a random exchange economy. The question is: at what prices does everyone stop wanting to trade differently? That balance point is the equilibrium.

A beginner might try: solve for a single set of prices that works forever. But that does not work because prices change over time and depend on what happened before (history matters). The old theory (Arrow-Debreu) handles a one-time trade perfectly, but it does not capture time, randomness, and how one person's action can change what options another person has. Radner extended it, but no one had a fast way to actually compute the equilibrium prices in these infinite-horizon random cases. Economists were stuck: they could prove equilibria exist, but they could not calculate them.

In one sentence. Turn the equilibrium problem into a two-player game where one player sets strategies and the other finds the best counter-strategy, then solve the game.

What it actually does

Outer player sets economy policy; inner player finds best response.

Measure how badly each player is doing

Compute exploitability: total regret if one player deviates.

Use small nudges on both players

Simultaneously push outer player down and inner player up to find the stable point.

Handle nested dependencies

Inner player's policy depends on outer player's; track this explicitly.

Repeat until stable

Both players reach a point where further moves help neither, because each is already doing the best it can given what the other is doing.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Instead of trying to solve for equilibrium prices directly, the paper changes the object being optimized: it moves from pricing to policies. A policy is a rule that says what to do (how much to trade) given the current state (what happened, what prices are, what you own). The paper optimizes the parameters of these policies (in this case, neural network weights) using a game-theoretic setup.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: exploitability

measured quantity  =  Exploitability (cumulative regret)

Exploitability is a measure of how badly a player would do if the other player found the absolute best strategy to punish them. It is the sum of regrets across all possible time steps: how much better could a player have done by switching strategy? If exploitability is zero, neither player can improve (because they already have no profitable move available), which means the policies have reached equilibrium.

The paper estimates exploitability by running simulations of the economy forward: the outer player uses the current policy, the inner player tries each possible deviation and measures how much better it would do. High exploitability means someone found a winning counter-move; low exploitability means the current policies are stable.

Paper reading. In Markov games, exploitability is the maximum cumulative regret any player can achieve by choosing a best-response policy to the current profile, summed over states and time.
Why it works

Why it works: the game-theoretic loop

The paper turns equilibrium-finding into a min-max game. Imagine two competing agents: the outer agent sets the economy policy, and the inner agent is told that policy and finds the absolute best counter-strategy. The outer agent wins if this counter-strategy does poorly (low regret), and the inner agent wins if it does well (high regret). The goal is to find a policy where the inner agent cannot do much better than following it, no matter what alternative move they try, because every deviation is either pointless or makes them worse off.

Why does this work? Because when the outer policy is at equilibrium, the inner agent (representing any opponent) cannot find a winning deviation—every possible move they try is worse than or equal to following the current policy. At that point, the outer policy is a fixed point: it is what an optimizer would naturally settle on if everyone played optimally against each other.

The algorithm alternates: update the outer policy to make the inner agent's best response weaker, then update the inner agent's best response to counter the new outer policy. These updates are small nudges in the direction that reduces regret, like water flowing downhill. The paper proves that after polynomial-many steps (feasible, not exponential), both players reach a stable point. Because the updates use neural networks as the policy function, the algorithm runs fast in practice.

The equation: minimizing exploitability

First in words:

The outer player minimizes maximum regret by adjusting policy parameters while the inner player simultaneously finds the best response. The algorithm updates both players using gradients.

Then the compact form:

min_omega max_sigma regret(omega, sigma) = sum over states and time of (value if inner deviates - value if inner follows policy)

A tiny worked example
Suppose the outer policy says: trade 50% of good X today.
Inner player tests a deviation: trade 60% instead.
Inner player's gain: 10 units of payoff over the full horizon.
Another deviation: trade 30%. Loss: -8 units (worse).
Regret = 10 units (the best alternative to following the policy).
Outer policy updates to reduce this 10-unit gap.
Next iteration: outer tweaks to 52%, inner re-tests deviations.
New regret: 7 units (lower).
After many iterations: regret shrinks toward zero, equilibrium found.

The outer player nudges policy to shrink the inner player's regret; the inner player finds the sharpest counter-move each round. This loop drives both toward equilibrium.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Direction of improvement changes mid-learning If the inner player's best response depends strongly on the outer policy, updating one can make the other's direction of improvement point the wrong way. The paper handles this by using two time scales: outer updates slower than inner, so the inner player can catch up each time the outer changes.
Neural network optimization is non-convex The policy is a neural network, so the space of all possible settings for its parameters is full of valleys and ridges. The algorithm is not guaranteed to find the single best solution, only a local stable point where further small moves stop helping. In practice, this works well if the space of parameters changes smoothly.
Bounded best-response mismatch required The theory assumes the inner player's best response does not change wildly when the outer policy shifts slightly. This holds in many economies but can break if the problem is highly nonlinear.
Done right: use bounded step sizes and two-timescale updates By taking small, carefully-scaled steps in the direction of improvement and updating the outer player slower than the inner, the algorithm avoids chasing its own tail and reaches a stable point.
