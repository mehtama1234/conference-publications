Cheapest way to rearrange one pile of things into another pile.

In plain terms

Imagine you have a pile of sand on one side of a floor and want to move it to match a different pile on the other side. The distance between the piles is not just how far apart they sit - it's about the actual cost of moving each grain of sand from its current position to where it needs to go. The Wasserstein distance captures this total transportation cost. Instead of sand, think about any distribution: a probability distribution over possible outcomes, or the set of features a model produces. To compare two distributions, you want to know how much effort it takes to morph one into the other.

There are many ways to do this rearrangement. You could move grain A to spot 1 and grain B to spot 2, or grain A to spot 2 and grain B to spot 1. Each choice has a different total cost. The Wasserstein distance finds the cheapest possible rearrangement - the one that minimizes transportation cost. This gives a fair measure of how different two distributions truly are, because the cost of moving something depends on distance: a long movement is more expensive than a short movement.

Why does this matter for machine learning? When you train a model, you want its output distribution to match a target. When you compare learned representations from two different methods, you want to know if they are genuinely similar. When you plan a path in a reinforcement learning problem, you want to match a desired trajectory distribution. In all these cases, the Wasserstein distance tells you not just whether they are different but how much work it would take to make them the same.

The equation
W(p,q)=\inf_\gamma E\|x-y\|
W(p, q)The Wasserstein distance between distribution p and distribution q; the final answer you compute.
pThe first distribution (for example, the current state of your data or model output).
qThe second distribution (for example, the target or ground truth you want to match).
gammaA transport plan: a way to pair up elements from p with elements from q so every element gets matched exactly once.
infThe infimum, meaning the smallest value you can achieve by trying all possible transport plans.
EExpected value, meaning the average cost across all the matched pairs in the chosen transport plan.
||x - y||The distance between a point x in the first distribution and a point y in the second distribution (how far apart they are).
Worked example
Two simple distributions: where should you move things?
Say distribution p puts mass at positions 0 and 2 (half at each).
Distribution q puts mass at positions 1 and 3 (half at each).
Plan 1: move 0 to 1 (cost 1) and 2 to 3 (cost 1); total = 2.
Plan 2: move 0 to 3 (cost 3) and 2 to 1 (cost 1); total = 4.
The Wasserstein distance picks Plan 1 (the cheap one): W = 1.0.
(This is the expected cost per unit of mass transported.)
Why it shows up

The Wasserstein distance appears across machine learning whenever you need to compare distributions and preserve spatial meaning. In reinforcement learning, when you want a learned policy to match a demonstration, you compare their return distributions using Wasserstein to ensure the agent learns the right behavior. In representation learning, when you want two neural networks trained on different data to have similar feature spaces, Wasserstein measures how far apart those learned distributions are. In interpretability work, when you want to explain a model's decision by finding a counterfactual example, you measure distance using Wasserstein so that small changes in the input correspond to small changes in meaning. In generative models and flow matching, Wasserstein guides the learning of transformations that morph one distribution into another.

What makes Wasserstein special is that cost depends on distance: if you move something a tiny bit, the cost barely changes, and if you move it far, the cost grows. This continuity is why it beats other distance measures like KL divergence for tasks where you care about spatial structure. KL divergence can assign huge penalties to small changes, making it harsh and unintuitive for comparing distributions that should be close in meaning. Every time a paper talks about optimal transport or matching distributions fairly, Wasserstein is doing the heavy lifting.
