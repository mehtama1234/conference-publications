Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Long-term time series forecasting (predicting many steps into the future) breaks when models try to read too much history at once. SPRINT solves this by reading the history at a lower resolution (fewer data points), making predictions separately for the slow trend and fast wiggles, then reconstructing the full forecast. The method runs only when making a new prediction (not during training), so it works as a plug-in to any existing time series model without retraining.

The sequence-length wall

Time series forecasting (predicting stock prices, weather, or sensor readings ahead in time) works best when a model reads a lot of history before making a guess. A time series foundation model (a large neural network trained to predict from any kind of time sequence) can handle broad domains well, but it hits a hard ceiling: if the sequence of past data is too long, the model either runs out of memory or takes forever to compute.

A naive forecast engine would read all available history directly. But when the sequence is very long (thousands or millions of points), the raw computation explodes — the model must process every single value through every layer, which is expensive. Engineers either cut the history short (losing information) or wait a long time (unusable in practice).

The paper's move, in one sentence. Forecast at a lower resolution by splitting trend from seasonality, downsampling each separately, predicting in that shrunk space, then re-expanding to the original resolution.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decompose the history

Split incoming time series into trend (slow, smoothed) and seasonal (repeating fast pattern) using a statistical decomposition.

Downsample both

Shrink the trend and seasonal sequences to 1/K resolution (e.g., every 5th point) to fit in memory and compute fast.

Predict low-res trend

Feed the downsampled trend into the model; it outputs a forecast at that shrunk resolution.

Interpolate trend back up

Expand the low-res trend forecast to full resolution using learned or linear upsampling.

Replicate seasonal and merge

Copy the seasonal pattern forward (since it repeats by definition) and add it to the expanded trend to recover the original scale.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the model reads history. Instead of consuming the raw, full-resolution sequence, it downsamples the history to a smaller number of points (keeping only every Kth value) before feeding it to the model. The downsampling is selective: it preserves the trend (the long-term drift) by filtering out fast wiggles, and handles seasonality (repeating patterns) as a separate copy-forward step, not as data to be predicted.

the object it moves  =  the input resolution of the sequence
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: effective sequence compression

measured quantity  =  how much the input shrinks during downsampling

The key hidden quantity is the compression ratio: how many points in the original history does the downsampled version represent? If you start with 10,000 time steps and downsample by a factor of 5, the model only reads 2,000 points. This number determines both memory saved and information lost.

The paper does not measure this as a single number but uses two separate measurements: one for trend (how much of the slow part can be predicted from a shrunken view) and one for seasonality (whether repeating patterns re-occur in the forecast horizon without being learned). The paper argues that trend benefits from downsampling (less noise) while seasonality should be replicated exactly (it is deterministic by definition).

Why it works

Why it works: decomposition + differential downsampling

The core insight is that time series have two very different components. The trend is smooth (it changes slowly), so it can be represented at a lower resolution without losing shape. The seasonal part repeats by definition (every day has roughly the same pattern, every year similar), so it can be generated by copying what already happened, not by predicting new values.

By splitting these two and handling them separately, SPRINT achieves two wins. First, the model receives a shorter sequence (downsampled trend), so computation and memory drop dramatically because there are fewer numbers to push through each layer. Second, the trend is easier to predict from a cleaner signal, because downsampling naturally averages out the fast jitter and noise, leaving only the true direction of drift — a smaller, steadier target makes the model's job simpler. The seasonal part is not predicted at all; it is simply replayed forward, which is free and correct.

Merging them back works because they operate at different scales: the trend sets the overall direction, and the seasonal wiggles are wrapped on top. The combined forecast is both faster (because the model reads fewer points) and more accurate (because predicting a clean trend is easier than predicting noisy raw data, and seasonal replication is exact by design), compared to the naive approach of reading all points raw and predicting them together.

The downsampling and reconstruction formula

First in words:

The forecast is trend upsampled back to full resolution plus seasonality copied forward.

Then the compact form:

forecast = Upsample(Model(Downsample(trend_history))) + Repeat(seasonal_pattern)

Every symbol:

forecastthe predicted next N time steps at original resolution
Downsample(trend_history)reduce trend to every Kth point; e.g., K=4 means 1000 points become 250
Model(...)the time series foundation model's prediction in downsampled space
Upsample(...)expand the model's prediction back to full resolution using interpolation
Repeat(seasonal_pattern)copy the last observed seasonal cycle forward N steps (it repeats)
Kthe downsampling factor (how many time steps to skip); larger K = faster but noisier
A tiny worked example
History: 1000 points. Downsample factor K=5 means we keep every 5th point: 1000 -> 200 points fed to model.
Model predicts 40 steps into future at downsampled resolution (40 points, spanning 200 original time steps).
Upsample the 40 predictions back to 200 points (interleave upsampling to match original resolution).
Seasonal pattern is 50 points (e.g., daily cycle in hourly data). Repeat the pattern 4 times to fill 200 forecast steps.
Merge: trend contributes the overall direction; seasonal contributes the daily wiggles. Output is 200 steps at original resolution.
Original model on 1000 points: expensive. Downsampled model on 200 points: 5x faster, 5x less memory. Reconstruction adds tiny cost.

The downsampling shrinks the input, so the model runs faster because there are fewer numbers to process through each layer, and reads a cleaner signal because averaging removes noise; upsampling reconstructs the full resolution, and seasonal replication adds back the repeating part, both fast and exact.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Seasonal pattern changes within the forecast window. If the repeating pattern does not actually repeat (e.g., the daily cycle shifts in April), simply copying it forward will be wrong. The method assumes seasonality is stable.
Downsampling factor too aggressive. If K is too large, the trend is too coarse. Interpolation back to full resolution will over-smooth or miss important jitters. A model trained on full resolution may not generalize well to very low resolution.
Decomposition fails for noisy or chaotic series. The statistical decomposition assumes signal + noise. If the data is mostly noise or has no clear trend-seasonal split, the split itself is fragile and reconstruction fails.
Done right. Use downsampling factors that match the data's typical cycle length (e.g., downsample by 24 for hourly data with a daily pattern). Verify that decomposition produces a strong trend and repeating seasonal on your dataset. Test on data where seasonality is truly stable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time series forecasting experiment with a dataset that has clear trend and seasonality (e.g., synthetic: linear trend plus sine wave; or real: temperature with daily and yearly cycles). Compare three forecasts: one with the foundation model on full history, one with SPRINT downsampling by 4, and one downsampling by 8. Measure accuracy (mean absolute error), memory peak, and wall-clock time. Plot the three forecasts and the true future; the downsampled versions should be faster and use less memory, and accuracy should improve (because the trend signal is less noisy) or stay similar (seasonal replication is free).
