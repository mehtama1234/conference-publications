Predicting properties and interactions

Helping researchers quickly predict what a molecule or drug will do—its toxicity, activity, or how it binds to proteins—without expensive experiments.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When a drug company tests a new candidate molecule, they ask dozens of questions: Is it toxic? Does it stick to the target protein? Does it get absorbed in the gut? Does it cross the blood-brain barrier? Each test takes time and money — sometimes months and hundreds of thousands of dollars per molecule. Most candidates fail, so you want to kill the losers early.

The bottleneck is that chemists must choose which candidates to test first, usually based on intuition or pattern recognition from past failures. What if we could predict those properties directly from the molecular structure? A machine learning model trained on past test results could screen thousands of candidates computationally and flag the winners for expensive lab testing. This would save time, money, and animals, and accelerate drug discovery.

The papers in this cluster predict molecular properties from structure: predicting how toxic a drug is, how strong it binds to a protein target, whether it will be absorbed in the body, how it will be metabolized. The challenge is that molecules are complex 3D objects, and what matters is not just what atoms they contain but how those atoms are arranged and bonded. A naive model might ignore the 3D structure and fail on molecules that look similar on paper but fold very differently. Some papers use graph neural networks — a way to represent molecules as connected nodes — so the model understands bonds and structure the way chemistry does, which means it can generalize to new molecules it has never seen because it learned the rules of bonding rather than memorizing specific structures. Others use 3D coordinates directly to predict binding strength. Still others combine multiple models to predict a whole profile of properties at once, which is more reliable than predicting each property separately because errors in one property can lead to errors in another, so joint prediction catches those mistakes.

How the work breaks down

The research in this subtheme splits into five natural directions: first, learning what a molecule does directly from its 3D structure by understanding bonds and electrons; second, predicting how molecules stick to proteins and each other; third, simulating how molecules move and change over time quickly enough to screen thousands of candidates; fourth, predicting how cells respond when you perturb them, which matters for drug testing; and fifth, predicting properties of crystals and materials, which is harder because atoms are arranged in repeating patterns rather than isolated molecules.

Molecular Structure & Properties

To predict whether a drug is toxic or active, you need to know what a molecule will do based on what atoms it contains and how they are bonded together. The key challenge is that molecules are 3D objects—the same atoms arranged differently can behave completely differently. A model that just memorizes specific molecular structures will fail on new molecules because there are billions of possible arrangements. Instead, you need a model that learns the rules of chemistry itself: how atoms bond, how electrons flow, and how the 3D structure shapes the molecule's behavior.

The Function-Centric Graph Neural Network approach represents a molecule as a network of connected atoms, which lets the model understand bonds the way chemistry does. It predicted electron density—how electrons are distributed around atoms—on large molecules up to 200 atoms, which is hard because you need to understand both local atom neighborhoods and how the whole structure fits together. Enhancing Molecular Property Predictions used explicit bond modeling, treating bonds as first-class information rather than just connections between atoms, and learned how bonds interact with nearby atoms, because ignoring bond types leads to errors on molecules with the same atoms but different bond patterns. By understanding bond types and atom-bond relationships, it achieved top results on molecular property benchmarks. Learning from the Electronic Structure of Molecules works at an even deeper level: instead of just the molecule's 3D positions, it learns from the electronic structure—the actual quantum behavior of electrons in the molecule, which determines how the molecule will actually behave. This requires handling many different element types across the periodic table and predicting Hamiltonians, the mathematical description of quantum energy levels. MOES-Pred uses molecule-specific noising strategies during training, tailoring the learning process to each molecule's unique properties, so the model learns what each molecule needs to stay accurate during learning. By excelling at both force prediction and downstream quantum chemical properties, it shows this tailoring works across different tasks. Together, these papers show that understanding electrons, bonds, and 3D structure as interconnected layers makes predictions more accurate, because chemistry is hierarchical: atomic structure determines electronic behavior, which determines molecular properties, so models must respect that chain.

Protein Binding & Interactions

A drug must bind tightly to its target protein to work, but not bind to off-target proteins, where it would cause side effects. Predicting binding strength is hard because it depends on the precise 3D fit between the drug molecule and the protein pocket—the shape, charge, and hydrogen-bonding patterns must align. When designing new drugs, you need to screen thousands of candidates and predict which will bind well, which is expensive to do in experiments. For protein-protein interactions (where two proteins must recognize and stick to each other), the screening problem is even larger: a cell has thousands of proteins, and you might need to predict millions of potential interactions.

Efficient Prediction of Large Protein Complexes addressed the hardest binding problem: predicting the 3D structure and interactions of enormous protein assemblies with thousands of atoms. It matched the accuracy of AlphaFold3 but used far less memory and handled larger complexes, which matters because many real biological structures are massive, and typical methods run out of GPU memory. Fast Proteome-Scale Protein Interaction Retrieval developed a fast method to find which proteins in a whole organism will interact with a target protein, screening millions of candidates in hours instead of months, because genome-wide studies need to test billions of pairs. I2Mole learned to capture the core substructures—the parts of molecules that are really important for binding—and used that to predict drug-drug interactions, which is relevant because many patients take multiple drugs at once and the drugs can interfere. KGOT unified two different types of information: a knowledge graph of known biological relationships and optimal transport, a mathematical tool for measuring distance between objects, to predict molecule-protein interactions with fewer false positives, because combining knowledge sources reduces guessing. Learning the Interaction Prior for Protein-Protein Interaction Prediction injected a geometric principle—that interacting proteins are complementary in shape—as a plug-in module, so any model could benefit without retraining, which means adding this principle doesn't require starting over. h-MINT improved binding affinity prediction by treating the binding pocket and ligand as a hierarchical network of interactions rather than flat lists of atoms, because binding happens through coordinated local contacts, not isolated atom pairs. Together, these papers show that binding strength depends on shape matching and chemical complementarity, and that learning these geometric principles separately from the base model gives faster predictions because separate modules train more efficiently, and more interpretable predictions because each module captures a specific chemical idea.

Fast Simulation & Molecular Dynamics

Once you know a drug binds to a target, you need to check whether it will move smoothly in the body, metabolize correctly, and not get stuck in the wrong places. These questions require simulating how atoms move over time—molecular dynamics—which is extremely slow because atoms vibrate and interact at tiny timescales. A real simulation might need billions of steps to cover one microsecond of real time. Machine learning can replace these slow quantum calculations with fast neural networks trained on reference simulations, but the neural networks must predict forces accurately across all positions the molecule might reach, including ones it has never been trained on.

Rapid Training of Hamiltonian Graph Networks Using Random Features trained models 150–600 times faster than before while keeping accuracy, because random features make the math simpler, and showed zero-shot generalization to systems with thousands of times more atoms, which means the model learned transferable patterns of how atoms push on each other, not just memorized specific sizes. Fast and Accurate Coarse-Grained Neural Network Molecular Dynamics simulated proteins not at full atom resolution but at coarse resolution—grouping nearby atoms into beads—which is cheaper because fewer objects means fewer calculations, and let it run 1000 nanoseconds per day on parallel computers, compared to microseconds per day at full resolution. A Cartesian-3j Framework for Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials showed that you don't need fancy spherical mathematics; simple Cartesian coordinates work just as well for predicting interatomic forces, making both training and running predictions faster because the coordinate system is simpler and standard libraries optimize it better. Geometric Graph Neural Diffusion for Stable Molecular Dynamics added a geometric constraint—preserving symmetries in molecular structure—which improved both accuracy and stability, especially when the molecule's topology (bonding pattern) changed during simulation. Mesh Based Simulations with Spatial and Temporal Awareness treated the simulation as a mesh of connected elements and learned which connections matter, improving long-horizon rollouts (predicting many steps ahead). PFT: Phonon Fine-Tuning improved vibration properties by tuning models after pretraining, raising phonon accuracy by 55%, which matters because how atoms vibrate affects material properties. These papers converge on one insight: molecular dynamics accuracy depends on learning stable, generalizable force patterns, and that speed comes from choosing representations (coarse graining, sparse connectivity) that capture the physics without unnecessary detail.

Cellular Response & Perturbation Prediction

When a drug enters a cell, it perturbs gene expression—it turns some genes up or down, which cascades into changes in hundreds of proteins and cell behavior. To know whether a drug is safe, you need to predict how a cell will respond to the drug's molecular presence. This is measured by sequencing: you take a cell, perturb it with a chemical or drug, wait hours, then measure which genes are turned on and how much RNA each produces. This is expensive and time-consuming, and you want to predict responses without running all the experiments—especially for rare cell types or combinations of drugs that no one has tested yet.

Adaptive Data-Knowledge Alignment in Genetic Perturbation Prediction trained a model to predict RNA levels (how much each gene is expressed) when you perturb a cell with a chemical, and aligned the model's learned patterns with known biological knowledge, so the model not only predicted accurately but also rediscovered known biology, which builds trust. CellBRIDGE learned how cells move through developmental stages when perturbed—tracking cell trajectories as they respond—rather than just predicting a single snapshot, which matters because cells' responses unfold over time. PerturbDiff: Functional Diffusion for Single-Cell Perturbation Modeling predicted how individual cells respond to perturbations using diffusion models (generative networks that gradually build up patterns from noise), generalizing better to untested perturbation combinations than existing methods, because diffusion models learn smooth transitions between states rather than memorizing discrete examples. What Makes a Representation Good for Single-Cell Perturbation Prediction systematically studied which learned features (representations) are most important for predicting combinatorial effects—when two perturbations are applied together, their effects don't simply add, because one perturbation changes how the cell responds to the second—and this paper showed how to represent that nonlinearity so models capture it. Together, these papers show that predicting cellular response requires models that understand both the snapshot of gene expression and how responses unfold dynamically, because cells change over hours as the perturbation cascades through the network, so a single time-point misses the actual response, and that learning features aligned with real biology improves both accuracy because biology is interpretable and more interpretable predictions because the model uses real biological concepts.

Crystal & Material Properties

Crystals are materials whose atoms are arranged in perfect repeating patterns—metals, semiconductors, ceramics. Their properties—hardness, conductivity, how much heat they absorb—depend on this regular lattice structure. Predicting crystal properties is harder than predicting molecular properties because the pattern repeats infinitely, and what matters is symmetry and long-range order, not just the local neighborhood of atoms. Quantum simulations of crystals (density functional theory) are slow and expensive, so machine learning models trained on simulated crystals can accelerate the search for new materials with desired properties.

Beyond Structure: Invariant Crystal Property Prediction with Pseudo-Particle Ray Diffraction developed a method that respects crystal symmetries—using pseudo-particles and ray diffraction concepts—to predict materials' properties, achieving top results on benchmark datasets, because symmetry is the deepest constraint in crystals and enforcing it helps models avoid learning spurious patterns. The paper on Diffusion Pretraining (using compressed representation spaces) for Crystal Property Prediction pretrained a generative model by learning to build crystal structures from noise on unlabeled data, then fine-tuned it for prediction tasks, and improved performance by 4–5% on real materials databases while maintaining accuracy even when training data is sparse, because pretraining on the structure itself teaches the model what crystals look like, so it needs fewer labeled examples to predict properties. MatRIS matched the accuracy of complex equivariant models while training 13 times faster, showing that carefully designed simpler models can reach the same quality if they're built to respect crystal structure symmetries, because respecting symmetry is more important than model complexity. MoMa: A Simple Modular Learning Framework for Material Property Prediction is a modular framework—mixing and matching components for different tasks—that improved average performance by 14% across 17 datasets and worked well in few-shot settings (learning from very few examples), because different material classes need different components, and modular design lets you pick the right combination. These papers show that crystal property prediction benefits from explicit symmetry constraints and modular, composable designs, which help models generalize to new crystal types because symmetry patterns repeat across all crystals, and modular components generalize because they isolate the knowledge needed for each task.
