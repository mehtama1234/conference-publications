Diffusion Negative Preference Optimization Made Simple

Train one diffusion model to learn from bad examples too, not just good ones, by flipping preference pairs instead of running two separate models.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Diffusion models paint images step by step, but they do not know which kinds of images humans like and dislike. Current methods teach them what is good by showing examples of preferred images. But they skip teaching what is bad. This paper shows how to do both with a single model: flip the preferred pairs upside-down during training, so the model learns to steer away from bad generations just as much as toward good ones, because presenting the same pair in both orders teaches both the pull-toward-good and push-away-bad directions.

Zero assumption version

One model learns only what humans want, never what they hate

Today's image diffusion models are trained on massive, messy internet data. They generate images that do not match what humans actually want—blurry faces, bad hands, wrong colors. To fix this, researchers collect pairs of images made from the same prompt, have humans vote on which is better, then train the model to copy the better one and avoid the worse one, because showing concrete good and bad examples teaches the model more efficiently than abstract rules.

But here is the trap: the model learns only from the winning image. It never learns what makes the losing image bad. A naive approach is to train a second model specifically on the bad examples, with inverted labels, then merge the two models at the end. But maintaining two models doubles the computational cost, and merging them weakens both signals—the good-preference learning gets diluted by the bad-preference one.

The world before: models trained only to imitate good examples, no explicit learning from bad ones. When it is time to generate images, two models must run, doubling the computational cost. Quality diminished by merging the two models together.

In one sentence. Train a single model to learn from both good and bad examples by flipping the bad examples during training, without the cost or quality loss of two separate models.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect preference pairs

Humans vote: image A is better than image B for this prompt. This direct comparison is more reliable than asking absolute quality scores, because comparing side-by-side is easier and more consistent than deciding a number on a scale.

Flip negative preferences

For the same pair, also train by saying: B is better than A (inverted label).

Use bounded objective

Prevent the model from collapsing to always picking one side; keep both signals alive.

Train one model jointly

Single model learns to push away from bad and pull toward good in the same pass.

Inference: no merging

Use the one trained model directly; quality is not diluted by combining two models.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the model learns from preference pairs. Instead of training two separate models or merging two models at the end, it trains a single model twice on each pair: once with the human-preferred order, and once with the order flipped.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: negative preference signal strength

measured quantity  =  Negative preference alignment

The hidden quantity is how strongly the model is steered away from undesirable images. Positive preference alignment points the model toward good images; negative alignment points it away from bad ones. Most methods measure positive alignment but leave negative alignment to chance. This paper explicitly captures negative signals by training the model to rank bad examples as worse.

The paper measures negative alignment by flipping preference labels during training and monitoring whether the model actually learns to suppress bad generations. Without a bounded objective, the model would collapse—it would just ignore one signal entirely. The bounded objective keeps both signals balanced, so neither overpowers the other, and the model learns genuine two-directional steering.

Why it works

Why one model can learn both good and bad at the same time

The mechanism rests on a simple idea: a preference pair teaches two lessons depending on which direction you read it. If a human says image A is better than image B, that is one lesson, because the difference between them shows which features matter. If you read it backward—B worse than A—that is the same information, just framed as an avoidance, because the same features that made B worse are what the model should learn to suppress. Training the model on both orderings makes it learn the full dimension: pull toward A, push away from B, because the model's internal numbers move in opposite directions on the two orderings, so both preferences get encoded.

The bounded objective is the linchpin. Without it, the model would optimize only one direction: either it always prefers the first image no matter which one you give it, or it always prefers the second, because unconstrained optimization finds the easiest shortcut. The bounded objective clamps the loss function so that both directions stay active. Think of it as a tether: the model is free to learn preferences, but not free to collapse into always picking one side. This keeps negative and positive signals in balance, so the model actually learns both steering directions inside a single set of weights, because the tether forces the model to find a solution where both orderings matter.

At inference, the model inherits both learned behaviors—it pulls toward good images and pushes away from bad ones—without needing a second model. No merging dilution, no doubled memory cost.

Math after the picture

The bounded preference objective

First in words:

Total loss is the learning objective on the positive preference pair, plus the learning objective on the flipped (negative) preference pair, but with a bound that prevents the loss on either pair from growing too large and drowning out the other.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_pos(x_better, x_worse) + L_neg(x_worse, x_better) + bound_penalty

Every symbol:

L_poslearning objective trained on the human-preferred pair (good, bad)
L_neglearning objective trained on the flipped pair (bad, good), capturing negative preferences
x_betterimage humans prefer
x_worseimage humans dislike
bound_penaltypenalty term that prevents either loss from dominating; keeps both signals alive
L_totalcombined objective the model trains to minimize
A tiny worked example
Image pair: (clear_face, blurry_face) is the preference; human prefers clear.
Positive objective: punish if model ranks blurry higher. Suppose loss is 2.5.
Negative objective: flip it; now train on (blurry, clear). Loss is 1.8.
Without bound: model could reduce L_neg to 0 by always picking blurry, killing negative learning.
With bound: penalty keeps both active. Total = 2.5 + 1.8 + small_penalty ≈ 4.4. Both signals survive.

The bounded objective is the key: it stops the model from collapsing into favoring one side always, so both pull-toward-good and push-away-bad learning signals stay alive in the trained weights.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

No bound, model collapses. If the bounded objective is too weak or missing, the model ignores one signal entirely—either always picking good or always picking bad. The two-way steering breaks.
Bound too tight, learning stalls. If the bound is too aggressive, neither signal gets strong enough. The model learns shallow preferences and makes only small changes.
Unbalanced preference data. If the collected preferences are skewed (most images look the same, or one type dominates), the model overfit to the easy cases and fails on rare ones.
Done right: set the bound by held-out validation. Use a separate set of preference pairs (not in training) to measure both positive and negative alignment. Tune the bound until both signals are equally strong.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny image diffusion model or use a pre-trained one. Collect or simulate a small set of preference pairs (e.g., 100 pairs: clear_image vs blurry, correct_hands vs malformed, good_colors vs washed_out). Train the single model on both positive pairs and flipped (negative) pairs, using a bounded preference objective. When generating new images, use the single trained model to generate images from prompts the model has not seen and measure: (1) how often the model produces bad images from the dispreferred class, because the negative preference training should suppress these (lower is better), (2) how often it produces good images from the preferred class, because the positive preference training should encourage these (higher is better), (3) total time to generate compared to a baseline that uses two merged models, because merging adds overhead.

quality_gain = (good_count_after / good_count_before) + (bad_count_before / bad_count_after), inference_speedup = time_two_models / time_single_model

The demo must show that a single trained model steering away from bad images produces equally aligned output to a merged two-model approach, and does it faster, because it avoids the quality loss and computational cost of maintaining and merging two separate models.
