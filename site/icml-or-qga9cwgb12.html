Zero assumption version

Teaching a fast generator from a slow one

Image generators like SDXL create pictures by slowly removing noise over many steps. To make them faster, you train a new model to do it in just a few steps, because fewer steps means the model runs much quicker on slower hardware. The slow model acts like a teacher, giving the fast student examples and feedback. But here is the trap: when the student makes a really bad picture, the slow teacher has never seen anything like it during its own training, so it gives bad advice. The fake teacher (a competing model running alongside to push diversity) also does not know how to help, because it too is trained from scratch on whatever the student is currently making, and if the student is lost, the fake teacher inherits that loss.

A naive approach: just follow whatever the slow teacher says. This breaks because the student wanders into bad regions early on, the teacher cannot tell it is lost, and the competition signal does not pull it back out. The student gets stuck making garbage and trains inefficiently.

In one sentence. When a student model falls into a bad training zone, both teachers are helpless, so explicitly spot bad zones and steer away.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the fake teacher (the competing model that provides repulsion) is trained. Normally it is trained equally on all examples. The paper makes it focus hard on the bad samples that the reward model flagged, making its repulsive signals especially strong in the forbidden zones, because a signal trained heavily on a specific failure mode learns to recognize and push away from that exact pattern.

the object it moves  =  Fake teacher training focus
The hidden quantity

Forbidden Zone: the hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Zone badness (from reward model)

A Forbidden Zone is a part of the training space where the slow teacher gives poor guidance and the student makes bad pictures. The paper detects it using a reward model (a separate pre-trained scorer) that evaluates each picture. Zones where the reward score stays low are forbidden.

The paper measures zone badness in two ways: first, by watching whether the reward score is improving as training goes on; second, by looking at the gap between what the slow teacher suggests and what the reward model says is actually good. When they disagree, the student is in a forbidden zone.

Why it works

Why focusing on bad zones fixes the problem

The core insight: when the student makes a bad picture, the fake teacher (the one that should push away from bad regions) is not trained hard enough to actually help. It sees the bad picture only once or twice per batch, so it does not build a strong repulsive wall.

AMD fixes this by explicitly training the fake teacher harder on low-reward samples. During each training batch, the paper reweights the fake teacher's learning so it focuses on the pictures the reward model flagged as bad. This makes the repulsive wall much steeper right where the student tends to wander—imagine building a cliff right in the forbidden zone—because a model that has seen a pattern many times learns to recognize and react to it with much stronger force. Now when the student drifts toward that zone, the repulsive signal is strong and pulls it back. The slow teacher meanwhile still provides the attractive pull toward good pictures. The result: the student stays on a safe path.

Importantly, this is self-correcting. If the reward model updates its view of good (though it does not change during training here), or if the student starts making new kinds of bad pictures, the zone detection auto-adjusts which regions to avoid.

Math after the picture

The math of zone-aware training

First in words:

Escape gradient equals the product of the reward model score and the repulsive gradient, plus the attractive gradient scaled by high-reward samples.

Then the compact form:

Escape = w_reward * gradient_repulsive + w_high * gradient_attractive ; where w = log(R(x)) if R low else 1.0

Every symbol:

w_rewardweight based on how low the reward is
gradient_repulsivesignal from fake teacher to push away
gradient_attractivesignal from slow teacher to pull toward good
w_highboost factor for high-reward samples
R(x)reward score of image x
A tiny worked example
Student generates 4 images with rewards: 0.3, 0.7, 0.8, 0.4 (on a 0-1 scale).
Image 1 (reward 0.3, low): repulsive gradient = 2.0, attractive gradient = 1.5. After AMD weight: escape = log(0.3) * 2.0 + 1.5 = -1.2 * 2.0 + 1.5 = -2.4 + 1.5 = -0.9 (strong repulsion).
Image 3 (reward 0.8, high): repulsive gradient = 0.5, attractive gradient = 2.0. After AMD weight: escape = 1.0 * 0.5 + 1.5 * 2.0 = 0.5 + 3.0 = 3.5 (strong attraction).
Result: bad samples get punished with negative gradients, good samples get rewarded. The net effect steers the student away from low-reward zones.

The math reweights the teacher gradients based on whether each sample is in a forbidden zone, so bad zones get strong repulsion and good zones get strong attraction.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reward model itself is bad. If the pre-trained reward scorer does not actually know what makes a good image, it will mark good regions as forbidden and steer the student wrong. AMD is only as good as the reward model.
New forbidden zone appears during training. If the student learns to make a new kind of bad picture that the reward model does not flag, AMD will not catch it. The paper assumes the reward model is fixed and covers the right failure modes.
Fake teacher overfits to zone repulsion. If the fake teacher spends so much time learning to push away from bad zones that it neglects general diversity, the student loses the competing signal and mode-collapses into a narrow slice of good pictures.
Done right. The reward model is pre-trained on real human feedback, the forbidden zones are common mistakes the model makes repeatedly, and the fake teacher is retrained gradually so it keeps its diversity signal.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a tiny student diffusion model to generate 28x28 images in 2 steps, distilling from a slow teacher. Use a simple pre-trained reward model (e.g. a small classifier trained to score image quality). Track three metrics: average reward score of generated images, average loss of the student, and the number of times the fake teacher is retrained on low-reward samples. Show that as AMD retrains the fake teacher more, the reward score goes up and the student loss goes down.

Escape quality = avg_reward_score - avg_training_loss ; Retrains_triggered = count(reward < threshold)

The demo must make it impossible to miss: when AMD detects a low-reward batch and retrains the fake teacher, the next batch of samples has higher reward score. That is zone escape.
