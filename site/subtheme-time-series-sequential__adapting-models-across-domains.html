Adapting models across domains

Time series models trained on one source (e.g., one hospital or factory) often fail elsewhere. Papers tackle domain adaptation, generalization, and alignment to new environments.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model trained on one hospital's patient data often fails on another hospital's data, even if they treat the same diseases. A model trained on one factory's equipment fails on similar equipment at another factory. A model trained on historical weather patterns for 2020 fails in 2024 because the climate has shifted. This is domain adaptation or distribution shift. The core issue is that the input sequences in the new domain are different from training data in systematic ways. The hospital might use different sensors. The factory might have different ambient conditions. The weather patterns have changed due to climate. If you retrain from scratch, you can handle these shifts, but retraining is expensive and requires lots of labeled data in the new domain.

The challenge is knowing what is truly invariant versus what is domain-specific. In healthcare, the biological principles of disease are invariant, but sensor calibration and patient demographics are domain-specific. In manufacturing, the physics of wear is invariant, but equipment models and operating conditions are domain-specific. A smart transfer approach learns the invariant part—say, how disease progresses—and adapts the domain-specific part—sensor offsets or population statistics. The harder case is when the domain shift is subtle. If a hospital's patient population becomes older, or more obese, or sicker, the distribution of vital signs changes in ways that are hard to characterize and adapt to without labeled data.

Papers in this cluster tackle alignment and adaptation. Some use domain adversarial training, which forces the model to learn representations that are useful for prediction but invariant to domain identity. Others use meta-learning: learn to learn in a way that transfers well. Some use unsupervised adaptation, where the model observes unlabeled sequences in the new domain and tunes itself to fit without ground truth. A few use causal models, assuming that the causal structure of a sequence is invariant even if the model's internal weights shift, and then adapt those weights. The underlying insight is that data in the wild is nonstationary—it drifts over time and across contexts—so models that can adapt are more valuable than models that are frozen.

How the work breaks down

Adapting models to new domains in time series breaks into three directions. First is aligning the model's reasoning across different time series, so it understands patterns in unfamiliar data. Second is learning which parts of the data are useful in any domain versus which are specific to one place. This matters because when you move to a new hospital or factory, you can reweight training toward good data or retrain selectively instead of from scratch, which saves time and labeled data. Third is generating synthetic time series that capture the domain's essential behavior without overfitting to specific conditions. These directions split because they target different levels: model reasoning, data quality and transfer, and data synthesis itself.

Aligning and reasoning across time series

When a model answers questions about time series from different sources or domains, it must align its understanding. A patient's heart rate pattern in one hospital system might be recorded in beats per minute; another hospital might record it alongside oxygen levels and blood pressure. The model needs to ground its reasoning in the patterns themselves, not in surface details like units or sensor specifics.

PATRA tackles this by learning to recognize and align the actual patterns in time series, then reason over them in a balanced way. Instead of treating every value equally, it identifies which parts of the sequence matter for answering the question—a sudden spike in heart rate, a sustained elevation, a slow decline—and uses those aligned patterns to answer accurately. This matters because when you move a model to a new hospital or dataset, the raw numbers change but the clinical patterns should stay consistent. By grounding the model in patterns rather than raw values, PATRA generalize better across different time series sources without retraining.

Assessing data quality and learning to transfer

When you have time series data from many sources—multiple hospitals, multiple factories, multiple weather stations—not all of it is equally useful for training. One hospital might have better sensors, cleaner records, or more complete patient histories, which means its data captures the true disease signal more faithfully. Another might have noisier data or missing values because its equipment or processes are less rigorous. If you train on low-quality data, your model learns to fit the noise and measurement artifacts, so the patterns it memorizes don't appear in cleaner domains. The challenge is sorting good data from bad without manually inspecting every sequence. If you had to hire an expert to label data quality, it would be slow and expensive. Doing it automatically means the model can learn what will transfer because it focuses on high-quality examples that reveal real patterns, not domain-specific quirks.

The LLM Judgment paper solves this by using a large language model to assign quality ratings to diverse time series, then meta-learning—learning how to learn—from those ratings. Instead of hand-labeling quality, the LLM identifies patterns like completeness (are there missing values?), consistency (do the values stay in a reasonable range?), and information density (does the sequence capture signal or mostly noise?). The model then learns to weight high-quality sequences more heavily during training, and to focus on features that appear in high-quality data across domains, because those features are more likely to be real patterns. They tested this on eleven different benchmarks across three kinds of tasks: classification, forecasting, and question-answering. On each one, quality-aware training improved how well models adapted to new domains, because models trained on curated data learned the underlying signal rather than domain quirks. The insight is straightforward: if you learn from high-quality examples, you pick up the real mechanisms, and those mechanisms work anywhere.

Generating and matching time series across domains

Time series generation—creating synthetic sequences that look realistic—is useful for domain adaptation because synthetic data lets you augment scarce domains or study what a model learns from. But many generative models rely on pairwise similarity: they compare each point to every other point to decide what sequence to generate next. This is slow and can miss important global patterns that span the whole sequence, especially when you're trying to adapt to a new domain with different patterns.

Winformer improves generation by transcending pairwise matching and capturing patterns that span longer ranges. It recognizes recurring structures and dependencies across the whole sequence, not just local similarities. This lets it generate time series that are more realistic and more faithful to domain-specific dynamics. When evaluated on twelve real-world generation tasks, Winformer outperformed the previous best methods by an average of 10.67%. For domain adaptation, this matters because better generated sequences can serve as both training data for under-resourced domains and as test cases to measure whether your transfer learning actually works.
