Nash Equilibria in Constrained Games

When multiple players each optimize their own choices but share a common constraint, a fair deal exists where no one wants to switch.

Zero assumption version

The shared-constraint trap

In real problems, multiple independent players compete or cooperate, each trying to optimize their own goals. Trucking companies minimize fuel cost. Traders minimize portfolio risk. Wireless networks maximize signal strength. The catch: they share a common resource or rule. Total fuel used across all trucks cannot exceed the city budget. Total trading volume cannot flood the market. Total radio power cannot exceed the FCC limit.

A beginner might hope each player can ignore the others and optimize alone—plug in the constraint and compute. But that breaks immediately: if one player bends the constraint to favor themselves, others lose their fair share. If all players optimize in isolation without coordinating, they often violate the shared rule or end up with an outcome where someone has a strong incentive to cheat. No stable deal emerges.

The method in one sentence. Prove a fair outcome exists where all players find their choices acceptable and the shared constraint holds (because fixed point theory guarantees it), then use an algorithm that nudges each player's choices toward higher rewards while a soft penalty pushes back if they approach the constraint boundary.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a feasible starting point

Initialize all players' strategies so the shared constraint is not violated.

Measure each player's incentive to move

Find which direction would improve each player's goal if they moved, because moving toward higher rewards makes sense only if it makes you better off.

Add a soft wall at the boundary

A log barrier method gently penalizes strategies that approach the constraint limit, so the algorithm avoids overshooting.

Adjust stepsizes adaptively

Move each player in the direction of higher rewards, but scale the step size differently: bigger steps when there is slack, smaller when near the edge.

Repeat until no one wants to move

Stop when the incentives for all players to move further shrink close to zero, signaling an acceptable deal has been found.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper identifies and updates the players' strategies (their action choices—routes, trades, power levels—and how much of the shared constraint each one uses) until all players agree no unilateral move helps them, because once no one wants to switch unilaterally, the deal is stable and no player needs to worry about being cheated.

the object it moves  =  strategies and constraint allocations
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: feasibility margin

measured quantity  =  Distance to constraint violation

The algorithm secretly tracks how close each player is to overshooting their share of the constraint. This distance is invisible in the problem statement, but it drives the algorithm's pacing: if a player has plenty of slack, the algorithm can take a larger step. If a player is already near the limit, smaller steps keep them safe.

The log barrier method estimates this distance and uses it to add a penalty when strategies drift toward the boundary, because a penalty that grows as you approach the edge naturally steers you away from breaking the shared rule. The algorithm then measures progress by watching how this margin shrinks and stabilizes: a vanishing margin signals an acceptable deal has been found.

Why it works

Why the algorithm converges

Each player is trying to improve their own goal (minimize cost, maximize profit) by moving their strategy in the direction of steepest ascent. But they cannot move freely—the shared constraint sits like a wall. The log barrier method builds a soft penalty that pushes back as any player nears the wall; the strength of the pushback increases the closer you get. This works like a spring: far from the edge it is relaxed, but as you approach it gets stiffer, redirecting players away from forbidden moves without locking them in place.

The adaptive stepsize then lets the algorithm be bold when there is plenty of room and cautious when any player is near the edge, because big steps when you have slack get closer to a good solution faster, while tiny steps when you are near the edge keep you safe. As all players nudge their strategies forward in parallel, the shared constraint stays satisfied, and their desires to move further (measured by how strongly their incentives pull them toward higher rewards) gradually shrink. When all incentives reach near-zero, no player wants to switch unilaterally—a stable deal has been found, and the algorithm stops.

The reason existence holds: under playerwise concavity, the set of all valid strategy combinations has special properties that fixed point theory exploits—a mathematical result that guarantees a fair deal exists somewhere. The algorithm then finds it by moving uphill on this well-structured space.

Math after the picture

The barrier-adjusted strategy update

First in words:

Each player updates their strategy by moving in the direction of their goal plus a term that penalizes getting too close to the constraint limit. The penalty strength depends on how far the log of the remaining slack is from infinity, scaled by a barrier coefficient.

Then the compact form:

theta^{t+1} = theta^t + alpha^t * (gradient_of_goal - barrier_coeff * gradient_of_log_barrier)

Every symbol:

theta^tplayer's strategy at iteration t
alpha^tadaptive stepsize at time t
gradient_of_goaldirection to improve each player's objective
barrier_coeffweight on the constraint penalty (how hard the wall pushes back)
gradient_of_log_barrierhow the penalty changes as you move toward the boundary
A tiny worked example
Truck 1: current cost gradient is 0.8 units/step, barrier gradient is 0.1 units (penalty as it nears the fuel limit). Net movement: 0.8 - (1.0 × 0.1) = 0.7 units.
Truck 2: cost gradient is 0.5, but it is close to its fuel limit, so barrier gradient is 0.4. Net movement: 0.5 - (1.0 × 0.4) = 0.1 units (it moves cautiously).
Truck 3: cost gradient is 0.9, barrier gradient is 0.05 (plenty of slack). Net movement: 0.9 - (1.0 × 0.05) = 0.85 units. Bold move because slack is high. All three stay feasible.

The barrier penalty acts like a spring that softly repels players from violating the constraint because the penalty grows (and thus pushes back harder) as you approach the boundary, while the uphill direction across their goal directs them toward better outcomes.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Non-concave games fail. If a player's goal is concave (curves downward like a valley) but the constraint is not, the fixed-point argument breaks, and equilibrium may not exist. The paper requires playerwise concavity, which is a real constraint.
Barrier coefficient tuning matters. If the penalty weight is too low, the algorithm ignores the constraint and strays infeasible, because a weak penalty is easily overridden by the goal-improvement direction. If it is too high, the algorithm gets stuck in the penalty and never improves goals, because the push-back from the barrier becomes so strong that no uphill movement is allowed. Finding the sweet spot is a tuning burden.
Convergence is slow for high precision. The algorithm's cost grows as roughly epsilon to the power of minus three to reach an acceptable solution with error smaller than epsilon, meaning tiny improvements near the goal take many iterations. For highly accurate answers, this cost grows fast.
With perfect information and adaptive stepsizes, convergence is guaranteed. The algorithm finds an equilibrium provably if players can measure their objectives perfectly and the stepsize adapts correctly. In practice, noisy measurements or fixed stepsizes can hurt.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple two-player game: players choose spending amounts to maximize a reward that depends on both choices, subject to a shared total-spending cap. Run the barrier algorithm and a naive independent-optimization baseline. Plot each player's objective over iterations and track whether the shared constraint is obeyed. The demo should show the barrier algorithm converging to a feasible, stable outcome where neither player wants to unilaterally deviate, while the naive method violates the constraint and leaves players unsatisfied.

stability_gap = max_over_players(how_much_player_would_gain_from_deviation), feasibility_gap = (total_spending_used - constraint_limit)

The demo must make plain that the barrier algorithm keeps the shared constraint honored because the log barrier penalty repels strategies away from the boundary, while also converging to outcomes where all players agree no individual deviation helps, proving the algorithm's core promise.
