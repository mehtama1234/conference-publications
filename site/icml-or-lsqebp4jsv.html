RL-Focal: Ensemble LLM Selection with Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning

When you have many AI models, use two separate reinforcement-learning agents to pick which ones to use for each task and then combine their answers smartly.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Running many large language models costs time and money. Rather than using all of them or manually deciding which ones to use, RL-Focal trains two reinforcement-learning agents (a kind of decision-making system that learns from rewards). The first agent learns which subset of models to pick for each new incoming task, balancing how much they disagree with how accurate they are. The second agent learns how to merge their answers when they conflict. The result is that a small team of selected models beats any single model, and adapts to each new task automatically.

Zero assumption version

The routing and fusion problem

You have ten smart language models (AI systems that answer questions). Should you ask all of them? That's slow and expensive. Should you pick your favorite three? That works OK, but a different task might need different models. Should you ask them all and average their answers? That treats a brilliant expert the same as a confused guess.

A beginner might try: pick the best-performing model on your test set and use only that one. It works, but the hidden issue is that different models are good at different kinds of reasoning. One excels at math, one at reading complex stories, one at spotting logical traps. Averaging all ten loses the specialists; picking one loses the others. You need a method that picks different experts for different jobs.

In one sentence. Train two learning agents: one picks a small customized team per task, the other resolves disagreements when the team gives conflicting answers.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Decider learns task-aware routing

For each incoming task, sample which models to include, then score how well they worked together based on accuracy and disagreement.

Reward signal guides selection

The Decider gets rewarded for picking models that: are accurate, disagree with each other, and work on small ensembles (not all ten).

Fusion learns conflict resolution

The Fusion agent sees conflicting model outputs and learns which one to weight higher based on task type and reasoning style.

Focal diversity measures correlation

Instead of treating all disagreements the same, focal diversity scores measure which disagreements matter (when good models differ) vs noise (when bad models agree).

Both agents adapt together

As Decider picks new team compositions, Fusion learns how to handle each team's particular conflicts, forming a dynamic feedback loop.

What the paper changes

The thing being changed

The paper changes which models get selected for each task and how their answers are weighted when combined. Rather than a fixed roster or a static averaging scheme, both the selection and the weighting adapt task-by-task as two reinforcement-learning agents learn from rewards.

the object it moves  =  model selection and answer merging rules
The hidden quantity

Focal diversity: measuring useful disagreement

measured quantity  =  focal diversity (error correlation structure)

When you ask three models a question, they might all give different answers, but that disagreement is NOT equally valuable. If all three are confused, their disagreement tells you nothing. If two are confident and correct and one is uncertain, their disagreement shows you that the third model is an outlier. Focal diversity measures the second kind: it scores whether disagreement comes from models being right vs models being wrong.

The paper estimates focal diversity by looking at which models agree on hard tasks vs easy tasks. If two models both fail on the same problem, they are correlated (they have similar weaknesses). If they succeed on different problems, they are diverse (they have complementary strengths). The Decider uses this measurement to pick model teams that have the right mix of agreement and disagreement — enough diversity to cover different reasoning styles, but not so much that half the team is useless.

Why it works

Why the two-stage approach works

The paper's core insight is that selecting and merging are two separate problems that require different learning. A single agent trying to do both gets confused: it would have to learn when to pick Model A vs Model B (thousands of combinations) AND how to merge any combination it picks. By splitting into two agents, each one becomes simpler and can specialize.

The Decider agent learns a routing policy: a rule that takes the incoming task description and outputs a probability for including each model. It improves this rule by getting a reward signal that says: pick small teams (to save cost), pick teams where models disagree on this task (diversity), and only pick teams that are actually accurate (performance). The key move is that this reward pushes the agent away from always picking the same 'safe' models — forcing it to explore diverse combinations.

The Fusion agent then assumes the Decider has already picked some team, and learns a merging policy: when these three models give conflicting outputs, how should I combine them? It sees a reward based on whether the merged answer is correct. Because it only controls the merging, not the team selection, it can learn a specialized weighting scheme for each team composition.

Focal diversity ties the two together: it tells the Decider which disagreements in past teams were actually predictive of good performance, so the Decider can favor those kinds of disagreements in future picks.

Math after the picture

The learning objective

First in words:

The Decider maximizes a reward that balances three goals: accuracy (how often the team gets it right), diversity (how much the focal diversity score says models disagree usefully), and team size (penalize large teams). The Fusion agent separately learns to weight models in each team to minimize answer error.

Then the compact form:

R_decider = accuracy(team) + alpha * focal_diversity(team) - beta * size(team)

Every symbol:

R_deciderThe reward score for a selected team
accuracy(team)What fraction of tasks the team got right
focal_diversity(team)How much models disagree in ways that matter
size(team)How many models are in the selected team
alphaHow much we value disagreement (a tuning dial)
betaHow much we penalize larger teams (a tuning dial)
A tiny worked example
Team X: Models A, B, C. They get 85% of tasks right. Focal diversity score 0.6 (moderate disagreement). Team size 3.
R_X = 0.85 + (1.0 * 0.6) - (0.1 * 3) = 0.85 + 0.60 - 0.30 = 1.15
Team Y: Models A, D, E. They get 87% of tasks right. Focal diversity score 0.2 (little disagreement, models behave the same). Team size 3.
R_Y = 0.87 + (1.0 * 0.2) - (0.1 * 3) = 0.87 + 0.20 - 0.30 = 0.77
Team X is rewarded higher (1.15 > 0.77) even though Team Y has higher accuracy, because X has better diversity. The policy learns to pick teams like X.

The Decider learns to pick teams that are good and diverse, not just accurate; the alpha dial controls how much disagreement matters vs pure accuracy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Focal diversity miscalculation. If the focal diversity measurement is noisy (based on too few task examples), the Decider picks teams with false disagreement that does not help, because the diversity score is unreliable and the Decider learned a routing policy based on that bad signal. The agents learn from incorrect signals and stall progress.
Overfitting to reward. The Decider learns to pick teams that fooled the Fusion agent on the training tasks, not teams that generalize to new tasks. If the reward does not match real-world performance (for example, if a metric of success is loose or domain-specific), the two agents can improve together on a mirage, because the RL training loop only sees the flawed reward and has no signal that it is wrong.
Computational cost from two-stage learning. Training both agents requires simulating many possible team selections and evaluations. If dataset or compute is limited, the agents may not converge before time runs out.
Done right: calibration on diverse tasks. Evaluate focal diversity on a held-out set of tasks, then train agents on a separate set. Use external benchmarks (like GSM8K, a math reasoning test) to verify the selection is actually picking smart teams, not gaming a narrow metric.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A runnable demo picks five models from a pool of ten language models and a task (like answering a multiple-choice reasoning question). It runs the Decider agent to sample a team, then runs the Fusion agent to merge the outputs. The metric is: does the merged answer beat the best individual model on that task? Repeat on twenty diverse tasks and measure the win rate and the average accuracy improvement.

improvement = average_accuracy(RL_Focal_teams) - average_accuracy(best_single_model)

The demo must show that RL-Focal teams match or beat the best single model on most tasks, and adapt (pick different teams) for different task types.
