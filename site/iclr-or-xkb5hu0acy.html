Hyperbolic Aware Minimization: Implicit Bias for Sparsity

HAM speeds up sparse learning by temporarily reshaping how weights respond to training nudges, helping useful weights escape zero while keeping beneficial sparsity.

The sparsity bottleneck

Training models to be sparse (mostly zero weights) is valuable: fewer weights means faster inference (when the finished model answers a new question) because the model has fewer arithmetic operations to perform, and fewer weights also means smaller files to store and load. But there is a catch: near zero, weights become frozen. The training algorithm tries to nudge a weight away from zero, but the nudge becomes tinier and tinier the closer the weight gets to zero. Weights that could be useful get stuck because the algorithm cannot push hard enough.

A naive approach tries to just add a penalty for non-zero weights (like saying penalties increase for each weight that is not zero). This does push weights to zero, but it also creates a grinding slowdown: any weight trying to move away from zero faces weaker and weaker nudges. The penalty plus the frozen responsiveness combine to trap useful weights at zero. So the model stays sparse but loses accuracy because it cannot activate the weights it needs.

In one sentence. HAM adds a temporary mirror step that rescales weight responsiveness near zero, letting useful weights escape zero faster because the training nudges become larger again.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Standard training move

Take one step with your normal optimizer (like gradient descent), nudging weights toward the loss reduction.

Step 2: Check the Riemannian metric

Measure how fast each weight moves in response to a nudge (this is called the Riemannian metric). Near zero, this metric is tiny—nudges barely move weights.

Step 3: Apply mirror descent

Use a mathematically different step (hyperbolic mirror descent) that rescales movements, making small metrics become normal-sized, unfreezing the bottleneck.

Step 4: Return to standard training

Go back to step 1 for the next iteration. You alternate: normal step, mirror step, normal step, mirror step.

Step 5: Measure the benefit

At the end, count zero weights (sparsity) and measure accuracy. HAM should improve accuracy compared to sparsity-only training because more useful weights escaped zero and are now active.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

HAM modifies the optimizer itself by inserting a mirror descent step after each standard update. The mirror step does not change the direction that training pulls weights; instead, it rescales how far weights move per nudge, especially near zero. This is a surgical change: only how responsive weights are to nudges changes, not which direction to go.

The hidden quantity

The Riemannian metric: how fast weights respond

measured quantity  =  Riemannian metric (step-size scaling)

The Riemannian metric is a hidden quantity that controls how far a weight moves in response to a training nudge. Think of it as the 'responsiveness' of each weight: a large metric means the weight moves a lot for a given push, a small metric means the weight barely budges. Near zero, sparsity-inducing training creates a very small metric, causing weights to freeze.

HAM estimates this metric using information about how the loss changes near each weight (called the Hessian or curvature). The paper shows that applying a mirror step on this metric estimate rescales the frozen weights back to normal responsiveness, unblocking their movement without sacrificing sparsity.

Why it works

Why the mirror step unfreezes weights near zero

Standard optimizers follow the gradient (the direction to reduce loss) scaled by a learning rate. Near zero, a weight with a tiny Riemannian metric does not move even if the gradient says 'go'. The metric acts as a brake: small metric, small steps.

HAM's mirror step applies a mathematical trick based on hyperbolic space. Instead of nudging weights in the normal way, it temporarily maps each weight to a hyperbolic version where tiny metrics become normal-sized. In this transformed space, a frozen weight can move. Then HAM maps back to the original space. The net effect: the weight moved in the original space, just through a detour. The sparsity-encouraging penalty still works (weights are still pushed toward zero), but now they can actually flip away from zero if the loss demands it.

Why does this work? In hyperbolic space, near-zero regions are stretched out. A weight at zero is infinitely far away, so there is no 'zero-is-close' trap. This breaks the freezing effect while preserving the push toward sparsity.

Math after the picture

The update equation with mirror descent

First in words:

The new weight is the current weight minus the learning rate times gradient, applied in hyperbolic space. The inverse Riemannian metric rescales this, so frozen weights become mobile.

Then the compact form:

theta_new = theta_old - lr * G_inv * grad_loss, where G_inv is the rescaled Riemannian metric from hyperbolic mirror descent.

Every symbol:

theta_oldcurrent weight value
theta_newupdated weight after one step
lrlearning rate (how big a step to take)
G_invrescaled inverse Riemannian metric (restored responsiveness)
grad_lossthe gradient, the direction to reduce loss
A tiny worked example
Weight w1 is at 0.02, gradient says move to 0.04 (increase by 0.02), learning rate is 0.1.
Without mirror: Riemannian metric G at w1 is 0.001 (tiny, frozen). Step = 0.1 * (1 / 0.001) * 0.02 = 0.1 * 1000 * 0.02 = 2.0. But the metric is inverted, so actual step = 0.1 * 0.001 * 0.02 = 0.000002 (frozen, barely moves).
With HAM mirror: hyperbolic mirror rescales G_inv to 0.1 (restored). Step = 0.1 * 0.1 * 0.02 = 0.0002. Weight w1 moves from 0.02 to 0.0202 (100x faster than frozen case).
The sparsity penalty still exists but weights can now escape zero if loss demands it.

The mirror step mathematically unfreezes weights by rescaling the inverse metric from tiny to normal-sized, letting the same gradient produce much larger steps.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Too many mirror steps. If the mirror step is applied too often or with wrong scaling, it can undo sparsity entirely, forcing weights away from zero. The balance (normal step : mirror step ratio) matters.
Wrong metric estimate. If the Riemannian metric is estimated poorly (e.g. the Hessian is computed inaccurately), the mirror step rescales incorrectly. The weight may move too much or too little. The paper uses curvature estimates which can be noisy.
Incompatible sparsification method. If the sparsity penalty is too steep (strong penalty for non-zero weights) or structured in a way that breaks the mirror-step math, HAM may not help because the mirror step may not be able to overcome such a strong opposing force. It works best with smooth, pointwise sparsity signals.
Done right. HAM works when applied in alternation (one normal step, one mirror step) with an accurate metric estimate and moderate sparsity pressure. The result: weights escape zero faster because mirror steps rescale responsiveness, useful weights survive and become active, and the final model is both sparse (many zeros for efficiency) and accurate (useful weights present and trained).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny neural network (e.g. a 2-layer network with 100 hidden units) trained on a small dataset (e.g. MNIST subset or synthetic data). Train it twice: once with a standard sparse optimizer, once with HAM. Log the fraction of non-zero weights over time and the validation accuracy. HAM should show faster growth in non-zero useful weights early in training—they escape zero sooner because the mirror step rescales their responsiveness—while staying sparse overall by the end. The demo should also show that accuracy is higher with HAM by the end of training, even though the final sparsity is similar, because more useful weights were activated during training.

speedup = (steps_to_reach_useful_weights_without_HAM - steps_to_reach_useful_weights_with_HAM) / steps_to_reach_useful_weights_without_HAM

The demo should make clear that the mirror step is not magic—it is a geometric trick that rescales how responsive weights are, unblocking the training algorithm from the zero-bottleneck without sacrificing the sparsity goal.
