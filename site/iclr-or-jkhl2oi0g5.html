BFM-Zero: Promptable Humanoid Control from One Shared World

One shared latent space lets a single policy control a real humanoid robot on any task without retraining.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

A humanoid robot can do many tasks: walk to a goal, copy a motion, respond to a reward signal. Normally, each task needs its own specialized policy. BFM-Zero learns one shared latent space (a common language) where motions, goals, and rewards all live together. This lets a single policy handle all three kinds of tasks without any retraining, and even learn new ones with just a few examples.

Zero assumption version

One robot, many specialists

A humanoid robot is like a musician who can play many songs. Today, we train a separate expert policy (a set of rules and weights) for each task: one policy copies motions, another reaches goals, a third responds to reward signals. When a new task appears, we retrain from scratch.

The naive fix would be to train one giant policy on all tasks at once. This usually fails because the tasks pull the learning in different directions—the policy learns to do none of them well. A policy optimized for motion tracking learns to track, not to reach goals. The two objectives clash.

The paper's move in one sentence. Learn one shared latent space where motions, goals, and rewards all speak the same language, so one policy can prompt any task without retraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Unsupervised learning first

Train on robot motions without task labels using a forward-backward model (predict next state, predict past state).

Map motions into latent space

Encode each motion trajectory into a fixed-length list of numbers in the shared space.

Add goals and rewards to the same space

Encode goal states and reward signals into the same coordinate system.

Train one policy to read prompts

Policy learns: given a prompt (motion, goal, or reward as a list of numbers), output the right action.

Test zero-shot and few-shot

New task appears: encode it into latent space, feed to policy, no retraining needed.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves from training separate policies to training one latent space that speaks all task languages at once. The single policy never changes; only what you feed it (the prompt in latent space) changes.

The hidden quantity

The shared latent space

measured quantity  =  Latent alignment across task domains

A latent space is an invisible map where similar things cluster near each other. Here, a motion that reaches a goal sits close to the goal state itself—they share coordinates. Rewards that reinforce similar motions sit nearby too.

The paper measures this alignment by training a forward-backward model (predict what comes next, predict what came before) on unlabeled robot data. If the space aligns motions and goals, a single policy can learn to read it. The paper validates this by checking if one policy can handle all three task types without task-specific retraining.

Why it works

Why one space works where three policies don't

Three separate policies pull learning in three directions and interfere. One shared space avoids that interference because all tasks speak the same language from the start.

The forward-backward model (unsupervised pretraining) builds a smooth map where nearby points in latent space represent similar behaviors. This map is task-agnostic—it learns motion structure without needing labels. Once that map exists, a single policy can learn to read it and route any prompt (motion, goal, reward) to the right action. A new task just adds a new prompt; it does not re-shuffle the space.

Domain randomization (changing physics parameters randomly during training) and asymmetric learning (stronger updates on certain motion types) help bridge the gap between simulation and real robot hardware, because when training sees many different physics setups, the learned policy becomes reliable against the real physics it will face. Reward shaping guides exploration toward useful behaviors early on by steering the learning toward regions of latent space that correspond to real, useful motions. Together, these prevent the one policy from overfitting to one task or getting stuck in poor local solutions, because it learns to navigate the whole latent space, not just one corner of it.

Math after the picture

The shared latent objective

First in words:

Loss = forward reconstruction error + backward reconstruction error + policy adaptation cost. Forward predicts the next state. Backward predicts the past state. Both forces the latent space to capture motion structure. Policy adapts to new prompts with small gradient steps in latent space.

Then the compact form:

L_total = ||s_{t+1} - f(z_t)||^2 + ||s_{t-1} - g(z_t)||^2 + lambda * ||grad_z L_task(pi(z))||^2

Every symbol:

z_tlatent vector at time t (the coordinates in the shared space)
s_trobot state (joint positions and velocities) at time t
f(z_t)forward model that predicts next state from latent code
g(z_t)backward model that predicts past state from latent code
pi(z)policy that takes latent prompt z and outputs robot action
L_taskloss for the current task (tracking, reaching, or reward)
lambdaweight controlling how easy the policy is to adapt (small lambda means easy zero-shot)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a motion trajectory gets encoded into latent vector z = [3.2, -1.5, 0.8].
Forward model predicts next state: ||true_s_next - predicted_s_next||^2 = 0.09. Large error means z does not capture the motion well.
Backward model predicts prior state: ||true_s_prev - predicted_s_prev||^2 = 0.04. Both errors add: 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.13.
Policy adaptation on a new task: gradient step on z costs lambda * 0.002 = 0.0002 (if lambda is 0.1). Small cost means the policy can easily specialize to the new task using just the latent prompt.

The forward and backward models squeeze all task structure into latent space; the policy just learns to read prompts from that space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Latent space collapses. If the forward-backward pretraining is weak, the latent space may encode motions poorly, and the policy cannot read them correctly. The fix is longer unsupervised pretraining or better forward-backward objectives, because more training time or clearer supervision signals force the forward and backward models to actually learn the structure of motion, rather than letting them cheat by ignoring parts of the trajectory.
Policy overfits to one task. If you train the policy too hard on motion tracking, it forgets how to reach goals. The fix is balanced training (sample tasks evenly) and gentle steering (small nudges to keep the policy from specializing too narrowly).
Sim-to-real failure. A latent space learned in simulation may not work on a real robot because real physics have friction, cable stretch, and sensor noise that simulation does not capture. Domain randomization and asymmetric learning help mitigate this by exposing the learning to varied conditions, but some tasks may still fail in the real world if the gap between simulated and real physics is too wide to bridge with just randomization.
Few-shot adaptation gets stuck. A new task may require a latent prompt from a region of space the policy never trained on. If the policy never visited that corner of the space, few-shot learning may get stuck in a bad local solution because it has no good weight values in that region to start from. The fix is better initialization (start closer to good solutions) or broader latent space coverage during pretraining (explore more of the map), because then the policy has seen many regions and can gracefully adapt to any new prompt.
Done right. Strong unsupervised pretraining builds a smooth, well-aligned latent space because forward-backward models learn the true structure of motions. Balanced multi-task training keeps the policy general because it avoids specializing to any one task. Domain randomization closes the sim-to-real gap because the policy learns invariances to physics variation. Few-shot optimization then enables rapid specialization to unseen tasks because the policy has already learned the latent space layout and just needs to adjust its reading head.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Simulate a humanoid robot in a physics engine (e.g., MuJoCo). Train a forward-backward model on unlabeled motion data. Encode three task prompts (a recorded motion trajectory, a goal position, a reward trajectory) into the latent space. Train one policy on a mix of all three tasks. Then test: feed the policy a new task prompt from a held-out test set (a motion it never trained on, a goal it never saw, a reward signal it never experienced). Measure: does the policy complete the task zero-shot, or does it need just a few adaptation steps?

Zero_shot_success = 1 if policy completes task without fine-tuning, 0 otherwise. Few_shot_steps = number of gradient updates needed to adapt to a new task in latent space.

The core thing to see: one policy reading a learned latent prompt beats training three separate policies. Zero-shot should work on at least 70% of new tasks.
