VideoPhy-2: Testing if Video Models Respect Physics

A dataset and automatic grader that exposes how poorly today's video-generation models handle real-world physics like momentum and collisions.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Video generative models (AI systems that create realistic videos from text descriptions) have become remarkably convincing — they can synthesize diverse scenes with correct lighting and motion. But do they actually understand the physical world? If you ask a model to show a tennis ball being hit, does it move correctly, or does it lag, disappear, or behave impossibly? This paper introduces VideoPhy-2, a benchmark dataset that tests video models on physical commonsense by asking them to generate videos of real-world actions, then checking whether the physics is right. The paper also trains an automatic grader that can quickly assess whether a generated video violates physical laws — no human annotation needed.

Zero assumption version

Video models look good but break on physics

Today's video generative models — systems trained to create realistic videos from text prompts — have gotten very good at visual quality. They produce detailed scenes with correct colors, lighting, and smooth motion. But they do not consistently respect the laws of physics. If you ask a model to generate a video of a tennis player serving, the model might show the ball moving impossibly (changing direction mid-air without any force), or it might show the ball continuing to move after it lands as if the ground did not stop it, or it might show the ball lagging behind as if it were not hit at all.

Existing benchmarks are too small or too simple to catch these failures. They lack fine-grained testing of specific physical laws (like conservation of momentum or mass), they do not involve human judgment to verify whether generated videos are physically plausible, and they do not provide automatic grading tools for researchers to quickly assess whether a new model is better or worse. Without a rigorous benchmark, researchers cannot see exactly where their models are failing, so they cannot target improvements.

In one sentence. VideoPhy-2 is a dataset of 4,000 prompts testing real-world actions, plus an automatic grader trained on human judgments to expose how poorly video models handle physics.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Curate seed actions

Start with 197 real-world actions (tennis serve, backflip, skateboard trick, etc.).

Generate detailed prompts

Use an LLM to create 20 different text descriptions for each action, yielding 4,000 prompts.

Generate videos from prompts

Feed prompts to three video models (HunyuanVideo, Cosmos, CogVideoX) to create videos.

Collect human annotations

Annotators rate each video on semantic adherence (does it match the prompt?) and physical commonsense (is physics correct?), plus classify which physical rules are followed or broken. Approximately 50,000 total ratings.

Train automatic grader

A multi-task neural network learns from human scores to predict semantic and physics ratings, and to classify rule violations.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper measures

The paper does not modify video models — it leaves them unchanged. Instead, it creates a new measurement tool: a grading dataset and an automatic evaluator (VideoPhy-2-AutoEval). The paper's object is the score that goes into a video — specifically, two separate scores: how well the video matches the text prompt (semantic adherence, 1–5), and how well the video obeys real-world physics (physical commonsense, 1–5). The paper introduces the joint score: a video gets a high joint score only if it scores at least 4 on both dimensions.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: rule fidelity

measured quantity  =  Rule violation rate

The paper measures which specific physical laws are broken in each video. Humans annotate whether each video follows, violates, or ambiguously handles physical rules like conservation of mass (an object does not disappear), conservation of momentum (a moving object keeps moving until a force stops it), contact dynamics (two objects cannot pass through each other), and gravity (objects fall). This is the rule violation rate — the fraction of generated videos that violate each physical law.

The paper finds that models are not uniformly bad. They handle some rules well (like simple gravity) but fail catastrophically on others (like momentum conservation). For example, a jetski might lag behind other objects when it should be moving at the same speed, revealing that the model struggles to enforce momentum. By breaking down failures by rule, the paper pinpoints exactly what physics understanding is missing.

Why it works

Why the automatic grader works

The core idea is simple: train a neural network to mimic human judgment. The paper collects approximately 50,000 annotations from humans who watch generated videos and score them. These 50,000 labeled examples become the training data. A multi-task model (a neural network with a shared backbone that handles multiple related tasks simultaneously) learns to predict three things from a video: a semantic adherence score (does the text prompt match?), a physical commonsense score (is the physics right?), and a classification of each physical rule (followed, violated, or unclear). Because the network is trained on real human annotations, it learns to spot the same patterns humans notice — a ball moving without being hit, momentum violation, objects passing through each other.

The mechanism relies on inter-task knowledge transfer — when a network learns multiple related tasks at once, the tasks help each other because they share low-level pattern detectors. Learning to spot semantic errors (e.g., detecting a person or an object) and learning to spot physics violations (e.g., detecting motion, collision, contact) both need to identify edges, motion direction, and temporal consistency. By training on all three tasks simultaneously (semantic score, physics score, rule classification), the network learns shared edge-detectors and motion-detectors that apply to all tasks, so the signals reinforce each other rather than competing. This is why a multi-task model outperforms three separate single-task models trained on the same data. Also, by training on video-language models like VideoLLaVA (which can reason about both video content and text), the automatic evaluator inherits both visual understanding and language grounding, allowing it to match prompts to videos and verify physics simultaneously.

The joint performance metric

First in words:

joint_score = fraction of videos where (semantic_adherence >= 4 AND physical_commonsense >= 4)

Then the compact form:

Joint = (count of videos where SA >= 4 AND PC >= 4) / (total videos)

Every symbol:

SAsemantic adherence score on 1-5 scale
PCphysical commonsense score on 1-5 scale
jointthe fraction of videos that pass both criteria
>= 4considered high quality, either in semantics or physics
A tiny worked example
Suppose 10 videos are generated. Five are rated: SA=5 (great prompt match), PC=2 (ball bounces oddly). Four are: SA=3 (prompt mismatch), PC=5 (perfect physics). One is: SA=5, PC=5 (excellent).
Joint performance counts only the last one: 1 out of 10 = 10% joint performance.
This strict metric reveals the core challenge: video models can nail visuals OR physics, but rarely both. High joint performance means the model truly understands both the prompt and how the world works.

The joint metric is strict: a video must score 4+ on both dimensions to count as success, because a model that makes pretty videos without physics is not useful for simulation.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Conservation law violations. Models are worst at momentum and mass conservation. A ball might disappear when it lands, or a moving object might stop abruptly without a collision. This suggests the model's training data is biased toward still-life scenes and does not deeply encode how motion persists.
Contact dynamics confusion. Objects sometimes pass through each other or fail to collide properly. The model does not learn solid contact as a hard constraint.
Scale and material breakdown. A heavy object might move as fast as a light one when hit with the same force. Material properties (rubber bounces, water splashes) are under-represented in training data.
AutoEval overfitting to benchmark. The automatic grader is trained on VideoPhy-2 videos only. It may not generalize to videos from new models with different visual artifacts or rare patterns of physical violations.
Done right. VideoPhy-2 succeeds by focusing on action-centric scenarios where physics is observable and testable (impact, fall, collision, throw) rather than ambiguous scenes. Human annotation of 50,000 examples ensures the ground truth is solid because multiple annotators rate each video and consensus scores filter out subjective disagreement, creating a reliable training signal. Open-source release of the dataset and AutoEval code allows researchers to improve because they can diagnose exactly which physical rules their models violate and build targeted solutions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A concrete demo would pick three video models (HunyuanVideo, CogVideoX, Cosmos), generate videos for a 100-prompt subset of VideoPhy-2 (focusing on actions where physics is clear: a ball rolling down a ramp, a person jumping, water splashing). Feed all generated videos to VideoPhy-2-AutoEval. Compute the semantic, physical, and joint scores for each model. Show a table of the automatic scores versus human ratings on a held-out test set, including their correlation (how well they agree). Finally, display side-by-side videos where models disagree with AutoEval, highlighting which physics rules AutoEval flagged as violated — this shows that AutoEval matches human reasoning and is interpretable, not a black box, because we can read off exactly which laws each model breaks.

Correlation(human_ratings, AutoEval_predictions) = Pearson r between human scores and model scores

The demo must make it impossible to miss: today's video models are visually convincing but physically incoherent. A ball bounces wrong, an object moves without being hit, momentum is not conserved. Only by measuring can researchers see exactly what to fix.
