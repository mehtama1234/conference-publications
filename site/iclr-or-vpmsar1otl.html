Textual Bayes: Uncertainty in LLMs via Prompt Sampling

Instead of tuning one prompt by hand, sample many prompts from a learned belief distribution and ask the LLM with each one, so you get both a better answer and a confidence score.

Paper explainersAll themesAlignment, Safety & Fairness

Large language models today give one answer, but they often hide uncertainty. When stakes are high—medical diagnosis, financial advice, safety-critical decisions—you need to know how confident the model really is. This paper treats the prompt itself as a dial to turn: by slightly tweaking the words and watching how the answers change, you can measure the model's true uncertainty, even in closed-source systems.

Zero assumption version

LLMs hide uncertainty behind one answer

Today's LLMs give a single response: yes or no, a number, a name. But the model may be guessing. In high-stakes domains—medicine, law, finance—a wrong guess with high confidence is dangerous. The problem is that most LLMs are black boxes: you cannot peer inside to measure how sure they are.

A beginner's first try: ask the LLM for a confidence score (e.g., rate your answer 0 to 100). This fails because the LLM will often guess a number that sounds reasonable, not admit it is lost. You need a signal that comes from changing the system itself, not just asking the model to introspect.

The core problem. One prompt, one answer, no way to see if the model is guessing or reasoning.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect a small dataset

Gather a handful of examples with ground-truth answers.

Define initial beliefs

Say what makes a good prompt in free-form text, e.g., be clear, ask for step-by-step reasoning.

Use MCMC to walk through prompt space

Start with one prompt, tweak it slightly, ask the LLM if the tweak improves answers on the small dataset, because only tweaks that help you fit the data are worth keeping.

Keep good prompts, reject bad ones

Build a collection of prompts that work well, weighted by how often they fit the data.

On a new question, sample and aggregate

Ask the LLM with each prompt in the collection, count how many say the same thing.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes which prompt you use. Instead of one hand-tuned prompt, it learns a distribution over prompts: a probability that each prompt-variant is the right one. This distribution comes from fitting to your data via Bayesian statistics.

the object it moves  =  prompt distribution
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: posterior uncertainty over prompts

measured quantity  =  prompt importance weight

The hidden quantity is how much each prompt-variant matters. In Bayesian statistics, this is the posterior probability: after seeing your data, which prompts are most likely to be the true good prompt? A high weight means that prompt was rewarded by your data; a low weight means it was punished.

The paper estimates this by running MCMC: each accepted prompt-variant in the chain contributes to the learned distribution. The more often MCMC accepts a variant, the higher its posterior weight.

Plain reading. Imagine you have five friends and you ask each to write you a text message. You collect all five versions and count how often your teacher says each one sounds good. The versions your teacher liked most are your best bet for the real thing.
Paper reading. In Bayesian terms, you want the posterior distribution: the most likely prompts after seeing your training data. MCMC samples from it by proposing prompt tweaks (via TextGrad) and accepting or rejecting them based on how well they fit the data.
Why it works

Why sampling prompts reveals true uncertainty

Here is the real causal chain: the LLM's answer depends on the phrasing of your question. Some phrasings are better, some worse. By tweaking the phrasing and watching what changes, you force the model to show you its real knowledge, not a memorized pattern. If the answer flips when you rephrase, the model was unsure all along. If the answer stays the same no matter how you phrase it, the model is genuinely confident.

Why is MCMC the right tool? MCMC walks through all the plausible prompt-variants, not just a random handful. It spends more time in regions where prompts work well (high posterior weight) and less time in regions where they fail. This biased walk ensures you explore the most useful part of prompt-space and ignore dead ends. By collecting many samples from this walk, you get a weighted representation of what a good prompt looks like, and you can measure how stable the model's answer is across all those good phrasings.

Math after the picture

The MCMC acceptance rule

First in words:

Accept the new prompt if a random number is below the ratio of how well each prompt fits the training data.

Then the compact form:

γ = min(1, p(D | θ_new) / p(D | θ_old))

Every symbol:

γacceptance probability, between 0 and 1
p(D | θ_new)how well the tweaked prompt fits all your training examples
p(D | θ_old)how well the old prompt fit those examples
θ_newthe candidate prompt after tweaking
θ_oldthe prompt you are currently holding
A tiny worked example
Old prompt answered 8 out of 10 training examples correctly (likelihood = 0.8).
New prompt answers 9 out of 10 correctly (likelihood = 0.9).
Acceptance ratio = 0.9 / 0.8 = 1.125. Since this exceeds 1, accept the new prompt with certainty.
Later: old prompt had 0.9, new proposal has 0.7. Ratio = 0.7 / 0.9 = 0.78. Flip a coin weighted 78% accept, 22% reject. If rejected, stay with 0.9.

The algorithm always accepts improvements and sometimes accepts small decreases, which lets it escape local dead-ends and explore the full space of good prompts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

MCMC convergence is slow. Walking through prompt-space can take hundreds or thousands of steps before the chain finds the high-value region. Each step queries the LLM, so cost grows quickly. For large datasets or very long prompts, this becomes impractical.
Approximations creep in. The true posterior over prompts is intractable, so MHLP uses approximations (e.g., a finite number of MCMC steps, a truncated TextGrad optimizer). These approximations introduce bias away from the true Bayesian answer.
Step size matters: tiny tweaks or large leaps both fail. MCMC only works well if the proposal (the step size of prompt tweaks) is tuned right. Tiny tweaks make the chain very slow to explore, because you explore inch by inch. Huge tweaks make the chain wasteful, because each new proposal is so different it usually fails. The paper uses TextGrad, which may not be the ideal tuning for every domain or prompt type.
Done right: cheap proposals. If you can use a fast open-source model to generate prompt tweaks instead of querying the expensive closed-source model for each step, cost drops by orders of magnitude.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple demo on a standard benchmark (e.g., a set of factoid questions with known answers). Implement MHLP using a free LLM (e.g., Llama-3.1). On each test question, run MCMC for a small fixed number of steps to collect a sample of prompts. Then ask your LLM with each prompt and measure: (1) the final answer's accuracy, (2) how much the answers vary across prompt-variants (big spread means low confidence, tight cluster means high confidence), (3) a calibration curve (does higher confidence rank match correctness?). Show that high-confidence answers are more often correct than low-confidence ones.

calibration = correlation(model_confidence, is_correct)

The demo must show that confidence (spread across prompts) tracks correctness (is the answer right). If confident answers are no better than uncertain ones, the method fails, because then you cannot use confidence to decide when to trust the model or ask for human help.
