Policy Newton in RKHS

A faster way to update RL policies by using a second-order optimizer on kernel-space decisions, with a clever math trick to avoid infinite-dimensional arithmetic.

Paper explainersAll themesReinforcement Learning

Most RL training nudges weights downhill in one direction at a time. Newton's method (a classic optimization trick) looks at how the loss slopes in all directions at once, then chooses the smartest step. Kernel methods (similarity-based functions that let us define policies without fixing their shape in advance) are flexible but slow to optimize. This paper combines both: it brings Newton's faster convergence to kernel-based policies by sidestepping a mathematical dead-end—explicitly building an infinite grid of numbers is impossible, so it uses a theorem to convert the problem into a finite grid that fits in memory.

Zero assumption version

Why current kernel-based RL stays slow

When an RL agent learns to make decisions, it updates its policy (its decision rule) by checking how a move turned out. A first-order method (like gradient descent) looks at the steepness of the error—the direction of fastest descent—and takes a step in that direction. It is simple but myopic: it never checks if the valley bends, so it often zigzags and wastes steps by trying many directions before settling on the right one.

A kernel method lets us define the policy on a flexible mathematical space (called RKHS, a reproducing kernel Hilbert space) without committing to a fixed number of parameters up front. But using a second-order optimizer (Newton's method) on kernel policies is considered intractable: Newton needs to build a full matrix of all second-order slopes (the Hessian, which describes how steeply the terrain curves in each direction) and invert it, and in kernel space that matrix is infinite-sized and cannot fit in memory.

The trap. First-order optimizers are slow; second-order optimizers are fast but considered impossible for kernel policies because the Hessian is infinite.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces first-order gradient updates (nudges in the steepest-descent direction) with second-order Newton updates (which account for how the loss curves in all directions). This makes the policy converge faster because Newton's method sees the shape of the entire hill or valley, not just its local slope.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Local curvature structure

measured quantity  =  Curvature information (the Hessian matrix)

The Hessian is a matrix that measures how much the loss changes in all directions at once, not just downhill. In RL, different weight directions matter unequally: some weights have steep cliffs (moving them wastes effort), others have gentle slopes (safe to move far). The paper estimates this structure using a cubic-regularized auxiliary objective, which is a mathematical way of capturing second-order slopes without computing them directly.

The Representer Theorem (a classical mathematical result) proves that the optimal policy can always be written as a weighted sum of kernel evaluations at the trajectory data points. So instead of an infinite Hessian (one row for every possible input), the paper only needs an N-by-N Hessian (one row per collected sample), making it computable.

Why it works

Why second-order optimization works on kernel policies

Newton's method works faster than gradient descent because it uses curvature information: it asks not just 'which way is downhill?' but 'what is the shape of the terrain?' A steep slope means you can take a big step without overshooting; a flat slope means take a tiny step to stay accurate. Standard Newton builds the full Hessian and inverts it, which costs cubic arithmetic (much work for large matrices) and is infeasible.

This paper sidesteps that trap using the Representer Theorem, a classical result stating that the optimal solution to a kernel optimization problem lives entirely in the span of kernel-evaluated data points. In other words: the best policy can always be written as a weighted average of kernel values at your collected trajectories. This means you do not need to consider the infinite space of all possible kernel evaluations; you only need to track N weights (one per sample). The Hessian shrinks from infinite to N-by-N. The paper then uses a cubic regularized objective (a mathematical proxy for the Hessian) to make the optimization numerically stable. The combined effect: Newton's curvature-aware updates are now computable, convergence is quadratic (distances to optimum roughly square at each step), and the agent reaches good policies in many fewer steps than first-order methods.

Math after the picture

The Newton step in finite dimensions

First in words:

The weight update equals: the learning rate multiplied by the inverse Hessian multiplied by the gradient. Symbols: theta_new equals theta_old minus (one divided by the Hessian) multiplied by the gradient of the loss.

Then the compact form:

theta_new = theta_old - (1/H) * grad_loss

Every symbol:

theta_oldcurrent policy weights
theta_newupdated policy weights after Newton step
HHessian matrix (N-by-N curvature information)
grad_lossgradient (direction of steepest descent)
1/Hinverse of the Hessian (asks: how much to move in each direction)
A tiny worked example
Suppose gradient is [4, 2] (first weight is steeper). Hessian is [[2, 0], [0, 1]] (first direction curves more than second).
Inverse Hessian is [[0.5, 0], [0, 1]] (flat direction gets bigger step).
Newton step: [0.5, 0; 0, 1] * [4, 2] = [2, 2]. First weight steps 2 units (half of gradient 4) because it is steep. Second weight steps 2 units (same as gradient 2) because it is flat. Net effect: Newton compensates for curvature, taking bigger steps in flat directions.

The Representer Theorem makes H computable: only N-by-N instead of infinite.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Small sample size breaks the trick. The Representer Theorem only promises that the optimal solution lives in the span of collected samples. If you have very few trajectories, you lack coverage: the optimal policy might require a direction you never sampled. The finite Hessian then becomes rank-deficient and cannot be inverted. Fix: collect enough diverse data before solving.
Kernel choice matters. The entire approach depends on choosing a kernel (a similarity function) that captures the task structure. A kernel that ignores important state features will produce a Hessian that is uninformative. Fix: tune the kernel or use a kernel family known to work on your task domain.
Cubic regularization must be tuned. The cubic-regularized auxiliary objective is only a proxy for the true Hessian. If the regularization weight is too high, the method becomes too conservative (steps too small). If too low, numerical instability creeps in. Fix: validate the regularization parameter on a validation set.
Done right. With sufficient, diverse trajectory data, an appropriate kernel, and careful regularization tuning, Policy Newton converges quickly with theoretical guarantees on local quadratic convergence rate.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal demo should run an RL agent on a simple task (e.g., asset allocation on synthetic price streams, or a toy continuous control problem) using both first-order (gradient descent) and second-order (Policy Newton) optimizers on the same kernel-based policy class. Plot the episodic reward over training steps, and measure the number of steps to reach a target performance threshold (e.g., 90% of max reward). The demo must show that Policy Newton reaches the threshold in far fewer steps than first-order (e.g., 50 vs 200 steps), because curvature-aware steps move closer to the goal each iteration, reducing the zigzagging that plagues first-order methods.

steps_to_threshold = iteration at which episodic_reward >= 0.9 * max_reward_ever_seen

If Policy Newton requires 50 steps and gradient descent requires 200 steps to reach the same reward on the same problem, the curvature-aware method is working as promised.
