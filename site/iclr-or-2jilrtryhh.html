Transformers as Measure-Theoretic Associative Memory

A Transformer that learns to pick the right group of tokens from a long, mixed pile based on what a query is asking for, provably does it well.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Transformers (a type of neural network that reads sequences of words or data points) retrieve information by scanning through context, but how they learn to do this from data was not clearly understood mathematically. This paper shows that a Transformer with learned attention (not fixed rules) that works on groups of words rather than single ones can provably learn to find and use the right group.

Zero assumption version

Why is retrieval from long context so hard to understand?

A Transformer reads a list of tokens (words or data points) and picks which ones matter for answering a question. In real life, that list can be very long and may contain many topics mixed together. For example, a document might talk about finance in one section and then sports in another, and the model must grab only the finance part when asked about money.

Before this paper, people built Transformers with either rules baked in (a frozen filter, a hardcoded way to pick relevant items) or with attention that could only average things together smoothly. But real Transformers use softmax attention, which can make sharp, spiky choices: pick this word, ignore that one. How a Transformer learns these sharp choices from data, mathematically, was a mystery.

A beginner might assume the Transformer just memorizes all the data, or that learning retrieval is too complicated to analyze. The problem is: we need proof that it actually works and that it does not need an impossible amount of training data.

The paper's move in one sentence. A Transformer with learned softmax attention learns to both identify which group of tokens is relevant and extract the answer from that group, with provably optimal sample efficiency.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Layer 1: embed and prepare

The first MLP layer turns raw tokens into richer features so the attention layer can work with them.

Layer 2: compute query scores

Attention compares the query against each token group using learned weights, assigning high scores to matching groups.

Layer 3: softmax concentrates

Softmax normalizes scores into probabilities, putting nearly all the weight on the one group that matches the query best.

Layer 4: extract the group

The model averages all tokens in the matched group, weighted by attention, to form a summary of the relevant information.

Layer 5: predict from the group

A second MLP layer takes that summary and produces the final answer.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper studies what happens when you train a Transformer to map from mixtures of groups (distributions) to predictions, where the Transformer must first recall the right group and then predict the answer. It shows that learned softmax attention can do both, sharply and efficiently.

the object it moves  =  attention weights
The hidden quantity

Sparse recall: the key hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  sparsity of group identification

Sparse recall means the attention mechanism focuses its weight on one relevant group and nearly ignores the rest. It is not a smooth, distributed attention that spreads weight across all groups; it is sharp, like a sharp spotlight on one pile and darkness everywhere else.

The paper measures sparsity by asking: how well do the learned attention weights concentrate on the right group? The method uses spectral properties of the data distributions to guarantee that softmax attention learns to give nearly all its weight to the matching group, even when the groups are mixed together in one long stream.

Why it works

Why the Transformer learns to recall sharply

The key is that softmax attention is learned, not frozen. When you train it on many examples where each input is a mixture of groups and you ask for predictions from one group, the Transformer learns that high scores on matching tokens and low scores on mismatched ones will minimize loss.

Softmax then converts those scores into probabilities: a small gap in scores becomes a huge gap in probabilities. If one group gets a score of 2 and another gets 1, softmax gives the first group 88% of the weight and the second only 12%. If the gap widens to score 2 versus 0, the first gets 99.8%. This nonlinearity is what creates sharp, spiky attention.

The paper proves that with enough training data, the learned scores will be large enough that softmax creates this sharp concentration on the right group. The proof uses spectral assumptions about how the different groups spread out in feature space, ensuring that the learned features actually separate the groups so attention can distinguish them. This is why you cannot do it with frozen kernels or linear attention: they cannot create the sharp concentration. Only learned softmax can.

The loss and how softmax sharpens it

First in words:

loss = squared difference between true answer and predicted answer, where prediction is the integral (average) of the recalled group weighted by attention

Then the compact form:

Loss = (y_true - integral of value_retrieved under attention)^2, where attention weights sum to 1 via softmax and concentrate on the right group

Every symbol:

y_truethe true scalar output for this query and mixture
integral of value_retrievedthe average of token contents from the matched group
attention weightslearned parameters that assign high score to matching group, converted to probabilities by softmax
softmaxfunction that turns scores into a probability distribution, making big gaps in scores into huge gaps in probabilities
A tiny worked example
Suppose the mixture has two groups: finance (tokens 1,3,5) and sports (tokens 2,4,6).
The query asks for finance information.
Learned scores: finance tokens get score 3, sports tokens get score 0.
Softmax with temperature 1: score 3 becomes weight exp(3)/(exp(3)+exp(0)+exp(0)) ≈ 0.95 per finance token (≈3 tokens × 0.95 = 2.85 total weight on finance), sports tokens get ≈0.05 each (3 tokens × 0.05 = 0.15).
The model extracts nearly all information from finance tokens and almost none from sports.
Gradient update: if finance tokens gave a wrong answer, the loss gradient targets the learned scores for finance tokens, strengthening their scores further.
After many examples: finance-query pairs converge to score 3+, sports-query pairs converge to score 0 or negative, sharpening the split.
Error decays exponentially as scores widen.

The softmax nonlinearity is the only mechanism that can turn learned score differences into sharp probability spikes and thus perfect recall.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Groups are not separable in feature space. If the input distributions do not have a spectral gap (the assumptions fail), then even learned softmax may not separate them. The learned scores could fail to create a big gap, so softmax cannot concentrate sharply.
Not enough training data. The theorem requires a sample complexity that depends on the dimension of the space and the spectral gap. If you have too few examples, the learned weights may not settle into the optimal scores.
Multiple groups are equally relevant. If the query could match two groups equally well, softmax will split attention between them, and recall is no longer sharp. The method assumes one group is clearly the target.
Spectral gap holds, sufficient data. The Transformer learns optimal, exponentially sharp recall and prediction, with sample complexity that does not grow with context length.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple experiment: generate a mixture of two Gaussian distributions in 2D, where each distribution encodes a different class or topic. Train a shallow Transformer (or softmax-attention model) on pairs of (mixture, query direction as a list of numbers, class) to predict which distribution the query came from and output a statistic of that distribution.

Measure two things: (1) Does attention weight concentrate on the right distribution as training progresses? Plot the attention weight on correct vs. incorrect samples over epochs. (2) Does the final prediction error decay exponentially with the number of training samples? Rerun with 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600 samples and plot log-error vs. sample count on a semilog scale. The line should be straight (exponential decay).

The demo makes impossible to miss that learned softmax attention sharply focuses on one group and that the Transformer achieves the sample complexity predicted by theory.

error = sqrt(mean squared difference between true group statistics and predicted group statistics); attention_concentration = average weight on correct group minus average weight on incorrect group

The exponential decay in error as sample count grows, matched against the theoretical prediction, is the smoking gun proof that learned softmax attention is optimal for this task.
