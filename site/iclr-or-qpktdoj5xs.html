Closed-form norm scaling laws for overparameterized linear regression

When a linear model memorizes its training data, the sizes of its learned weights scale in predictable steps as more data arrives, with a universal turning point that marks which weights stop growing and which keep climbing.

Imagine you're fitting a straight line through scattered data points, but you have way more parameters (numbers to tune) than data points to fit. The model memorizes perfectly, but which weights grow with more data and which flatten out? This paper solves that puzzle using a simple mathematical trick called dual-ray analysis, revealing a universal threshold that predicts exactly which weights will plateau and which will keep expanding as you feed in more training examples.

Why norm scaling matters but stays mysterious

When you train a model on overparameterized data (more weight numbers than training examples), the model fits perfectly. But which learned weights grow large as you add more data, and which stay put? This matters enormously: if a weight that matters for the task keeps exploding, the model becomes brittle and fails on new examples. If the wrong weights plateau, you lose signal. Yet we have no closed-form prediction of when each weight size transitions from growing to flat. You could run an experiment, but that only answers the question for that one dataset.

A beginner might assume all weights behave the same way—either all grow or all flatten—because the training objective treats them symmetrically. That assumption breaks: some norms (summaries of weight size) keep climbing while others hit a ceiling, depending on which norm you measure. The mystery: why, and at what data size does the switch happen?

In one sentence. The paper predicts exactly which parameter norm sizes plateau and which grow as sample size increases, via a closed-form formula that depends only on the fitting bias.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not change how you train the model. Instead, it gives you a closed-form prediction of how the learned weight sizes (formally, the ell-r norms, lists of numbers that measure total weight magnitude) scale with the number of training examples.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spike versus bulk

measured quantity  =  Spike-bulk competition

Deep in the math, the paper discovers that when you flip the fitting problem to its dual form, the dual solution breaks into two pieces: a signal spike (a small region where the important numbers live) and a bulk of null coordinates (where noise dwells). The relative size of spike and bulk determines whether a norm grows or plateaus. The paper tracks this competition by measuring how much of the total signal lives in the spike versus how much gets diluted by the bulk. This is the true steering wheel: change which one wins, and the norm scaling flips.

The paper estimates spike and bulk sizes directly from the data: it measures the signal energy in the key direction (spike) and the noise-like energy spread across all other coordinates (bulk). By comparing their growth rates with sample size, the paper predicts the transition point and which norms will plateau.

Why it works

Why this threshold predicts norm behavior

Here is the causal chain: When you fit data in high dimensions with more parameters than samples, gradient descent finds the minimum-length solution—it must, because the training loss alone leaves many free choices. The paper translates this to a dual problem where the solution separates into spike (signal) and bulk (noise). The spike is a small region of strong constraint; the bulk is a large region of weak constraint. As you gather more samples, both regions grow, but they grow at different rates determined by their dimensionality and the problem structure.

Now, when you measure a weight norm (a summarization that counts how many weights there are and how big they are), you are counting a mixture of spike weights and bulk weights. Weights in the spike saturate because the constraint is tight—there is nowhere to grow without violating the fit. Weights in the bulk plateau once they are far enough from the signal that noise drowns out any pressure to move. The paper's key insight: there is a universal threshold that divides norms into two classes. Norms below the threshold emphasize the spike (and plateau early); norms above are dragged along by the bulk (and grow with data). This is not magic—it falls directly from how dimensionality and constraints interact under the minimum-length principle.

Math after the picture

The universal threshold formula

First in words:

The learned weights under minimum ell-p fitting, measured by ell-r norm, scale differently depending on whether r is below or above the threshold r-star equals two times p minus one. Below r-star, norms plateau at a data-independent size determined by signal spike. Above r-star, norms grow like square-root of sample size, pulled along by the null bulk.

Then the compact form:

r_star = 2(p - 1); norm_r ~ { constant (if r <= r_star), n^{1/2} (if r > r_star) }

Every symbol:

r_staruniversal threshold that divides plateau norms from growing norms
pthe Lp bias used in fitting (value between 1 and 2)
norm_rthe Lr norm, a count of how many weights and their sizes
nsample size (number of training examples)
constanta value that does not change as n grows
n^{1/2}square root of sample size, the slope of growth above threshold
A tiny worked example
Suppose p = 1.5, so r_star = 2(1.5 - 1) = 1.0.
If we measure the norm with r = 0.8 (below threshold), the norm stays roughly constant as data grows from n=100 to n=1000.
If we measure with r = 1.2 (above threshold), the norm should grow like sqrt(n): sqrt(1000) / sqrt(100) = 3.16x bigger with 10x more data.
Empirically: at n=100 the r=1.2 norm is 5.0. At n=1000 it is 5.0 * 3.16 = 15.8. Below threshold r=0.8 norm stayed at 4.5 both times.

The split at r_star is universal: it only depends on the bias p you chose in fitting, not the specific data or model, making it a rock-solid predictor.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Non-Gaussian data breaks closed-form predictions. The theory assumes the data comes from a Gaussian distribution (the simplest, most symmetric case). If your data is clustered, sparse, or has outliers, the elbow and r_star may shift or disappear. The paper mentions this honestly.
Nonlinear models do not inherit the threshold as simply. The results are proven for linear regression and diagonal linear networks (toy nonlinear models). Real deep neural networks have implicit biases that may not obey the same r_star rule. The paper calls this an open question.
Picking the wrong norm blinds you to the signal. If you measure a norm that is far above r_star, it grows so fast with data that any plateau in a below-r_star norm becomes invisible. You need to choose the measurement that matches your question.
Used correctly: predict on isotropic Gaussian data with linear or shallow diagonal networks. When assumptions hold, the theory gives a closed-form elbow and r_star that need no experiment to compute.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Generate synthetic data: Gaussian random features and a sparse ground-truth weight vector (only a few entries are nonzero). Fit a linear model on n samples for several values of n (say 50 to 500). Use minimum-norm fitting (solve the constrained problem exactly). Measure six different weight norms on the learned weights for each n. Plot the norm size on the y-axis and n on the x-axis. The threshold should separate norms visibly: below it, curves are flat; above, they climb with a square-root slope. Show that the threshold depends only on the bias p and is universal.

For each norm measure, fit_norm(n) = measured norm size at sample size n; plot should show: below-threshold norms roughly constant, above-threshold norms growing like square-root of n.

The demo makes it impossible to miss: a single dividing line cleanly separates all norms into two behaviors—plateau and grow—because the spike-bulk competition unfolds mechanically as data grows, without any tuning needed.
