ReLaSH: Generating Synthetic Hypergraph Data

To build realistic synthetic hypergraphs with attributes, train a model to compress the data into a shared compressed space, then sample from that space using a distribution-free method.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

A hypergraph is a network where each connection can link more than two things at once. In a regular network, each edge connects exactly two things. Generating synthetic hypergraphs—realistic copies of real hypergraphs that preserve patterns but are not copies—is hard because hypergraph data has three quirks: sparse connections (most possible edges don't exist), mixed data types (some attributes are numbers, some are categories), and discrete choices (each link is either present or not). ReLaSH solves this by working in a compressed space.

The naive approach and why it breaks

A hypergraph is a network of multi-way relationships. A regular network has edges like 'Alice knows Bob'—two entities. A hypergraph can have 'Alice, Bob, and Carol worked on Project X together'—any number of entities in one connection. Real hypergraphs appear in social networks (groups of friends), biology (genes that interact), and medicine (patients with multiple symptoms).

If you try to build synthetic hypergraphs with standard methods—the tools used for regular graphs or simple data—they fail. Most generative models assume all data points are the same type or follow a simple pattern. Hypergraphs are not simple: each connection has a discrete structure (who is in it), and each connection has attributes (metadata like time, weight, category). Building a model that respects both at once, while accepting that most possible connections don't actually exist, breaks existing approaches.

The method in one sentence. ReLaSH compresses the observed hypergraph connections and their attributes into a shared compressed space (a list of numbers for each connection), then samples new compressed versions from that space and decodes them back into realistic synthetic hypergraph data.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compression step

Train a model that compresses each observed hyperlink and its attributes into a single list of numbers.

Space reconstruction

Learn what the space of valid lists looks like—which regions are dense, which are sparse, without assuming a named shape.

Sampling

Draw new random lists from the reconstructed space using the method.

Decoding

Feed each sampled list back through the trained compression model to get out a new hyperlink and its attributes.

Output

Assemble the decoded hyperlinks and attributes into a new synthetic hypergraph.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how you represent hypergraph data for generation. Instead of trying to generate hypergraph data directly (which is hard because the data is discrete and sparse), ReLaSH generates lists of numbers in a learned compressed space, where the data looks continuous and dense, then decodes back.

The hidden quantity

The joint compressed space

measured quantity  =  latent vector (compressed representation)

The compressed space is a list of numbers that encodes both the hyperlink structure (which entities are in the connection) and its attributes (metadata like time or category). The likelihood model learns to map observed hyperlinks and attributes to these lists, and to decode them back without losing information.

The paper measures success by checking whether samples from the reconstructed compressed space decode into hypergraphs that match the original in structure (entity frequencies, group sizes) and attribute distributions (time ranges, categorical mixes).

Why it works

Why this works: the causal chain

The core insight: real hypergraph data is sparse and discrete in the original space, but when you compress it into a learned compressed space, it becomes denser and more regular. This makes it much easier to sample from, because sampling algorithms work best when data sits in thick clouds rather than scattered points, and when you can draw from a continuous space rather than making discrete choices.

Here is the chain. First, the likelihood model learns to map each observed hyperlink-plus-attributes pair to a list of numbers by minimizing error—the model learns to compress without losing information. This creates a compressed space where the original data sits. Second, that space is now easier to work with: the lists are continuous (infinitely many possible combinations), and the observed data is denser (more points per region) than in the original sparse setting. Third, the distribution-free method learns to estimate which regions of that compressed space correspond to real hyperlinks, without assuming a bell curve, triangle, or any other named shape. Fourth, when you sample from that learned space and decode, the model's learned reverse mapping produces realistic new hyperlinks because it is biased by training to respect the patterns it has seen.

The compression and decoding equation

First in words:

likelihood = sum over all observed pairs of log probability that the model assigns to that hyperlink and its attributes. The model learns to maximize this, so it compresses to a latent vector and back without losing information.

Then the compact form:

LL = sum_i log p(hyperlink_i, attributes_i | parameters)

Every symbol:

LLhow well the model explains all the data
ieach observed hyperlink and its attributes
hyperlink_ithe entities in that group
attributes_ithe metadata (time, category, weight, etc.)
parametersthe model settings (the adjustable numbers inside the model)
p(...|parameters)the probability that the model assigns to that data given its settings
A tiny worked example
Observed: Hyperlink {A, B, C} with time=morning, weight=2.
Model compresses to [0.8, -0.3, 0.5, 0.2, 0.9] (5 numbers).
Likelihood model assigns probability 0.85 to this pair.
Contribution to total: log(0.85) = -0.163.
Repeat for all 1000 observed hyperlinks; sum all to get total.
During training, model adjusts settings to raise this sum (makes high-probability things even higher).
Once trained, model can compress (data -> numbers) and decompress (numbers -> data) reliably.

The likelihood score drives the model to preserve all the structure and attributes in its compressed list, so samples decoded from similar lists also preserve those patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mode collapse in the generator. If the distribution-free method only learns to sample from the densest regions of compressed space, synthetic hypergraphs will all be very similar—no diversity. This happens if the method has too few internal numbers or if the reconstruction step is too smooth.
Attribute-hyperlink mismatch. The model trains to encode and decode jointly, but if the likelihood model is weak, it may not capture attribute patterns well. Synthetic hyperlinks might come with realistic attributes but unrealistic combinations (e.g. attributes that never co-occur in reality).
Sparsity in decoder output. Real hypergraphs are sparse (most possible edges do not exist). If the likelihood model was trained on dense data or with a loose decoder, synthetic data may generate too many edges.
Done right. The model trains on both hyperlink structure and attributes jointly, the distribution-free method learns a faithful map of which regions are valid, and the decoder biases toward the sparse, realistic regime seen in training.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Pick a real hypergraph dataset (e.g. a social network or biological interaction network with attributes like timestamps or categorical labels). Train ReLaSH on half the data. Generate synthetic hypergraphs from the same size as the held-out half. Compare the synthetic data to the held-out half on: entity degree distribution (how many groups each entity appears in), hyperlink size distribution (how many entities per group), and attribute statistics (mean, variance, and frequency of each attribute). The synthetic data should match all three statistics closely.

MMD (maximum mean discrepancy between real and synthetic distributions)

A good demo makes it impossible to miss that synthetic data preserves both the network structure and the real-world patterns in the attributes, because matching statistics (degrees, sizes, attribute ranges) shows the compression and sampling process has learned and reproduced the real data's constraints.
