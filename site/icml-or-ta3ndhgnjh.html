Enhancing Affine Maximizer Auctions with Correlation-Aware Payment

The paper keeps the truth-telling guarantee of affine maximizer auctions, but adds a payment term that uses information about how bidders' values move together, so the seller can charge more accurately when values are correlated.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

In a multi-item auction, the seller wants two things at once: bidders should find it best to report their true values, and the seller should collect as much revenue as possible. Affine maximizer auctions already satisfy the first goal because their allocation and payment rule are built to make lying unhelpful. The weakness is that their payment formula is rigid. When bidders' values are correlated, meaning one bidder's signal reveals something about the others, that rigid payment leaves money on the table. This paper adds a correlation-aware payment term that depends on information from the other bidders while preserving the truth-telling guarantee. It then learns the allocation weights and the extra payment term together with a training procedure that also controls whether winners still prefer participating to walking away.

Zero assumption version

Classic truthful auctions leave revenue unused under correlation

An affine maximizer auction chooses the allocation that maximizes a weighted version of reported welfare plus constants chosen by the designer. With the standard payment rule attached to that allocation, each bidder does best by reporting truthfully. That property is called dominant-strategy incentive compatibility, which means truthful bidding is the best move no matter what the others report.

The trouble is that the payment rule does not react to statistical dependence between bidders. If bidder A's report strongly predicts that bidder B also values an item highly, the auctioneer has learned something useful about how much rent the winner can be charged. Standard affine maximizer payments do not fully use that information, so revenue can be far below the best revenue that a correlated setting allows because the mechanism leaves cross-bidder information unused when setting the payment.

The core problem. Truthful affine maximizer auctions use a fixed payment form, so they cannot price winners sharply when bidders' values reveal information about one another.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Keep the affine maximizer allocation

Choose winners by maximizing a weighted welfare score, so the allocation stays inside a truthful mechanism family.

Add a payment adjustment

For each bidder, add an extra payment term that is computed from the others' reports and the learned mechanism settings.

Train for revenue

Optimize the allocation weights and payment adjustment on sampled valuation profiles so expected seller revenue goes up.

Penalize participation violations

Include a loss term for individual rationality, which means a truthful winner should not end up with negative utility.

Post-process if needed

Apply a correction step to remove the remaining participation violations because small violations can remain after finite-data training.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not abandon the affine maximizer structure. Instead, it extends the payment side. The allocation rule stays in a family where truthful bidding is already understood. The new degree of freedom is an extra payment function that can react to the information contained in the other bidders' reports, which is exactly where correlation shows up.

the object it moves  =  the payment rule
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: participation slack

measured quantity  =  how close a truthful bidder is to regretting participation

Revenue cannot be studied alone. A seller can always raise payments by charging winners too much, but then some truthful winners would rather stay out. The paper tracks a participation slack term that measures how far a truthful bidder's utility is below zero. If that term is positive, the mechanism violated individual rationality on that sample.

This quantity matters because the extra payment term increases expressive power. More expressive payments can fit revenue better, but they can also overshoot. By keeping the participation slack inside the training objective, the paper forces the learned mechanism to trade off revenue against the risk of charging honest bidders more than the value they receive.

Why it works

Why it works: use correlation only where truthfulness allows it

In truthful auction design, the dangerous place to add flexibility is the part of the payment that depends on the bidder's own report in a way that changes the incentive to lie. The paper avoids that. It keeps the affine maximizer allocation rule, then adds a payment term designed around information from outside the bidder whose incentive is being protected. Because the bidder cannot manipulate that outside information through a unilateral lie, the extra term can be added without breaking the truth-telling structure of the mechanism family.

This is exactly why correlation matters. In an independent setting, the other bidders' reports do not say much about your value. In a correlated setting, they do. A payment adjustment built from those reports can estimate how much surplus the winner is likely to enjoy and can charge closer to that amount. That raises revenue because the payment rule is no longer forced to ignore the statistical relation across bidders.

The learning setup matters because the designer does not derive a closed-form optimal payment for every statistical setting. Instead, the paper represents both the affine maximizer components and the correlation-aware payment with neural networks, which are flexible function approximators, and then fits them on samples from the target valuation pattern while penalizing individual rationality violations.

Math after the picture

The equation: base payment plus correlation term

First in words:

The final payment equals the standard truthful payment plus an extra term that depends on the reports of the other bidders.

Then the compact form:

p_i = p_i^base + p_i^cor(b_-i)

Every symbol:

p_ifinal payment charged to bidder i
p_i^basethe usual payment from the affine maximizer mechanism
p_i^cor(b_-i)correlation-aware adjustment computed from the other bidders' reports
b_-iall reported bids except bidder i's own report
A tiny worked example
Suppose bidder 1 wins one item.
The standard affine maximizer payment for bidder 1 is 8.
From bidder 2's report, the learned adjustment predicts that winner surplus is probably high, so it adds 1.5.
Final payment = 8 + 1.5 = 9.5.
If bidder 1 values the item at 12, truthful utility is still 12 minus 9.5 = 2.5, so participation is preserved on this sample.

The whole design challenge is to learn a useful adjustment from other bidders' reports without pushing truthful utility below zero too often.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Correlation is weak or misspecified. If the value pattern is close to independent, the extra payment term has little useful information to exploit. A highly flexible payment network may then fit sample noise instead of real structure.
The IR penalty is too small. If training focuses almost only on revenue, the learned adjustment can drift into profiles where truthful winners would rather not participate. The post-processing step can repair some of that, but large violations mean the mechanism was trained in the wrong region.
Sampled valuation profiles are not representative. The learned mechanism only sees the valuation patterns it is trained on. If deployment data has a different correlation structure, the payment adjustment can charge too little or too much because it learned the wrong relation across bidders.
Done right. In auction settings with real cross-bidder dependence and enough training data, the added payment term captures revenue that the fixed affine maximizer payment cannot reach, while the truthful allocation structure remains intact.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a two-bidder, two-item synthetic auction environment where each bidder's private value includes both a personal component and a shared component. Train a standard affine maximizer baseline and a correlation-aware version on samples from that environment. Measure expected revenue, the fraction of sampled profiles with negative truthful utility, and whether a bidder can gain by deviating from truthful reporting on a grid of reports.

IR_violation_rate = sampled_profiles_with_negative_truthful_utility / total_sampled_profiles

The demo should show one clean contrast: when values move together, revenue rises only after the payment rule starts using that shared information.
