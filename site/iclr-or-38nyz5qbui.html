Learning from Synthetic Data Improves Multi-hop Reasoning

Models taught on invented logical puzzles learn to chain reasoning steps, then apply that skill to real-world knowledge questions.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

LLMs need to reason across multiple steps to answer hard questions, but training them usually requires expensive human-written answers or slow AI verifiers. This paper discovers that teaching a model on synthetic data—cheap invented puzzles with no real-world knowledge—works surprisingly well. The model learns the fundamental skill of chaining information across steps, which transfers directly to real-world benchmarks.

Zero assumption version

Why training data is hard to get

To teach an LLM to answer multi-hop questions—questions that need chaining facts together—you need training examples with correct answers. The human way is expensive: hiring people to write thousands of answers. The AI way is to use an LLM verifier to check answers automatically, but that is slow and costs money every time you run it.

A beginner might assume synthetic data (invented examples) cannot teach real reasoning. After all, if the puzzle has no connection to real facts, how can it teach anything useful? That intuition breaks down because reasoning is a skill, not a database.

The paper's move in one sentence: Train on cheap rule-generated synthetic puzzles; the model learns to chain reasoning steps and applies it to real questions.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Generate unlimited synthetic data

Use rule engines to create logical puzzles (family trees, truth-teller riddles) instantly, free, with verifiable answers.

Run on-policy RL training

Fine-tune an LLM using reinforcement learning: feed puzzle, sample answer, reward correct reasoning chains.

Train at scale

Unlike human-annotated or AI-verified data, synthetic scales to hundreds of thousands of examples.

Extract the reasoning skill

The model learns not trivia but the technique of linking fact-to-fact-to-answer across multiple hops.

Apply to real questions

Test on HotpotQA, SQuAD, TriviaQA: the chaining skill works even though training data had no overlap with test knowledge.

What the paper changes

What the paper trains on

The paper changes what data the LLM is trained on: rule-generated synthetic datasets instead of human-annotated or AI-verified data. Two specific synthetic environments generate puzzles: Family-Relationships (determine how two people are related in a randomly generated family tree) and Knights-Knaves (identify truth-tellers from liars given random logical statements).

The hidden quantity

The reasoning skill: composition across hops

measured quantity  =  ability to chain knowledge steps

The paper names and measures one core quantity: the model's ability to compose (chain together) knowledge across multiple reasoning steps. This is not a property of the model's weights, but of its behavior—how well it can take one piece of information, connect it to another, then another, and arrive at a conclusion. Multi-hop reasoning is exactly this chaining.

The paper measures this indirectly: by observing that models trained on single-hop synthetic data (questions answered in one step) fail on multi-hop real questions, while models trained on multi-hop synthetic data succeed. This shows the model learned the specific skill of chaining, not just memorizing patterns.

Why it works

Why synthetic reasoning data works

Reasoning and knowledge are two different things. When you train on a puzzle with no real-world knowledge (a made-up family tree), the model cannot cheat by memorizing facts. It must learn the actual reasoning technique: take claim A, follow its implication to claim B, then claim C, then answer. This learned chaining skill is portable—it works on real questions too.

Why does knowledge-free training avoid memorization? Because synthetic data has zero overlap with the model's pretraining knowledge. The model cannot improve by replaying what it already knows. It must bootstrap a new skill. Once learned on invented examples, that skill applies to any knowledge-grounded question.

The paper tests this by stratifying: single-hop synthetic data teaches single-hop reasoning and does not improve multi-hop performance. Multi-hop synthetic data teaches chaining and transfers to multi-hop real questions. This causal signature proves the model learned composing, not retrieval.

Math after the picture

How the training signal works

First in words:

reward = sum of step scores: score each intermediate fact link, scale by how correct the final answer is

Then the compact form:

R = (1 / num_steps) * sum_i [ score_i * final_correct ] where score_i measures how well step i chains to step i+1

Every symbol:

Rtotal reward for one attempt
num_stepsnumber of reasoning steps (hops) in the question
score_igradient-based score of how well the model linked fact i to fact i+1
final_correct1 if the final answer is correct, 0 otherwise
A tiny worked example
Question: A is related to B, B to C, C to D. What is A to D? (3 hops)
Model's reasoning: step 1 link 'A→B' scored 0.8, step 2 link 'B→C' scored 0.9, step 3 link 'C→D' scored 0.7, final answer correct
Reward = (1/3) * (0.8 + 0.9 + 0.7) * 1 = 0.8
Each wrong intermediate link gets lower score; if final answer is wrong, entire reward is 0. Training nudges the model to link all steps correctly.

The reward scales with both the quality of each link and whether the chain leads to the right answer, which forces the model to learn accurate step-by-step reasoning because any weak link lowers the total reward.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Synthetic data mismatch. If the synthetic reasoning structure does not match real questions, transfer fails. Training only on single-hop problems does not teach multi-hop composition. The puzzle grammar must match the target task.
Overfitting to the puzzle domain. If synthetic data teaches tricks specific to one puzzle type (e.g., family-tree syntax), the model may not generalize to different question phrasings or domains.
RL instability at scale. Reinforcement learning can diverge if reward signals are noisy or if the model overoptimizes spurious patterns. Large model scale (4B parameters) makes this harder to debug.
Done right: use multi-hop puzzles and validate transfer. Structure synthetic data to match the reasoning hops in real benchmarks. Stratify evaluation by question difficulty to confirm the model learned chaining, not shortcuts. Scale gradually and monitor for reward instability.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small LLM (say, 0.6B parameters), generate 10,000 synthetic multi-hop reasoning puzzles using a rule engine, and fine-tune the model using reinforcement learning for one epoch. Then evaluate on real multi-hop benchmarks like HotpotQA (which requires chaining knowledge from two Wikipedia articles to answer). Show that: (1) performance improves on real multi-hop questions, (2) performance does not improve on single-hop questions as much, because this pattern proves the model learned the specific technique of chaining rather than memorizing knowledge, and (3) the improvement holds even though the synthetic data shares zero facts with the real benchmark, confirming the learned skill is reasoning structure not fact recall.

transfer_gain = accuracy_on_real_benchmark_after_training - accuracy_before_training, stratified by question hop count

The core proof: a model trained on invented logic with no real-world knowledge solves real-world reasoning questions better, because it learned the transferable skill of chaining reasoning steps—technique that works regardless of whether the facts are synthetic or real.
