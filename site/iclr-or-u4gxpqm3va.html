Complex-Valued Neural Networks for Waveform Generation

A neural vocoder keeps real and imaginary parts of sound spectrograms coupled during learning, so the model can treat them as one integrated thing instead of two separate puzzles.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Sound is made of vibrations at different frequencies and phases. A spectrogram is a chart of those frequencies and their phase angles (which way each frequency is tilted in the cycle). Today's neural vocoders turn spectrograms back into audio waves, but they process the real and imaginary numbers separately, like solving two unrelated math problems. ComVo couples them using complex-valued neural networks, so the model sees them as one interconnected thing, because real and imaginary parts of a frequency always move together in the original sound. This brings the feedback closer to the actual structure of the sound, which makes training more efficient because when real and imaginary are coupled in the loss, the model stops trying to fix one part in a way that breaks the other, so each training step makes progress without backtracking. The paper shows this cuts training time from 183 hours to 138 hours, a 25 percent improvement.

Zero assumption version

Why splitting causes trouble

Sound is a wave moving up and down in time. When you record a sound and look at it as a spectrogram, each tiny piece of the sound becomes two numbers at each frequency: a real part (how loud the wave is tilted one way) and an imaginary part (how tilted the opposite way, 90 degrees shifted). These two numbers together describe the exact shape and tilt of that frequency's wave at that moment.

A neural vocoder is a model that learns to turn a spectrogram back into the original audio wave. Today's vocoders use real-valued neural networks, which process the real and imaginary parts in completely separate channels, like two independent teams solving two unrelated puzzles. This is like building a house where the foundation and walls never talk to each other. The catch: the real and imaginary parts of a spectrogram are not independent. They carry information about the same underlying frequency's phase angle and magnitude.

The problem in one sentence. Separating real and imaginary parts breaks the natural coupling between them, so the model learns slower because it has to solve two unrelated puzzles instead of one, and audio quality suffers because the two channels give contradictory feedback about what the phase should be.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

ComVo swaps the processing pathway: instead of sending real and imaginary parts down separate channels in a real-valued network, it uses a native complex-valued neural network layer (called CVNN), where each neuron processes the real and imaginary components jointly at the same time, treating them as one algebraic object.

The hidden quantity

The alignment quantity: how coordinated the nudges are

measured quantity  =  Phase coherence (how well real and imaginary nudges work together)

When you train a neural network, you compute gradients (the direction to nudge each weight). In split real-valued vocoders, the gradient for the real part and the gradient for the imaginary part are computed independently, so they point in different random directions—like two teams pulling at different angles. Phase coherence measures whether gradients in the real and imaginary domains are working toward the same goal (the same phase angle on the spectrogram) or fighting each other.

ComVo estimates this by observing how much the model's predictions for phase angle drift and whether adversarial feedback in the complex domain naturally aligns the two gradients. The paper shows via gradient graphs that using native complex arithmetic reduces the total number of computational nodes by roughly half, meaning the gradient computation is simpler and more aligned.

Why it works

How fused complex arithmetic fixes the problem

A complex number is like a coordinate on a 2D plane: one axis is the real part, one is imaginary. When you do arithmetic on complex numbers natively, you multiply following the algebra rule that treats both parts as one object, preserving the shape. If you split them into two separate channels, the model has to learn which interactions matter—it is solving an unnecessary puzzle. Fused complex arithmetic tells the model from the start: these two axes are linked.

During adversarial training, the discriminator gives feedback on how far the generated spectrogram is from the target. If that feedback is given in complex form (not split into two real scores), the nudges flowing into the generator naturally prefer to move both the real and imaginary parts together, as a unified phase-and-magnitude pair, because the loss itself is computed from the complex distance (not two separate real losses), so the gradient naturally encodes the coupling. Phase quantization (discretizing phase angles to a grid) adds a second boost: rounding the angles stabilizes the nudges because tiny jittery changes in phase no longer produce wild swings in the adjustment direction—quantizing removes the noise. Finally, the block-matrix scheme is a computational rewrite: fusing the four separate multiplications needed for one complex multiply into a single operation cuts the size of the backward-pass computation graph by half, because instead of doing four real multiplies and then combining, you do one combined operation, so learning is faster and less wasteful.

Math after the picture

The complex-valued update rule

First in words:

For a complex weight z (real part x, imaginary part y), the gradient is computed as a pair, and the update nudges both parts in the direction that reduces loss for the complex number as a whole, not each part separately.

Then the compact form:

z_new = z - lr * (1/2) * (dL/dx - i * dL/dy)

Every symbol:

zthe complex weight (z = x + i*y)
xreal part of the weight
yimaginary part of the weight
iimaginary unit (i*i = -1)
dL/dxhow much loss changes when you shift the real part
dL/dyhow much loss changes when you shift the imaginary part
lrlearning rate (step size)
z_newthe weight after one update step
A tiny worked example
Suppose a complex weight z = 0.3 + 0.4i (real=0.3, imaginary=0.4).
The gradient says: dL/dx = 0.2, dL/dy = 0.1 (loss is sensitive to both parts).
With lr=0.01: update = 0.01 * (1/2) * (0.2 - i*0.1) = 0.001 - 0.0005i.
New weight = (0.3 + 0.4i) - (0.001 - 0.0005i) = 0.299 + 0.4005i.
Notice: both real and imaginary moved together as one step, honoring their coupling.

The conjugate gradient (dL/dz-bar) encodes both components, so one update step moves the complex weight as a unified point, not two independent numbers moved separately.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Phase quantization bins too coarse. If you round phase angles to only a few bins (e.g., just 0° and 180°), you lose the ability to model smooth phase rotations. The audio sounds choppy.
Complex layer initialized poorly. Complex weights need careful random initialization (the magnitude of the real and imaginary parts must both be small, and independent). Bad initialization can cause the model to diverge.
Discriminator does not see the coupling. If the discriminator still splits real and imaginary (instead of using native complex feedback), the generator gets contradictory signals and training slows down. Both generator and discriminator must use native complex arithmetic.
Done right. When the discriminator operates in the complex domain and phase quantization uses enough bins (e.g., 16 angles), the model learns the underlying waveform structure because the loss is now measuring actual complex distance (not two separate failures), and produces natural-sounding audio faster because the gradient descent steps are now aligned with the true structure of the sound.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a mini vocoder on a toy waveform (e.g., a short musical note or speech segment). Train two versions side by side: one with split real-valued layers (baseline), one with native complex-valued layers and phase quantization. Measure training time per epoch and the final audio reconstruction error (distance between the generated waveform and the target, measured in time domain). Also compute the backward-pass graph complexity (number of steps in the computation graph) by inspecting the PyTorch graph before and after applying the block-matrix optimization.

Audio fidelity = 1 - (sum of squared differences between generated and target waveform) / (sum of squared target waveform)

The demo should make it impossible to miss that complex-valued processing trains faster because the backward-pass graph is smaller, produces audio closer to the target because the loss is aligned with the sound structure, and the block-matrix rewrite actually shrinks the computation count in half, not just speeds up a hidden black box.
