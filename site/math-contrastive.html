Contrastive learning (InfoNCE)

Teach a model to recognize which items are similar by penalizing wrong matches.

In plain terms

Imagine you're learning to recognize your friend's voice. Every time you hear a voice, you have to decide: is this my friend, or someone else? The catch is, you don't have labeled recordings. Instead, you have many voice clips, and you know that clip A and clip B are from the same person (maybe they're the same sentence spoken twice), while clip C is from a stranger. You learn by guessing wrong at first, then adjusting. Each time you guess wrong, you pay a penalty. Over time, your internal model learns to map "my friend's voice" and "my friend's voice again" to nearly the same place in your head, and "stranger's voice" far away. That's contrastive learning: learn similarity by being told which pairs belong together and which don't.

The math formalizes this penalty. When you see a pair that matches, you give it a high similarity score. When you see all the wrong options ("distractors"), you give them low scores. The loss function asks: how confident are you that the correct match ranks higher than all the distractors? If the correct match ranks far below a wrong option, the penalty is huge. If it ranks highest, the penalty is tiny. Over thousands of examples, the model learns feature representations (ways of encoding data) that naturally cluster similar items together.

The equation
L=-\log\frac{e^{s_+}}{\sum e^{s}}
LThe loss: a single number measuring how wrong the model's guess was. Smaller is better.
s_+The similarity score between the input and the matching item, computed by the model. Higher means more similar.
sA vector of all similarity scores: the score for the correct match (s_+) plus scores for each distractor (wrong options).
e^sExponential of each score. This turns scores into positive numbers that can be compared as probabilities.
Σe^sThe sum of all exponentials: treating the scores as an unnormalized probability distribution over all options.
e^s+ / Σe^sA probability: the model's predicted chance that the correct match is the right answer (given all other options).
-log(...)Negative log: a penalty that grows larger as the predicted probability shrinks toward zero.
Worked example
Scoring a matched pair against three distractors
Suppose similarity scores are: s_+ = 3.0 (correct match), distractors = [1.5, 0.2, -0.5].
Exponentials: e^3 = 20.1, e^1.5 = 4.5, e^0.2 = 1.2, e^-0.5 = 0.6.
Sum: 20.1 + 4.5 + 1.2 + 0.6 = 26.4.
Probability of correct: 20.1 / 26.4 ≈ 0.76 (76% confident).
Loss: -log(0.76) ≈ 0.28 (small penalty, good guess).
Why it shows up

Contrastive learning appears across representation learning, multimodal models, self-supervised learning, graph neural networks, and reinforcement learning. Whenever a model must distinguish one correct option from many plausible wrong ones, this penalty drives the learning. In self-supervised learning, you don't have manual labels, so you use the data itself: two augmentations of the same image match, others don't. In multimodal learning (text + image), you learn that caption A matches image A but not image B. In reinforcement learning, you contrast good actions against bad ones. The formula naturally balances two competing pressures: pushing the correct match's score higher, and pushing all the distractors' scores lower. This forces the model to learn *discriminative* features—ways of encoding data that separate different categories rather than just memorizing patterns.
