VeriTime: Time Series Reasoning via Process-Verifiable LLM Training

Teaching small language models to reason about time series by building datasets where each step of reasoning can be checked as right or wrong, and training them with rewards based on that process, not just final answers.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Language models are now good at long reasoning tasks because they can be trained to think step-by-step through a problem. Time series data (daily stock prices, sensor measurements, weather patterns) is everywhere, but getting small language models to reason well on time series tasks is still hard because there is no good training data for it. VeriTime solves this by creating datasets where each reasoning step is verifiable, arranging training examples from easy to hard, and using a training method that rewards correct thinking.

Zero assumption version

Time series reasoning without the right training data

Time series appears everywhere: stock prices, temperature readings, patient vital signs, sensor measurements. These sequences have patterns — a price might spike, then stabilize; a temperature climbs, then falls. Humans can reason about what comes next or what caused a change. Language models have recently learned to do this too, by thinking aloud in a sequence of steps before answering. But this reasoning ability was trained on text data. When you point a language model at time series and ask it to reason step-by-step, it struggles because it has never seen training examples where each step of the reasoning was marked as correct or incorrect.

A beginner might try just fine-tuning a language model on time series questions with correct answers. The problem: the model sees only the final answer, not which thinking steps were right or wrong. It might learn to output the right answer by memorizing surface patterns, but it is not learning genuine reasoning. Even worse, it wastes training time on bad reasoning paths that happen to hit the right answer.

In one sentence. Small language models cannot reason about time series because there are no datasets with step-by-step verified reasoning, and existing training methods do not reward correct thinking.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Build verified datasets

Generate time series reasoning examples paired with step-by-step annotations that mark each reasoning step as correct or incorrect.

Arrange by difficulty

Sort training examples using a principled difficulty hierarchy and group them by task type so easy examples come before hard ones.

Reward correct thinking

During training, reward the model not just for right answers but for correct reasoning steps in between.

Train in two stages

First stage trains on verifiable process data; second stage refines using multi-objective rewards that value both answer and reasoning.

Evaluate reasoning quality

Test on diverse time series tasks to confirm the model reasons step-by-step and matches larger proprietary models.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training data and the training objective. Instead of examples with only final answers, it uses examples where each reasoning step is labeled as correct or incorrect. Instead of training to match only final answers, it trains to score reasoning steps correctly and to choose sequences of correct steps.

the object it moves  =  Training examples and reward signals
The hidden quantity

Process correctness: which reasoning steps are actually right

measured quantity  =  Process correctness per step

A hidden quantity in this work is process correctness: for each reasoning step, whether it logically and factually follows from the time series data. A naive model might guess the right final answer by copying an observation without reasoning through what it means. But if every step is marked right or wrong independently, the model cannot cheat this way.

VeriTime estimates process correctness by having a verifier (a rule or another model) check each step against the time series and the previous step. This creates labels: Step 1 correctly identifies the trend; Step 2 incorrectly predicts the next value because it ignored the drift. Now training can reward true reasoning, not lucky guesses.

Plain reading. Process correctness asks: is this reasoning step actually true, given the data? This is different from asking whether the step happened to help reach the final answer, because a model might get the right answer by accident without correct reasoning. VeriTime labels each step independently of the final outcome.
Paper reading. The paper calls this process-verifiable data. A verifier checks each step of the reasoning trace against ground truth, and the model is rewarded for sequences of correct steps, not just correct final answers.
Why it works

Why this works: the three ingredients

Traditional language model training on time series stops at the final answer. VeriTime's mechanism rests on three moves. First, verifiable process data lets the model see exactly which reasoning steps are right or wrong. This is like learning algebra by seeing each line of work marked as correct or incorrect, not just the final answer. The model can now spot which thinking patterns lead to sound reasoning.

Second, scheduling examples by difficulty means the model builds mental operations step by step. Early examples teach basic pattern recognition (does this series trend up?); later examples demand chaining those patterns (if it trends up and accelerates, what does that mean for a 10-step forecast?). This avoids the problem where a model trained on hard and easy examples mixed together gets confused by conflicting signals.

Third, the training reward is tailored to time series reasoning. Instead of a single loss signal (did you get it right or wrong?), the model receives multi-objective rewards: one for correct steps, one for choosing valid sequences, one for final correctness. This is more informative than a single signal. Because each reasoning step is marked independently, training can reinforce good reasoning even if the final answer is unlucky (e.g., a correct reasoning path that leads to an edge-case forecast).

Math after the picture

The training reward

First in words:

Total reward equals step correctness reward plus sequence coherence reward plus final answer reward, where step correctness reward sums how many reasoning steps the verifier marked as correct.

Then the compact form:

Reward = alpha * (# correct steps) + beta * (sequence score) + gamma * (final accuracy)

Every symbol:

alphaweight on how many steps were marked correct
betaweight on how well the steps connect logically
gammaweight on whether the final answer matches ground truth
# correct stepsthe count of reasoning steps the verifier approved
sequence scorea score from 0-1 measuring logical flow between steps
final accuracy1 if final answer is right, 0 if wrong
A tiny worked example
Suppose alpha=0.5, beta=0.3, gamma=0.2. A model's trace has 6 reasoning steps, 5 marked correct by the verifier, sequences flow well (score=0.9), and the final answer is right (1.0).
Reward = 0.5 * 5 + 0.3 * 0.9 + 0.2 * 1.0 = 2.5 + 0.27 + 0.2 = 2.97.
Another trace: 6 steps, only 3 marked correct, sequences jumbled (score=0.4), final answer is right (1.0).
Reward = 0.5 * 3 + 0.3 * 0.4 + 0.2 * 1.0 = 1.5 + 0.12 + 0.2 = 1.82.
Even though both got the final answer right, the first trace earns 2.97 and trains the model to reason genuinely; the second earns only 1.82, so training moves away from lucky guesses.

The reward directly measures process correctness (how many steps were verified as right) plus sequence coherence, so training naturally reinforces step-by-step reasoning over memorized shortcuts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Verifier mistakes. If the process verifier (the rule or model that marks steps as correct or incorrect) makes errors, the training signal is noisy. A model trained on mislabeled steps will learn incorrect reasoning patterns. Mitigation: use a high-confidence verifier (e.g., a well-calibrated symbolic rule or a large trusted model) and spot-check labels.
Scheduling bias. If the difficulty hierarchy is wrong — for example, easy and hard examples are still mixed — the model gets conflicting signals and training destabilizes. A task labeled easy might actually require earlier skills the model has not learned.
Reward imbalance. If alpha, beta, gamma are miscalibrated, the model optimizes the wrong signal. For example, if gamma (final answer weight) is too high and alpha (step correctness) too low, training reverts to guessing the right final answer without reasoning.
Done right. VeriTime succeeds when verifiers are trustworthy, the difficulty schedule respects task prerequisites, and multi-objective weights balance process correctness with final accuracy. Real results show small 3B–4B models can match proprietary models.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should train a small 1B–3B language model on a single time series task (e.g., forecasting or anomaly detection) using three conditions: (1) standard fine-tuning with only final-answer labels, (2) fine-tuning with verifiable step labels but no scheduling, and (3) VeriTime (step labels plus difficulty scheduling plus multi-objective rewards). For each, track the accuracy on a held-out test set and separately log the fraction of reasoning steps the model gets right independently of final accuracy. Measure process correctness by asking a verifier to score each step of the trained model's reasoning.

Process correctness per task = (# steps model generated that verifier marked correct) / (total # steps in trace). Accuracy per task = (# correct final predictions) / (# test examples). Compare all three training methods side-by-side.

The demo must show that VeriTime trains more honest step-by-step reasoning than final-answer-only methods, and that small models trained this way match larger models on time series reasoning tasks.
