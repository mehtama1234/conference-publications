Scale-Partitioned Uncertainty Rectification for Robust UAV Interception

The paper divides a UAV detection task by how far away the target is, trains each distance-band separately to keep working even when camera blur and noise get worse, and fixes the loss function to ignore noise without losing hard cases.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a drone chases another drone at high speed, the target looks different at different distances — close up it fills the screen, far away it is tiny. A standard detector trained on all distances together struggles because it is tuned for the average case and breaks down at the extremes. This paper solves that by training three separate detectors for close, medium, and far range, each designed to keep working even when camera blur and noise get worse, then combines them so that each distance band keeps its edge during real flight.

Zero assumption version

The target vanishes at the edges

A drone chasing another drone must lock onto it at any distance: far away (when it is a speck), medium range (when it fills part of the view), and close up (when it is huge). A standard detector, trained on a mix of all distances, learns weights that work OK on average — but the trade-off cripples its view of the extremes. A tiny target gets lost in the background noise. A huge target blurs into the edges.

Even worse, the dataset itself is imbalanced: real-world flight logs have many medium-range sightings and few truly close or truly distant ones. The detector sees the medium cases 10 times more often and abandons the rare ones. When a drone then faces an actual close or distant target during flight, the detector misfires — no lock, no interception.

The paper's one-sentence fix: Divide the target distances into three bands, train each band separately to keep working even when camera blur and noise arrive, and glue them back together so the final detector is strong everywhere at once.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Partition the training data

Split all images by target distance: close (up to 5 meters), medium (5 to 20 meters), far (beyond 20 meters).

Training per partition to handle difficult conditions

For each distance band, train a detector to stay accurate even when camera blur and noise get worse, by penalizing real mistakes strongly and ignoring sensor noise.

Anti-noise loss function

Use a loss function that penalizes predictions that are wildly off, but does not throw away the hard cases that matter for learning.

Control-aware tuning

Tune the loss to care most about the exact accuracy the drone's steering system needs to keep the target centered in view.

Fuse the three experts

Combine the three detectors so that at runtime, each distance band uses its own expertly-trained rules.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how the training loss function treats hard cases (images with strong noise or extreme scale). Instead of treating all hard cases the same, it separates the true hard cases that teach the model from the pure noise cases that only confuse it.

the object it moves  =  the loss function weighting
The hidden quantity

How much does each example teach?

measured quantity  =  uncertainty (which examples matter)

When training a detector, not every image teaches it equally. A clear image of a medium-range target teaches a lot. A blurry, noisy image of a distant target is confusing — it could be genuine signal under bad conditions, or just sensor noise. The paper names this uncertainty: for each example, how confident should we be that the prediction error is real and worth learning from?

The paper estimates uncertainty by looking at whether a prediction was wrong by a big amount (likely real signal) or by a tiny amount (possibly just noise). Then it re-weights the loss so that clear signal is learned from, ambiguous cases are learned from gently, and pure noise is ignored.

Plain reading. Uncertainty is how likely each prediction error comes from the camera or world, not from model ignorance. High uncertainty on an error means ignore it; low uncertainty means that error is real and worth fixing.
Paper reading. The paper weights each training example by its uncertainty. Large errors get a high weight because they represent real signal that the model should learn. Small errors get a low weight because they likely come from camera noise — penalizing them wastes training effort. By adjusting weights this way, the model focuses on correcting true mistakes and ignores sensor artifacts.
Why it works

Why splitting by scale and reweighting loss keeps the model accurate everywhere

The core idea has two parts. First, a detector trained on a balanced mix of distances learns a compromise that works nowhere well. By training three separate detectors — one for each distance band — each can learn the exact features that matter at that scale. Close targets need sharp edge-detection; distant targets need motion patterns. Mixing them forces the model to blend both skills and lose precision in each.

Second, even within a distance band, some errors come from camera blur or sensor noise, not from the model not knowing the target. A standard loss treats all errors equally and wastes learning power on noise. The uncertainty-rectified loss says: if a prediction error is small (like 0.5 pixels), it might be sensor noise — penalize it gently. If an error is large (like 10 pixels), it is real signal — penalize it hard. This way the model learns to correct genuine mistakes and ignores camera quirks.

Math after the picture

The uncertainty-rectified loss equation

First in words:

For each prediction, compute how far off it is. Estimate the uncertainty of that error (is it real or noise?). Scale the penalty by that uncertainty weight — high certainty of real error means a large penalty, low certainty means a small penalty. Sum over all training examples.

Then the compact form:

Loss = sum_i w_i * L_regression(pred_i, target_i)

Every symbol:

w_iuncertainty weight for example i (0 to 1; high means high confidence the error is real)
L_regression(pred_i, target_i)the error between predicted and true target location for example i, measured in pixels
sum_iadd up the weighted loss over all training examples
pred_ithe detector's predicted target position for image i
target_ithe true target position for image i
A tiny worked example
Example A (clear image, target at 100m): pred=101.5, target=100. Error=1.5 pixels. Uncertainty w=0.9 (very clear). Weighted loss = 0.9 * 1.5 = 1.35.
Example B (noisy image, target at 100m): pred=100.3, target=100. Error=0.3 pixels. Uncertainty w=0.2 (very noisy). Weighted loss = 0.2 * 0.3 = 0.06.
Model strongly learns from A (error is real signal), barely learns from B (error is likely sensor noise). Same-sized adjustment to weights treats A 22x harder than B.

Low uncertainty weight on small errors means the model does not waste training time on sensor noise; high weight on large errors means real mistakes are corrected.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Picking the wrong distance boundaries. If the scale bands are too narrow, one band starves of data. If too wide, targets within a band still span a huge range of appearances. The paper does not say how to set these thresholds — they were tuned by hand for the UAV task.
A target that is between bands. At runtime, if the true distance sits exactly between two bands (say, 4.9 meters when close is 0-5, medium is 5-20), the router must guess which detector to use. Misrouting breaks the whole advantage.
Uncertainty estimates themselves can be wrong. If the method for guessing which errors are real and which are noise is off, the re-weighting will penalize the wrong cases, and the model learns the wrong lessons.
Done right: distance bands are validated on held-out data; the router is probabilistic (can blend detectors); uncertainty estimation is cross-validated against ground truth labels.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a detector that tracks a drone target in a video. Show that when you train it the standard way (all distances mixed), it locks onto medium-range targets but loses the far ones. Then train it the paper's way (split by distance, re-weight by uncertainty). Show that it now holds lock at all three ranges, even when you add camera blur or fog to the video.

The paper's claim is simple: a detector trained separately per distance band, with uncertainty-aware loss, will outperform one mixed detector in real noise, because each band can learn its own features without compromise and the loss function learns from real errors while ignoring camera noise.
