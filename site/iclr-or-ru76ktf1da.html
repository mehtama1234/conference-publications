How the optimizer shapes the error surface and makes models mergeable

When you train two separate models and try to merge them, they merge well only if the optimizer introduced just the right amount of noise—too little and they get stuck in isolated valleys, too much and they get mushy.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Imagine training two separate neural networks to solve different tasks. After training, you want to combine them into one model that does both. Intuitively you'd think that just averaging their weights would work, but it doesn't—the merged model often gets worse at both tasks. This paper shows why: the way the optimizer trains each model leaves them in completely different regions of weight space where errors rise sharply if you move even slightly. Whether you can successfully merge them depends on a single hidden quantity called the effective noise scale—a measure of the random jiggling that occurs during training.

Zero assumption version

Two trained models live in incompatible valleys

When you take two neural networks trained on different tasks or with different random seeds and try to average their weights, the merged model almost always gets worse at both tasks. This happens because each network, during training, settles into a different local valley—a region where that particular weight combination gives low loss but moving the weights in almost any direction makes loss jump up. These valleys sit far apart from each other in weight space.

A beginner might assume that valleys close to each other would merge well, but that is backwards. The real problem is that neither individual network knows about the other. Each optimizer (like Adam) jostles the weights around during training with a particular amount of randomness based on how you set the learning rate, batch size, and weight decay. If both networks experienced the same amount of jostling, they end up in compatible valleys. If one was jostled more than the other, their valleys become incompatible—sitting at different heights or separated by steep cliffs.

In one sentence. The optimizer's choice of learning rate, batch size, and weight decay controls how much random jiggling happens during training, and this jiggling determines whether two independently trained models end up in compatible regions or incompatible ones.

What the paper changes

The one thing the optimizer determines

The paper shows that the effective noise scale—a single number combining learning rate, batch size, weight decay, and data augmentation—is what the optimizer implicitly controls during training. This quantity directly shapes where in weight space each model settles. Change the noise scale, and you change the structure of valleys where the model ends up.

The hidden quantity

The effective noise scale—how much the optimizer jiggles the weights

measured quantity  =  effective noise scale

The effective noise scale is a single number measuring the amount of random jiggling that the optimizer introduces into the training process. Think of it as the width of the random wobbles in the weight updates at each step. A higher noise scale means the optimizer makes larger, more chaotic jumps; a lower one means more cautious, deterministic steps. It unifies several optimizer and data choices: larger learning rates increase the noise; smaller batch sizes increase the noise (because each batch-based gradient is noisier when the batch is tiny); stronger weight decay adds dampening (which paradoxically amplifies noise through how the optimizer's long-term movement pattern settles); and data augmentation also increases the effective randomness in the signal.

The paper estimates this quantity by analyzing SGD and Adam's implicit behavior on different tasks and architectures. This noise scale determines which region of weight space a trained model will live in after convergence. Models with the same effective noise scale end up in compatible regions and merge well; models with mismatched noise scales end up in incompatible valleys.

Why it works

Why effective noise determines mergeability

During training, the optimizer does not just follow a smooth path downhill. At each step, it uses only a small batch of data to estimate the full gradient, so the update direction is always noisy—a random direction with a signal mixed in. This noise is the reason the model does not get perfectly stuck in the first local minimum it finds; instead, it jostles around and explores the space. The amount of jostling is controlled by the learning rate (bigger steps mean more drift), the batch size (smaller batches give a noisier signal), and weight decay (acts like a damper but changes the pattern the optimizer eventually settles into).

Here is the causal chain: when you train two models with the same effective noise scale, the optimizer in both cases explores regions of weight space with the same structure. Both models are jostled by the same kind of random walk, so they end up in regions that lie close to each other in weight space and at similar loss heights. When you average the weights of two models in similar regions, the average typically stays near the loss surface, so merging works. But if one model experienced twice the noise as the other, the two optimizers explored regions with completely different structures. One might settle in a smooth, wide valley; the other in a narrow, steep ravine. Averaging weights from these incompatible regions lands you on a ridge between them—high loss everywhere.

The non-monotonic relationship is key: too little noise and each model converges to a sharp, isolated minimum, impossible to merge with another. But too much noise and the model never converges cleanly—it ends up in a flat but high-loss region that does not merge well either. There is a sweet spot in the middle where the effective noise is just right for the optimizer to find flat, wide valleys that multiple independently trained models can occupy simultaneously.

The effective noise scale equation

First in words:

Performance gain from merging equals a measure of how well the merged weights stay near the loss surfaces where each individual model converged. This gain depends on the difference in effective noise scales between the two models.

Then the compact form:

effective_noise = (learning_rate / batch_size) * (1 + weight_decay / learning_rate) + data_augmentation_effect

Every symbol:

learning_ratehow large each weight update step is
batch_sizehow many training examples in each update
weight_decaystrength of the penalty for having large weights
data_augmentation_effectadditional randomness from shuffling and transforming the training data
effective_noisefinal measure of how much the optimizer jostles the weights during training
A tiny worked example
Setup A: learning rate = 0.001, batch size = 32, weight decay = 0.0001 -> effective noise ≈ 0.00004 (low)
Setup B: learning rate = 0.01, batch size = 8, weight decay = 0.001 -> effective noise ≈ 0.0015 (higher)
When you merge models from Setup A and Setup B, their effective noise scales differ by ~37×
Models trained with such different noise settle in incompatible valleys, so merging degrades both tasks
Setup C: same settings as B but weight decay = 0.0001 -> effective noise ≈ 0.00128
Models from Setup B and C have much closer effective noise, so they merge better because they occupy nearby regions

The effective noise scale predicts whether two models will merge well: similar noise means they explored regions with the same structure and ended up in compatible places.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Mismatched optimizer settings. If you train one model with learning rate 0.001 and another with 0.01, their effective noise scales differ dramatically. The regions of weight space they explore have fundamentally different structures, so their valleys are incompatible. Merging will fail even if both models are well-trained individually.
Unequal convergence. If one model has not fully converged (still has high training loss), its effective noise scale is ambiguous—the model has not yet settled into a stable region. Trying to merge it with a fully converged model lands you in an unstable region, destroying performance.
Non-scale-invariant architectures. This analysis assumes the model uses batch normalization or layer normalization, which makes the loss scale-invariant (doubling all weights does not change the loss). For architectures without normalization, weight decay affects the effective noise scale differently, and the theory breaks down.
Extrapolating beyond the sweet spot. There is a specific optimal noise level for your task and architecture. With too little noise, each model converges to a sharp, isolated point and cannot merge with another. With too much noise, the optimizer never settles cleanly—it produces models that are mushy and high-loss—so merging also fails. You cannot simply train with more extreme settings and expect better merging, because at both extremes you lose the flat, wide valleys that enable successful merging.
Done right. Train all models with the same optimizer settings (or settings that produce similar effective noise scales), ensure they converge fully, measure effective noise before merging, and stay in the noise range that works best for your architecture based on testing. Then merging succeeds predictably.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small toy example: train two neural networks on CIFAR-10 with different class splits (e.g., one on classes 0-4, one on 5-9) using varying learning rates, batch sizes, and weight decay values. For each pair of hyperparameter combinations, compute the effective noise scale and then merge the models using linear interpolation or task arithmetic. Measure how well the merged model performs on both task splits (or a held-out full-dataset validation set). Plot the merging performance versus the difference in effective noise scales between the two models.

merging_quality = accuracy_on_merged_model - mean_accuracy_of_singles, plotted against abs(noise_scale_A - noise_scale_B)

The plot should show a clear U-shaped curve: merging fails (negative quality) when noise scales differ greatly, succeeds (positive quality) when they match, and deteriorates again at extreme noise levels. This proves that effective noise scale is the hidden quantity controlling mergeability.
