DualFlow: Two-Person Motion via Retrieval and Rectified Flow

DualFlow generates realistic two-person dances and gestures by retrieving motion examples and using a straight-line path instead of noise diffusion, so it is 2.5x faster while keeping both dancers in sync.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

Making two people move together (one leads, one follows, or they mirror) is hard because you need to control position, timing, and how they respond to each other. DualFlow solves this by doing two things: first, it retrieves real dance clips that match the music and text description, then it uses those clips as a guide while drawing a straight line from noise to real motion instead of the curved, wiggly path older methods use. The straight line reduces the steps from 50 to 20, so generation is faster. Real exemplars keep the motion plausible and well-coordinated, so it is smoother.

Zero assumption version

Why two-person motion is stuck

Most motion-generation systems focus on one person moving alone (a single dancer, a single gesture). When you want two people moving together, the problem splits into two incompatible branches: one system for interactive motion (both dancers respond to each other in real time, like improvisation), and a completely separate system for reactive motion (one follows the other, like a copy-cat game). Each system has its own training recipe, its own way of reading the text prompt, its own way of understanding music. A beginner trying to use both at once would have to run two separate models and hope they produce compatible motion. They do not. A person leads, the follower becomes jerky or out-of-sync.

The core problem is semantic grounding. You say 'spin to the left while mirroring the partner,' but the model has no way to anchor 'spin' and 'mirror' to real motion exemplars it has seen. It generates motion from scratch, inventing plausible-sounding moves, not using patterns from actual dance.

In one sentence. DualFlow unifies interactive and reactive two-person motion into one system and grounds generation in retrieved real-dance clips.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Retrieve motion examples

From the music and LLM-decomposed text, fetch real dances that match spatial relations, body movements, and rhythm.

Encode conditioning signals

Transform text (via CLIP embeddings, which turn words into numbers), music (via Jukebox embeddings), and retrieved examples into compact representations.

Denoise along a straight line

Start with random noise for both dancers. At each step, pull both toward the denoised motion, following a straight interpolation rather than a curved path.

Apply causal masking for reactivity

If reactive mode: actor freely moves, reactor sees actor's past but not future, responding frame-by-frame like a human echo.

Synchronize with loss penalties

Training adds a synchronization loss that penalizes the dancers drifting apart, keeping their timing and spatial alignment tight.

What the paper changes

What changes: the path and the guide

The paper changes two things. First, instead of starting at random noise and iteratively denoising via curved (diffusion) paths, it uses rectified flow: a straight line from noise to real motion. Second, it anchors that path using retrieved motion exemplars—real dance clips fetched by music and text—rather than generating motion purely from learned patterns.

the object it moves  =  denoising path + retrieval grounding
The hidden quantity

The synchronization error between dancers

measured quantity  =  inter-person temporal drift

The paper introduces a synchronization loss that measures how much the two dancers are drifting apart in time. If one dancer is on beat and the other lags by half a frame, that is drift. The paper computes this by looking at the distance between corresponding joints (hips, shoulders) at each time step and penalizing large misalignment. Without this loss, the generator can produce each dancer's motion independently and they end up like two people dancing to different songs.

DualFlow estimates this drift during training by comparing the motion of dancer A and dancer B frame by frame, computing the spatial distance between matched joints, and summing these distances as a penalty. A large penalty means the dancers are out of sync; training nudges the model to minimize it.

Why it works

Why it works: straight lines, retrieved anchors, and causal response

Rectified flow works because a straight path from noise to real motion is simpler and faster than the curved, diffusion-style paths that other methods use. When the model is learning to go from point A (noise) to point B (real motion), a direct line has fewer steps to accumulate error. Instead of guessing at each turn for 50 steps, the model only needs 20 steps.

Retrieval grounding works because retrieved dances act like training wheels. Instead of the model inventing 'what does a spin to the left look like,' it sees real examples of spins and copies their patterns. The cross-attention layers ask: 'Which retrieved example is most similar to what we are generating right now?' and pull the generation toward that example. This keeps the output plausible and semantically faithful to the text.

For synchronization, the causal masking (in reactive mode) lets the reactor see the actor's past motion but not future motion, so it must respond like a real human would: one frame at a time. The synchronization loss then penalizes any drift, so training shapes the model to keep both dancers locked together.

Math after the picture

The contrastive rectified flow objective

First in words:

Total loss equals the main flow loss plus a weight times the synchronization penalty. The flow loss measures how far off the denoised step is from the real motion. The synchronization loss sums the squared distances between the two dancers' joint positions at every frame. Training adjusts the model to minimize both simultaneously.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_flow + lambda * L_sync where L_sync = sum_t ||pos_A(t) - pos_B(t)||^2

Every symbol:

L_flowthe loss that learns to denoise straight from noise to real motion
L_syncthe sum of squared distances between dancer A's and B's joint positions across all frames
lambdaa weight controlling how much we care about sync vs. motion quality
pos_A(t)the position of dancer A's body at frame t
pos_B(t)the position of dancer B's body at frame t
||·||^2squared distance (how far apart they are)
A tiny worked example
Suppose at frame 5, dancer A's hip is at position (1.0, 2.0, 3.0) and dancer B's hip is at (1.1, 2.3, 3.2).
Distance = sqrt((1.1-1.0)^2 + (2.3-2.0)^2 + (3.2-3.0)^2) = sqrt(0.01 + 0.09 + 0.04) = sqrt(0.14) ≈ 0.37.
Squared distance: 0.37^2 ≈ 0.14. With lambda=10, this frame contributes 10 * 0.14 = 1.4 to L_sync.
If they were perfectly aligned, distance = 0 and this frame contributes 0. Training learns to generate motion that minimizes these gaps across all frames.

The loss pulls both dancers toward the same positions at each frame, while the retrieval guide ensures those positions look like real dance.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Retrieval database is poor. If the motion database does not contain exemplars matching the text (e.g., no hip-hop clips for a hip-hop prompt), the generator has no good reference and produces generic or misaligned motion. The output quality depends entirely on retrieval coverage.
Causal masking breaks when actors are unpredictable. In reactive mode, the reactor must predict the actor's move after seeing it. If the actor does something unexpected—behavior outside what the training data showed—the reactor has no future context and can lag or respond awkwardly.
Synchronization loss ignores collision avoidance. Two dancers can be synchronized frame-by-frame but still overlapping (elbows poking through bodies, feet clipping into the ground). The sync loss only cares about distance, not whether bodies intersect. Separate penalty functions handle foot contact and joint limits, but they do not guarantee collision avoidance between the two dancers.
Done right: retrieval, straight paths, and causal flow. When the retrieval database is diverse, the actor moves within learned patterns, and all three penalty functions (flow, sync, collision-prevention) are tuned, DualFlow produces coordinated, expressive, realistic two-person motion in 20 fast steps.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small motion generator that takes a text prompt ('both dancers spin left, then the follower mirrors') and a music clip. Retrieve 5 similar dances from a database. Run DualFlow for 20 steps, starting from random noise. Measure three things: FID (how realistic the pose is compared to real motion), R-precision (how well the generated motion matches the text description), and sync error (sum of distances between the dancers' joints).

The demo must show that: (1) retrieval actually changes the output because retrieved exemplars steer the model toward real patterns, unlike generation-from-scratch which invents plausible but generic moves; run with and without retrieval and observe the semantic quality difference; (2) 20 steps is faster than 50-step diffusion because fewer denoising steps mean fewer GPU computations, yet still produces good quality because the straight-line path accumulates less error; (3) the two dancers stay in sync throughout the sequence, not drifting, because the synchronization loss penalizes joint position differences during training.

Sync error = mean_t( sum_joints( ||pos_A_joint(t) - pos_B_joint(t)||^2 ) ) over all frames t

The demo is successful if sync error stays below 0.1 (dancers remain within a hand's width of their partner) while FID stays near 80 (realistic motion) and generation takes under 2 seconds on a single GPU.
