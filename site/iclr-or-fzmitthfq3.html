FALCON: Spatial Foundation Priors for Vision-Language-Action Models

A robot learns to act in 3D by feeding position-and-depth information to its action head instead of treating the scene as flat 2D pixels.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Today's robot models read an image as 2D pixels and output arm movements, but they struggle when scenes have depth, clutter, or objects at different heights. FALCON solves this by computing depth and position information from the RGB image and feeding those 3D clues directly to the part of the model that decides what actions to take, so the robot's action predictions are grounded in real 3D space.

Zero assumption version

Why 2D encoders fail in 3D worlds

A vision-language-action model (VLA) is a system that reads an image, understands a language instruction like 'pick up the red cup', and outputs arm joint angles and gripper commands. Most VLAs treat the image as a 2D grid of pixel colors. They learn to predict actions that happen to work on the training scenes, but when the scene changes—a cup sits in a shadowy corner instead of bright open space, or the camera angle shifts—their predictions fail.

A beginner might try to add depth sensing (a depth camera that outputs distance to each pixel) or use special 3D sensors, but depth cameras are expensive, fragile, and only work indoors. The robot can't be retrained every time a new sensor becomes available. The real problem: the model's internal reasoning never explicitly reasons about where things are in the room. It learns pixel patterns, not where objects sit in space.

The method in one sentence. FALCON extracts where objects are and how far away they are from the RGB image alone using a spatial foundation model, compresses that position-and-depth information into a list of numbers (tokens), and feeds those numbers directly to the action-prediction part of the model.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Input image and instruction

At each moment, the robot sees an RGB image and a language command.

Extract position and depth

A pretrained spatial foundation model (like MASt3R) reads the RGB and outputs lists of numbers that encode where things are and how far away they are: depth, surface direction, object boundaries.

Compress to one list

The many lists of numbers are pooled (the max value kept across all lists) to create a single compressed list containing the spatial essence.

Project into the language-vision space

A small learnable adapter (linear layer with zero initialization) projects that compressed spatial list into the same feature space as the pretrained language-vision model.

Fuse and predict actions

The spatial list is combined with the language and vision features, and fed to the action head to predict arm joint angles and gripper commands grounded in real positions and depths.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper injects lists of position-and-depth numbers into the action head—the part of the model that outputs arm movements. Instead of the action head receiving only language and 2D vision features, it now also receives 3D position information. Importantly, these lists go to the action head, not into the pretrained vision-language backbone, so the semantic reasoning (understanding what the instruction means) stays intact while position and distance get grounded where it matters: in action decisions.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: spatial grounding

measured quantity  =  spatial grounding quality

Spatial grounding is the degree to which the model's internal numbers reflect where things actually are in the world—their distance, orientation, boundaries, and relationships. A model with poor grounding guesses action angles from 2D pixel patterns; one with good grounding computes actions from real positions and distances. You cannot measure it directly from action errors alone (a lucky guess might work), but you can infer it by testing whether actions work on scenes the robot has never seen before, cluttered spaces, objects at different scales, and different viewing angles.

FALCON measures grounding indirectly by training the spatial number lists to predict actual distance and pose when available, and by testing whether actions stay accurate when distance or pose information is removed at test time. If a robot trained on image-plus-depth stays accurate on image-only, its spatial grounding is solid.

Why it works

Why feeding position-and-distance lists to the action head works

The core idea: understanding language and computing positions are different jobs. Language reasoning (understanding 'pick up the red cup') needs semantic context and learned associations; position reasoning (converting a goal into arm angles and gripper state) needs real distances and object locations. FALCON splits them. The pretrained language-vision model keeps doing what it does best (semantic understanding), while the position-and-distance lists handle locations. This separation means the robot gains spatial awareness without forgetting the semantic knowledge it learned during pretraining.

The adapter design (initialized to zero) ensures that at the start of training, the position-and-distance lists contribute nothing to the action prediction. Training then gradually learns when spatial information matters. This slow insertion prevents catastrophic forgetting—the robot doesn't suddenly unlearn how to understand language. The spatial foundation model (MASt3R) is pretrained on millions of images to output accurate position-and-distance lists, so the robot doesn't have to learn spatial reasoning from scratch; it reuses that knowledge. Depth, pose, and other spatial sensors, when available, further supervise the spatial lists during training, making them even more accurate, but the model remains functional with just images because the spatial foundation model learned to infer distance from color and shading.

Modality transferability arises because position and distance are modality-agnostic: whether you see depth from a camera, pose from IMU, or infer it from images, the underlying fact (where an object actually is) is the same. By training the spatial lists to capture this invariant spatial structure, FALCON ensures that actions work whether depth is present or absent at test time.

Math after the picture

Training objective: grounding via auxiliary supervision

First in words:

Total loss equals MSE of predicted pose angles versus ground truth, plus lambda times BCE of gripper binary state versus ground truth.

Then the compact form:

L = sum_{i=t}^{t+C-1} [ MSE(a_i_pose, a_hat_i_pose) + lambda * BCE(a_i_gripper, a_hat_i_gripper) ]

Every symbol:

a_i_posepredicted arm pose angles at step i (6 dimensions)
a_hat_i_poseground truth pose angles at step i
MSE(x, y)mean squared error: average of (x - y) squared over all dimensions
a_i_gripperpredicted gripper state (open or closed) at step i
a_hat_i_gripperground truth gripper state at step i
BCE(x, y)binary cross-entropy: a measure of error for yes-or-no predictions
lambdaa weight controlling how much to penalize gripper errors relative to pose errors
Cthe action prediction horizon (how many steps ahead to predict)
A tiny worked example
Suppose at step i the arm needs to move to angle vector a_hat = [0.1, 0.2, 0.15, 0.05, 0.3, 0.1] (6 angles) and close the gripper (1).
The model predicts a = [0.08, 0.19, 0.14, 0.06, 0.35, 0.09] (close to truth) and gripper = 0.85 (confident but not certain).
MSE = ((0.08-0.1)^2 + (0.19-0.2)^2 + ... + (0.09-0.1)^2) / 6 = (0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0001 + 0.0001 + 0.0025 + 0.0001) / 6 = 0.000550
BCE(0.85, 1) = -[1*log(0.85) + 0*log(0.15)] = -log(0.85) ≈ 0.163
With lambda = 0.5, the loss for this step = 0.00055 + 0.5 * 0.163 ≈ 0.0818.
Spatial tokens are trained under 3D conditions (depth or pose available) to reconstruct those 3D quantities, which further refines 3D grounding.

Pose errors are penalized equally for all 6 angles; gripper error is separate and scaled by lambda. When spatial lists are accurate, they reduce pose and gripper errors because the model reasons from real positions and distances instead of pixel patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Spatial model outputs garbage. If the spatial foundation model (MASt3R) fails to extract accurate position and distance from the RGB image—for example, in very dark scenes, heavily reflective surfaces, or exotic camera models it hasn't seen—the spatial lists will be unreliable, and the robot's actions will be misdirected. Tests under clutter and scale variation will reveal this; if the model fails on those, suspect the spatial encoder.
Modality mismatch at test time. If the robot is trained on image-plus-depth but at test time only images are available, the spatial lists must generalize from having seen depth during training to inferring distance from images alone. FALCON mitigates this by training the spatial encoder to handle both modes, but if the image signal lacks cues (uniform walls, featureless objects), the model can still fail. The paper tests this by training on some combinations and testing on others to verify it stays accurate.
Adapter initialization too large. If the zero-initialized adapter is replaced with large random weights, it would force the spatial tokens to dominate the action prediction from the start of training, overwriting the semantic knowledge from pretraining. The model would unlearn language understanding. Zero initialization is critical for preserving the pretrained backbone.
Done right. When spatial lists are accurate, the adapter is zero-initialized, and training incorporates extra 3D supervision (depth and pose when available), FALCON outperforms 2D baselines and maintains high accuracy across real-world tasks involving clutter, scale variation, and unseen objects, because position-aware predictions are more stable than pixel-based guesses.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a robot arm (real or simulated) with a camera. Record three short trajectories: (1) a clean scene where the target object is at arm height and well-lit, (2) a cluttered scene with shadows and multiple objects at varying heights, (3) the same object scaled 30% larger or smaller. Train a 2D VLA baseline on the clean trajectory, then test it on all three and measure success rate (did the arm reach and grasp the object?). Now train FALCON on the same clean trajectory using only RGB, and test on all three. FALCON should maintain high success on all three; the 2D baseline should drop sharply on clutter and scale variation.

Generalization gap = (success_rate_on_clutter + success_rate_on_scale_variation) / 2 for FALCON minus same for 2D baseline.

If FALCON scores 75% on clutter and scale-varied scenes while the 2D baseline scores 45%, the spatial grounding has proven its worth: the model no longer guesses from pixel patterns; it reasons from real positions and distances.
