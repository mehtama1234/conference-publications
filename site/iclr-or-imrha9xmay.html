YoNoSplat: Feedforward 3D Reconstruction

A single feedforward model reconstructs 3D scenes from photos by learning to predict both the 3D point cloud and the camera positions, avoiding training collapse through gradual mixing of real and predicted camera poses.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

Reconstructing a 3D scene from photos is usually slow—either the photos must have exact camera positions written down beforehand, or a computer must spend time solving for them. YoNoSplat trades optimization for speed: a single neural network sees your photos once and immediately outputs the full 3D scene and all camera positions, ready to render. The trick is teaching it without the model getting confused about which of two contradicting jobs is actually its job.

Zero assumption version

Why fast reconstruction is stuck

Today there are two ways to build a 3D scene from photos. First: if you have exact camera poses (you know precisely where each camera was pointing), you can quickly fuse all the photos into a 3D model. Second: if you don't have poses, the computer must solve for them—trying millions of tiny adjustments until everything lines up. This takes minutes or hours per scene.

A beginner asks: why not train a neural network once on lots of photo collections, then use it to instantly predict both the 3D point cloud (the blob of points that make up the scene) and the camera poses all at once? The answer: if you tell the network to predict both things simultaneously, it gets confused. The network's gradients (the signals telling it to move its weights in a certain direction) point in contradictory ways—the cloud-learning signal says move this way, but the pose-learning signal says move that way. The two tasks tangle together, and training collapses.

The core problem. Jointly predicting the 3D point cloud and camera poses in one network creates contradictory learning signals that destabilize training, making the feedforward approach impossible.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Extract image features

A pretrained vision model reads all photos and creates a numerical description of each.

Fuse across images

Special layers called attention blocks (which compare one photo to all others) mix information from all photos to understand the full 3D scene.

Predict point cloud and poses

The network outputs the 3D point cloud (a blob of Gaussian splats) and where each camera was.

Align to world coordinates

Use the predicted camera poses to rotate all local point-cloud pieces into one shared world frame.

Render and check

Synthesize images from the 3D model using the predicted poses, compare to originals.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

YoNoSplat changes how training unfolds over time. Instead of using the model's predicted camera poses from day one (which confuses learning), or always using ground-truth poses (which does not prepare the model for real inference), the paper introduces a scheduling rule: use ground-truth poses at first, then gradually swap in the model's own predictions as training continues.

the object it moves  =  Training schedule: ground-truth poses then mixed poses then predicted poses
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Exposure bias

measured quantity  =  Exposure bias (distribution mismatch)

Exposure bias is a mismatch between training and test time. At training, the network practices on perfect camera poses (ground truth). At test, it must work with its own imperfect poses. This gap is an invisible trap: the model never trains on realistic pose errors until it is too late, and its predictions are built for a world that does not exist at deployment.

YoNoSplat estimates exposure bias by tracking how often the model's predictions diverge from ground truth, and by slowly introducing real prediction errors into training. The Intrinsic Condition Embedding (a subnetwork) also tracks how sensitive the point cloud is to camera intrinsics (focal length, etc.), catching another hidden source of scale confusion.

Why it works

Why the mixing strategy works

The core idea: if you ask a network to predict both the point cloud and poses at once, it will try to make them agree with each other rather than with the truth. It is the classic chicken-and-egg: slightly wrong poses make the cloud look wrong; wrong cloud makes pose refinement useless. Both errors feed back into each other, and training spirals into collapse.

Mix-forcing breaks the spiral by removing the pose question early on. For the first half of training, the network is told: forget about predicting poses, I will give you perfect poses—just learn to predict the point cloud correctly. This builds a strong foundation (what the paper calls a prior—a learned expectation of what realistic 3D scenes look like). Once point-cloud learning is solid, the network gradually starts predicting poses too, but now it has a stable foundation. By the time poses are fully its responsibility, the point-cloud network is confident enough to absorb pose errors without collapsing. The mixing schedule is a curriculum: start easy, gradually increase difficulty.

The training loss and mixing ratio

First in words:

Total loss = rendering loss + weighted intrinsic loss + weighted pose loss; mixing ratio alpha increases linearly from 0 to 1 over training steps

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_render + lambda_intrin * L_intrin + lambda_pose * L_pose; alpha(t) = (t - t_start) / (t_end - t_start) if t_start <= t <= t_end, else clipped to [0, 1]; poses_used = alpha * predicted_poses + (1 - alpha) * ground_truth_poses

Every symbol:

L_renderLoss measuring difference between rendered and real images (combination of pixel error and perceptual distance)
L_intrinLoss measuring difference between predicted and true camera focal length
L_poseLoss measuring difference between predicted and true relative camera positions
lambda_intrin, lambda_poseWeights balancing how much each loss contributes to training
alpha(t)Mixing ratio at training step t, ranging from 0 to 1
t_start, t_endStep numbers marking when mixing begins and completes
predicted_poses, ground_truth_posesCamera position predictions from network vs. real recorded positions
A tiny worked example
Suppose training runs for 150k steps, with mixing from step 30k to step 120k.
At step 30k: alpha = 0, uses 100% ground-truth poses (fully teacher-forcing).
At step 75k (midway): alpha = (75k - 30k) / (120k - 30k) = 45k / 90k = 0.5, uses 50% predicted plus 50% ground-truth (equally mixed).
At step 120k: alpha = 1.0, uses 100% predicted poses (fully self-forcing).
At step 150k: alpha still 1.0 (clamped), continues with fully predicted poses to convergence.
The gradual transition allows the point cloud to stabilize early, then adapt to realistic pose errors late.

The mixing ratio alpha is the key: it is a schedule that turns on exposure to real pose errors slowly, avoiding the cliff where naive training collapses.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong mixing schedule. If you start introducing predicted poses too early, the network collapses (same as pure self-forcing). If you wait too long, the network never adapts to real pose errors and performs poorly at test. The schedule must be tuned per dataset.
Scale ambiguity. Without seeing the camera intrinsics (focal length, etc.), the network cannot tell if a blurry far object is small-and-close or big-and-far. YoNoSplat adds an Intrinsic Condition Embedding module to predict and embed this information, but if this module is bypassed or misconfigured, scale collapses.
Limited generalization. The model trains on specific datasets and camera intrinsics. If you feed it photos from a wildly different camera or scene type, point-cloud and pose predictions degrade. The method requires diverse training data covering the target domain.
Done right. A properly tuned mix-forcing schedule, combined with intrinsic embedding and diverse training data, yields both fast inference and accurate point cloud and poses even when input photos are uncalibrated.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of 20 photos of a simple scene (a toy, a room corner, a small object). Run YoNoSplat's trained model on these photos. Measure two things: (1) does the reconstructed 3D model look correct when rendered from the input camera angles, and (2) are the predicted camera poses close to the true poses (if you have them). Compare this to a naive network trained with ground-truth poses only (teacher-forcing) and tested on the same photos—it should fail to predict realistic poses. Then compare to a naive network trained with predicted poses from the start (self-forcing) and watch it collapse during training.

Rendering error = mean absolute difference between real and rendered images; Pose error = mean distance between predicted and true camera centers; Geometry error = mean distance from predicted to true 3D points.

The demo must make clear that mix-forcing yields low errors on all three metrics, while pure teacher-forcing fails at pose accuracy and pure self-forcing collapses completely.
