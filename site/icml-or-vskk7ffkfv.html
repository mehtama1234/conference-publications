Representational Similarity and Model Behavior in Multi-Agent Interaction

Measure how similar two models are internally and show that higher similarity helps cooperation while reducing originality.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

When two language models work together, their success depends not only on raw capability but also on how similarly they organize language and concepts. This paper studies that relation directly across many model pairs and many interaction games. The main result is a tradeoff: pairs with more similar internal representations cooperate better, while pairs with less similar representations produce more original outputs. That tradeoff matters because agent design often assumes that stronger individual models automatically make stronger teams, even though team behavior also depends on how well the members align internally.

Zero assumption version

Why team behavior cannot be predicted from single-model scores alone

Two models can perform well on their own and still work poorly together. In cooperative tasks, a pair needs compatible ways of parsing words, instructions, and intermediate statements. In creative tasks, some mismatch can help because the pair explores different directions instead of repeating the same one.

That means pair design needs a quantity beyond standalone accuracy. The paper studies representational similarity as that quantity because it may explain why some pairs coordinate smoothly while others produce broader but less aligned outputs.

The central move. Use internal similarity between two models to predict pair behavior, because shared internal structure can help coordination and reduce diversity at the same time.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  pairwise representational similarity

The hidden quantity is how similarly two models encode the same inputs. This is not visible from the final chat alone because two pairs can produce similar outputs even when one pair reached them through closely aligned internal states and the other through very different ones.

The paper finds that higher similarity, especially in early layers, is associated with better cooperation. That likely happens because early processing shapes shared lexical and semantic grounding, so both models attach similar structure to the same prompts and intermediate messages.

Why it works

Why similarity helps coordination and hurts originality

Cooperation improves when two models organize language and task cues similarly because one model's intermediate message is then easier for the other to interpret as intended. Shared grounding reduces coordination errors, so the pair can spend less effort repairing misunderstandings.

Originality can fall for the same reason. If both models map prompts into similar internal structures, they are more likely to propose related continuations and related plans. That reduces the range of explored directions because the pair starts from similar internal assumptions.

Early layers matter most because they shape basic word and concept processing. If those foundations align, later interaction has a stable common base. If they diverge early, later coordination has more opportunities to drift even when the models are individually strong.

Behavior changes with similarity

First in words:

A compact summary is that cooperation tends to rise with representational similarity while originality tends to fall, because shared internal structure helps agreement and reduces divergence.

Then the compact form:

pair_score = coop_weight * similarity - originality_weight * similarity + base_quality

Every symbol:

pair_scorea simplified summary of how similarity affects interaction behavior
coop_weighthow much similarity helps shared-goal coordination
originality_weighthow much similarity reduces varied idea generation
similaritymeasured representational closeness between the two models
base_qualitypair quality coming from factors other than similarity
A tiny worked example
Pair A has similarity 8 on a ten-point scale, while Pair B has similarity 3.
In a cooperation task with coop_weight = 4 and originality_weight = 1, Pair A gets more coordination benefit because 4 * 8 is larger than 4 * 3.
In an originality task, the same Pair A loses more diversity because 1 * 8 is larger than 1 * 3.
So Pair A is the stronger choice for strict coordination tasks.
Pair B can be the stronger choice for idea-generation tasks because its lower similarity preserves more varied directions.

The actual analysis is statistical and layer-wise, but the core tradeoff is simple: similarity helps alignment because shared internal structure reduces misread messages, and it reduces diversity because both models start from more similar assumptions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Similarity can be confounded with shared training data. If two models are similar because they were built from very similar data and design choices, the measured relation may partly reflect common origin rather than a universal interaction law.
The task choice may change the tradeoff. A task that needs both close coordination and fresh ideas may not fit the simple similarity-versus-originality split because it demands both alignment and variation at different stages.
Similarity metrics may miss useful differences. If the metric compresses internal structure too aggressively, two pairs can look equally similar even when one shares task-relevant concepts and the other shares only shallow patterns.
Done right. The study is most useful when model designers match pair similarity to task needs, because coordination-heavy tasks benefit from closer internal alignment while originality-heavy tasks may benefit from more difference.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small testbed with several model pairs and two game types: one that rewards strict coordination and one that rewards varied idea generation. Measure a layer-wise similarity score for each pair, then compare that score against task results for both game types.

The demo should show that high-similarity pairs win more often on coordination tasks while lower-similarity pairs produce broader outputs on originality tasks, because that is the paper's central tradeoff.

selection_value = cooperation_need * similarity + originality_need * (1 - similarity)

A convincing demo must show that pair selection should depend on the job, because the best partner for agreement is not always the best partner for broad exploration.
