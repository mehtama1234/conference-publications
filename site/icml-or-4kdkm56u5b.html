Not All Frequencies Are Equal: Energy-Adaptive Diffusion for Time Series Forecasting

Diffusion models for time series should add noise at different rates to different frequency bands based on how much signal energy each one carries.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Time series forecasting predicts future values by learning from past data. Recently, diffusion models (a method that trains by gradually adding random noise to a signal and then learning to reverse it) have been applied to this task. But existing diffusion approaches make a key mistake: they assume all frequency components of the signal should be corrupted at the same speed. In reality, time series have non-uniform energy distribution — high-frequency details (rapid changes) carry less signal energy than low-frequency trends (long-term patterns). This paper proposes EADiff, which adapts the noise schedule to match each frequency band's energy level.

Zero assumption version

Uniform noise overwhelms weak signals

Time series contain patterns at many speeds. Low-frequency trends (things that change slowly over months) carry the most energy. High-frequency noise (things that change fast, hour to hour) carries much less. A naive approach would be: add the same amount of random noise to every frequency at the same rate during diffusion training. This breaks in two ways. First, high-frequency details are already weak, so adding uniform noise to them washes them out completely, leaving only corrupted noise — the model cannot learn from data where the signal disappears. Second, the strong low-frequency trend barely gets disrupted, so the model never sees how this trend progressively deteriorates under increasing noise, which means during generation the model cannot reliably reverse that degradation because it was never trained on it.

A beginner might try: just dial down the noise globally. But that does not work — dialing down noise makes low frequencies even harder to learn from (they need strong perturbation to be predictable), while high-frequency jitter still gets drowned out.

In one sentence. The paper adaptively scales noise strength per frequency band based on how much signal energy that band carries.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Split into frequencies

Break the time series into frequency bands using wavelets (a mathematical tool that separates a signal by speed).

Measure energy per band

Calculate how much signal strength lives in each frequency band.

Noise schedule from energy

Create a learnable rule that adds strong noise to high-energy bands and gentle noise to low-energy bands.

Condition on slow trends

Give the model the low-frequency part as a guide so it learns what fast changes should look like given the trend.

Weight the loss by frequency

When training, penalize errors on high-energy bands more and low-energy bands less, matching what the model should focus on.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The noise schedule (the amount of random noise added at each training step) is no longer uniform across all frequencies. Instead, it is a learned function that adapts per frequency band and per time series instance.

the object it moves  =  Noise schedule
The hidden quantity

Energy per frequency band

measured quantity  =  Signal energy distribution

The paper measures and uses a hidden quantity: how much of the total signal power lives in each frequency band. This is not explicitly called out in the abstract but is the core insight — if you know a band is weak (low energy), you know adding strong noise will overwhelm it because the noise magnitude will dwarf the original signal, making it impossible to reconstruct; if it is strong (high energy), it can handle stronger perturbation because the original signal is large enough that the model can still trace its shape through the noise.

The paper estimates this by decomposing the time series using wavelets and computing the energy (squared magnitude) in each band. Then the model learns a modulation function that takes this energy as input and outputs the noise strength for that band.

Why it works

Why it works: adapting noise to signal strength

Diffusion training works by gradually corrupting a signal with noise, then learning a model to reverse that corruption. For this to work, the model needs to see a steady difficulty curve: easy examples early (small noise), hard examples later (large noise). But if you add uniform noise, weak frequency bands hit maximum difficulty immediately (they are washed out) while strong bands barely change. So the model wastes capacity learning to denoise the already-weak bands from garbage — those bands carry no useful information at that noise level — and it never sees strong bands at a range of difficulties, which means it cannot learn how strong bands degrade gradually.

EADiff fixes this by making the noise schedule match each band's energy. High-energy trends can take stronger noise without disappearing, so the model sees them across a full difficulty spectrum. Low-energy details get gentler noise, so they stay visible even when perturbed. Now every band provides a balanced learning signal. Second, by conditioning on low-frequency trends as a guide, the model learns that trends are the scaffold and details decorate around them. Finally, by weighting the training loss higher for high-energy bands, the model focuses effort on getting the important patterns right. Together, these pieces let the model respect the natural multi-scale structure of the time series throughout training and generation.

Math after the picture

The noise-modulation equation

First in words:

For each frequency band, the noise level added at training step t is proportional to the signal energy in that band, modulated by a learnable function.

Then the compact form:

sigma_band(t) = f_theta(E_band) * sigma_base(t)

Every symbol:

sigma_band(t)Noise standard deviation for this frequency band at step t
f_theta(E_band)Learned modulation function that takes energy E_band and outputs a scaling factor
E_bandSignal energy (squared magnitude) in this frequency band
sigma_base(t)Base noise schedule as a function of diffusion step t
A tiny worked example
Low-frequency band: E = 100 (high energy), f_theta(100) = 1.2 (strong noise). sigma_band(t) = 1.2 * sigma_base(t).
Mid-frequency band: E = 30 (medium energy), f_theta(30) = 0.8 (medium noise). sigma_band(t) = 0.8 * sigma_base(t).
High-frequency band: E = 5 (low energy), f_theta(5) = 0.3 (gentle noise). sigma_band(t) = 0.3 * sigma_base(t).
Same base schedule sigma_base(t), but adapted per band. Weak bands get much gentler perturbation; strong bands sustain the full corruption.

The learned function f_theta adjusts noise strength based on signal energy, so each band receives perturbation matched to its actual strength.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Wrong energy estimate. If the wavelet decomposition or energy calculation is noisy or biased, the modulation function learns a wrong mapping. High-noise data with misleading high-energy jitter will get over-perturbed. Fix: use stable energy estimators and validate on clean data first.
Over-conditioning on trends. If the low-frequency conditioning is too strong, the model memorizes the trend and fails to generate diverse plausible details around it. The forecast becomes rigid. Fix: balance trend strength so it is a guide, not a straitjacket.
Limited to stationary or slowly drifting series. If energy distribution shifts suddenly (regime change, structural break), the fixed schedule becomes invalid. The method assumes energy is stable within a forecast window. Fix: detect and re-estimate energy for new regimes.
Done right. Validate energy estimates on held-out clean examples. Tune conditioning strength by observing generated samples. Apply to forecasting tasks where frequency content is stable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple time series forecasting test: generate a synthetic time series with a strong low-frequency sine wave (slow trend) plus weak high-frequency noise (fast jitter). Split it into train and test. Train a diffusion model twice: once with uniform noise, once with EADiff. Generate forecasts for 30 timesteps ahead on both models. Measure reconstruction accuracy separately for the low-frequency trend (using a low-pass filter to extract it) and the high-frequency component (using a high-pass filter). Plot both.

trend_accuracy = 1 - MSE_trend(forecast_low_freq, truth_low_freq); detail_accuracy = 1 - MSE_detail(forecast_high_freq, truth_high_freq)

The demo should make impossible to miss: EADiff preserves the slow trend AND the fast details simultaneously, while uniform noise trades off between them (good trend but bad details, or vice versa).
