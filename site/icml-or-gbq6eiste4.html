DeepSight: Predicting Future Road Scenes to Drive Better

A driving model learns to predict what the road will look like seconds into the future, then uses that foresight to plan safer, smarter driving moves.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Most self-driving systems decide what to do based only on what they see right now. DeepSight adds foresight: it predicts what the road scene will look like in the next 2 seconds, frame by frame, and uses those predictions to plan ahead instead of react. When the road gets tricky (a traffic light, a detour, an emergency vehicle), it can also ask a language model for common-sense reasoning about what to do. The result is safer driving in rare, hard situations.

Why Today's Self-Driving Systems Stumble

When a self-driving car picks its next move, it looks at cameras pointed at the road right now and predicts where to go next—like you deciding to turn based only on what is in front of your face at this instant. It works on normal, well-lit days when the road is clear. But when something surprising happens—a traffic light changes, a cone marks a detour, an ambulance honks—the system has no idea what is coming, so it reacts too late or too fast.

A beginner might think: just add more camera views or look further ahead in the image. But that does not work. The reason is that the road 2 seconds from now is not written in the image you see now—it is only visible if you understand what people and cars nearby are going to do. A pedestrian who is moving toward the curb will be in a different spot. A car merging will have shifted lanes. The system would need to predict all those moves, which is hard and gets bottlenecked by computing power.

The core insight. Instead of guessing trajectories of every car and person, predict a compact summary of what the entire road will look like in the future.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode current scene

Vision model extracts dense features from current camera images in bird's-eye-view format.

Predict future latent codes

Model generates a compact code (latent feature) for each of the next 5 time steps (0.5s each), capturing semantic road layout, vehicles, and pedestrians.

Compute trajectory loss

Current and predicted frames are fed to a trajectory predictor; loss measures how well predicted future helps predict correct steering/speed, because the same learned features must encode information useful for both understanding the past and planning the future.

Detect complexity

Model assesses if the scene is normal (highway) or tricky (traffic light, detour, emergency). If tricky, activate reasoning.

Adaptive reasoning step

If needed, query a language model for common-sense ("what do you do at a red light?"), then blend that reasoning into the driving decision.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most driving models output one predicted trajectory (a path) at a time and make decisions reactively. DeepSight changes what gets predicted: instead of guessing individual future object positions, it predicts latent semantic codes (learned numerical summaries) of what the entire road environment will look like, frame by frame, for the next 2 seconds. This is not a movie (too much texture data); it is a compact semantic snapshot—enough to encode "car in left lane, pedestrian on curb, green light"—but not pixel-level detail.

the object it moves  =  latent semantic codes for future frames
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: latent semantic features

measured quantity  =  latent semantic feature (compact learned summary)

A latent semantic feature is a list of numbers (typically 100–1000 numbers) that the model learns to create during training. Each number encodes a small piece of meaning: one might represent "is there a car in the left lane?" (as a floating-point number between 0 and 1), another "how fast is traffic moving?". The model does not hand-engineer these meanings; instead, training forces the model to discover which features help predict the next driving move, because loss only shrinks when predictions become accurate, so the model converges toward features that capture real patterns in the world. The result is a compact description of the road scene that is much smaller than a full image but still captures everything needed for safe driving.

DeepSight measures this quantity by training the model to predict these latent features for five consecutive future frames, one every 0.5 seconds. The paper uses a pre-trained vision model (called DINOv3) to extract ground-truth latent features from real future video frames (obtained during training with real driving data), then trains DeepSight to predict those features. During driving, the model generates predicted latent features for the next 2 seconds without seeing the real future, and uses them to decide steering and speed.

Why it works

Why predicting the future works for driving

The core mechanism is this: if a driving model knows what the road will look like in 2 seconds, it can plan a path that avoids hazards that have not happened yet. A human driver does this all the time—you see a car slowing down ahead and ease off the gas before you get close, not after. DeepSight teaches the model to predict upcoming road states, then trains it to choose actions that look good in both the present frame (to avoid immediate collisions) and in the predicted future frames (to avoid problems that are coming). The key is that predicting semantic features (not pixel data) is fast, because semantic features are lists of a few hundred numbers while full video is millions of pixels per frame, so the computation shrinks by a factor of thousands. You can generate five frames in one forward pass using learnable query tokens (special parameters that ask the model to produce multiple outputs in parallel), which makes foresight computationally cheap enough to run in real-time on driving hardware.

When scenes are unusual (e.g., a construction zone, a traffic light, an emergency vehicle), the model has learned when to ask a language model for help. The language model is a large pre-trained system trained on text from the internet; it knows common sense ("when you see a red light, you stop", "an ambulance has priority") because it learned patterns from billions of words about how humans reason about driving. DeepSight learns to detect when a scene is unusual and activates this reasoning, because scenes that differ from training data need external knowledge to avoid mistakes, and blending the language model's answer into its trajectory decision combines fast learned reflexes with reliable explicit reasoning. This hybrid approach avoids running the language model constantly (which is slow and expensive) while still accessing its reasoning for hard cases, so the car is both fast and smart.

How the model trains

First in words:

Total loss = trajectory error + reasoning error + future-frame prediction error

Then the compact form:

L_total = CE(T_pred, T_gt) + CE(T_cot, T_gt_cot) + MSE(F_pred, F_gt)

Every symbol:

T_predsteering and throttle predicted by the model
T_gttrue steering and throttle from the expert human driver
T_cottext reasoning (chain-of-thought) generated by the model
T_gt_cottext reasoning extracted from the expert driver (or from a language model asked the same scenario)
F_predlatent semantic features predicted for the next 5 frames
F_gtground-truth latent features extracted from real future video frames using DINOv3
CEcross-entropy loss: counts the number of mistakes when predicting class tokens or text
MSEmean squared error: average squared difference between predicted and actual features
L_totalthe combined training error; lower is better
A tiny worked example
Scenario: model predicts steering angle. Correct answer (from human driver) is 5 degrees left, which encodes as token 42 (out of 100 possible). Model predicts token 37 (3 degrees left). Cross-entropy loss for trajectory: 2.1
Model predicts reasoning: "I see a red traffic light, so I should slow down." Ground truth reasoning: "Traffic light is red, I need to brake." Text tokens partially match (both mention red light, slow/brake). Cross-entropy loss for reasoning: 1.8
Model predicts latent features F_pred = [2.3, 1.5, 0.8, 2.1, 1.9] for five frames (each is a simplified one-number example; real ones have hundreds). Ground truth F_gt = [2.0, 1.8, 0.9, 2.2, 2.0]. MSE loss = average of (2.3-2.0)^2, (1.5-1.8)^2, ... = 0.03
Total training loss: 2.1 + 1.8 + 0.03 = 3.93. During training, the model adjusts its internal weights to shrink this number. Over thousands of driving scenarios, the model learns to predict trajectories, reason about hard cases, and foresee upcoming road states.

The three loss terms force the model to do three things at once: steer correctly, explain its reasoning when needed, and predict what the road will look like next. All three are equally weighted in training, which means the model's internal parameters adjust to reduce all three errors at the same rate, so none gets ignored while the model perfects the others.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Predicted future diverges from reality. If the model's five-frame prediction is wrong (e.g., it predicts the car ahead will keep moving straight, but the car suddenly brakes), the driving decision built on that false prediction will be unsafe. This happens when the test scenario is very different from training data (a rare intersection layout, an unusual weather event).
Language model reasoning is slow or wrong. The adaptive reasoning mechanism asks a large language model (a pre-trained system trained on internet text) for help on unusual scenes, because such models have broad knowledge that the driving model lacks. If the language model is slow (it takes time to generate text), the entire driving decision is delayed and the car misses the moment to brake. If the language model gives nonsensical advice (it can hallucinate or confabulate), the car might follow that bad advice and crash.
Latent features do not capture the right concepts. The model learns latent features that help it predict trajectories during training, but those features might not encode the most safety-critical information. For example, features might encode texture (wet vs dry pavement) but miss red-light detection because that was rare in training data.
Done right. The model is trained on diverse, expert-driven scenarios with clear labels for hard cases (traffic lights, construction, emergency vehicles). Testing happens in closed-loop simulation (the car drives the whole route, not just one frame). This catches most problems before deployment.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple simulation where a self-driving car navigates a route with both normal driving (highway, empty road) and one tricky scenario (a red traffic light, a construction cone, an ambulance horn). Run two versions: one that predicts future latent features and one that does not. Measure success as: Did the car stop at the red light? Did it slow down when the ambulance appeared? Did it navigate around the cone safely without leaving the road?

Success rate = (correct decisions on normal scenes + correct decisions on tricky scenes) / total decisions

The demo should make clear that foresight prevents disasters that pure reaction-based driving causes. Without foresight, the car brakes too late or swerves too fast. With foresight, the car begins its safe move before the hazard forces it.
