Differentially Private Equilibrium Finding in Polymatrix Games

Players in a network can reach a fair deal without revealing their secret values, because the noise protecting privacy grows weaker as more players join.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

In multi-player games, players negotiate over a network to reach a balance where nobody wants to switch strategies. But their secret values must stay private: a player will not reveal her utility matrix (the payoff she gets for each action) to avoid exploitation. This paper shows that distributed algorithms can compute fair equilibria with noise that shrinks as the network grows — the first to prove this is possible without giving up either accuracy or privacy.

Zero assumption version

Secret values and fair deals do not mix

In real markets and security games, multiple players need to agree on a strategy — where each player uses the same move in every paired interaction with neighbors. Each player's secret payoff (how much she gains from each outcome) must stay hidden to prevent someone from taking advantage later.

A naive approach: send all secret values to a central server, which computes the fair deal and tells everyone the answer. But when no trusted central server exists, players must negotiate in a distributed way — each player talks only to neighbors and keeps updating her strategy. The problem: as players share their strategy updates over the network, an attacker listening to the edge between two neighbors might infer the secret payoffs from those messages.

Prior work tried to add noise to hide the secrets, but noise breaks accuracy: the more players you add, the more noise you need to cover for the larger network, and the equilibrium gets worse. Conversely, to get an accurate answer, you had to use little noise and accept a huge privacy leak. The bind: you could not have both.

The dilemma. Distributed equilibrium finding with privacy protection was impossible: noise to hide secrets made answers inaccurate; small noise for accuracy leaked secrets.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Each player estimates her payoffs from neighbors.

Computes the gradient (direction to move her strategy) by averaging payoffs from pairwise games with neighbors.

Add noise scaled to degree.

Noise is inversely proportional to how many neighbors a player has; low-degree players get more noise.

Each player sends noisy strategy to neighbors.

Neighbors observe the noisy move but cannot reliably infer the secret payoff underneath.

Each player updates using a regulated step.

Use a proximal gradient step (a smooth adjustment rule) with regularization to dampen large moves.

Repeat; noise budget shrinks as N grows.

As more players join, the per-player noise can decrease, improving accuracy while keeping privacy.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the noise injection and update-dampening strategy in a distributed equilibrium algorithm. Instead of adding a uniform noise amount to every player, it scales the noise and the regularization (a penalty that dampens large strategy moves) inversely with each player's degree (number of neighbors). A player with few neighbors gets more noise and stronger dampening, because the direction she wants to move her strategy is more sensitive to her neighbors' hidden payoffs.

the object it moves  =  The noise scaling rule and regularization weight
The hidden quantity

Sensitivity and the privacy cost

measured quantity  =  Gradient sensitivity to changes in payoffs

The hidden quantity is how much a player's strategy gradient shifts when one neighbor's payoff changes by one unit. In formal terms: if player i's payoff matrix changes slightly, how much does her gradient (the direction she wants to move) change? A low-degree player (few neighbors) has a large sensitivity: one neighbor's payoff shift has a big effect on her gradient, so that change leaks into the network. A high-degree player's gradient is diluted by many neighbors, so a single neighbor's change has little effect.

The paper estimates this sensitivity and uses it to set the noise amount: add more noise to players with higher sensitivity. This way, even if an attacker listens to all network messages, the observed strategy changes look the same whether player A's payoff was high or low. Formally, the attacker cannot tell the two scenarios apart because the noise scrambles the signal completely; the observed messages are equally likely in both cases.

Why it works

Why adding noise by degree shrinks the privacy cost

The core insight: sensitivity to payoff changes is *inversely proportional to degree*. A player with degree d (d neighbors) sees each neighbor's payoff weighted by 1/d in her gradient. If d is small (few neighbors), a single neighbor's payoff change has a large relative effect — high sensitivity. To hide this, you need noise that is *proportional* to sensitivity. So noise scales inversely with degree: low-degree players get high noise, high-degree players get low noise.

In a growing network, average degree grows. As N increases, most players gain more neighbors, so each neighbor contributes less weight to that player's gradient (dilution), so a single neighbor's payoff change has less effect on the gradient (lower sensitivity). The algorithm sets noise proportional to sensitivity, so lower sensitivity yields smaller noise. As the network grows, noise shrinks naturally for most players. Smaller noise yields closer answers to the true equilibrium because noise is a random perturbation to the update direction, and less perturbation keeps the updates on course. Smaller noise yields better privacy because less noise means less randomness to mask the secret payoff. Prior methods required more noise as N grew, forcing a choice; this paper's degree-scaled approach breaks that link.

The regularization (a penalty on large strategy moves) works hand-in-hand with noise: high noise makes updates random; without regularization to oppose large moves, random noise would push the strategy far off course. Regularization acts like a spring pulling the strategy back toward a baseline, resisting large moves. Strong regularization means even a large noisy update only nudges the strategy a little. The regularizer strength is scaled inversely by degree: low-degree players (who receive high noise) get strong regularization, so their noisy updates do not overshoot. This is why noise and regularization together work: noise protects privacy, regularization prevents noise from derailing the algorithm.

Math after the picture

The regularized update rule

First in words:

New strategy equals old strategy minus learning rate times (noisy gradient plus regularization penalty). The regularization penalty is the strength (scaled inversely by degree) times the change from the stored baseline strategy.

Then the compact form:

pi_i(t+1) = argmin_p [ (1/T) sum_t <p - grad_i_noisy(t), p> + lambda / deg_i * ||p - pi_i_baseline||^2 ]

Every symbol:

pi_i(t+1)the new strategy of player i at iteration t+1
grad_i_noisy(t)the noisy gradient of player i at time t (includes noise scaled to 1/deg_i)
lambdathe regularization strength
deg_ithe number of neighbors of player i
pi_i_baselinethe strategy player i stored from a previous phase (a reference point)
Tthe number of iterations so far
A tiny worked example with two players
Suppose player 1 has 2 neighbors (degree 2); player 2 has 6 neighbors (degree 6).
True gradient for player 1: direction to move is (0.8). Noise injected: scale is 1/2 = 0.5, so add noise ~0.5 * (random). Noisy gradient: ~0.8 + 0.25 = 1.05.
True gradient for player 2: direction is (0.8). Noise injected: scale is 1/6 ≈ 0.17, so add noise ~0.17 * (random). Noisy gradient: ~0.8 + 0.05 = 0.85.
Regularization: player 1 (low degree) gets stronger penalty (lambda/2); player 2 (high degree) gets weaker (lambda/6).
Player 1 moves cautiously (high regularization dampens noise-induced drift); player 2 moves more freely (low noise, low regularization).
Result: both players' payoffs stay roughly hidden (attacker cannot distinguish real from adjacent games), but player 2's answer is more accurate because her noise is smaller.

The noise and regularization are both inversely proportional to degree. Low-degree players get more noise, which hides their payoffs from eavesdropping attackers; the strong regularization resists that noise and keeps their updates stable, so the noise does not throw them off course.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Graph structure matters. In a star graph (one central player, all others with degree 1), the center player sees all payoffs directly and must add enormous noise. The method only guarantees improvement as the graph becomes more uniform.
Convergence speed slows with noise. Adding noise to gradients increases variance in player updates, so equilibrium is reached more slowly. The trade-off between privacy and convergence speed is real.
Coarse correlated equilibrium, not Nash equilibrium. The algorithm finds coarse correlated equilibrium: no single player can improve by switching strategies alone. A true Nash equilibrium (no coalition improves together) requires the optimizer to enforce more constraints. More constraints enlarge the optimization problem, and larger problems converge slower because the algorithm must search a larger space. The paper chose the weaker equilibrium notion to keep the problem small enough to converge quickly even with privacy noise present.
Done right. Use the degree-scaled method on networks where degrees are reasonably uniform; set noise levels according to each player's degree. For networks where some players have many neighbors and others have few, consider pre-processing to balance the topology first.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Simulate a small network of 8-16 players arranged on a graph (a ring or a grid). Each player has a random payoff matrix (kept secret). Run the algorithm with degree-scaled noise and regularization. At each iteration, measure two metrics: (1) how far the current strategy profile is from the true equilibrium (the exploitability gap), and (2) how much an attacker can distinguish between two adjacent games by eavesdropping on player messages (the privacy leakage). Plot both metrics over iterations.

Exploitability = max_i (utility_i(best_response) - utility_i(current_strategy)); Leakage = KL divergence between observed messages in game G and adjacent game G'

The key observation: as N grows, exploitability shrinks to zero and leakage shrinks to zero simultaneously, whereas naive noise-addition methods force you to choose one or the other.
