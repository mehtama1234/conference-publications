Verifying Chain-of-Thought Reasoning via Its Computational Graph

To verify if a reasoning step is correct, trace the model's internal circuits and recognize error fingerprints in how information flows.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Language models answer hard questions by thinking step by step, but how do you know if step 3 is actually right or just sounds plausible? Most methods guess by looking at the final answer or checking the numbers. This paper cuts deeper: it watches the model's internal wiring (the circuits that compute each step) and finds that correct and incorrect reasoning leave different structural signatures in that wiring. By recognizing these signatures, you can catch errors before they propagate, and even fix them.

Zero assumption version

Why it is hard to catch reasoning mistakes

When a language model solves a multi-step math problem, it generates one sentence at a time, building a chain of reasoning. The problem: the model sounds confident at every step, whether it is right or wrong. If you only look at the final answer, you might miss that step 2 was the real culprit. If you train another model to judge correctness, it is still a black box—you have no idea why it thinks step 2 failed, which means you cannot learn from that failure or fix it.

A beginner might try training a second model to score each reasoning step as good or bad (like a human grader), but that model is also opaque. You know it flagged an error, but not where in the model's internals the error lived, so you cannot trace why it happened or what to fix.

The core problem: Other methods tell you that a step is wrong, but this paper shows where in the model's wiring the error lives.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces the model's standard neural network layers (MLPs: multi-layer perceptrons, the dense computation blocks) with sparse, interpretable surrogates called transcoders. A transcoder is trained to mimic what the original layer does, but it represents its computation as a sparse sum of named, interpretable features instead of a dense list of numbers. This swap lets you see the model's reasoning as a sparse, traceable chain of features—a circuit—rather than a black box of numbers.

The hidden quantity

The error fingerprint: structural signatures in computational graphs

measured quantity  =  Graph structure as error signature

The paper measures an abstract quantity: the structural properties of each reasoning step's computation graph. A computation graph is a map of how information flows from input to output through the model's internal features. The paper hypothesizes that when a step's reasoning is correct, its graph has one shape (number of nodes, edges, depth, connections); when reasoning is wrong, it has a different, recognizable shape. The paper calls this the error fingerprint.

The paper estimates this by building the actual graph using an algorithm that traces high-impact information flow backward from the model's output logits. Then it extracts numerical features from the graph structure: how many nodes, how many edges, how many layers deep, what fraction of features are active per layer, degree distribution (how many connections per node). These numbers become the signal that a classifier learns to recognize as 'correct' or 'incorrect.'

Why it works

Why tracing circuits finds reasoning errors

Here is the chain of cause and effect. First, when a language model reasons step by step, each step depends on earlier ones. If step 2 is wrong, it is because the model's internal features made a wrong choice. The model's layers are connected in a fixed architecture, so information flows along predictable paths. When step 2 is correct, the relevant features (neurons or transcoder dimensions) light up in a certain pattern and collaborate in a particular way. When step 2 is wrong, the model's features are still firing, but they are collaborating differently—activating in the wrong order, or the wrong subset of features, or with different connection strengths.

By replacing dense layers with sparse transcoders, you make this collaboration visible: you can see which named features are active and which edges carry information. When you trace backward from the final output logit through the step's computation, you build a graph that captures this collaboration. The graph's shape—how many features, how they connect, how deep the information flow runs—is thus correlated with whether the step is correct. Importantly, the paper finds that this shape differs between correct and incorrect steps in a way that a simple classifier (boosted decision trees) can learn. The classifier does not understand reasoning by itself; it just recognizes the geometric pattern of the graph. When it sees a step with a different chain of reasoning, its graph's shape tells the classifier whether that step is likely wrong, because the shape matches patterns from past mistakes.

The classifier decision boundary

First in words:

score = GBC(graph_features) = GBC(num_nodes, num_edges, depth, activity_per_layer, ...) where graph_features are topological properties extracted from the attribution graph, and GBC is a gradient boosting classifier trained on step-level correctness labels

Then the compact form:

P(incorrect | step) = GBC_classifier(G_features)

Every symbol:

G_featuresvector of structural properties from graph G (node count, edge count, depth, sparsity, degree distribution, ...)
GBC_classifiertrained gradient boosting model that maps graph structure to correctness probability
P(incorrect | step)probability that this reasoning step contains an error, given the graph structure
stepone line of reasoning in the chain-of-thought
A tiny worked example
Step A (correct math): graph has 12 nodes, 18 edges, depth 5, avg degree 3.0, entropy 2.1. Classifier scores: 8% error risk.
Step B (incorrect math): graph has 9 nodes, 12 edges, depth 3, avg degree 2.7, entropy 1.4. Classifier scores: 82% error risk.
Same step type, very different graph shapes. The classifier learned that shallow graphs + low entropy = high error. Step B's shape matches the error fingerprint.

The classifier does not compute reasoning correctness itself; it recognizes whether the step's computation graph shape resembles earlier correct or incorrect examples.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Transcoders are expensive to train. Replacing every MLP layer requires training a transcoder for each one. This is computationally heavy and limits how big a model you can instrument. A very large model might be too costly.
Error fingerprints are task-specific. A graph structure that signals error on math problems may not signal error on reading comprehension. The classifier must be retrained for each new reasoning domain, so the method does not generalize for free.
The paper assumes access to step-level labels. Building the dataset requires a human or judge model to label each step as correct or incorrect. For real-world reasoning, getting accurate labels is hard.
When circuit analysis is done well. If transcoders accurately capture the model's computation, and the reasoning task has clear right/wrong steps, the method reliably flags errors and even guides interventions.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small demo: pick a language model (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B or smaller), pick a reasoning task with ground-truth correct/incorrect steps (e.g., arithmetic or boolean logic), train a transcoder for one of its MLP layers, construct the attribution graph for one correct reasoning step and one incorrect step, extract graph features (node count, edge count, depth, sparsity), plot both graphs side by side, and show that their shapes are visibly different. Then train a simple classifier on 10 correct and 10 incorrect steps, and have it predict a different step's correctness based only on graph structure. The demo succeeds if the structure-based predictions beat random guessing and the graphs look recognizably different.

accuracy = (true_positives + true_negatives) / total_steps

Once the demo works, the main lesson becomes clear: you do not need to understand what reasoning is; you just need to recognize the geometric shape that correct reasoning leaves in the model's internals, versus the shape that errors leave.
