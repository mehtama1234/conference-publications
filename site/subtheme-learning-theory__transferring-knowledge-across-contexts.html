Transferring knowledge across contexts

Understanding when knowledge learned in one setting can help in a different setting, and the conditions under which transfer learning succeeds or fails.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

A model trained to recognize dogs in photographs can often recognize dogs in paintings, sketches, or even cartoons, even though it was never trained on these styles, which suggests that the model learned something general about dog-ness rather than specific pixel patterns. A language model trained on English text can often answer questions about historical events it was not explicitly trained to answer because it learned underlying reasoning patterns. A robot trained to grasp cylinders can often grasp boxes, even though it was trained with different objects. Transfer learning — using knowledge learned in one context to improve learning in another context — is one of the most useful and common techniques in machine learning, meaning many practitioners rely on it to reduce training time and data requirements. Yet when does transfer help and when does it hurt, since sometimes knowledge from the old task misleads rather than guides, wasting the learner's time on false patterns? This subtheme investigates: what conditions allow knowledge to transfer safely so you can rely on it, and how do you measure whether transfer is actually occurring rather than just coincidental similarity by comparing to a baseline, which determines whether an experiment actually validates the technique?

The difficulty is determining what transfers and what does not. Low-level features like edges and textures in images transfer well across visual domains, but high-level concepts may not (a model trained to recognize dogs may struggle with cats if it learned dog-specific features). Transfer can also fail silently: a model may perform well on the new task not because of genuine transfer but because both tasks are easy. Negative transfer occurs when knowledge from the source task interferes with learning the target task — the model must unlearn incorrect assumptions. Additionally, you must quantify how different two tasks are; two tasks that seem similar might require different models, while two tasks that seem different might share deep structure.

Researchers analyze source and target domains, measuring their similarity through theoretical and empirical metrics to quantify how close they are. They study domain adaptation techniques that adjust a model trained on one domain to work on a different domain with limited retraining. They investigate feature reuse — what parts of a model (layers, connection-weight patterns or decision nodes, individual neurons) encode transferable knowledge and which parts are task-specific — so they know what can move and what must stay fixed. They analyze when transfer helps by comparing transfer learning to training from scratch, isolating the benefit of the source task. They also study negative transfer, identifying which source tasks hurt which target tasks and how to select source tasks wisely, to avoid picking knowledge that misleads. The work includes investigating task relationships and hierarchies, asking whether learning on many related tasks jointly (multitask learning) improves performance compared to learning each task independently, since tasks may help or hinder each other in unexpected ways.

How the work breaks down

Transfer learning splits into five natural directions because different obstacles arise: measuring whether transfer is actually happening (compared to random luck) matters so you know the improvement comes from reuse rather than easy tasks, understanding when and why it fails helps avoid wasting time on wrong source tasks, adapting models to new domains enables cost-effective deployment, handling changes in what data you see solves real-world drift, and building theories about what conditions allow knowledge to move between tasks provides guarantees so practitioners know when they can trust transfer.

Measuring Transfer Actually Occurs

A core problem in transfer learning is knowing whether your model improved because it really learned something that transfers, or just because the new task happens to be easy. When you train a model on new data starting from a pretrained checkpoint versus training from scratch, both might end up at high accuracy—but did the earlier training actually help? Temporal Generalization performs this kind of reality check, testing methods that claim to predict future data by checking whether they outperform just using the most recent model. The Generalization Spectrum takes this idea further and creates a measurement system that distinguishes three types of transfer: when a model learns something in one context and uses it in a slightly different context (memorization to near-transfer), when the model learns abstract reasoning that applies broadly (in-context learning), and when improvements on one task actually expand what the model can do on other tasks. This subtheme needs these measurement tools because claiming transfer happens is meaningless without a baseline—you must show that transfer added value beyond what you'd get from other sources like task similarity or random chance.

When Source Knowledge Harms Learning

Transfer sometimes fails because the source task teaches the wrong lessons. A model trained on one task might learn patterns that actively mislead it on the target task—this is called negative transfer. On the Bayes Inconsistency of Disagreement Discrepancy Surrogates addresses a specific problem: when you measure whether two tasks are similar enough to transfer between, the measurement itself can be wrong under adversarial conditions, so you may pick source tasks that harm rather than help—this happens because the measurement approach breaks down when data is adversarially chosen. Understanding Transfer Learning of RNA Foundation Models identifies a key mechanism: pretrained representations are not automatically transferable. The paper shows that frozen representations from pretraining often don't help on new tasks, but pretraining helps by speeding up how the model trains (optimization initialization) when the pretraining objective aligns with the downstream task—the model converges faster to better solutions, not because frozen features transfer, but because the starting point is better. Residual Feature Integration Sufficient to Prevent Negative Transfer provides a theoretical guarantee: if you add a small connection that lets the model choose to use or ignore the pretrained features, convergence is no worse than training from scratch—this proves the model won't get stuck learning the wrong lessons from the source.

Adapting to New Domains with Limited Retraining

When you have a model trained on one domain and need to apply it to a different domain, full retraining is expensive. This group tackles domain adaptation—adjusting a model to work on new data without starting over. Quantifying Cross-Domain Knowledge Distillation in the Presence of Domain Shift analyzes a common strategy: teaching a large pretrained model to a smaller student model, then using the student on the new domain. Surprisingly, even when the domains differ substantially, there is a regime where the student generalizes better than the teacher—this happens because the student's smaller size acts as a regularizer (forces the model to avoid overfitting to the source domain), which helps more than the teacher's larger capacity when shifting domains. Finding this regime requires understanding when domain differences help rather than hurt, which this paper quantifies. Coupled Training with Privileged Information uses a two-model approach: one model sees extra information during training that won't be available at test time, and this helps the main model learn better features because observing the privileged information guides what patterns to learn. The paper shows this consistently outperforms doing it in two separate steps (first learning from privileged info, then retraining) because training jointly lets the models influence each other; it includes guarantees so you know when this approach won't fail. Geometric Rate-Distortion Invariance for Domain Generalization provides a theory for domain adaptation: instead of assuming all domains look the same, treat domain shift as a controlled problem of balancing accuracy against resistance to shifts. The paper offers finite-sample guarantees that the adapted model will work on new domains within bounded differences from the source. Discriminative Alignment for Deep Representations under Label Shift handles a specific domain problem: when the test set has different class frequencies than training (label shift). The method adds penalties that discourage the model from overcomplicating its decisions, aligning how the model separates classes—it improves accuracy in both situations where you have very little target data and where the shift is extreme, because it learns to separate classes properly despite the changing frequencies.

Understanding Task Relationships and Shifts

Transfer depends on how related source and target tasks are. Some shifts are predictable; others sneak up. This group studies the hidden structure of tasks and the patterns in how data changes. Covariate-Guided Clusterwise Linear Regression for Generalization to Unseen Data takes the view that different parts of input space may need different models—some regions transfer well from source to target, others don't. The method learns which inputs should use global models (transfer everywhere) and which need instance-specific fitting, with theoretical guarantees on error bounds. Identifying Partially Observed Causal Models from Heterogeneous Nonstationary Data proves something counterintuitive: when data comes from different distributions (heterogeneous) or its distribution shifts over time (nonstationary), you can still identify the underlying cause-and-effect structure. This matters for transfer because knowing which variables cause which outcomes tells you what to focus on moving between domains. Neyman-Pearson Classification under Both Null and Alternative Distributions Shift addresses a decision problem: when the data distribution shifts, both the typical case and the rare case change—your classifier must handle both. The paper gives guarantees that errors of both types stay under control despite the shifts. Testing For Distribution Shifts with Conditional Conformal Test Martingales solves a detection problem: how do you know when the data distribution has shifted enough to hurt your model? The test provides anytime validity (you can stop checking whenever you want and still have valid conclusions) because of its mathematical construction with martingales (probability sequences with special properties), and it detects shifts faster than standard methods because it uses the data stream more efficiently. When More Data Doesn't Help reveals a hard constraint on multitask learning: sometimes having more data per task doesn't solve the problem—the fundamental difficulty of the tasks means they conflict with each other (one task's learnable patterns may contradict another's), and no amount of data can overcome that because the conflict is structural, not statistical. This cautions against assuming transfer always scales with data size.

Learning Under Sparse Data and Difficult Distributions

Transfer is most valuable when you have limited target data or the target distribution is hard. This group studies how to learn efficiently under scarcity. Beyond Spectra: Eigenvector Overlaps in Loss investigates how solutions in optimization space align between tasks. Previous work blamed a mathematical issue called poor Hessian conditioning, but this paper reveals the real culprit: the eigenspaces (the principal directions where the loss function changes) must align between source and target. When they do, the model can transfer smoothly; when they don't, reuse fails. Hedging on the Frontier: Learning New Tasks with Few Samples tackles few-shot learning: with only a few examples of a new task, which model should you pick? The method prunes candidates based on monotonicity (if one model outperforms another on some tasks, it should on others) and hedges by staying near the frontier of trade-offs. On the Computational Complexity of Performative Prediction addresses strategic scenarios where the prediction affects the outcome—a model predicts loan defaults, but the prediction changes lending behavior. Finding optimal solutions becomes computationally hard (PPAD-complete), equivalent to finding Nash equilibria in games. Particle Flow for Learning from Label Proportions handles weak data: instead of knowing each example's true label, you only know the proportion of positive labels in groups. The method uses particle flow dynamics to infer labels and works on both tabular and image data with convergence guarantees. Positive Distribution Shift as a Framework for Understanding Tractable Learning shows that some hard learning problems become easy if the data distribution shifts in certain ways—when the shift selects for simpler structure in the target domain, standard training methods (which update weights by following the direction that reduces loss most quickly) can solve problems that seemed intractable in the original domain. Transfer Learning in Nonparametric Regression with Deep ReLU Networks establishes error bounds and convergence rates for deep networks under hierarchical composition structure—the theory proves that deep transfer can overcome the curse of dimensionality when the target task shares hierarchical structure with the source.
