The Geometric Origin of Grokking: Accelerating Generalization via Active Structural Reorganization

This paper argues that grokking begins when the model's internal states for discrete input pieces reorganize in angle and relative direction, then speeds that reorganization up with a loss term that pushes representations away from unhelpful crowded arrangements.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Grokking is the training pattern where a model memorizes training examples early, stays bad on test data for a long time, and then suddenly begins to generalize much later. The paper's main claim is that this delayed jump is not primarily about loss scale or weight norm. It is about a structural reorganization of learned representations. As training continues, the internal states for the discrete input pieces move into a configuration with cleaner angular separation and more task-aligned relations. The authors then turn that observation into an intervention called R2G loss. R2G adds a repulsion term that pushes representations away from crowded or misaligned arrangements, so the reorganization happens earlier and generalization arrives sooner.

Zero assumption version

Memorization can fit labels long before the representation supports rule learning

In many grokking tasks, the model reaches near-zero training error quickly. That shows the network can store input-output pairs. But test accuracy remains poor because the internal representation has not yet organized examples according to the rule that generated the labels. Training loss alone does not reveal this gap. Two models can fit the training set equally well while having very different internal structure.

The paper studies that hidden structure and claims that late generalization begins when representations separate according to task-relevant relations, especially angular relations among the internal states for the discrete input pieces. Until that reorganization happens, the classifier reads out memorized patterns that do not extrapolate. After it happens, the readout can exploit a cleaner rule-aligned arrangement and test accuracy rises quickly.

The paper's diagnosis: Grokking is delayed because the network first learns a training-set solution with poor representation structure, then only later reorganizes those representations into a rule-supporting arrangement.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes training, not architecture. It adds an auxiliary R2G term to the normal task loss. That term acts on hidden representations and encourages separation in their relative directions, based on the paper's observation that grokking coincides with this kind of structural reorganization.

the object it moves  =  training objective over hidden representations
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: representation organization before the classifier layer

measured quantity  =  Whether internal states encode rule roles or just memorize examples

Training accuracy does not tell us whether the network learned the rule or stored enough case-specific shortcuts to cover the training set. The hidden quantity is the organization of the internal states for the discrete input pieces inside the network. If states that play the same rule role point in consistent directions and states that play conflicting roles separate, the final classifier can apply one rule across many unseen inputs.

The paper emphasizes angular organization because length alone is not the key signal. Two states can have large magnitude and still be poorly arranged for generalization if they point in almost the same direction when the task needs them to separate. The experiments and ablations argue that the decisive transition is a change in relative direction, not merely a change in scale.

Why it works

Why repulsion can accelerate grokking

Early in training, the standard loss-minimization update rule often finds a short path to memorization because it only needs to reduce training loss on seen examples. That path does not require representations to align cleanly across rule-equivalent cases. As long as the final layers can separate the training set, the optimizer has little pressure to organize the hidden states into a transferable form.

R2G adds that missing pressure. By penalizing crowded or overly aligned hidden states, it makes memorization-only arrangements less comfortable. The optimizer can still fit the training data, but now it is steered toward a solution where examples that should play different roles occupy more distinct directions. Once those directions are cleaner, the readout layer needs fewer case-specific adjustments and can express the underlying rule on unseen inputs.

This explains why the intervention accelerates rather than replaces grokking. The paper does not claim that repulsion creates generalization from nowhere. It changes the path through the space of learned weight settings so that the structural reorganization that would otherwise happen late begins earlier.

Math after the picture

The training objective

First in words:

The final objective is the original task loss plus an auxiliary penalty that increases when hidden representations are too crowded in direction.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_task + lambda * L_R2G

Every symbol:

L_taskordinary prediction loss on the training labels
L_R2Grepulsion penalty applied to hidden representations
lambdaweight that controls how strongly repulsion influences training
L_totalcombined objective optimized during training
Worked example: why the extra term changes the path
Suppose two hidden states from different rule roles point in nearly the same direction.
Under the task loss alone, the model can still fit the training labels by adjusting later weights.
After adding R2G, that crowded pair contributes extra loss.
Training can reduce the extra loss by moving the two states into more distinct directions.
Once many such pairs are separated, the hidden space becomes easier for the final classifier to use as a rule-based representation rather than a table of memorized cases.

The exact form of the repulsion term matters less than its role: it adds pressure for structural separation among hidden states.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Repulsion can separate states that should stay close. If the penalty is applied too strongly or at the wrong layer, it can push apart examples that share the same rule role and damage the representation instead of cleaning it.
Not every delayed generalization pattern is the same. The paper studies specific algorithmic and linguistic settings. Other tasks may show delayed generalization for optimization reasons that this representation-based story only partly explains.
The useful structure may depend on layer choice. If the monitored hidden states are too early, they may not encode the rule yet. If they are too late, the readout may have already collapsed several useful distinctions.
Done right. The intervention is most convincing on tasks that already exhibit grokking under standard training, because then the paper can show that the same late transition happens earlier after adding the repulsion term.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Train a small transformer (a sequence model built from repeated blocks that let each position reweight other positions in the same sequence) on a standard grokking task such as modular arithmetic. Save hidden representations during training and project them into two dimensions for visualization. Compare a baseline run and an R2G run. The demo should show three curves over time: training accuracy, test accuracy, and a separation score based on relative direction among hidden states. The important result is that the separation score rises before or alongside the jump in test accuracy, and that the R2G run reaches this transition earlier.

gap = test_accuracy - train_accuracy

The central check is causal order: representation reorganization should appear before the improvement in generalization, not merely after it.
