SCENT: Geometry-Aware Optimization for Risk That Counts Everything

When losses pile up exponentially, measure how the objective curves locally and use that curvature to step carefully, avoiding overflow and underflow.

Paper explainersAll themesOptimization

Some machine learning problems ask: what does the average loss look like across a huge universe of unlabeled cases? This abstract-averaged-loss (called entropic risk) is cheap to state but expensive to compute—the summation inside has billions of items. This paper proposes SCENT, a training algorithm that sidesteps the summation trap by solving a twin problem called the dual formulation. The key trick is to equip each training step with a local curvature measurement (Bregman divergence from an exponential function). Where the exponential curve bends sharply (near large values), this measurement tells the algorithm to shrink the step size so numbers do not overflow. Where the curve is gentle (near the center), it expands the step so training stays fast. This adaptive scaling keeps all values stable—no overflow—because small steps in steep regions cannot produce giant jumps that overflow, while staying fast in safe regions. The result is faster convergence than fixed-step methods because the algorithm never wastes steps recovering from numerical disasters.

The Averaging Trap: Infinite Sums and Broken Training

Many real problems ask: given a huge pool of unlabeled items (think: all possible sentences, all possible images on the internet), what is the average loss when we apply our model to each? Computing this average means summing the losses over billions or trillions of items. Even if each individual loss is fast, the sheer count makes the sum too expensive to evaluate.

A naive approach: approximate the sum by sampling a few items and averaging their losses. This works in simple cases, but when losses can be very large or very small (spanning many orders of magnitude), the averages become unreliable. The numbers overflow or underflow—they become infinity or zero—and the training algorithm collapses. Standard training methods (like gradient descent, which nudges weights by a fixed amount in the direction of steepest descent) assume the numbers stay sensible. They do not adapt to the wild scale of compositional losses.

In one sentence. SCENT reformulates the problem as a twin task where we optimize two variables together instead of one, using steps that naturally respect the shape of exponential growth and decay.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Reformulate the problem

Instead of averaging losses directly, set up two variables: one estimates the average, the other acts as a backstop guardrail.

Measure local curvature at each point

Compute the Bregman divergence (a bent-distance measure that bends sharply where exponential functions curve steeply) at the current position.

Take a curvature-aware step

Use this curvature to scale each training update: where the curve bends sharply (steep regions), take smaller steps; where it is flat, take bigger steps.

Update both variables together

Step the model weights in the direction of steepest descent; step the guardrail variable to keep it aligned with where the average should be.

Repeat and converge

After many steps, both variables stabilize; numbers never explode; the model weights settle at a good solution.

What the paper changes

What the Paper Changes

The paper changes the way each training step is computed. Standard methods (like random gradient descent) use a fixed step size or a size that only depends on the loss history. SCENT instead computes the step size locally using Bregman divergence, which is a curvature measure shaped by the exponential function. This curvature tells us whether we are in a steep region (where numbers might overflow) or a shallow region (where we can afford a bigger nudge), and the algorithm adapts the step size accordingly.

The hidden quantity

Bregman Divergence: The Curvature Measure

measured quantity  =  Bregman divergence from exponential

The Bregman divergence is a way to measure distance between two points that is not straight-line distance; instead, it bends to match the local curvature of a function. In SCENT, this function is the exponential (like e to the power of x). Because losses pile up exponentially (one outlier loss of magnitude 100 pulls the average much harder than ten losses of magnitude 10), the Bregman distance naturally weights big changes near the tails more carefully than small changes in the middle.

The paper estimates this divergence at each step to determine how far and in which direction to nudge the model weights. Where the exponential curves sharply (tail regions), the Bregman distance is stretched, so nudges are smaller. Where it curves gently, nudges are larger. This auto-adapted scaling keeps all numbers in a safe, sane range.

Plain reading. Bregman divergence is like a bent ruler that stretches and shrinks depending on how sharp the exponential curve is at each point. This keeps the model from overshooting into overflow or getting stuck.
Paper reading. The Bregman divergence induced by a negative exponential function uses the ratio of exponential values at two points to measure distance in a curved space. Formally, D(u, v) grows when the exponentials (exp(u) and exp(v)) differ greatly, capturing how much the function curves at that location.
Why it works

Why Dual Formulation Plus Curvature-Aware Stepping Works

The core insight: do not try to average losses directly when the sum is huge. Instead, reformulate the problem as two linked variables. One variable is the model weights; the other is a dual variable that acts as a shadow—it estimates what the average loss should be, and it feeds back to constrain the model's training step. This reformulation is mathematically equivalent to the original problem, but it splits a hard single task into two simpler linked tasks.

Next, standard training steps (fixed-size nudges) fail here because losses grow exponentially—one giant loss drowns out the others, causing overflow. SCENT instead computes the step size using Bregman divergence. Because Bregman distance measures how sharply the exponential function curves at each point, it naturally shrinks steps near the tails (where numbers might overflow) and expands steps in the center (where they are safe). This curvature-aware scaling means the algorithm never overshoots into overflow and never gets stuck where gradients are undetectably small.

Finally, when both variables step together using this curvature measurement, the dual variable keeps nudging the model toward weights that would produce a more stable and computable average loss, while the model weights actually move to lower that loss. The two variables pull toward each other. This coupling brings convergence fast and keeps all numbers in a stable range.

Math after the picture

The Dual Optimization Equation

First in words:

Penalty = average of losses + lambda times (divergence penalty that grows when model weights stray from the dual variable's estimate)

Then the compact form:

min_w max_u [ L(w,u) + lambda * D_B(u, u_old) ]

Every symbol:

wthe model weights we want to optimize
uthe dual variable that estimates and constrains the average
L(w,u)the compositional loss: log of the average of exponentials of individual losses
lambdathe coupling strength (how much the dual variable constrains the model)
D_B(u, u_old)the Bregman divergence from the negative exponential; measures geometry-aware distance
Tiny numerical example
Suppose three loss values are 2, 5, 8 (tiny for clarity).
Naive average: (2 + 5 + 8) / 3 = 5.
Entropic average (Log-Expectation-Exponential): log((exp(2) + exp(5) + exp(8)) / 3) = log((7.4 + 148.4 + 2981) / 3) = log(1045.6) ≈ 6.95.
The entropic average is higher because exp(8) ≈ 2981 dominates; it drags the average up even though 8 is only one of three values.
Bregman divergence near u = 6: D_B(6.1, 6.0) ≈ 0.1 × (1 + exp(6)) ≈ 0.1 × 402 ≈ 40. Small move, big penalty, because we are in the tail of exponential growth.
Bregman divergence near u = 2: D_B(2.1, 2.0) ≈ 0.1 × (1 + exp(2)) ≈ 0.1 × 8.4 ≈ 0.84. Same move, tiny penalty, because curvature is gentle in the center.

The algorithm steps the dual variable u using Bregman divergence as its metric; this ensures the model and dual variable stay synchronized and convergence is stable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Minibatch too small. If the minibatch has only a handful of examples, the sample average is noisy, and the Bregman divergence estimate becomes unreliable. The algorithm may still step, but with inflated uncertainty. Use minibatches large enough that the measured average (the fraction of losses in each range) is stable.
Lambda tuning. The coupling strength lambda controls how much the dual variable constrains the model. If lambda is too small, the two variables decouple and convergence becomes slow. If lambda is too large, the model overshoots and oscillates. Requires tuning per problem class.
Non-convex objectives. The convergence proof in the paper assumes the loss is convex (one global minimum, no local traps). For non-convex problems (like neural networks), the algorithm does not have a formal guarantee; it works in practice because the curvature-aware scaling still prevents overflow and keeps steps stable, making it converge faster than standard SGD.
Done right. Pre-normalize losses to a bounded range (e.g., clip or standardize). Use large enough minibatches and tune lambda conservatively. On convex problems, SCENT trains stably without overflow because the curvature scaling automatically shrinks steps wherever the exponential curve bends sharply (in regions where numbers could explode), keeping numbers in safe ranges. This stable stepping lets training run all the way to a good solution because the model never gets stuck recovering from overflow, and every step makes forward progress. The convergence rate improves to order one divided by square root of steps, which is faster than standard methods because the algorithm adapts to the local curve steepness and never takes oversized steps that waste iterations.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple extreme classification task: given a text snippet, predict whether it belongs to one of 10,000 rare categories. Losses will be skewed (a few categories appear often, most are vanishingly rare). Implement two trainers: one using standard SGD on the Log-Expectation-Exponential loss, one using SCENT. Run both on the same data for 500 training steps. Track: (1) did either overflow? (2) how fast did the average loss drop? (3) after training, on held-out test examples, what fraction of predictions are correct?

stability = (1 - overflow_events / total_steps) times 100 percent, convergence_speed = loss_at_step_100 minus loss_at_step_500, final_accuracy = correct_predictions divided by test_examples

SCENT should show zero overflows while standard SGD overflows repeatedly; SCENT's loss should drop at least twice as fast; final accuracy should be higher because SCENT trains reliably to the end.
