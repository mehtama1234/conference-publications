Strat-Reasoner: Strategic Reasoning in Multi-Agent Games

An LLM learns to win multi-agent games by reasoning about what other players will do, then adjusts its strategy based on group performance.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

LLMs can solve many puzzles, but they fail at games with multiple opponents. The hard part: you cannot just predict what will happen next, because other players are also thinking about what you will do. This creates a circular puzzle that single-player reasoning tools cannot handle. Strat-Reasoner teaches an LLM to win by integrating other players' thoughts into its own reasoning, comparing its logic step-by-step against others, and tuning its strategy to beat the group average.

Why LLMs lose at games with multiple players

An LLM is trained to predict the next words in text. That works fine for answering questions or writing essays. But in a game with other players, predicting the next move is not enough. If you are playing rock-paper-scissors against a human, you need to guess what the human will throw. The human knows you are trying to guess, so the human will try to throw what beats your guess. Now you are in a loop: you predict them, they predict you predicting them. Single-player reasoning breaks here.

The standard way to learn games is called reinforcement learning (RL). The computer tries different moves, gets a score (a number saying how good that move was), and tweaks its strategy to get a higher score next time. But in multi-player games, the non-stationarity problem hits: if you learn to beat Alice, then Bob changes the game because Bob does not play like Alice. There is no steady target to aim at. Standard RL assumes the world stays the same; it does not. Also, RL gives you a reward signal for the final move, but with many thinking steps in a row (a chain of reasoning), it is hard to know which thinking step deserves the credit for winning or losing. Did you lose because step 1 was bad, or step 5 was bad? This credit assignment problem makes learning slow and unreliable.

In one sentence. The method teaches an LLM to reason about other players' thoughts, score its own reasoning against the group, and tune its strategy using those group-level scores.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Integrate other players' thoughts

When an LLM reasons through the game, it now includes predictions of what each opponent will think and do.

Generate multiple reasoning chains

The LLM samples many different thought sequences (different ways to reason through the same game state).

Compare chains using CoT scoring

A separate evaluator (a Chain-of-Thought comparison module) grades each reasoning chain on its logical quality, not just the final score.

Compute group-relative advantage

For each chain, subtract the average chain quality across all other LLMs from this LLM's chain quality.

Train policy on relative scores

Update the LLM's strategy weights to produce higher-scoring chains, using those group-relative advantage signals.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training signal (the reward that teaches the LLM which moves are good). Normally, RL gives a yes/no or a single number (you won or lost). Strat-Reasoner gives a scored reasoning chain: a ranking of how logical and sound the entire chain of thought is, measured against a group average instead of an absolute benchmark.

the object it moves  =  reward signal
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: chain reasoning quality

measured quantity  =  reasoning chain quality

A reasoning chain is the sequence of thinking steps an LLM uses to decide its move. For example: (1) opponent Alice always copies my last move, (2) so if I throw rock, Alice throws rock, (3) I should throw paper to beat rock, (4) I choose paper. Each step is a guess or a deduction. Strat-Reasoner measures how good this entire chain is, not just whether the final move wins.

The paper measures chain quality using a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) comparison module: another LLM that reads two reasoning chains side by side and says which one is more logical and sound. It is a pairwise scorer. The score is not absolute (10/10 for a perfect chain); it is relative: this chain's quality minus the average quality of all other LLM players' chains in that game. This group-relative measure is the hidden quantity.

Why it works

Why it works: building a mutual model

In a single-player game, you just predict what will happen and optimize for winning. In a multi-player game, you must model what others will do, and they are modeling what you will do. Strat-Reasoner addresses this by making the mutual reasoning explicit: each LLM's chain of thought now includes a predicted chain of thought for other players. This is recursive reasoning: each agent reasons about the other agents' reasoning, building a chain that nests thoughts inside thoughts.

The second key move is scoring chains, not just final outcomes. If the LLM wins by accident, but its reasoning was sloppy, reinforcement learning should not reward the sloppiness. By grading the reasoning quality (via CoT comparison), Strat-Reasoner tells the LLM: good reasoning is good, regardless of luck. This solves part of the credit assignment problem, because the chain-level score pins down which thinking steps led to high-quality logic.

Third, the group-relative advantage signal solves non-stationarity. If the LLM's reasoning is better than the average opponent, it gets a positive signal (a higher score to encourage), because it is doing something the other players are not doing well enough. If its reasoning is worse, it gets a negative signal, because the other players are already beating it without effort. This is a moving target instead of a fixed target. When all players learn and improve together, the benchmark shifts, but the learning stays stable because the signal is always relative to the current group, not to a fixed rule. This prevents the target from disappearing as opponents adapt.

The group-relative advantage equation

First in words:

For each reasoning chain from player i, the advantage is the chain quality score of player i minus the average chain quality score of all other players in that round.

Then the compact form:

A_i = Q(chain_i) - (1/N-1) * sum_{j != i} Q(chain_j)

Every symbol:

A_iadvantage for player i
Q(chain_i)chain quality score for player i (from CoT comparison)
Q(chain_j)chain quality score for other player j
Ntotal number of players
A tiny worked example
Three LLMs play a game. CoT scorer rates their chains: LLM_A chain gets 8, LLM_B chain gets 6, LLM_C chain gets 4.
For LLM_A: average of others = (6 + 4) / 2 = 5. Advantage = 8 - 5 = +3 (positive reward).
For LLM_B: average of others = (8 + 4) / 2 = 6. Advantage = 6 - 6 = 0 (no change).
For LLM_C: average of others = (8 + 6) / 2 = 7. Advantage = 4 - 7 = -3 (punishment).
LLM_A's weights move to repeat high-quality chains. LLM_C's weights move away from its low-quality chains. Relative grading avoids a fixed target.

This equation replaces the usual RL reward (which is zero-sum or fixed-outcome) with a group-relative signal. A player improves its strategy when its chain quality exceeds the group average, because that difference tells the player exactly how much better it is, and the signal stays usable even as all players improve together.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

CoT comparison module learns incorrectly. If the evaluator (the side model that scores chains) is poorly trained, it will give wrong scores, and the LLM will learn the wrong strategy. If the scorer rewards flashy but illogical reasoning, the LLM will copy that.
Opponent model is incomplete. If the LLM's guess about what an opponent will do is wrong, the entire reasoning chain is built on false assumptions. The chain quality score reflects that false reasoning (the CoT scorer will grade it lower if it detects broken logic), and the group-relative signal helps by comparing to what other LLMs did. But if all LLMs make the same wrong guess about the opponent, the group-relative signal will not catch it.
Instability with many players. As more players enter the game, each LLM's reasoning must track more opponent models. The reasoning chains grow longer and more complex. The group-relative signal also becomes more fragile: if one other player learns very fast and dominates, the relative advantage signal may collapse.
Done right: explicit opponent modeling. When the LLM builds a clear model of each opponent's decision-making (not a vague guess), and the CoT evaluator is trained to rank chains on true logical soundness, the method is stable and learns fast.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a multi-player coordination game: three LLMs (or three copies of the same LLM with different random seeds) play a simple game, e.g., a numbered auction where the highest bidder wins but pays their bid. Train one version of the LLM with standard RL (no opponent modeling) and another with Strat-Reasoner. Run both versions for 100 training steps on the same game. Log each LLM's reasoning chain at each step, and the CoT scorer's grade for that chain. Compute the group-relative advantage for each player at each step.

improvement = (strat_reasoner_avg_win_rate - baseline_avg_win_rate) / baseline_avg_win_rate * 100%

The demo must show that Strat-Reasoner's reasoning chains improve in logical quality over steps (because the CoT scorer grades each chain, giving the LLM direct feedback on reasoning soundness), that the group-relative advantage signal is consistently positive for the winning LLM (because winning LLMs produce better chains than the others), and that the LLM's strategy converges to a stable pattern (because the training signal from relative advantage pushes it toward consistent, high-quality reasoning).
