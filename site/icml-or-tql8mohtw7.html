Reductive Lie Neurons for Matrix Data

A neural network that handles matrix-shaped data by enforcing a mathematically rigid symmetry rule that survives group transformations.

Zero assumption version

Tabular data and symmetries break existing layers

Most neural network layers treat inputs as flat lists of numbers. But scientific data often comes in matrix form: a drone's velocity paired with position uncertainty (a covariance matrix), the inertia of a rotating shape, or the numbers inside a 3D Gaussian splat. When the physical world rotates or transforms, these matrices obey specific transformation rules.

A naive approach: feed the matrix entries into a standard neural network layer, treating them like any other numbers. But this breaks the symmetry—the output changes shape in the wrong way when you rotate the input. The layer does not know that rotation happened. Existing equivariant networks handle this only for compact groups (like rotation), not general matrix groups. They also tie the architecture to one specific symmetry, forcing redesign for each new task.

The gap. General matrix data needs neural layers that strictly respect how matrices transform—without rebuilding the layer for every new symmetry.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces a new building block: the Reductive Lie Neuron layer. Each layer applies operations (linear maps, commutators, contractions) designed to respect matrix transformations. The key change: instead of a generic activation, ReLNs use a custom bilinear form (a way to multiply two matrices together) that automatically stays invariant under the group action.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Invariance under group action

measured quantity  =  Adjoint-invariant bilinear form

The paper measures a mathematical property: does the layer stay the same when you apply a group transformation to the inputs and outputs? This is called equivariance. The hidden anchor is the bilinear form—a rule for combining two matrices that does not change when the group acts on them. Think of it as a measuring tape that gives the same reading no matter how you rotate the whole scene.

Most existing forms (the Killing form) become degenerate (useless) on certain matrix spaces. The paper constructs a non-degenerate adjoint-invariant bilinear form—one that works reliably across reductive Lie algebras and never breaks down.

Why it works

Why it works: Symmetry as a structural constraint

The core insight: if you force the layer to respect how matrices transform under the group, it automatically handles any rotation, permutation, or other symmetry—because the mathematics of the group does the work for you. You do not hardcode each symmetry; you build the layer so that symmetry compliance is automatic.

Here is the chain: (1) Input matrices transform via the adjoint action—what happens when the group acts on the algebra. (2) The two weight matrices apply channel-wise linear maps, chosen to preserve the adjoint action. (3) The Lie bracket (commutator) combines two matrix features in a way that respects the algebra structure—if you rotate the inputs and outputs, the commutator relationship holds. (4) The invariant bilinear form contracts the result into a scalar that never changes when the group acts. (5) Stacking these layers means the whole network output transforms correctly: rotate the drone's velocity + covariance, the network output rotates the same way. Once one layer is equivariant, and you stack equivariant layers, the whole stack is equivariant.

Math after the picture

The invariant bilinear form equation

First in words:

The special inner product combines two terms: a traceless part (the Killing form on the algebra) and a trace part (the inner product on all-scalar matrices). Both parts scale correctly under group transformations, and together they avoid degeneracy.

Then the compact form:

B(X, Y) = 2n * tr(X' * Y') + tr(X) * tr(Y)

Every symbol:

X, YTwo matrices in the Lie algebra gl(n)
X', Y'The traceless parts (remove the scalar diagonal)
tr(...)Trace: sum of diagonal entries
2nScaling factor that ensures non-degeneracy
B(X, Y)The bilinear form: a scalar that is invariant under group action
A tiny worked example
Suppose n=3 (three-dimensional matrices) and X = [[2, 0, 0], [0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 2]] (identity times 2).
Then X' (traceless part) = [[0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 0]], and tr(X) = 6.
Let Y = [[1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 0]] (2x2 identity block).
Then Y' = [[0.33, 0, 0], [0, 0.33, 0], [0, 0, -0.67]], and tr(Y) = 2.
Bilinear: B(X,Y) = 2*3 * tr(0,0) + 6*2 = 0 + 12 = 12.
Rotate both X and Y by a group element: B(Ad_g(X), Ad_g(Y)) = 12 (unchanged, as required by invariance).

The bilinear form is a measuring stick that gives the same reading no matter how the group transforms the input, because both its terms scale together with the group action.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Singular bilinear form. If the form degenerates (becomes zero on non-zero inputs), the layer produces no information. ReLNs avoid this by construction: the non-degenerate form is built into the architecture.
Mismatch between group and data. If you tell ReLN the data is GL(3)-equivariant but it is actually only rotation-equivariant, the layer respects more structure than needed, wasting capacity. Match the symmetry claim to the actual data.
Large matrix dimensions. As n (matrix size) grows, computing the Lie bracket and bilinear form grows as the cube of n. Very high-dimensional geometric data may become slow.
Done right. Carefully specify which group acts on your matrices (is it GL(n), rotations, or a reductive subalgebra?). ReLNs transfer across subgroups without redesign—use this to test once and apply widely.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small 3D drone state estimator: inputs are a sequence of noisy velocity measurements and their uncertainty covariances. The network predicts the drone's final position. Train an ReLN-based encoder and compare it to a standard equivariant baseline (EMLP). Test consistency by rotating the input covariance matrices by random angles; both networks should produce outputs that rotate the same way. Measure: (a) prediction accuracy (RMSE on test positions), (b) rotational consistency (do rotated inputs produce rotated outputs?), (c) compute latency (milliseconds per forward pass).

Rotational consistency = fraction of test examples where rotating input covariance by angle theta also rotates the output by theta (within numerical precision)

The demo should show that ReLNs maintain symmetry automatically—rotate the input data, the network output rotates exactly as the mathematics requires, without explicit training for that rotation.
