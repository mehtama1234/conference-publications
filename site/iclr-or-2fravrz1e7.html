InputDSA: Comparing Recurrent and Externally Driven Dynamics

InputDSA separates a system's intrinsic behavior from how external inputs drive it, letting you compare the core dynamics of two systems fairly even when they receive different inputs.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When two systems behave similarly, is it because they have the same internal machinery, or just because they are being poked the same way? InputDSA answers this by splitting a system into two parts: its intrinsic dynamics (how it naturally evolves) and its input response (how external signals push it around). This matters for any real-world system—brains, neural networks, robots—because they are always receiving inputs, and comparing only their outputs misses whether the two systems are truly built the same way.

Zero assumption version

Why comparing systems is tricky

When you watch two systems act—say, two brains solving the same task, or two neural networks learning the same game—they might look identical. But a naive person might just compare their visible outputs: if both systems produce the same answers, assume they work the same way. This breaks immediately in the real world. Two systems can produce identical outputs while being built completely differently, especially if one system is nudged by strong external inputs while the other barely receives any signals at all.

The old method, Dynamical Similarity Analysis (DSA), tried to fix this by looking at the internal dynamics—the state-transition machinery—rather than just the final outputs. But DSA made a critical assumption: it pretended the system had no external inputs. It only measured the intrinsic part. So if System A and System B were truly identical inside, but System A received constant external signals while System B received none, DSA would wrongly say they are different. Real-world systems are never autonomous; they are always receiving inputs from their environment.

In one sentence. InputDSA disentangles a system's internal machinery from the effect of external inputs, letting you measure how similar two systems are even when they are driven by completely different input signals.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Embed the data

Stack the observations (the states the system passes through over time) into a high-dimensional table, one time-step per row.

Step 2: Separate signal from noise

Use a matrix trick (singular value decomposition, a standard data compression tool) to filter out the measurement noise and keep only the true signal.

Step 3: Fit two separate machines

Estimate two operators: A (how the system naturally transitions from one state to the next) and B (how much each input can nudge that transition).

Step 4: Compare both pieces

Measure the angular distance (like a geometric angle) between the A operators of two systems, and separately between their B operators.

Step 5: Decode the answer

A small angle means the intrinsic parts are similar; a large angle means they are different. Do the same for input sensitivity.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper extends the old DSA method by adding a second operator (B, the input-response table) that the old method never estimated. Where DSA said 'I will measure only the intrinsic state-transition operator A', InputDSA says 'I will measure both A and B, from the same data, using a more careful decomposition algorithm.'

the object it moves  =  The state-transition operators: intrinsic (A) and input-driven (B)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: subspace angle between operators

measured quantity  =  Angular distance of state-transition machinery

When you have two systems, each with its own state-transition operator A (the internal machinery), how do you measure if they are the same? You cannot just subtract them (that makes no sense—they live in different numerical coordinate systems). Instead, InputDSA measures the angle between the subspaces they span. Think of it like this: each operator defines a direction in a high-dimensional space (the direction in which the system's state naturally flows). A small angle means both systems flow in nearly the same direction; a large angle means they flow in very different directions.

InputDSA estimates this angle by fitting a linear state-space model (using an algorithm called subspace identification, specifically N4SID or PO-MOESP) to the data. From the fitted A and B operators, it computes the subspace angle using standard linear algebra. This angle is the named hidden quantity: the subspace angle of the dynamical operators.

Why it works

How the paper makes the comparison work

The core insight is that you can split any dynamical system into two separate pieces: intrinsic (its natural tendency to evolve) and input-driven (how external signals push it). Mathematically, the system's state at the next time-step is a linear combination of two parts: first, the intrinsic part (the current state fed through operator A); second, the input-driven part (the current input fed through operator B). If you can estimate both A and B from noisy data, you can then compare two systems by measuring the angle between their A's and separately between their B's.

The trick is fitting A and B simultaneously. Old methods used Direct Numerical integration or assumed perfect knowledge of the inputs. InputDSA uses a control-theory algorithm called subspace identification (specifically, Dynamic Mode Decomposition with control, or DMDc) which works even when the true inputs are partially unknown or very noisy. The algorithm embeds the time-series data into a high-dimensional space, separates the signal from noise using singular value decomposition (a matrix compression tool), and then uses reduced-rank regression to fit the best A and B that explain the data. Once you have A and B, the subspace angle between two systems' A matrices tells you if their intrinsic dynamics are aligned; the angle between their B matrices tells you if they respond to inputs the same way.

The core equation and how it works

First in words:

state at next time-step = (intrinsic operator times current state) + (input-response operator times current input). The subspace angle is computed from the principal angles of the estimated operators.

Then the compact form:

x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t

Every symbol:

x_tthe state (values inside the system) at time t
x_{t+1}the state at the next time-step
Athe intrinsic-dynamics operator (how the system naturally evolves)
Bthe input-response operator (how external signals push it)
u_tthe external input signal at time t
A tiny worked example: one time-step
Suppose a system has state x_t = [2, 3] at time t, receives input u_t = [1], and we have fitted operators A = [[0.5, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8]] and B = [[0.3], [0.2]].
Intrinsic part: A x_t = [[0.5, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8]] × [2, 3] = [0.5×2 + 0.1×3, 0.2×2 + 0.8×3] = [1.3, 3.0].
Input-driven part: B u_t = [[0.3], [0.2]] × [1] = [0.3, 0.2].
Next state: x_{t+1} = [1.3, 3.0] + [0.3, 0.2] = [1.6, 3.2]. The system evolved naturally to [1.3, 3.0], then the input nudged it to [1.6, 3.2].

This linear equation captures the split: the system moves according to A, and inputs perturb that motion according to B. Fitting both from data is the core trick.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

High noise drowns the signal. If the measurement noise is much louder than the true signal, the subspace identification algorithm cannot reliably separate A and B. The fitted operators become garbage.
The system is too nonlinear. InputDSA fits a linear model (A and B are constant matrices). If the true system is highly nonlinear, the linear fit is a crude approximation and the comparison may be meaningless.
Inputs are partially unknown or misaligned in time. InputDSA can substitute surrogate inputs if the true inputs are missing, but if the timing is off (inputs are recorded at slightly different times in the two systems), the fitted B will be wrong.
The state is not fully observed. If you can only measure part of the system's state (not all of its internal variables), the fitted A and B may not represent the true dynamics. The algorithm can handle partial observability, but the quality degrades.
Done right: use plenty of clean data, align inputs in time, and fit the model only on data from the regime you care about (not a mix of different operating modes). These practices ensure the A and B operators truly represent the dynamics, and the subspace angle is trustworthy.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy example: create two identical dynamical systems (pick the same A and B for both), but drive them with completely different input signals. Use InputDSA to fit the A and B operators from the noisy trajectories you observe. Measure the subspace angle between the fitted A's (should be small, confirming they are identical) and between the fitted B's (might be larger due to noise, but the angle between the intrinsic parts should still reveal the truth).

Intrinsic-similarity error = angle between true A and fitted subspace angle. Input-similarity error = angle between true B operators and fitted angle. Both should shrink as noise decreases and data length increases.

The demo should make it impossible to miss: even when the two systems are driven by wildly different inputs and their raw outputs look completely different, InputDSA correctly identifies that their cores are identical.
