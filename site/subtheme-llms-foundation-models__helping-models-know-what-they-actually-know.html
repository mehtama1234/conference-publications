Helping models know what they actually know

These papers work on making models give honest confidence about their answers and admit uncertainty instead of guessing confidently when they do not actually know.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

When a model is wrong, it usually sounds completely confident. You ask it a question, it generates an answer with full certainty, and later you discover the answer was hallucinated—the model made something up. Papers in this area work on calibration and uncertainty: teaching models to express genuine confidence when they know something and genuine doubt when they do not. The core idea is that if a model can admit uncertainty, downstream systems can decide whether to use the answer, ask a human, or search for more information instead of blindly trusting wrong answers.

The challenge is that calibration is separate from accuracy—a model can be accurate but overconfident, or accurate but underconfident. Papers study multiple approaches: some train models with confidence scores so they learn to output a number between 0 and 1 reflecting genuine uncertainty, others use ensemble methods where multiple models vote and disagreement indicates uncertainty, and others study how to extract uncertainty from the model's internal states during generation. The tricky part is that some uncertainties are learned during training and hard to change later.

The payoff is systems that are more reliable and trustworthy because they tell you when to trust them. In high-stakes applications like medicine or finance, knowing when a model is uncertain is as important as knowing when it is right because a wrong answer from a confident model is dangerous. Uncertainty estimates also help with efficiency: if you know a model is uncertain, you can route the query to a human expert or use a different method, saving resources because you do not waste time on low-confidence answers. This area also helps researchers understand what models have actually learned versus what they are just pattern-matching by looking at which answers the model is sure about, which reveals what is real understanding versus lucky guessing because true knowledge should be consistent.

How the work breaks down

When models express confidence about their answers, the work here splits into five natural directions: extracting calibration signals from the model's internal states and focus patterns (attention — where the model concentrates on each input), teaching models to output explicit confidence scores during generation, using multiple runs or model disagreement to measure uncertainty, adapting calibration when models are deployed in new scenarios, and detecting when models are hallucinating or making things up.

Internal Signals for Uncertainty from Attention

A model's internal states during generation carry signals about what it is confident about and what it is uncertain about, but these signals are often buried and hard to extract. The challenge is finding the right pattern inside the model to predict genuine uncertainty without retraining or slowing down the speed at which the model generates answers.

CORAL uses a lightweight steering method that reads the model's internal activations — the numbers flowing through its layers — during the generation of each output (inference — the step where the model produces answers) to predict whether the current answer is correct and adjust the confidence prediction accordingly. It improves both accuracy and calibration (expected calibration error) on held-out benchmarks without needing to retrain the model, transferring the same gains to new datasets automatically because the internal signals it uses to steer the model are generalizable across different data distributions. Efficient Hallucination Detection focuses specifically on attention heads — the model's internal mechanisms for deciding which parts of the input to focus on — and shows that certain heads are naturally sensitive to when the model is hallucinating, meaning they pay attention differently when the model is making something up versus when it is grounded. The ACUTE Protocol takes a broader view by working with the full set of activations to operationalize calibration, utility, and trust, showing that the model's internal layer-by-layer responses are interpretable signals for confidence when properly decoded. All three agree: the model already knows when it is uncertain; you just need to read the right internal signals at the right time to expose that knowledge.

Consistency and Beam Search for Uncertainty

A simple way to estimate uncertainty is to run the model multiple times and see if it gives the same answer each time — high disagreement means the model is unsure. The challenge is doing this efficiently without blowing up the compute cost, and extracting the right measure of disagreement from the sampled outputs.

Don't Throw Away Your Beams uses beam search — a standard technique that generates many candidate outputs in parallel — to measure consistency without extra sampling, since beam search already runs many decoding paths simultaneously. It shows that consistency from beams is a stronger signal than consistency from random sampling (multinomial), and the variance is lower because beams explore promising paths systematically, making it cheaper to estimate uncertainty at test time by reusing computation that already happened. SelfReflect shows that models cannot directly tell you their own uncertainty through reasoning or finetuning alone, but if you sample multiple outputs and feed them back into the model's context (letting it see what it previously said), the model can then generate a faithful summary of where its beliefs diverge, which reveals genuine uncertainty when the model has conflicting outputs. Eliciting Numerical Predictive Distributions shows that the model's embeddings — its learned representations of concepts — already carry information about the numerical uncertainty in a prediction, so you can extract quantified confidence directly from the embedding space without sampling at all. All three show that sampling or running multiple decodes reveals what the model is uncertain about, and feeding that information back or reading embeddings can quantify the uncertainty.

Explicit Confidence Scores and Semantic Calibration

Some papers train models to output explicit confidence numbers alongside their answers, or study how well the models' naturally expressed confidence aligns with accuracy. The hard part is getting the model to output honest, well-calibrated confidence that reflects what it actually knows, not just what it learned to say during training.

Calibrating Verbalized Confidence uses self-generated distractors — alternative incorrect answers the model generates — and checks whether the model remains confident in its original answer or wavers when facing incompatible claims. This coherence-based approach produces less saturated confidence estimates (more granular, not just 'very confident' or 'not confident') that are more usable downstream. Trained on Tokens (tokens — small pieces of text that the model processes one at a time), Calibrated on Concepts shows that base models before instruction-tuning are already remarkably well-calibrated for semantic content in open-ended question-answering, but the instruction-tuning process and chain-of-thought reasoning systematically break that calibration, pulling answers toward high confidence even when wrong — revealing that calibration quality is partly destroyed by training, not just missing from the start. Estimating Semantic Alphabet Size uses information theory — measuring the diversity of outputs — to estimate semantic entropy (how much the model's outputs disagree) and flag incorrect answers better than simpler methods because disagreement among outputs reveals uncertainty the model experiences internally. Probability-Entropy Calibration combines both confidence probabilities and entropy measures as an elastic indicator for when to fine-tune: the method identifies which samples the model is uncertain about and flags them for targeted training, improving both in-distribution reasoning and out-of-distribution transfer. Together, these show that base models start well-calibrated and training can degrade calibration, and that explicit confidence measures work better when grounded in multiple signals (coherence, diversity, entropy) rather than single-number predictions because multiple signals capture different aspects of uncertainty.

Conformal Prediction for Guaranteed Confidence

Some applications need formal guarantees that the model's confidence levels are honest under any distribution, even after the model is deployed. Conformal prediction is a mathematical framework that wraps around any model and guarantees that if the model says it is confident, it is actually correct at a specified rate, with hard bounds on false-discovery rates.

CiteGuard applies conformal prediction to fact-checked generation: it ensures that when the model makes a claim supported by retrieved documents, at most 10% of accepted claims are actually false (or whatever target false-discovery rate you set), while retaining 86-92% of truly supported claims. Beyond Reactivity: Proactive Adaptive Conformal Inference handles distribution shifts — when the data the model sees during deployment is different from training — by proactively adjusting calibration before the error rate drifts, reducing deviation from target by up to 60% because it detects when predictions are starting to fail and corrects earlier, and recovering coverage 2.5x faster when data suddenly changes because adjustment happens continuously rather than waiting for large failures. Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction addresses the same problem on different benchmarks, showing that conformal methods remain efficient even when data distribution shifts substantially. SCOPE: Selective Conformal Optimized Pairwise LLM Judging uses conformal inference to pick which comparisons the model should judge (vs. abstaining), maintaining false-discovery rate guarantees at 0.097-0.099 while accepting 2.4x more judgments than naive approaches under the same risk constraint. All four show that mathematical guarantees are achievable: conformal prediction transforms any confidence estimate into formal error-rate bounds, trading some coverage to get hard guarantees that the model is honest about uncertainty.

Detecting Hallucination and Monitoring Real-World Deployment

Even with good calibration on clean benchmarks, models deployed in the real world encounter new scenarios and may hallucinate — generating confident false information. Detecting hallucination and monitoring when models are degrading is critical for safe deployment.

Cognitive Fatigue formalizes a pattern: autoregressive models (which generate one token — a piece of text — at a time) slow down and repeat themselves as they generate longer sequences, and this degradation is predictable with a lightweight Fatigue Index that reaches an AUC (area under the curve) of 0.95 for flagging tasks where the model will fail. Innovation provides theoretical grounding by characterizing hallucination through incompleteness in training data — when the model never saw the combination of concepts it is now generating, it hallucinates. Beyond Raw Detection Scores: Markov-Informed Calibration detects machine-generated text (a form of hallucination) more accurately by using Markov chains to model the sequential patterns in text, recognizing that models generate different patterns than humans. Why Keep Your Doubts to Yourself (Agora) reduces the cost of uncertainty estimation in multimodal systems by letting models communicate doubts to each other and routing uncertain queries differently, achieving both higher accuracy (8.5% better on MMMU) and lower computational cost (3x savings). Beyond Accuracy: Are Time Series Foundation Models Well-Calibrated studies whether specialized models for time-series data are well-calibrated, showing that foundation models for this domain do maintain calibration better than baselines. Generalized Correctness Models learns to predict whether the model will be correct by encoding its prediction history, transferring learned confidence patterns across different models and datasets because correctness prediction itself is a learnable skill. Together, these six show that hallucination detection and quality monitoring in production require multiple signals: fatigue patterns, text statistics, multimodal uncertainty, and historical performance data.
