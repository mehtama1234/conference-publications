Quantum Algorithms for Triangle Cut Sparsification

The paper uses faster quantum triangle-listing routines to build much smaller graphs that still preserve triangle counts across every cut, which is useful when the graph's higher-order structure matters more than individual edges alone.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Many graph algorithms focus on edges crossing a cut: how many direct links connect one side of the graph to the other. But for clustering, community structure, and local cohesion, triangles often carry more signal than edges. A triangle cut asks how many triangles are split by a partition, so preserving triangle counts across cuts preserves more group structure. The challenge is scale. Counting or listing triangles is already expensive in large graphs, and building a sparse graph that preserves triangle-cut values everywhere is harder still. This paper attacks that bottleneck with quantum algorithms. It first changes triangle listing by using a heavy-light decomposition of vertices together with quantum walks and Grover search, then uses that routine to construct triangle cut sparsifiers of near-optimal size.

Zero assumption version

Why edge sparsifiers are not enough

A standard cut sparsifier keeps far fewer edges while preserving, for every vertex partition, approximately how many edges cross the cut. That is useful when the downstream task depends mostly on edge connectivity. But many graph tasks care about third-order structure: whether three vertices form a closed triple. Triangles encode local transitivity and cluster density, which are central in community detection and related measures.

If you sparsify only for edges, triangle information can be badly distorted. A graph may preserve edge cuts well while destroying the number of triangles that straddle different partitions. So the right object is a triangle cut sparsifier: a smaller graph that preserves triangle counts across all cuts up to a multiplicative error. The obstacle is algorithmic cost, because even finding the relevant triangles fast enough is difficult on large graphs.

The main move. Use a quantum routine for finding triangles, then sample and reweight graph structure so the reduced graph preserves triangle-cut values instead of only edge-cut values.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Target triangle cuts instead of edge cuts

Define preservation in terms of triangle counts crossing all partitions, because the goal is to keep higher-order graph structure.

Partition vertices by degree

Separate heavy and light vertices, because different degree regimes support different triangle-search strategies.

Run quantum triangle listing

Use quantum walks and Grover search to find triangles with fewer search steps because those tools reduce repeated checking inside the heavy-light subproblems.

Sample structure for sparsification

Keep only enough weighted edges to preserve triangle-cut quantities within a chosen error tolerance.

Return a near-optimal small graph

Construct a sparsifier of size about n over epsilon squared, which matches the known lower-bound scale up to logarithmic factors.

The paper changes the primitive that drives the sparsifier construction. Instead of relying on classical triangle processing, it uses a new quantum triangle-listing routine as the core computational engine.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  triangle contribution across cuts

The important hidden quantity is how much each local graph region contributes to the triangle count of many possible cuts. A single edge can participate in many triangles, and each triangle can influence multiple partition statistics. The sparsifier has to preserve that aggregate contribution, not just the raw edge count.

This is why triangle listing matters so much. To know which edges can be removed or downsampled safely, the algorithm needs information about where triangles live and how densely they concentrate around different vertex-degree patterns. The heavy-light partition exposes that structure so the quantum search procedure can focus effort where triangles are most costly to find.

Why it works

Why faster triangle listing leads to better sparsification

Triangle cut sparsification is only practical if the algorithm can access triangle structure without scanning the graph too expensively. A naive routine would spend most of its time checking pairs of neighbors and repeatedly rediscovering the same local motifs. The paper changes that step by separating vertices according to degree. High-degree vertices are limited in count but costly individually because each touches many neighbor pairs, while low-degree vertices are numerous but local inspection stays smaller because each touches fewer neighbor pairs. This split lets the algorithm route different triangle configurations to the search strategy that matches their degree pattern.

Quantum walks and Grover search reduce the effective search cost for locating triangles in those subproblems. The precise runtime depends on the number of vertices, edges, and triangles, but the key point is that triangle listing becomes faster over broad internal setting ranges than with the best classical bounds. That speedup matters because the sparsifier construction builds directly on triangle information: once the algorithm can identify where the triangle mass lies, it can sample and weight edges so the reduced graph keeps the right triangle-cut statistics.

The final size guarantee is important. The sparsifier has about n over epsilon squared weighted edges up to logarithmic factors, and the paper also proves a matching lower-bound scale of omega n over epsilon squared for any epsilon-triangle cut sparsifier. So the contribution is not only the quantum search routine. It is also that the output size is essentially the smallest one could hope for, meaning the method is near-optimal both in representation size and in the scale of the computation driving it.

The preservation target

First in words:

For every cut, the number of triangles crossing that cut in the sparsifier should stay within a multiplicative factor of one plus or minus epsilon of the original graph's triangle count for that cut.

Then the compact form:

(1 - epsilon) * T_G(S) <= T_H(S) <= (1 + epsilon) * T_G(S)

Every symbol:

Sone choice of vertex subset defining a cut
T_G(S)number of triangles in the original graph G associated with cut S
T_H(S)corresponding triangle-cut count in the sparsifier H
epsilonallowed relative preservation error
A tiny worked example
Suppose one cut in the original graph has 100 relevant triangles.
Set epsilon = 0.1, so the sparsifier is allowed a 10 percent relative error.
Then the sparsifier should report a triangle-cut count between 90 and 110 for that cut.
If another cut has 40 triangles, the allowed range is 36 to 44.
The guarantee must hold for every cut at once, not only on average over a few sampled partitions.
That is why the sampling step must respect global triangle structure rather than local edge density alone.

This is the central contract of a triangle cut sparsifier: the reduced graph is useful only if all cuts keep approximately the right triangle counts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The model of access to the graph matters. Quantum speedups depend on the assumed query access and data-loading model. If graph access is slow or poorly structured, the theoretical runtime gain may shrink in practice.
Very sparse triangle structure reduces the benefit. If the graph contains few triangles or the downstream task barely depends on them, the extra machinery for triangle preservation may not justify itself compared with edge-based simplifications.
Approximation tolerance still matters. A small epsilon gives tighter preservation because the allowed error band is narrower, but it forces a larger sparsifier and more work. A large epsilon gives a smaller graph but may distort the higher-order structure that clustering methods need.
Done right. The method is strongest when triangle structure is central to the application, the graph is large enough that triangle processing dominates cost, and the quantum access model assumed by the algorithm is realistic for the deployment setting.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a moderate-size graph benchmark with known community structure and substantial triangle density. Implement a classical triangle-cut approximation routine and a simplified oracle version of the paper's pipeline that uses a lower-search triangle-listing backend to drive sampling. Then construct sparsifiers at several epsilon values.

The demo should evaluate two things: how the reduced graphs compare in size, and how well they preserve triangle-cut values over many sampled partitions. It should also run a downstream clustering score that depends on triangle structure to show that edge-only preservation is not enough.

triangle_cut_error = max over sampled cuts of |T_H(S) - T_G(S)| / T_G(S)

The decisive result is that the smaller graph remains faithful to higher-order structure, not only to raw edge connectivity.
