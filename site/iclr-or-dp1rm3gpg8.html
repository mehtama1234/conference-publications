Fast Protein Interaction Retrieval via Residue Factorization

RaftPPI replaces slow pairwise protein comparison with a two-stage retrieval trick: embed each protein once into a compact summary, then use fast similarity search instead of checking every pair.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Finding which proteins interact with each other in a cell is slow. Current methods look at every pair of proteins and compute detailed residue-by-residue similarity scores, but checking all pairs in a proteome is impossibly expensive. RaftPPI solves this by computing a one-time summary for each protein (a compact list of numbers encoding the protein's shape), then using fast similarity search to find candidate interaction partners — reducing the search from months to minutes while keeping the accuracy high.

The scale problem in protein matching

Proteins interact by touching each other at specific points on their surfaces. To predict if two proteins will interact, a detailed method must compare their shapes residue-by-residue (a residue is one building block in the protein chain, like a bead on a string). This is accurate but slow.

The naive approach: take all N proteins in a proteome, compute residue-level similarity scores for every possible pair, and rank them. That is N squared pairs, each pair needing L squared comparisons (L is the protein length). For the human proteome (about 20,000 proteins of average length 400), that is 400 billion billion individual residue comparisons. On a GPU, this takes months. The problem is obvious: there are far too many pairs to check.

In one sentence. RaftPPI replaces exhaustive residue-level comparison of every protein pair with a one-time summary and fast retrieval.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Approximate residue interactions

Use a Gaussian kernel (a smooth hill-shaped similarity function) instead of raw residue-by-residue scores.

Speed up kernel computation

Gaussian kernels are slow to compute, so use random Fourier features: random probe numbers that approximate the kernel quickly.

Collapse to low-rank scoring

Decompose the weight matrix (which scores which residues interact) into a rank-r product: fewer numbers to compute.

Pool into one summary

Aggregate all residues in a protein into a single compact list of numbers, computed once per protein.

Fast nearest-neighbor retrieval

Use standard fast-search indices to find the top-k most similar summaries, replacing exhaustive pairwise scoring.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces exhaustive pairwise residue comparison with a two-stage pipeline: first, encode each protein once into a compact summary; second, use fast similarity search to find candidate pairs, then optionally refine with residue-level scoring.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: embedding quality

measured quantity  =  coverage (percentage of true interactions found)

The key hidden quantity is coverage: when you retrieve the top k percent of all possible pairs, how many of the true interacting pairs do you find? A low-rank embedding loses information, so coverage drops. The paper measures this by checking: of the true positive interactions in the benchmark, what percentage appear in the top 20% of retrieved pairs?

RaftPPI achieves high coverage through three mechanisms: (1) the Gaussian kernel approximation preserves distance structure better than a raw neural network because the Gaussian is mathematically smooth and continuous, so nearby pairs stay nearby, (2) the random Fourier features sample the kernel accurately enough to keep nearest-neighbor order mostly correct because random features that sum to smooth functions give good coverage of the space, and (3) the low-rank factorization is constrained to preserve the most informative interactions because the training procedure learns to weight factors by how well they predict true positives.

Why it works

Why it works: the speed-accuracy trade-off

Here is the causal chain. Residue-level PPI models are accurate because they capture fine details: which exact amino acids on protein A touch which amino acids on protein B. But comparing every residue pair across all protein pairs is impossibly expensive.

RaftPPI breaks this into two stages. First, it approximates the residue-level scoring with three tricks: a Gaussian kernel (which has nice mathematical properties that let you avoid explicit residue-by-residue loops), random Fourier features (which evaluate a Gaussian kernel quickly using random probe vectors instead of checking every residue), and low-rank factorization (decompose the weight matrix into a product of thin matrices). Together, these shrink the computation from checking all residues in every pair down to computing each protein's encoding once.

Second, RaftPPI pools all residues in each protein into a single compact list of numbers. This summary is a lossy compression: you lose fine residue-level information, but you gain the ability to use fast similarity search. Instead of checking all protein pairs, you build an index on the summaries, then retrieve the top k% candidates by looking up the most similar ones. The true positives, if similar enough, rank high in the retrieval order and are found quickly.

The trade-off is explicit: you accept some loss of coverage (you miss a few true interactions) in exchange for massive speed gains (months become minutes). The paper shows that the loss is small (75% coverage instead of 100%) and the speed gain is huge (6 minutes instead of 4.9 months), making the trade worthwhile.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

interaction score for two proteins is approximated by pooling a low-rank factorized Gaussian attention over their residues

Then the compact form:

score(prot_i, prot_j) ≈ sum_k lambda_k * (embed_i^k · embed_j^k)

Every symbol:

score(prot_i, prot_j)how likely proteins i and j interact, a single number
lambda_kimportance weight for the k-th factor, learned during training
embed_i^kthe k-th component of protein i's embedding, computed by pooling residues
embed_j^kthe k-th component of protein j's embedding
sum_ksum over all rank-r factors (typically rank = 10 to 50)
A tiny worked example
Suppose we use rank r = 3. Protein A's embedding is [4.2, -1.5, 3.0], protein B's is [3.8, 2.1, -2.5].
Importance weights are lambda = [2.0, 0.5, 1.5].
Factor 1: 2.0 * (4.2 * 3.8) = 2.0 * 15.96 = 31.92
Factor 2: 0.5 * (-1.5 * 2.1) = 0.5 * (-3.15) = -1.575
Factor 3: 1.5 * (3.0 * -2.5) = 1.5 * (-7.5) = -11.25
Total score: 31.92 - 1.575 - 11.25 = 19.095. A high score means they likely interact.

The embedding is computed once per protein; then you just take dot products with lambda weights to score any pair instantly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Embedding rank too low. If you shrink rank r too much to save time, the embedding loses too much information. Coverage drops below 50%, and you miss most true interactions in the top 20% retrieval.
Gaussian kernel approximation breaks down. Random Fourier features work well for smooth kernels but can fail if the residue similarities are not smooth or clustered in unexpected ways. The approximation error accumulates.
Retrieval index not accurate enough. Approximate nearest-neighbor search (e.g. FAISS) trades accuracy for speed. If the index skips too many true candidates, coverage suffers.
Overfitting in low-rank factorization. If the training set is small or biased, the low-rank factors may memorize the training proteins' interactions instead of learning a general pattern that works on new protein pairs.
Done right. Tune rank and Gaussian bandwidth to balance coverage and speed for your proteome size, validate on held-out protein pairs, and use an approximate search index tuned for your embedding dimensionality.

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small-scale version of RaftPPI: take 100 proteins from a PPI benchmark (e.g. a subset of D-SCRIPT), encode each to a rank-5 list of numbers using a pretrained low-rank Gaussian kernel model, index them with a fast similarity search, and retrieve the top 20% of candidate pairs. Count how many of the true interacting pairs you find (coverage). Time the retrieval step. Compare to a brute-force residue-level baseline on a smaller subset.

coverage = (count of true interactions found in top 20%) / (total true interactions)

The demo must show that retrieval is at least 10x faster than residue-level comparison (because you skip the expensive pairwise residue scoring and instead use a pre-computed index), and coverage is above 70% (because the Gaussian kernel approximation preserves enough distance information to rank true pairs high in retrieval order).
