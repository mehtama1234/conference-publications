The Value of Information in Human-AI Decision-making

A method to show a human exactly which information from an AI model will most help them make better decisions together.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

When a human and an AI system work together to make a decision (like a doctor reading an X-ray with an AI diagnosis tool), they are strongest when they use complementary information — each seeing something the other missed. But today's AI explanation tools show what the model thinks is important, not what the human actually needs to see. This paper introduces a framework and a new explanation method called ILIV-SHAP that identifies and highlights the specific pieces of AI information that will most help a human make better joint decisions, because humans have a limited focus window and will concentrate on whatever information appears most emphasized, so showing only the AI's unique insights directs their focus to the reasoning gap between them.

Zero assumption version

Why humans and AI assistants get stuck together

When a doctor uses an AI tool to help diagnose a disease, or when a person uses an AI detector to spot deepfakes, the AI is supposed to catch things the human missed, and the human is supposed to catch things the AI missed. In theory, they outperform each other alone, because each agent has a different set of sensory strengths and reasoning habits — the AI notices pixel-level patterns while the human notices large-scale shape and overall anatomy. In practice, this only works if the human knows which specific pieces of information the AI is using. Right now, AI explanation tools like SHAP show the human what inputs the model weighted most (a pixel that turned the vote from disease to no-disease), but they don't show the human what kind of reasoning gap they actually have.

A naive approach would be to show the human everything the model considers important. But that drowns the human in noise — much of what the AI finds important might be something the human could already see, or red herrings that don't help the human's own reasoning, because human attention is a bottleneck (a person can only focus on so much at once), so a long list forces the human to parse every item and guess which ones are new to them, wasting time and diluting focus. The human needs to see only the AI's information that is truly complementary: the information the AI is using that the human would miss on their own.

The method. ILIV-SHAP identifies which AI information is complementary to what humans can see, and highlights only that information to the human.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Map what each agent can see

Define which information a human can extract from the raw input (the image, the text) on their own.

Step 2: Find AI's reasoning path

Trace which input features the AI model uses to make its prediction, using SHAP (a scoring method).

Step 3: Subtract human overlap

Remove from the AI's reasoning any information the human could see. What's left is the AI's unique insight.

Step 4: Highlight complementary features

Show the human only those features — the ones the AI uses that the human would miss alone.

Step 5: Measure if the human makes better decisions

Test: does the human make fewer errors when they see this complementary information versus a generic explanation?

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies how an AI explanation is presented to a human. Instead of showing all the features the model thinks are important (as vanilla SHAP does), it filters the explanation to show only the features that represent information the human would not see on their own. It is a reweighting of the model's explanation, not a change to the model itself.

the object it moves  =  AI explanation presentation
The hidden quantity

Complementary information

measured quantity  =  Human-AI information gap

The complementary information is the set of facts, patterns, or features that the AI model uses in its reasoning but that a human observer would be unlikely to notice or extract from the raw input on their own. For example, in an X-ray, a human radiologist notices large masses and texture; the AI might also catch subtle pixel-by-pixel changes (shifts in brightness from one spot to the next) that hint at early disease before a visible lesion appears. That pixel change is complementary information — it is information the AI has that the human lacks.

The paper measures this by asking: given a human's ability to understand the raw input, and given the AI model's attention (via SHAP scores), which of the model's important features are outside the human's natural window? The difference is the complementary information. ILIV-SHAP scores each feature by how much it contributes to the AI's decision, weighted down if the feature is something a human could already see.

Why it works

How filtering explanations helps humans decide better

The core mechanism is simple: a human has limited attention. When you show a human a long list of reasons why the AI thinks the answer is X, most of those reasons might be things the human could already see. The human's brain skips over them, looking for new information. But if the explanation mixes the AI's unique insights with redundant information, the human's attention gets scattered, and they might miss the one new thing the AI saw.

By filtering the explanation to show only information that is complementary — that is, information outside the human's natural window — the method makes the human's attention land exactly where it should. The human sees fewer reasons, but each reason is something they would not have thought of on their own. This change in attention shifts the human's reasoning, because they now have access to the AI's blind spots. The result: human + AI together make better decisions, because the information each one brings is truly additive.

This is not about changing the AI model or the human's ability. It is about directing human attention to the exact information gap between them. The explanation becomes a translation layer: instead of showing the AI's full reasoning, it shows the gap the human needs to fill.

The equation: weighted explanations

First in words:

complementary_score_i = ai_importance_score_i * (1 - human_visibility_score_i). For each input feature, multiply the AI model's importance by how invisible that feature is to humans.

Then the compact form:

ILIV_i = SHAP_i * (1 - h_i)

Every symbol:

ILIV_ithe final explanation score for feature i that the human sees
SHAP_ihow important the AI model thinks feature i is, measured by SHAP
h_ihow visible feature i is to a human observer (0=human sees it clearly, 1=human would miss it)
1 - h_ihow invisible feature i is, equivalently, how complementary it is
A tiny worked example
Feature A (subtle pixel gradient): SHAP score 0.8 (AI thinks it is important), human visibility 0.9 (humans usually see it) -> ILIV = 0.8 * (1 - 0.9) = 0.08. Hidden in the explanation.
Feature B (texture pattern): SHAP score 0.7, human visibility 0.1 (humans rarely see it) -> ILIV = 0.7 * (1 - 0.1) = 0.63. Prominently shown.
Feature C (large mass outline): SHAP score 0.6, human visibility 0.95 (humans always see it) -> ILIV = 0.6 * (1 - 0.95) = 0.03. Nearly hidden.
Result: the explanation highlights Feature B (the AI's true blind-spot insight) and buries the obvious ones.

The equation simply downweights any feature that humans can already see, so the human's attention lands on the AI's unique contributions.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Human visibility scores are wrong. The method requires a definition of what a human can see in the input. If this definition misses something (like a human noticing very subtle texture), the method will mark that texture as human-seeable and hide it from the explanation, even though this human does not see it. The explanation becomes unhelpful.
The AI model is not actually using different information. If the AI is making decisions using mostly the same features a human sees, there is little complementary information to highlight. The filtered explanation becomes empty or trivial, and the human sees almost nothing new, so their decision does not improve, because without new facts to incorporate, the human's reasoning stays unchanged and the two agents end up saying the same thing.
The human ignores the complementary information. Even if the explanation shows the AI's unique insight, the human might distrust it, or misinterpret it, or weight it less than their own visual impression. The information must be not only highlighted but also explained in a way the human can act on.
Done right. The human's visibility is well-calibrated to the task (tested on a sample of humans performing the task), the AI is using genuinely different features, and the explanation is phrased in a way the human understands and trusts. Then the human-AI pair makes decisions that truly outperform either alone, because now the human knows exactly what information they were missing and can combine their own strengths (big-picture reasoning, intuition about context) with the AI's strengths (spotting subtle pixel-level details), and the two together cover more ground than either could alone.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should simulate a human-AI decision task (chest X-ray or deepfake detection). For each example, run three conditions: (1) human makes the decision alone, (2) human sees the full SHAP explanation, (3) human sees the ILIV-SHAP (complementary-only) explanation. For each, record the accuracy or error rate. The hypothesis is that the human + ILIV-SHAP error rate should be noticeably lower than human + vanilla SHAP.

improvement = (error_with_vanilla_shap - error_with_iliv_shap) / error_with_vanilla_shap * 100%

The demo must show that removing redundant information from the explanation actually helps the human focus on the AI's unique insights and make fewer mistakes, because attention is measurable (we can count which explanations the human reads and where they click) and outcome is measurable (we can count correct versus incorrect decisions), so a good demo proves causation by showing the correlation breaks down for generic explanations but holds strong for complementary ones.
