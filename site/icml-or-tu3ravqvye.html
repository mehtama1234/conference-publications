PhaseCoder: Understanding Spatial Audio with Any Microphone Setup

A neural model (one that learns by examining examples) teaches language models to reason about sound direction from any microphone arrangement by learning the raw phase differences between channels.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Today's language models trained on images and text are blind to spatial audio — where sounds come from. And sound-localization models are locked to specific microphone setups. PhaseCoder solves both by teaching a neural network to read raw multichannel audio and microphone positions, turning spatial sound patterns into tokens (numerical lists) a language model can reason over — regardless of which microphone arrangement is used.

Zero assumption version

Language models only listen, they don't locate

Today's multimodal language models take images, text, and sound, but they treat audio as a mono stream — a single track with no information about where the sound is coming from. A robot hearing a voice command cannot tell if the person is to the left or behind it. Meanwhile, sound-localization models exist, but they are built for fixed setups: a microphone array with one specific arrangement. Move those microphones or change how they are arranged (say, from a straight line to a circle), and the model breaks.

A naive attempt: train one localization model for each specific microphone arrangement (4-mic line, 8-mic circle, 16-mic grid). But this is wasteful — the physics of sound is the same whether you use two microphones or eight, whether they form a line or a circle. The real answer is not to memorize each particular arrangement but to understand the underlying sound patterns that work anywhere.

The problem in one sentence. Language models need to understand spatial audio from any microphone arrangement, not just mono sound and not locked to one fixed setup.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Most spatial audio models hardcode one specific microphone arrangement into their architecture — separate branches for arrays with 4 mics in a line, 8 mics in a circle, or 16 mics in a grid. PhaseCoder does the opposite: it takes microphone positions as explicit inputs to the neural network, so the same model works for any arrangement without retraining.

The hidden quantity

The phase difference — what the model measures

measured quantity  =  Phase offset between microphones

When sound travels through space, it hits different microphones at slightly different times. This time delay shows up as a phase shift (a rotation in the wave). If microphone A receives a wave at phase 0 degrees and microphone B receives it at 45 degrees, that 45-degree gap is the phase difference.

PhaseCoder measures phase differences between all pairs of microphones across many frequency bands. The pattern of these differences encodes the sound's direction, because the delays depend on where the sound comes from relative to the array. A sound coming from the right produces a specific phase pattern, while one from the left produces a different pattern. The neural network learns to recognize these patterns and map them to direction, using the microphone positions to calibrate what each pattern means.

Why it works

Why it works: the neural network learns the layout mapping

Sound travels in straight lines at a fixed speed. When it hits an array of microphones, each one receives the sound at a different time depending on its position relative to the source. A microphone closer to the sound gets hit first; one farther away gets hit later. This time difference creates a phase shift in each frequency band.

The essential insight is that phase patterns are not hardcoded into the model from the start — they emerge from understanding the microphone arrangement. If you move a microphone, the phase pattern changes in a predictable way. The neural network can learn this relationship: given the microphone coordinates and the observed phase pattern, infer the sound's direction. Because the network processes both raw audio and coordinate inputs, it learns to generalize: it sees many examples of different arrangements and their corresponding phase patterns, so it builds an internal model of how arrangement shapes phase. This lets the same weights work for an array the model has never seen before.

The spatial tokens output by PhaseCoder are not tied to any specific arrangement. They encode direction in a universal form that a language model can understand: tokens that mean the same thing whether they came from a 4-mic straight line or an 8-mic circle.

Math after the picture

The core equation

First in words:

For each microphone pair and frequency band, measure the phase difference. A neural network scores which phase patterns predict the sound direction most reliably, weighted by how much that pair and band contribute across all different arrangements seen during training.

Then the compact form:

direction_likelihood = sum_{pairs i,j} sum_{bands k} attention(phase_diff_ij_k, coords) * learned_weight_ij_k

Every symbol:

phase_diff_ij_kThe phase shift (in degrees or radians) between microphone i and j at frequency band k
coordsThe physical coordinates (x, y, z) of all microphones in the array
attention(...)A learned neural mechanism that weighs how relevant each phase-pair-band is given the microphone layout
learned_weight_ij_kScalar importance learned during training, saying how much this pair and band contributes to the final direction estimate
A tiny worked example
Suppose 2 microphones 10cm apart, sound from 60 degrees left, frequency band 4kHz.
Phase difference between mics at 4kHz: 45 degrees (because sound takes 0.29 milliseconds longer to reach the far mic).
Neural network (given coords = 0.1m apart) learns: phases in 4kHz band for 10cm mics are very informative for 60-degree left (weight 0.9).
Same 45-degree phase in a 16-mic array: network uses the coordinate inputs to re-weight it (perhaps 0.7 instead of 0.9) because more mics mean some redundancy.
Result: the direction estimate stays correct, and the final token encodes direction in a way that works for new arrangements.

The neural network learns to map phase patterns to direction by seeing how they vary across training examples with different microphone arrangements.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Microphone positions given incorrectly. If the coordinate input is wrong (e.g., you say the microphone is at 0 meters when it is really at 1 meter away), the neural network cannot correctly map phases to direction. It relies on the coordinates being roughly accurate.
Sound sources very close to the array. Phase-based localization assumes plane-wave arrival (sound coming from far away). If a speaker is centimeters from the array, the wave is spherical, and phase differences behave differently. The model may fail on very close-range audio.
Microphone failures or electrical noise. If one microphone produces garbled or silent output, it corrupts the phase differences for all pairs involving it. The model has no explicit error-correction for bad channels.
Trained on some arrangements, tested on very different ones. PhaseCoder generalizes best when it has seen a wide variety of array shapes, sizes, and spacing during training. If all training examples are linear arrays with 1-meter spacing and you test on a circular array with 0.2-meter spacing, the model may fail because it never learned that scale.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Record a voice speaking from multiple directions using a simple 4-microphone array (e.g., a small square, 10cm on a side). Run PhaseCoder on the raw audio and the coordinate positions. Verify that the spatial token correctly ranks the sound direction (e.g., 60 degrees left, 10 degrees up). Then, without retraining, rearrange the same 4 microphones into a line or circle, record the same speaker, and confirm the direction estimate is still accurate.
