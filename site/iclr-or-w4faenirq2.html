RedSage: A Cybersecurity Generalist LLM

Mixing real cybersecurity documents with agent-simulated expert workflows teaches LLMs to handle security work without exposing private data.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Large language models are everywhere now, but they rely on cloud servers that see your data. In cybersecurity, where secrets matter most, that is a dealbreaker. RedSage solves this by taking a general open-source model and teaching it cybersecurity from the ground up — both through real security documents and by simulating how experts actually work — so organizations can run a capable security assistant on their own servers without uploading anything.

Zero assumption version

The privacy trap of general LLMs

When you use ChatGPT or Claude through the cloud, the model answers your question, but a record of your question lives on someone else's servers. In cybersecurity, where a single leaked exploit or password matters, that is unacceptable. Organizations need an assistant that knows about zero-days and attack patterns — but they cannot afford to send that information to the cloud.

A beginner's first try: take an open-source model (trained on general internet text) and just use it for security questions. It fails because the model has never seen offensive techniques, security frameworks, or real exploit code. It gives generic answers or makes security mistakes. Open models can recite the name of a tool but do not understand how to deploy it.

The move: Feed a model real cybersecurity documents and simulated expert conversations to teach it domain knowledge before deployment.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Filter the web for security

A classifier trained on labeled security data picks out cybersecurity documents from a massive web corpus.

Continual pretraining

The base model learns patterns from those 11.8B tokens of real security text without changing the task it is solving.

Agents generate expert conversations

Simulated expert workflows create 266K question-and-answer pairs covering real security scenarios.

Supervised fine-tuning

The model learns to imitate those expert answers by predicting them from prompts. Alignment then ranks the expert responses higher than weaker ones, because when the model picks between two possible answers, good ranking means it prefers the one an expert would give.

Rigorous evaluation

A benchmark of 30K multiple-choice and 240 open-ended security questions measures both domain expertise and general reasoning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the training data itself — both what the model reads and what it learns to predict. Most prior work either pretrains on domain text (but skimps on conversation examples) or fine-tunes on conversations (but skips pretraining). RedSage does both: it starts by reading 11.8B tokens (a token is a single word or word fragment) of real cybersecurity documents, then learns from 266K agent-generated expert conversations. The combination is the point.

the object it moves  =  training data: domain pretraining plus agentic augmentation
The hidden quantity

Domain adaptation: the quantity the paper measures

measured quantity  =  domain-specific knowledge transfer

Domain adaptation is the degree to which a model has absorbed the practices, vocabulary, and reasoning patterns of a specific field. In cybersecurity, it means understanding offensive tactics, defensive frameworks, tool usage, and how to respond to threats. The paper measures this by asking: how much better does a cybersecurity-trained model answer security questions compared to a general model?

RedSage estimates domain adaptation through RedSage-Bench, a benchmark mixing multiple-choice questions (which test factual knowledge) and open-ended scenarios (which test reasoning). The paper also measures transfer: does domain pretraining improve performance on general tasks too? If it does, that means the domain knowledge is not brittle, because adding cybersecurity text actually strengthens the model's ability to reason overall — the two reinforce rather than conflict.

Why it works

Why both pretraining and agent-simulated conversations matter

Here is why this combination works: pretraining on real documents teaches the model which ideas belong together (exploit code, attack surface, remediation). But the model reads passively — it absorbs pattern matching, not reasoning. Agent-simulated expert conversations teach reasoning by example. An agent playing a security analyst does not just list facts; it walks through a scenario step by step: identify the vulnerability, check the threat intelligence, propose a fix. The model learns to imitate that structured, multi-turn thinking.

Together, they fill two gaps. Pretraining alone leaves the model confused about how to apply its knowledge in a realistic conversation. Agent augmentation alone leaves the model without a deep foundation of security concepts. Pretraining gives the model the building blocks; augmentation shows how experts assemble them. And critically, because both sources are domain-specific, the model learns to prioritize security patterns over generic internet text — when it later sees a new security question, it reasons as a security person, not a general internet-trained chatbot.

Math after the picture

The training objective

First in words:

total loss equals supervised fine-tuning loss plus a preference alignment loss, weighted by a balance parameter

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_SFT + alpha * L_DPO, where L_SFT = -sum_i log P(response_i | prompt_i) and L_DPO scores preferred responses higher than rejected ones

Every symbol:

L_SFTsupervised fine-tuning loss: how well the model predicts expert answers
L_DPOdirect preference optimization loss: penalty for ranking worse responses higher than better ones
alphabalance weight: how much preference alignment matters relative to supervised learning
P(response_i | prompt_i)probability the model assigns to the correct expert response given a security prompt
preferred vs rejectedin preference learning, pairs of responses from the same prompt, labeled by quality
A tiny worked example
Prompt: A Linux server is exposing SSH on port 22 to the internet. What is the risk?
Expert response (preferred): Attackers can brute-force credentials. Mitigate by restricting port 22 to trusted IPs via firewall rules.
Generic response (rejected): SSH is a protocol. Attackers might try to connect.
L_SFT for this prompt: model assigns 92% probability to expert answer, so loss = -log(0.92) = 0.083 (low, good).
L_DPO penalty: model ranked generic answer at 35% vs expert at 92%. DPO pushes the model to widen that gap, adding ~0.15 to loss.
Total loss for this example: 0.083 plus alpha times 0.15. If alpha is 0.5, total is 0.158. Gradient descent (the algorithm that adjusts weights to reduce loss) nudges the model to prefer expert reasoning next time.

The math combines two goals: memorizing correct cybersecurity answers, and learning to rank expert reasoning higher than shallow responses.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overspecialization. If pretraining is too narrow (only offensive techniques, no defense), the model memorizes attack trivia but cannot advise on mitigation. RedSage avoids this by mixing offensive tactics, defensive frameworks, tools, and general reasoning.
Training-test data leakage. If an expert conversation used in fine-tuning accidentally overlaps with a benchmark evaluation question, the model appears smarter than it really is. RedSage adds a decontamination step removing 2.96% of benchmark data to eliminate leakage.
Agent-simulated conversations that are unrealistic. If agents do not understand security well enough to generate plausible scenarios, fine-tuning on bad examples teaches the model bad practices. The paper mitigates this by having expert humans review multi-turn quality and enforce diversity and realism.
Done right. RedSage combines pretraining breadth (real documents covering frameworks, tools, exploits, defense) with agent augmentation quality (human-reviewed expert workflows) and decontamination (no training-test overlap). The model learns to reason as a security analyst without memorizing brittle answers.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show that RedSage understands real security work that a general model would bungle. Take a realistic cybersecurity scenario: an analyst has a server log with failed SSH attempts from multiple IPs, finds the service is exfiltrating data, and has 30 minutes to respond. A general model would give generic advice ('check firewall settings'); RedSage should ask clarifying questions, recommend isolation tactics, cite a relevant NIST framework, and suggest persistence detection steps.

domain reasoning quality = (number of actionable steps proposed) × (accuracy of tool recommendations) / (false security advice given)

The demo must show that RedSage does not just know security words — it reasons like an analyst, with concrete steps and awareness of constraints.
