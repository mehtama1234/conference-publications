OWL: Geometry-Aware Spatial Reasoning for Audio

An audio model learns to pinpoint where sounds come from by training on paired audio and room depth pictures, then reasons step-by-step about sound direction and distance.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Audio models today hear binaural sound (two ears) but lack a sense of where each sound is coming from in the room. This paper introduces OWL, which grounds audio in 3D space by training on paired binaural recordings and panoramic depth images, then chains those spatial clues together to answer questions like 'Which sound is left of the listener?' with both a location and an explanation for how it figured it out.

Zero assumption version

Audio models guess wildly about sound location

When you hear a cat meow, your brain instantly knows if it is to your left or right, how far away, and what angle. Audio models today (called ALLMs, audio large language models) have no real sense of space—they see binaural audio (stereo: left ear and right ear channels) but treat those signals as unstructured noise, discarding the spatial clues baked into the tiny timing and volume differences between ears.

A beginner might try: train a model to predict direction labels (left, right, up, down) from binaural audio, ignoring what the room looks like. This breaks in three ways. First, it only predicts coarse bins instead of fine angles ('o'clock-level' precise). Second, when multiple sounds play at once, without understanding the room's layout the model cannot separate them. Third, it answers in one step ('turn left now'), missing the chance to show its reasoning step-by-step ('sound is at 3 o'clock, distance 3.5 meters, so it is left of me').

The bottleneck. Audio models lack 3D spatial grounding, so they cannot pinpoint sound location or explain their reasoning.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: grounding audio in 3D room structure

The paper replaces an audio encoder trained on sound alone with SAGE, a new encoder trained jointly on audio AND 3D room layout. At training time, SAGE sees three inputs together: binaural audio, a panoramic depth map (showing what the room looks like in all directions), and the ground-truth room impulse response (the acoustic signature of that space). At inference (when you ask the model a question), it needs only the audio—the spatial knowledge is already baked into the learned weights.

the object it moves  =  the audio encoder supervision signal
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Room Impulse Response alignment to 3D layout

measured quantity  =  Room impulse response (RIR) alignment score

A room impulse response (or RIR) is the acoustic fingerprint of a space—if you snap your fingers, the RIR describes how sound bounces off walls and decays. Instead of measuring success purely by whether the model predicts direction correctly, SAGE adds an auxiliary task: predict the RIR from the audio and depth features. This forces the encoder to learn the relationship between the audio's acoustic patterns and what the room actually looks like in 3D.

The paper estimates this alignment by computing two losses during training. First, an L1 error: how far off is the predicted RIR from the true RIR? Second, an Energy Decay Curve (EDC) loss: does the predicted RIR's energy fade at the right rate? A smaller prediction error means the encoder has learned to map binaural cues to room structure more faithfully.

Why it works

Why it works: supervision from 3D layout teaches the model to hear space

Normally, training an audio encoder to predict direction forces it to extract only the minimal acoustic clues needed for that one label (left, right, up). The model ignores everything else—room echoes, decay patterns, subtle timing shifts—because they do not change the final label.

By adding the RIR prediction task during training, the paper forces the encoder to learn a richer internal representation. Every audio feature must now explain two things: (1) What direction is the sound? (2) How does the acoustic energy decay in this room? A feature that captures the reverb tail length helps both the direction task (because rooms shape how sound travels) and the RIR task (because reverb is the RIR). This joint learning pulls out spatial structure the single-task version would throw away.

Then, at test time, the model discards the RIR prediction head. But the encoder weights have been shaped by the layout supervision—they now contain an internal map of space. When the model sees a new query ('Which sound is left of the listener?'), it chains predictions together step-by-step (sound type, direction, distance, reasoning), leveraging that spatial backbone to answer with fine-grained ('3 o'clock, 3.5 meters') rather than coarse labels.

Math after the picture

The training loss balances perception and acoustic layout matching

First in words:

Total loss is a blend of detection accuracy, distance error, direction error, and RIR prediction error, where the RIR prediction includes both amplitude mismatch and energy-decay mismatch.

Then the compact form:

L = η1 * (Lcls + Ldis + Ldoa) + η2 * (||R - R_pred||_1 + λ * LEDC(R, R_pred))

Every symbol:

Lclscross-entropy loss for event classification accuracy
Ldismean absolute error in distance prediction
Ldoamean absolute error in direction-of-arrival angle
||R - R_pred||_1absolute error between true and predicted room impulse response
LEDC(R, R_pred)energy decay curve mismatch via Schroeder integration
η1, η2learnable weights balancing perception vs. geometry tasks
λstrength of energy-decay supervision relative to raw amplitude
A tiny worked example
Suppose a training sample has η1=1, η2=0.01, λ=0.1.
Perception loss (cls, dis, doa): 0.8. Layout loss (RIR + EDC): 5.0.
L = 1 * 0.8 + 0.01 * 5.0 = 0.8 + 0.05 = 0.85.
If we removed the RIR term, L would be only 0.8.
The layout supervision adds 5% to the total loss, gently steering the encoder to model space without drowning out the main task.

The formula shows that adding RIR prediction as a soft constraint (with small η2) guides the audio encoder to learn room layout without overwhelming the direction and distance tasks.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overfitting to synthetic room impulse responses. If the training data uses only simulated rooms, the model may not generalize to real recordings with complex acoustic artifacts. The paper trains on a mix of in-room and out-of-room scenarios to mitigate this.
Depth image misalignment at test time. SAGE is trained with panoramic depth images but discards them at inference. If the model relied too heavily on the depth signal, removing it could degrade performance. The paper uses projection layers to fuse audio and depth only during training, so audio features must learn to encode spatial layout independently.
Chain-of-thought explanations may be plausible but wrong. Multi-step reasoning can produce fluent wrong answers. The paper uses curriculum learning (starting with simple perceptual tasks, then adding reasoning) to build a foundation before introducing explanation generation.
Done right. By training on large paired datasets (1.1M QA pairs) and using staged curriculum learning, the model learns strong spatial features and trustworthy reasoning chains.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple test: (1) Record binaural audio of a person speaking at a known location in a room (e.g., 3 o'clock, 2 meters away). (2) Feed the audio to the model. (3) Ask: 'Where is the speaker and is they to the left or right of the listener?' (4) Check: (a) the predicted angle matches the true angle within a few degrees, (b) the predicted distance matches within a meter, and (c) the model outputs a step-by-step explanation ('Sound is speech at 3 o'clock, distance 2 meters, so speaker is to the right').

direction_error = abs(predicted_angle - true_angle); distance_error = abs(predicted_distance - true_distance); reasoning_correct = (step_1 matches true event) AND (step_2 matches true azimuth) AND (step_3 matches true distance)

The demo must show that without layout training, the model makes coarse errors (50° off, distance unknown) and gives no reasoning; with OWL, it pinpoints the source and explains its steps.
