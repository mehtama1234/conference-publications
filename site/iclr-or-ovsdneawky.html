FilMaster: Scene-Wide Camera Planning from Film References

To make AI-generated films feel cinematic, plan camera moves scene-by-scene using real film examples, not shot-by-shot.

Paper explainersAll themesGenerative Models & Diffusion

AI systems can generate video now, but the results look templated and emotionless. Each shot is created alone, so the camera language has no rhythm and shots don't talk to each other. FilMaster solves this by doing what film editors do: retrieve example scenes from real films, use those as a blueprint to re-plan all the shots in a scene together (so they make sense as a group), and then treat audio and pacing the way professional post-production does—simulating how an audience actually feels.

Zero assumption version

Why templated films feel lifeless

Video generation models can create clips, but only if you ask for one shot at a time. The typical system takes a script, generates each camera angle separately, then strings them together. The result: every angle is generic, every transition is flat, nothing breathes or builds. Real films have rhythm—shots are chosen to build tension, show relationships, guide your eye. An AI that generates independently has no way to do that.

A beginner might try: just generate more shots and pick the best ones. But picking from random shots is like editing a film by throwing darts. You need reference examples that show how real cinematographers solve this exact story problem. And you need to think about the whole scene at once, not one shot at a time.

The move. Retrieve entire scenes from real films that match your scene's setup and story, use those as blueprints to re-plan your camera language for all shots together, then tune the pacing and sound using simulated audience feedback.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Encode the scene context

Take the script description of a whole scene (all shots, all story beats, all locations).

Retrieve real film examples

Search a database of 440,000 annotated film clips; find the top shots that tell a similar story.

Plan camera language

An LLM reads the scene and the reference shots, then writes a detailed plan for each shot's camera angle, movement, and look.

Generate video and sound

Use the plan to generate video clips that follow the new cinematography, and create audio descriptions for dialogue and ambiance.

Simulate audience, adjust rhythm

Imagine how an audience would feel at each moment; adjust pacing and sound to match real post-production choices.

What the paper changes

The object being changed

The system does not change the video generation model itself. Instead, it changes the input to the video model: instead of a loose description of one shot, you give it a carefully planned camera-language description that was designed by an LLM reading real film examples. That description specifies shot type, camera angle, movement, and atmosphere for the entire scene, not just one clip.

the object it moves  =  Camera-language plan (generated by LLM + real film references)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: cinematic coherence

measured quantity  =  Coherence between shots in a scene

Real films succeed because each shot's camera language makes sense in context—the angle chosen tells you where to look, the movement builds emotion, the cut to the next shot flows naturally. The paper calls this coordinated camera language: shots are chosen so they work together, not in isolation. The coherence score measures how well the shots in a scene relate to one another and to the story.

FilMaster estimates this by retrieving scenes from real films: if you can find real examples where cinematographers solved a similar story problem the same way, that's a strong signal that your plan has high coherence. The reference retrieval acts as a coherence check—real films are proof that this combination of shots works for audiences.

Why it works

Why it works: the causal chain

Existing systems generate shots independently, so they miss the relationships between shots. FilMaster changes this by using scene-level queries instead of shot-level ones. When you ask for a scene, not a single shot, the retrieval system can find examples where cinematographers already solved a similar grouping problem. Those examples contain implicit rules about how angles, movements, and edits work together in a narrative.

The LLM then reads both the story and the reference examples, and writes a plan that respects both. This forces coherence because the LLM is optimizing for match to the references, not just describing one shot. The post-production module reinforces this by simulating audience response: scenes with poor pacing or mismatched sound are downweighted, so the system learns that shots only work together if the rhythm and audio support the visual story.

The mechanism is: examples from working films constrain the search space, the LLM plans all shots together using those constraints, and then audience simulation validates that the rhythm holds.

Math after the picture

The retrieval and re-planning equation

First in words:

Coherence score equals the average similarity between your scene's context vector and the reference scene vectors, weighted by how well the LLM's re-planned shots match the reference cinematography.

Then the compact form:

coherence = mean_ref( sim(scene_context, ref_context) * match(plan_shots, ref_shots) )

Every symbol:

scene_contextthe script and story beats of your scene, encoded as a vector
ref_contextthe scene description of a real film reference, encoded the same way
sim(·,·)cosine similarity, a measure of how much two vectors point in the same direction (0=opposite, 1=identical)
plan_shotsthe camera language descriptions generated by the LLM for your scene
ref_shotsthe camera descriptions annotated in the real film reference
match(·,·)how closely the LLM's plan matches the reference cinematography, scored by an LLM evaluator
A tiny worked example
Suppose you have a scene: a tense conversation between two characters in an office.
You search 440K films for similar scenes. You find 3 strong matches:
- Reference A (corporate thriller, close dialogue): sim = 0.89, match = 0.85 (good alignment).
- Reference B (romantic scene, close dialogue): sim = 0.72, match = 0.60 (weaker—wrong emotion).
- Reference C (interrogation scene, tension): sim = 0.91, match = 0.92 (very strong).
Coherence = mean(0.89 * 0.85, 0.72 * 0.60, 0.91 * 0.92) = mean(0.757, 0.432, 0.837) = 0.675.
A coherence of 0.675 is solid—your re-planned shots match professional examples for this story beat.

High coherence means your LLM-generated camera plan matches how real cinematographers would shoot a similar scene.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Bad reference retrieval. If your scene is unusual—for instance, a love scene in a spaceship—the retrieval system may find only weak matches, because such scenes are rare in film databases. References may miss the specific emotional tone you need, and the LLM has poor examples to learn from. Coherence drops.
LLM hallucination in camera planning. The LLM must read references and write a detailed camera plan. If it misreads the references or invents unrealistic camera moves, the plan becomes incoherent. The simulated audience feedback helps catch this because an incoherent plan will lead to awkward pacing or mismatched sound, which the simulated audience scores as low engagement. However, a plan that is subtly wrong but still feels natural to audiences may pass undetected.
Mismatch between plan and generated video. The video generation model may not follow the detailed camera-language plan exactly. If the generated video ignores the plan, the coherence is lost. The system relies on video models respecting the input description, which is not always reliable.
Done right: diverse and expressive results. When references are strong, the LLM writes coherent plans, and the video model follows the plan, the system produces films that feel cinematic—camera language is expressive, shots flow naturally, and pacing builds emotional weight.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small film scene: a 3-shot dialogue between two characters (wide shot of room, close-up of speaker A, close-up of speaker B). Generate it twice—once with the baseline (shot-by-shot) and once with FilMaster (scene-aware retrieval + LLM planning). The demo should show that FilMaster's version has smoother editing, better pacing, and camera angles that feel intentional, not random. You can measure this by asking people which version feels more cinematic, or by scoring technical coherence (do the camera angles match real films in similar scenes?).

cinematic_quality = (reference_match_score + audience_engagement_score + shot_coherence_score) / 3

The demo must make it impossible to miss: the FilMaster version looks and feels like a real film, while the baseline looks like a robot generated it.
