Practical and Scalable Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Without the Metropolis Test

This paper makes unadjusted Hamiltonian and Langevin samplers usable in practice by tuning the integration step from a user-chosen bias budget instead of relying on a Metropolis accept-reject correction.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Many Bayesian models require drawing samples from a probability distribution (a rule that assigns probability across possible settings of the unknown quantities) in thousands or millions of dimensions. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo do this well because they use position, momentum, and local slope information to move far without random-walk behavior. The usual safety device is a Metropolis test, which rejects proposals that changed the target density too much during numerical integration. That removes long-run bias, but it also wastes work because each rejected proposal still required a full simulation. This paper asks a different question: if we are willing to tolerate a small controlled long-run bias, can we pick the integration step automatically so the total estimation error is lower at a fixed compute budget? Their answer is yes. They show that the change in energy during numerical integration tracks the bias introduced by skipping the Metropolis test, then use that relation to choose the largest step that stays inside a user-given bias tolerance.

Zero assumption version

The real tradeoff is not bias versus no bias, but bias versus wasted compute

Sampling methods for smooth high-dimensional targets simulate continuous-time dynamics, then replace the true dynamics with a finite-step numerical integrator. If the step is not tiny, the simulated path no longer preserves the exact target distribution, so averages computed from the chain drift away from the truth. Standard Hamiltonian Monte Carlo fixes that drift with a Metropolis-Hastings step, which accepts or rejects the whole proposal so the chain keeps the exact stationary distribution.

That correction is not free. A rejected proposal means the sampler spent many slope evaluations to produce a state it will throw away. In large problems, especially when each slope evaluation touches a huge model or a large physical system, those rejections dominate wall-clock cost. Unadjusted samplers skip the accept-reject step and often move through the space faster, but practitioners do not know how large a step is safe. The paper fills that gap by turning a vague warning, namely that larger steps create more bias, into a tunable procedure with a user-facing bias tolerance.

The core move: Measure how much the numerical integrator changes the Hamiltonian energy, then choose the step size so that this energy error stays small enough to keep long-run estimation bias below a target level.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Choose a bias budget

The user specifies how much long-run estimator drift is acceptable relative to finite-sample noise.

Run short pilot trajectories

The sampler tries candidate step sizes and records the induced change in Hamiltonian energy after numerical integration.

Convert energy error into bias control

The method uses a derived relation between energy error and stationary-measure bias, so the measured energy change becomes a practical proxy for how far the unadjusted chain moved away from the exact target.

Pick the largest safe step

Among candidate steps, the algorithm selects the biggest one whose estimated bias stays below the requested tolerance, because that step gives the cheapest transitions while still respecting the error budget.

Sample without Metropolis correction

The final chain keeps every proposal and gains speed because it avoids expensive rejected trajectories.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper does not invent a new sampler from scratch. It changes the control logic around existing unadjusted Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and unadjusted underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo. Instead of fixing a step by hand or defaulting to a Metropolis-corrected chain, it estimates the amount of integration-induced bias from observed energy changes and uses that estimate to set the step automatically.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: stationary bias from integration error

measured quantity  =  How far the chain's long-run average moved away from the true target

The quantity that matters is not the one-step integration error by itself. What matters is the stationary bias, meaning the difference between averages taken under the unadjusted chain's own long-run distribution and averages under the exact target distribution. A step can look harmless on one trajectory but still distort the chain's long-run behavior if it systematically pushes states toward some regions and away from others.

The paper uses energy error as a measurable handle on this hidden distortion. In Hamiltonian methods, the exact flow preserves the Hamiltonian. A finite-step integrator changes that energy. If the average energy increase stays small, then the mismatch between the unadjusted chain and the target remains small as well. The paper proves this relation for Gaussian targets and then checks numerically that the same rule works on harder models.

Why it works

Why the energy signal is enough to tune the sampler

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo augments the state of the unknown quantities with momentum, then follows dynamics that trade potential energy for kinetic energy while preserving total Hamiltonian energy. In exact arithmetic, that conservation means the continuous path is aligned with the target density. A numerical integrator breaks exact conservation, so the proposal after several leapfrog-style steps is generated by a nearby but not identical flow. The resulting chain still moves in a structured way, but its stationary distribution shifts.

The useful fact is that this shift is not arbitrary. The same discretization errors that create stationary bias also appear as a mismatch in Hamiltonian energy. That makes energy change a diagnostic quantity that can be estimated cheaply during pilot runs. If a candidate step causes large positive energy drift, then the simulated dynamics are no longer close to the exact flow and the long-run chain is likely to be biased. If the drift stays within a tight band, then the chain is close enough that the residual bias is smaller than the finite-sample uncertainty the user was already going to accept.

This is why unadjusted sampling can beat Metropolis-corrected sampling at fixed compute. The corrected method spends compute preventing every residual bias term, even when that bias is already far below the noise from using a finite number of samples. The tuned unadjusted method stops paying for that correction once the remaining bias is negligible relative to sampling noise, so more compute goes toward exploring new states instead of rejecting old ones.

Worked example: choosing between three step sizes
Suppose the user says that long-run bias up to 0.01 is acceptable for the target expectation they care about.
Pilot with step 0.02: mean energy change is 0.0006, which maps below the bias budget.
Pilot with step 0.05: mean energy change is 0.0030, still below the budget.
Pilot with step 0.08: mean energy change is 0.0115, now above the budget.
The tuner keeps step 0.05 because it is the largest step that still satisfies the bias rule.
That step is faster than 0.02 because it covers more distance per slope evaluation, and it is safer than 0.08 because the inferred stationary distortion remains below tolerance.

The exact conversion from energy error to bias depends on the target class and the sampler, but the operational rule is simple: larger persistent energy drift implies a larger mismatch between the unadjusted chain and the desired target.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Tolerance chosen without reference to estimator noise. If the user asks for an unrealistically tiny bias budget, the tuned unadjusted method collapses back toward very small steps, and the compute advantage disappears because the method is forced to behave almost like an exact sampler.
Energy drift can miss task-specific observables. A single energy-based criterion controls overall stationary distortion, but some observables can be more sensitive than others. A practitioner still needs to check whether the chosen tolerance is small enough for the quantities they plan to estimate.
Theory is strongest for Gaussian targets. The rigorous bound is derived for Gaussian distributions. The paper reports numerical success beyond that case, but the same guarantee does not automatically transfer to every nonlinear target distribution that arises after Bayesian updating.
Done right. The method is a strong fit when slope evaluations are expensive and the user only needs Monte Carlo error small enough for downstream estimation, because then avoiding rejected trajectories creates a large compute gain.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Set up a Bayesian target distribution produced after combining a starting belief with observed data, where exact expectations are known or can be estimated very accurately, then compare three samplers under the same compute budget: Metropolis-corrected HMC, unadjusted HMC with a hand-picked step, and unadjusted HMC with the paper's tuning rule. For each method, measure wall-clock time, effective sample size, mean Hamiltonian energy drift during pilot runs, and final estimation error on several observables. The demo should show that the tuned unadjusted method reaches lower total error than the corrected baseline because it spends less compute on rejected trajectories while keeping its bias inside the target budget.
