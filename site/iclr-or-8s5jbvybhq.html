Remotely Detectable Robot Policy Watermarking

Embed a hidden spectral signal into robot motions so you can prove ownership by watching video alone.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robot policies are valuable intellectual property. When someone deploys your trained policy without permission, you have no way to prove you own it using only video footage or external sensors. This paper solves that by hiding a watermark signal inside the robot's actions that only shows up in the frequency-domain (like how a dog whistle is silent to humans but dogs hear it). The watermark is invisible to the user but detectable to the owner.

Zero assumption version

Why robots need watermarks

When machine learning trains a robot policy (the decision-making model that outputs actions), that policy becomes a competitive advantage: it took months and expensive trials to develop. If someone steals the policy and runs it on their own robot, you have no proof you created it. A naive attempt would be to mark the policy in its weights, but weights are private—an auditor watching the robot operate only sees the physical motions, not the numbers inside.

Worse, even if you could mark the weights, external observers see a distorted version of the policy: the robot's body filters actions, the environment adds noise, the camera records from an angle, sensors miss details. A watermark designed for clean signals inside the computer falls apart in the physical world.

The problem. Embed a watermark a robot policy that survives noisy real-world observation and can be detected from video alone.

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the policy's action outputs by injecting a small, deliberate noise pattern at specific frequencies. This noise is tiny enough that it does not change the policy's behavior noticeably, but it is large enough to survive all the filtering and distortion that happens in the real world.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Spectral Coherency

measured quantity  =  Spectral Coherency

Spectral Coherency is a number (between 0 and 1) that measures how strongly a specific frequency appears in an observed signal. It is like asking: if I play a pure tone at a particular pitch and listen carefully, how much of that pitch do I actually hear in the recording? A score of 1 means the frequency is clearly there; a score of 0 means it is absent.

The paper computes this by breaking the video into time windows, running a frequency analysis on each window (called Welch's method), and measuring the strength at the watermark's secret frequencies. When those frequencies were hidden in the policy by design, the Spectral Coherency will spike, proving provenance.

Plain reading. Spectral Coherency tells you how much of a specific frequency you see in a video of a robot. If you injected that frequency into the policy on purpose, a high Spectral Coherency proves the video is of your policy.
Paper reading. The paper formalizes Spectral Coherency using Welch's method (a standard signal-processing technique that divides a long signal into segments, computes the frequency content of each, and averages the results). For a Linear Time-Invariant system (the robot body behaves as a stable filter: it delays signals uniformly without distorting frequencies), the coherency magnitude equals 1 if the input frequency matches perfectly.
Why it works

Why frequency-domain detection survives the real world

The naive approach is to inject a pattern directly into the policy actions and look for it in video—but physical distortions destroy timing. The robot's body introduces delay, the camera frame-rate smears the signal, and noise garbles it. Time-domain matching fails.

Frequency-domain analysis avoids this because it looks for repeated cycles at a specific pitch, not exact timing. A delayed, noisy, stretched copy of a pure tone still has that pitch buried inside. The watermark lives in the frequency content of the signal, not its precise timing, so it survives the Physical Observation Gap—the distortion between the clean policy output and what a camera sees.

This is why it works: if you know the secret frequencies you embedded (a list only the owner knows), and you can recover the robot's motion from video, then you can search for those frequencies in the frequency spectrum. A spike at exactly your secret frequencies, where random noise would be flat, proves you own the policy.

The equation behind watermark detection

First in words:

Detection score is the maximum spectral coherency magnitude at the secret frequency band, computed by searching over all hypothesized policy speeds and using Welch's method to estimate the spectrum.

Then the compact form:

detection_score = max_over_f_in_secret_band( avg_over_segments( |cross_spectral_density(motion, watermark_at_f)| ) )

Every symbol:

motionthe extracted time-series of robot motion from video frames
watermark_at_fthe known colored-noise pattern regenerated at frequency f using the secret key
cross_spectral_densitythe magnitude of the frequency overlap between motion and watermark pattern
avg_over_segmentsWelch method averaging: divide into overlapping windows, compute spectrum per window, average
max_over_f_in_secret_bandsearch across the secret frequency range for the strongest coherency peak
detection_scorea number from 0 to 1; closer to 1 means ownership is confirmed
A tiny worked example
Suppose you embedded a watermark at frequencies 5 Hz and 12 Hz (your secret key).
Auditor records robot video and extracts motion values over 10 seconds: [0.05, 0.03, 0.04, -0.02, ...].
Welch's method divides this into 4-second windows (8 windows total). For each window, compute the frequency spectrum.
Window 1 spectrum shows peaks at noise frequencies (0.2, 0.8, 3.4 Hz) with low magnitudes (~0.1 each).
Window 1 spectrum shows peaks at 5 Hz and 12 Hz with magnitudes 0.85 and 0.81.
Average across all windows: coherency at 5 Hz = 0.83, at 12 Hz = 0.79, at random frequencies = 0.12.
Detection score = max(0.83, 0.79) = 0.83. Threshold is 0.7, so ownership is confirmed (0.83 > 0.7).

The spectral approach survives distortion because the frequency content of a delayed, noisy, compressed signal still contains the original frequencies; timing errors wash out in the frequency domain.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Timing offset unknown. If the auditor does not know when the policy started running, the time-stretch hypothesis fails. Fix: use Generalized Cross-Correlation Phase Transform (GCC-PHAT) to align the observed signal even if start time is unknown.
Policy speed varies. If the robot slows down or speeds up during execution, the observed motion stretches or compresses unpredictably, because the timing between action steps changes. This distorts the time-to-frequency mapping: the watermark was at 5 Hz in the original policy but appears at 4 Hz in the slowed video. Fix: the detector tries multiple hypothesized policy speeds and picks the one with the strongest coherency, which automatically finds the true speed.
Watermark too strong. If you inject too much noise, the robot's policy degrades noticeably, or the extra motion becomes obvious to observers. The paper proves that small injections preserve the action distribution, but real deployments must tune the injection magnitude.
Done right. Use a secret key to generate colored noise at secret frequencies. Inject it into policy actions at an amplitude small enough to preserve performance. When an auditor records video, use the same secret key to regenerate the watermark, compare via frequency analysis, and detect Spectral Coherency peaks at the secret frequencies.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should train a robot policy (via reinforcement learning), embed a watermark at three secret frequencies (e.g., 8 Hz, 13 Hz, 20 Hz), run the policy on a simulated or real robot, record the video or motion-capture data, extract the motion sequence, compute the frequency spectrum using Welch's method, and confirm that Spectral Coherency peaks at exactly those secret frequencies while remaining near zero at other frequencies.

detection_accuracy = (number of correct peaks at secret frequencies + number of correct near-zero values at random frequencies) / total_frequencies_tested

The demo must show a clear spike in Spectral Coherency at the three secret frequencies and a flat baseline elsewhere, proving that the watermark survives the translation from clean policy output to noisy video.
