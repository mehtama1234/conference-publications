Temporally Detailed Hypergraph Neural ODE for Disease Progression Modeling

When tracking how a patient's disease unfolds over time, a chart showing which complications follow which lets a neural network learn what to predict next.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Doctors know that diseases unfold on predictable paths: diabetes leads to kidney damage, which then leads to heart problems, because damage to the small blood vessels in the kidneys follows the high blood sugar damage in the rest of the body. But patient records arrive at random times (a visit in January, then May, then September), and diseases don't wait—they progress in the gaps between visits. This paper weaves clinical knowledge into a network that learns disease paths, connecting each complication not just to the next one but to all the way stations a patient must cross, and tracking exactly when each stage was reached.

Zero assumption version

Disease paths hide in messy timing

Disease progression is like climbing stairs: diabetes, then kidney damage, then heart failure. Doctors know these sequences exist. But hospital visit records are scattered: a patient visits in January, then not again until May, then September. The disease is progressing the whole time, even when the patient is not in the hospital.

A beginner might try: feed all past visits into a model and ask it to predict the next complication. This breaks because the model does not know that certain progressions are medically impossible (you cannot skip steps), and it does not know how to handle the gaps in time. It sees January and May and September and has to guess what was happening in the hidden months.

The real problem. Hospital visits are irregular, but the disease evolves continuously in the gaps between them, and valid progression paths matter.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

1. Build trajectory hypergraph

Encode each known disease path as a set of nodes (markers) and record when each was first observed.

2. Assign attention weights

For each marker at the current visit, compute which other markers in the same trajectory matter most.

3. Weight inter-trajectory links

Learn which progression paths influence each other based on shared markers.

4. Evolve state continuously

Neural ODE solver pushes the patient's hidden health state forward through time, even in gaps.

5. Predict next complication

Decode the evolved state to forecast which markers will appear at the next recorded visit.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of just recording which complications a patient has at each visit, the paper stores the exact time each complication was first observed, and groups them by their known clinical trajectory. Then it lets the network learn: how important is each marker for predicting what comes next (attention weights), and how do different progression paths talk to each other (trajectory weights).

the object it moves  =  timestamps within disease pathways
The hidden quantity

Importance of markers at each stage

measured quantity  =  Time-aware marker importance

The hidden quantity is: for a patient currently showing marker X, which other markers in the same disease pathway matter most for predicting the next complication? This is not fixed. If a patient has diabetes but no kidney damage yet, kidney problems matter a lot; but if kidney damage is already present, the network shifts focus to heart-related markers. The network learns these shifts via cross-attention, which is a scoring system that compares the current marker to all other markers in the pathway and assigns each a weight.

The paper estimates this by building an attention mechanism that looks at observed markers (things that have already happened to the patient) and unobserved markers (complications that lie ahead), and gives each a relevance score based on timing and position.

Why it works

Why representing time inside the pathways works

The old way stored each disease stage as a fixed snapshot: diabetes at visit 1, kidney damage at visit 2. But that erases the actual timings. The patient with diabetes for 10 years before kidney damage appears is very different from one where kidney damage shows up in 6 months, because the longer the wait between complications, the slower the patient's disease is progressing—a critical pattern for forecasting what comes next. The new way records the calendar date each marker appeared, so the model learns: diabetes in 2020, kidney damage in 2023 means two years of invisible progression happened between them, because real disease does not pause between doctor visits.

Because the model knows these dates, the Neural ODE (a differential equation solver) can evolve the patient's hidden health state smoothly through the gaps. When a model learns that diabetes in 2020 and kidney damage in 2023 are three years apart, it learns that the disease takes time to progress—a key pattern that a model without dates would miss. At visit 1 in January, the state is updated by the markers seen then. Then the ODE solver runs the state forward through all of February, March, April, and May—months the doctor never saw—using the learned progression rules. The hypergraph tells the ODE: these markers come in order, these pairs influence each other, that one always follows this one. When the patient shows up in May, the state is already in the right ballpark, informed by clinical knowledge about what should have happened.

The attention mechanism makes this precise: it learns which markers in the pathway are most predictive given where the patient is now. A patient showing diabetes but no kidney damage gets attention focused on kidney-related markers. One with both gets attention shifted to heart and stroke markers. This is learned from data, not hard-coded, so it adapts to real patient differences (some develop kidney problems faster, some slower).

Math after the picture

The continuous-time evolution equation

First in words:

The rate of change of the patient's hidden state equals the negative of the Laplacian matrix (which encodes the graph structure and learned pathway weights) times the current state, plus a learned influence term from observed markers and risk factors.

Then the compact form:

d/dt S(t) = -L_tilde(t) * S(t) + h(x(t); Theta)

Every symbol:

S(t)the patient's hidden health state at time t (unknown between visits)
L_tilde(t)the learnable hypergraph Laplacian that knows disease pathways and marker importance
x(t)observed markers and patient risk factors at time t
h(x; Theta)a learned function that maps observations to state-change influence
d/dtrate of change in time
A tiny worked example
Suppose S(t) = [0.2, 0.5, 0.1] (patient state: low diabetes, medium kidney, low heart issues).
L_tilde(t) learned from data might be [[2, -1, 0], [-1, 2, -0.5], [0, -0.5, 1]] (encodes diabetes-kidney link, kidney-heart link).
h(x(t)) = [0.1, 0.2, 0.05] (observed new marker pushing kidney state up).
Then -L_tilde * S = -[[2, -1, 0], [-1, 2, -0.5], [0, -0.5, 1]] * [0.2, 0.5, 0.1] = [-0.3, -0.1, -0.15].
Adding h: d/dt S = [-0.3, -0.1, -0.15] + [0.1, 0.2, 0.05] = [-0.2, 0.1, -0.1].
Over one month, state evolves by roughly [-0.2, 0.1, -0.1], moving toward next complication.

The Laplacian is built from the hypergraph structure (which markers belong to which pathways) and learns which edges matter most. This drives smooth evolution through time gaps.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Pathways are incomplete or wrong. If the clinical knowledge used to build trajectories is outdated or misses a real progression path, the network is constrained by false rules. A patient following an unrecorded pathway will confuse the model.
Attention weights overfit to training patient patterns. The cross-attention learns patient-specific importance weights. If a rare patient type appears at test time, the learned weights may not adapt well, especially if the training set was small.
Neural ODE solver diverges in large time gaps. If a patient does not visit for two years, the ODE must evolve the state forward 730 steps. Numerical errors accumulate, and if the trajectory Laplacian is poorly learned, the state can drift far from reality.
Done right. The method works best when trajectories are based on solid clinical consensus, training data is large enough to learn stable attention patterns, and patient follow-up visits are frequent enough that the ODE does not have to extrapolate wildly.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy dataset: 100 synthetic patients following three known progression pathways (diabetes-kidney-heart, diabetes-stroke, hypertension-afib). Assign each complication a calendar date of first occurrence, spread visits at random times. Train TD-HNODE on 80 patients and test on 20 held-out patients. Measure: (1) do predictions respect the clinical trajectories (i.e., never predicts heart failure without kidney damage first)? This works because the hypergraph constrains predictions to follow the known rules. (2) Is prediction accuracy higher on patients with longer gaps between visits (where learning continuous evolution should help)? This should hold because the ODE has learned to extrapolate through gaps using the time-aware pathway structure. (3) Can attention weights explain which markers the model is watching at each stage (visualize the learned importance)?

accuracy_gap = P(correct next marker | gap > 6 months) - P(correct next marker | gap < 2 months)

If accuracy_gap is positive, the continuous-time model is actually learning something real about progression dynamics, not just memorizing visit patterns.
