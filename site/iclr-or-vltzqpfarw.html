STABLE: Shift-Tolerant Portfolio via Diffusion and Black-Litterman

When market regimes shift, use a generative model to imagine stock returns under the current regime, then let portfolio theory pick the safest mix.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Stock markets change personality: in good times, all boats rise together; in recessions, correlations flip and safe stocks become risky. Picking a portfolio that works in any regime is hard. STABLE does it by first using a generative model (diffusion) to imagine what each stock will return given the macroeconomic conditions right now, then feeds those imagined returns into Black-Litterman portfolio theory, which spreads your money across stocks to balance return and risk.

Zero assumption version

Why old portfolio theory breaks in a changing market

Pick a portfolio in January using the patterns from the past 10 years. Then a pandemic hits in March and everything changes: oil tanks, some stocks stay safe, others crumble. Your portfolio gets wiped out.

This happens because old portfolio theory (the standard way to pick stock mixes) assumes the world stays the same. It looks back at 10 years of returns and says, 'Here is how risky each stock is; here is how they move together.' Then it spreads your money to balance return and risk. But if market conditions flip, those old patterns are useless.

A beginner might try: just pick the stocks that have been safest for the longest. But that does not work—the safest past stocks are often the riskiest in the next regime, because correlations flip.

The problem in one sentence: Picking a safe portfolio is impossible if you do not know what regime the market is in right now.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Sense the regime

Feed macroeconomic data (unemployment, interest rates, oil prices) into the model.

Imagine stock futures

Generative model imagines what each stock's returns could be given that regime.

Harvest the imagined returns

Extract the mean return and covariance (how returns move together) from the imagined futures.

Feed to portfolio theory

Black-Litterman module weights stocks to maximize risk-adjusted return.

Deploy

Allocate money according to the weights; recompute daily as regime signals change.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: the market regime

measured quantity  =  Market regime (macro conditions)

A market regime is the personality of markets right now: booming and stable, or in recession and volatile, or stagflation-plagued. It is invisible—you cannot point to it like a stock price. But macroeconomic data (unemployment, interest rates, sentiment surveys) hint at which regime you are in.

STABLE uses the macroeconomic context as input and tunes the generative model to produce imagined stock returns that are consistent with that regime. The model learns to say: 'If unemployment is rising and the Fed is hiking, growth stocks will tank and bonds will shine.' The regime signal is embedded in the model's choices of what returns to imagine.

Why it works

Why regime-aware estimates fix the problem

Old portfolio theory uses one set of statistics (average return, volatility, correlations) computed from 10 years of history. It does not know that correlations are different in booms and busts. So it makes mistakes: it recommends stocks that were safe in the last regime but will blow up in the next.

STABLE's generative model learns to imagine stock returns as a function of the macro regime. When the model sees 'recession signals' in the input, it generates imagined futures where bond-like stocks do well and cyclical stocks tank. Those regime-specific imagined futures become the inputs to Black-Litterman portfolio theory.

Black-Litterman then spreads your money to maximize risk-adjusted return using the correct regime-specific estimates. Because the estimates now match reality (the regime the market is actually in), the portfolio adapts—it survives regime shifts instead of being blindsided.

Math after the picture

The Black-Litterman weighting formula

First in words:

Weight for stock i equals a base weight, adjusted up by a confidence-weighted view of that stock's return relative to the market average.

Then the compact form:

w_i = w_market_i + (1 / lambda) * Sigma^{-1} * (mu_view_i - mu_market_i) / sigma_view_i^2

Every symbol:

w_ifinal weight (fraction of portfolio) for stock i
w_market_ibaseline weight for stock i (e.g., equal-weighted)
mu_view_ipredicted return for stock i from generative model
mu_market_iexpected market average return
sigma_view_iuncertainty in that prediction (lower is more confident)
Sigmaa table showing how each pair of stocks moves together
lambdarisk aversion (higher = prefer safer stocks)
A tiny worked example
Suppose the market average return is 8%. Generative model predicts Stock A returns 12% (confident) and Stock B returns 9% (uncertain).
Black-Litterman sees Stock A's edge (12% vs 8%) is real and weights it higher.
Stock B's tiny edge (9% vs 8%) is uncertain, so it barely budges from the baseline.
If macro regime shifts and predictions change, the weights recompute daily: no portfolio is fixed for 10 years.

The formula says: start with a baseline portfolio, then tilt toward stocks the regime-aware model predicts will do well, adjusting confidence based on how certain that prediction is.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Macro data is lagged or wrong. If unemployment data comes out two weeks late or Fed signals are misread, the regime signal is stale. The model imagines returns for the wrong regime, and the portfolio is misaligned. Fix: use higher-frequency sentiment data or explicit regime-switching models.
Generative model has not seen that regime before. If a financial crisis is brand new (e.g., pandemic), the model was trained on historical data it never saw. Its imagined returns are plausible but not accurate. Fix: calibrate confidence; do not trust predictions in regimes outside training data.
Covariance structure breaks in extremes. In panic crashes, correlations all converge to 1 (everything falls together). The Black-Litterman module assumes covariances are stable, so diversification fails when needed most. Fix: use dynamic covariance models or limit the fraction of money put into any one position.
Done right: Regime signals are updated daily from real-time macro data, confidence intervals are wide for regimes outside training, and the portfolio is stress-tested on historical crises. Rebalancing is gradual to avoid whipsaw.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Take three years of daily market data from 2020 through 2023, covering boom, COVID crash, and recovery. Split into train and test. Train the generative model on year 1 and 2. On year 3, compare: (1) a naive portfolio built on year 1 data only, (2) STABLE re-estimating regime and re-weighting daily. Measure which one has higher Sharpe ratio and lower max drawdown over year 3.

Sharpe ratio = (mean daily return - risk-free rate) / daily volatility; max drawdown = (peak value - trough value) / peak value.

STABLE's portfolio should have lower drawdown in the COVID crash (because the regime signal switched) and similar or better Sharpe ratio overall.
