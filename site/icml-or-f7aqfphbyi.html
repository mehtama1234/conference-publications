Batch Normalization for Complex Domains

Batch normalization, the technique that stabilizes neural network training, is extended to work in curved mathematical spaces where standard distance rules break down.

Why standard batch normalization fails in curved spaces

Batch normalization is the workhorse of modern deep learning. During training, it rescales hidden activations (the intermediate numbers inside each layer) so that each layer's inputs sit in a nice predictable range. This prevents training from becoming chaotic because extreme values trigger extreme learning steps, overshooting or undershooting the target, and improves accuracy by keeping learning stable.

But batch normalization assumes flat space: a grid where distance works like the distance on a city street. Many real problems live in curved spaces instead. Network graphs, radar signals in the Siegel disk domain, rotation matrices—these spaces have their own rules for what distance and average mean. A beginner might try to just apply standard batch normalization anyway, but this assumes straight-line distances that do not exist on the curved surface. The model trains poorly because the normalization is mathematically wrong for the space it lives in.

The fix. Adapt batch normalization to curved spaces by computing means and distances along the surface's own shortest paths instead of straight lines.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

Standard batch normalization computes means and distances in flat space. This paper replaces that with curved-space operations: Fréchet means (the true center point on a curved surface, found by minimizing distances along shortest paths) and geodesic distances (shortest curves connecting points on the surface). The network's internals now use surface paths instead of straight-line approximations.

The hidden quantity

The center point on the curved surface

measured quantity  =  Fréchet mean on the curved surface

Batch normalization needs to know the center point of a batch. On a flat plane, the average of all points is the center. On a curved surface, you cannot just add coordinates—averaging coordinates gives a point off the surface. The Fréchet mean is the single point on the curved surface that minimizes total shortest-path distance to all batch members. It is the true center of the batch.

The paper computes this Fréchet mean by solving an optimization problem: find the point that makes the sum of squared shortest-path distances to all batch members as small as possible. Why it works

Why curved-space normalization stabilizes training

Training nudges weights to reduce error. The size of each nudge is set by a learning direction—which way to move and how far. In flat space, this direction is intuitive: move straight toward lower error. But in curved space, a straight-line move can exit the space or land on the wrong side. You must move along the surface's shortest paths (geodesics) to stay within the domain.

When you normalize using the correct surface paths, you tell the network: this is what the center of your batch looks like on your space, now rescale toward it. This keeps hidden activations in a predictable range even when the underlying space is curved. Because the operations follow the surface's shortest paths, learning steps are well-aimed and consistent; training does not destabilize. The network learns faster because each learning step moves confidently toward the goal, and reaches higher accuracy.

The normalization equation

First in words:

Find the point on the surface that minimizes the sum of squared shortest-path distances to all batch members, then rescale each member proportionally closer to that point along the shortest path.

Then the compact form:

m = argmin_{p on surface} sum_j d_shortest(x_j, p)^2; x_j_normalized = phi(m, x_j, t) where t is the rescaling factor

Every symbol:

mFréchet mean: the center point on the curved surface
x_jthe j-th point in the batch
d_shortest(a, b)shortest-path distance along the curved surface from a to b
phi(m, x_j, t)the point that sits t fraction of the way from m toward x_j along the shortest path
trescaling parameter controlling how close to normalize
Tiny worked example
Batch on Siegel disk: x1, x2, x3, x4 (four 2x2 complex matrices).
Shortest-path distance d(x1, x2) = 1.2, d(x1, x3) = 0.8, d(x1, x4) = 1.0 (using the disk's distance rules).
Try candidate center c1: distances sum to (1.2 + 0.8 + 1.0)^2 = 9.0. Try c2: distances sum to (0.9 + 0.7 + 0.9)^2 = 5.29. Fréchet mean is near c2.
Rescale x1 halfway toward Fréchet mean: x1_normalized sits at position t=0.5 on the shortest-path curve.
Each point moves closer to the center on the curved surface, staying on the surface and obeying its distance rules.

The key is solving for the Fréchet mean using shortest-path distances; then rescaling along shortest paths ensures all operations stay on the curved surface and follow its distance rules.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Fréchet mean is expensive to compute. Solving for the exact center point requires iterative optimization, especially on high-dimensional domains like Siegel spaces. If the batch is tiny, computation cost may overwhelm the benefit.
Shortest-path distance estimation is inexact. On some complex domains, computing true shortest-path distances requires solving differential equations. Numerical approximations can introduce drift, especially over many layers.
Not all curved domains have efficient Fréchet mean algorithms. The method works well on structured spaces like Siegel disks and Poincaré balls, but less-standard domains may lack a fast solver.
Done right: precompute distances using domain-specific methods. For radar and graph tasks, the domain structure is fixed. Pre-caching shortest-path distances and using fast Fréchet solvers keeps overhead low.
