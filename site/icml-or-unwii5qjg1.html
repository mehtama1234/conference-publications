Torque Variation Score: Diagnosing Motion Imitation Errors

A physics-based score that separates bad policy choices from intrinsically difficult motions by measuring how much torque variation a motion requires.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

When a humanoid robot fails to imitate a motion, is the policy not smart enough, or is the motion just hard to learn? A metric that only tracks imitation error conflates these two problems. This paper proposes Torque Variation Score, a measure of how much the forces at each joint must change to correct small motion errors, revealing which motions create reward spaces so flat that even good policies cannot find the learning signal.

Zero assumption version

The diagnosis problem: error is not the whole story

Humanoid robots learn to imitate human motions by training on pose-capture data and using reinforcement learning (a method that nudges the policy by making small rewarding actions slightly more likely). When a trained policy fails on a motion, the only number we get is imitation error: how far the robot's pose drifted from the capture. But that number hides two different failure modes: a policy that did not train well enough to handle an easy motion, or a policy that hit a fundamental barrier because the motion's physics creates learning traps.

A naive approach: measure only error. But an error of 50mm could mean the policy is broken, or could mean that particular motion requires impractically fast torque changes that no learner can chase. Without knowing which, teams cannot diagnose whether to retrain the policy or accept the motion as unimitable.

The method in one sentence. Measure how much the forces at each joint must change to fix a tiny misalignment, revealing which motions have steep torque demands that create reward spaces so flat the learning signal vanishes.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a motion clip.

Any 100-frame segment of humanoid motion capture data.

Compute the Jacobian.

For each pose, find how much each joint's torque must change if a limb slips by 1mm. This is the torque sensitivity.

Sum the torque ranges across joints and time.

Big changes mean the motion demands a wide torque space; small changes mean relaxed demands.

TVS is that total demand magnitude.

Higher TVS means steeper slopes in the learning surface, making policy gradient nudges (tiny weight adjustments following the steepest descent direction) harder to aim precisely.

Compare error to TVS.

High error with low TVS indicates policy failure. High error with high TVS indicates motion is physically hard to learn.

What the paper changes: a diagnostic lens

Before, teams looked at one number: imitation error. The paper adds a physics-based ranking of motions by their torque demands, making it possible to split the sources of high error into policy limitation versus motion difficulty.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: torque variation demand

measured quantity  =  torque variation: how much forces must swing

In rigid-body physics, every joint can apply a force (torque) in a range. When a humanoid's pose drifts by 1mm, the physics equations tell us exactly how much torque at each joint must change to bring it back. If a motion's poses are steep hills on the torque map, then even a tiny slip demands enormous torque swings. This torque swing demand is the hidden quantity.

The paper computes it by building the Jacobian: a table showing for each pose how much torque each joint needs per millimeter of correction. The paper then sums these sensitivities across all joints and all time-steps in the motion, weighting different components of variation equally. A high total means the motion is torque-demanding; a low total means it is torque-relaxed. This separates motions into easy and hard independently of any policy's skill.

Why it works

Why it works: flat reward surfaces trap learning

Reinforcement learning nudges policies by computing which actions shrunk the error most. It finds the direction of steepest descent in the reward surface and takes a step. If the surface is steep, each step points clearly downhill. If the surface is flat—nearly the same reward across many actions—the signal is drowned in noise and the policy spins its wheels.

High-TVS motions create flat surfaces because fixing a pose error requires perfectly balanced, high-magnitude torque adjustments across many joints at the right moments. Tiny changes in policy output produce zero change in imitation error (flat). Low-TVS motions have gentle slopes: changing policy output quickly moves the reward needle. So a high-TVS motion will defeat even a well-trained policy; a low-TVS motion failure reveals a policy that missed its learning target.

Math after the picture

The score combines three components

First in words:

TVS is the weighted sum of spectral diversity (how many torque directions matter), variance diversity (torque spread across joints), and segment diversity (torque swings over time), capturing the total effective dimensionality of torque variation demanded by the motion.

Then the compact form:

TVS = w_s * S_spectral + w_v * S_variance + w_t * sum_k S_segment_k

Every symbol:

TVSthe final torque variation score (higher = harder motion)
S_spectralhow many independent torque directions are active in the Jacobian
S_variancehow differently each joint's torque demand varies
S_segment_ktorque spread in the k-th time window
w_s, w_v, w_tequal weights combining the three diversity measures
A tiny worked example
Motion A (simple squat): only hip and knee joints swing torque 10-20 Nm. Spectral diversity = 2 (two active directions). Variance spread = low. Segment changes = small. TVS ≈ 5.
Motion B (acrobatic flip): all 6 leg joints swing torque 80-120 Nm, each varying wildly by time-step. Spectral diversity = 6. Variance spread = high. Segment swings = large. TVS ≈ 45.
A policy trained on a dataset of 100 motions mixed 50-50 with type A and B shows: 95% success on low-TVS type A, 30% success on high-TVS type B. Error is not random; it tracks TVS.

The three terms capture how many torque dimensions matter, how unevenly they load each joint, and how they change frame-to-frame, together revealing the true difficulty of the learning task.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Jaccobian is approximate. TVS uses linearization: it assumes a tiny pose slip. For very large errors, the linear approximation breaks and torque demand estimates drift away from reality. TVS works when comparing motions within similar pose neighborhoods.
Dataset capture noise. Motion capture data is noisy: sensor jitter, marker drift, and interpolation artifacts inflate apparent torque swings. Noisy mocap clips get artificially high TVS scores, conflating real difficulty with recording garbage. Filtering and re-interpolating the capture data removes these spurious spikes, revealing the true torque demands of the intended motion.
Humanoid-specific. TVS measures torque, which only makes sense for articulated bodies with defined joint limits and actuators. It does not directly apply to soft-body characters, hand grasping, or environments where contact dynamics dominate.
Done right: clean capture, low-noise data, focus on motions within learned policy's comfort zone. TVS shines when comparing imitation failures across a curated motion set where capture quality and policy training conditions are held constant.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a minimal test: (1) pick 20 short motion clips with TVS scores ranging from 5 to 50; (2) train a standard imitation policy (e.g., PHC+) on all 20 with identical hyperparameters; (3) measure final imitation error (MPJPE) on each clip; (4) plot TVS on the x-axis, error on the y-axis. Show that high-TVS motions cluster toward high error, and low-TVS motions cluster toward low error. The plot must show clear separation: the policy cannot overcome physics—torque variation demand predicts failure.

correlation = Pearson_r(TVS, imitation_error)

The demo must make this visible: two identical policies trained identically on different motions fail in a pattern that TVS predicts perfectly, proving the method splits policy limitation from motion hardness.
