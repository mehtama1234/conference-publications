Smoothing the Loss Landscape to Fine-tune Large Time Series Models

Before fine-tuning on a new task, blend a pre-trained time series model's weights with random weights to erase sharp, overfitting-prone features while keeping broad pre-trained knowledge, so the model adapts well without memorizing.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Large time series models are pre-trained on massive datasets and should adapt well to new tasks, like language models do. But when you directly fine-tune them on a new dataset, they often perform worse than training a fresh model from scratch. The reason: the error surface (think of each point as a particular set of weights, and each point's height as the error at that setting) is jagged with sharp, narrow valleys and pointy features, making learning get trapped and overfit. This paper proposes Smoothed Full Fine-tuning: before adapting to the new task, blend the pre-trained weights with the weights of a randomly initialized model. This smooths the surface, letting the model learn without getting stuck.

Zero assumption version

The pre-trained model refuses to adapt

Large time series models work well after pre-training on vast datasets. A naive engineer tries to fine-tune them on a new downstream task: take the weights, run a few training steps on the smaller new dataset, and hope it learns. But it often fails catastrophically—sometimes worse than just training a fresh model from the start.

The core culprit is the shape of the error surface. The error surface is defined by a point for each possible set of weights, with the height at that point being the error on the current task. The pre-trained model sits at some location on this surface, but the surface itself is hostile: it has sharp, narrow valleys, cliffs, and flat regions. When training nudges the weights even slightly to improve on the new task, it can fall into a sharp valley with no good escape route, or it overfits by memorizing the small dataset rather than learning real patterns. The surface's shape actively prevents good learning.

In one sentence. Before fine-tuning, blend the pre-trained model's weights with a randomly initialized model's weights to smooth out sharp, pointy features in the error surface.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Create auxiliary model

Initialize a new model with random weights (no pre-training).

Interpolate weights

Blend the pre-trained weights with the random weights using a blend parameter (call it alpha) between 0 and 1, so the final weight is mostly pre-trained but slightly nudged by randomness.

Smooth the error surface

The blend erases sharp valleys (randomness kills the pointy structure) while preserving flat, general regions (pre-training's broad knowledge survives).

Fine-tune the blend

Now train this smoothed model on the downstream dataset. The error surface is no longer pointy, so learning does not get trapped or overfit.

Retain pre-training

Because alpha is large (not 0.5), the smoothed model is still mostly pre-trained; it does not reset to random.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper modifies the model weights before fine-tuning. Instead of using the pre-trained weights directly, it replaces them with a weighted blend of pre-trained and random weights.

the object it moves  =  model weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: error surface sharpness

measured quantity  =  surface sharpness

The error surface has a shape. In some regions, it is a sharp, narrow valley: tiny changes to weights cause huge changes in error. In other regions, it is a broad, flat area: weights can move around without much consequence. Sharpness is the property of being narrow and pointy; flatness is the opposite. A sharp minimum is bad for learning because it locks the model into a specific solution; a flat minimum is good because it continues to work well even when weights shift a little, and it handles new data better.

The paper estimates surface sharpness indirectly: by checking how poorly pre-trained models fine-tune (overfitting signals sharp valleys), and by comparing the error at points along the line between pre-trained and random weights (if the error stays low and flat in between, the surface is locally smooth).

Why it works

Why it works: the smoothing principle

The pre-trained model has learned broad, useful patterns for time series tasks. But it has also learned dataset-specific quirks encoded as sharp, pointy features in the weight space. When you fine-tune on a new dataset, those sharp features cause overfitting: the model clings to the old quirks and does not adapt.

The randomly initialized model has no learned patterns, so its error surface is smooth and flat—there are no sharp valleys because there are no dataset-specific features to lock in. When you blend (mix) the two sets of weights, the sharp features get diluted. The random weights push the model slightly away from the sharp valleys, smoothing them out. At the same time, broad, flat regions survive the blending because they are already smooth. The result is a model that retains the general pre-trained knowledge but has lost the sharp, overfitting-prone structure.

Mathematically, the blending perturbs sharp minima (small random nudges push the model out of narrow valleys) but leaves flat regions largely intact (they continue to work well even with small perturbations). Once the model is in a flatter basin, fine-tuning can proceed without getting trapped or overfitting, and the model's learning steps can move freely to adapt to the new task.

Math after the picture

The blending equation

First in words:

The smoothed weights are a weighted average of pre-trained weights and random weights, controlled by a single blending parameter alpha. The parameter alpha is typically close to 1, so most of the final weight comes from pre-training.

Then the compact form:

w_smooth = alpha * w_pretrain + (1 - alpha) * w_random

Every symbol:

w_smooththe final smoothed weight
w_pretrainthe pre-trained weight value
w_randomthe random weight value
alphathe blending parameter, typically 0.8 to 0.95
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight in the pre-trained model is w_pretrain = 2.0 (learned value).
The same weight position in a random model is w_random = -0.5 (random initialization).
Using alpha = 0.9, the smoothed weight is: w_smooth = 0.9 × 2.0 + 0.1 × (-0.5) = 1.8 + (-0.05) = 1.75.
The smoothed weight is mostly pre-trained (1.75 vs 2.0) but slightly perturbed by randomness (-0.05), diluting any sharp features.
With ten thousand weights, this small perturbation smears out sharp valleys without erasing the learned patterns.

This simple blend perturbs the model just enough to escape sharp minima but stays close enough to pre-training to retain general knowledge.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing the blend ratio too equal (mostly random). If you blend equally with randomness, you lose most of the pre-trained knowledge. The model becomes close to random, so it does not benefit from pre-training anymore. The fine-tuning performance drops.
Choosing the blend ratio to favor pre-training too much (all pre-trained, no smoothing). If you use all pre-trained weights with no random blend, you do not add any smoothing, so you are back to direct fine-tuning. The error surface remains sharp, and overfitting returns.
Different random seeds give different results. The random initialization is not unique. Running the method twice with different random seeds produces slightly different smoothed models. The method is not fully deterministic, so results can vary.
Done right: blend ratio between 0.8 and 0.95 (mostly pre-trained). In practice, this range retains pre-trained knowledge while smoothing enough to prevent overfitting. The optimal ratio depends on the specific dataset and model size.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small time series forecasting task. Take a pre-trained time series model (e.g., a model with two layers trained on 100 timesteps of synthetic data), then fine-tune it on a smaller, different downstream task (e.g., 50 timesteps, a different pattern). Measure error on the downstream task under three conditions: (1) direct fine-tuning (no smoothing), (2) smoothed fine-tuning with the optimal blend ratio, and (3) training from scratch. The demo should show that smoothed fine-tuning (2) beats both direct fine-tuning (1) and training from scratch (3) in terms of final error and training stability.

downstream_error = mean_absolute_error(predictions, ground_truth); relative_improvement = (direct_error - smoothed_error) / direct_error * 100%

The key observation: smoothed fine-tuning avoids the overfitting collapse that plagues direct fine-tuning, and does so while keeping the model mostly pre-trained.
