VoG: Enhancing LLM Reasoning through Stepwise Verification on Knowledge Graphs

An LLM reasoning system checks its own thinking step-by-step against facts stored in a knowledge graph, correcting errors as it discovers them.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

Large language models often make up facts or contradict themselves when reasoning through knowledge-heavy questions, because they lack access to reliable external facts and no way to verify their own answers. VoG is a system that lets an LLM plan its reasoning first, then iteratively check each step against a knowledge graph (a database of real facts), and revise its plan whenever it finds a mistake. This creates a feedback loop: retrieve facts, verify the reasoning, spot errors, fix them, and move forward with a corrected plan.

Zero assumption version

LLMs make things up when facts matter

A language model generates text one word at a time based on statistical patterns. For routine tasks this works fine, but for tasks that depend on real facts—like answering 'who won the Nobel Prize in 2023?' or 'what proteins are involved in photosynthesis?'—the model often produces plausible-sounding but incorrect answers, either because it never learned the right fact or it learned conflicting facts and picked the wrong one. The model has no external reference to check against, and no mechanism to notice or correct its own mistakes mid-reasoning.

A naive fix would be to just prepend the relevant facts to every prompt. But which facts? The model still needs to understand which ones apply, combine them correctly, and detect when new steps contradict earlier ones. A static list of facts cannot adjust as the reasoning unfolds and reveals what the model actually needs.

The core issue. LLMs hallucinate because they reason blind, with no way to look up facts or spot their own contradictions as they work through the answer.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Generate a reasoning plan

The LLM writes out the full sequence of steps it thinks it needs, without pausing to look anything up yet.

Retrieve facts from the graph

VoG queries the knowledge graph for facts relevant to each step of the plan, using the plan itself as a guide for what to look up.

Verify each step

The system checks whether each step's claim is supported by the retrieved facts, or contradicted by them.

Measure confidence and consistency

VoG computes two scores for each step: how certain the evidence makes us about that step, and how well it aligns with the rest of the reasoning.

Revise the plan smartly

When verification finds errors, VoG uses a bandit strategy (picking the most valuable edits) to revise the plan, then loops back to retrieval.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Standard retrieval-augmented LLM systems (systems that look up facts while reasoning) either embed the facts into the initial prompt once, or retrieve at random points. VoG changes the retrieval and revision strategy: it makes the LLM commit to a full plan first, then iteratively retrieves facts to verify that plan, and treats revision as an optimization problem guided by measured rewards rather than a fixed routine.

the object it moves  =  retrieval strategy and revision loop
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: alignment score

measured quantity  =  plan-evidence alignment score

VoG must decide which parts of the reasoning plan are most broken and need fixing. It measures this with a hidden quantity: for each step, how well does the evidence retrieved from the knowledge graph actually support that step? This is alignment. High alignment means the facts agree; low alignment means contradiction or missing support.

The paper estimates alignment by combining two signals: uncertainty (how much confidence do we have in the facts we retrieved?) and semantic consistency (how well do the facts overlap in meaning with the step's claim?). These two signals feed into a multi-armed bandit, a decision-making technique that picks which step to revise next based on which revision would gain the most value.

Why it works

Why iterative verification and revision works

A single pass of retrieval and revision is not enough because reasoning is a chain: if step 2 is wrong, step 3 might be built on that mistake. VoG loops because fixing one step often requires different facts or logic for downstream steps. The loop converges because each revision increases alignment (it finds new facts that support the corrected step), and each iteration can retrieve new, more relevant facts guided by the updated plan.

VoG does not revise blindly. It measures alignment for every step, ranks them by how much they could improve with revision (using the bandit strategy), and focuses effort on the highest-impact changes, because steps with low alignment to the knowledge graph are the ones where new facts will have the most effect on correctness. This is more efficient than rewriting every weak step, and it avoids cascading errors where fixing one mistake accidentally breaks another, because the bandit strategy picks one step at a time and re-checks its alignment after each fix, so it will not pile multiple risky changes together. The bandit strategy balances exploration (trying revisions on uncertain steps where we might learn something new) with exploitation (going deep on steps we already know are broken).

Math after the picture

The alignment equation

First in words:

The alignment score for a step is the sum of the knowledge graph's support for that step (measured as certainty in the facts) times a weight showing how consistent those facts are with the step's meaning.

Then the compact form:

alignment_i = sum_j( confidence_j * semantic_overlap(fact_j, step_i) )

Every symbol:

alignment_ialignment score for step i (how much the knowledge graph backs it up)
confidence_jhow confident we are in fact j from the knowledge graph
semantic_overlap(fact_j, step_i)how much the meaning of fact j overlaps with step i (ranges from 0 to 1)
sum_jsum across all facts retrieved for this step
A tiny worked example
Step: 'Photosynthesis requires carbon dioxide.'
Retrieved facts from knowledge graph:
Fact 1: 'CO2 is essential for photosynthesis.' (confidence 0.95, semantic overlap 0.9)
Fact 2: 'Plants absorb gases during photosynthesis.' (confidence 0.80, semantic overlap 0.6)
Fact 3: 'Argon is inert.' (confidence 0.99, semantic overlap 0.1)
alignment = (0.95 * 0.9) + (0.80 * 0.6) + (0.99 * 0.1)
alignment = 0.855 + 0.48 + 0.099 = 1.434
A high score (>1.0 here) means strong backing. A low score would trigger revision.

The bandit strategy picks steps with low alignment to revise first, because those are where new facts from the knowledge graph can do the most good.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Knowledge graph is incomplete. If the knowledge graph lacks a fact the LLM needs, VoG cannot retrieve it, and the reasoning stays wrong. The system can only verify against what is stored.
Retrieval query is badly written. If the LLM's initial plan describes a concept poorly, the retrieval query derived from it will miss relevant facts. Garbage in, garbage out.
Circular errors on revisiting. If revision introduces a new error that looks plausible, the next loop might retrieve false facts that reinforce it, leading to a new hallucination.
Done right. VoG works best when the knowledge graph is reasonably complete for the domain, the LLM can articulate its reasoning clearly enough to guide retrieval, and revision focuses on the steps that matter most (which the bandit strategy ensures).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple multi-step reasoning task: for example, 'Describe the role of proteins in photosynthesis in three steps.' Create a small knowledge graph (even 20-30 facts is enough) about photosynthesis. Run an LLM once without VoG (just give it the question), measure how many statements are correct. Then run the same question through VoG: have it generate a plan, retrieve facts from your knowledge graph, measure alignment, revise the low-alignment steps, and loop until alignment plateaus. Compare the final answer to the naive one.

improvement = (correct_statements_with_VoG - correct_statements_without) / correct_statements_without * 100%

The demo must show that repeated retrieval + verification catches hallucinations the LLM would have made on the first try, and that the reasoning becomes internally consistent (later steps do not contradict earlier ones).
