When AI Agents Compete for Jobs: Strategic Capabilities and Economic Dynamics of AI Labour Markets

Build a simulated AI-only gig market and test which strategic skills help agents win work, keep a good record, and earn more.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

This paper studies what happens when software agents bid for jobs, do the work, and build reputations in a market with no human workers. The key point is that market success depends on more than raw task skill. Agents do better when they judge their own skill accurately, track rivals, and plan across many rounds because those abilities help them choose better bids, avoid damaging failures, and invest effort where future payoff is higher.

Zero assumption version

Why an AI labor market is hard to reason about

A normal benchmark tells you whether an agent can solve one task. It does not tell you what happens when many agents chase the same jobs over time, when each agent can split effort across multiple jobs, and when a bad result damages future hiring chances. Those market effects matter because a strategy that looks good on one task can fail over many rounds if it causes low-value bids, missed deadlines, or reputation loss.

The paper builds a simulated market so that these effects can be measured directly. Jobs arrive, agents choose where to bid, completed work changes profit and reputation, and later hiring depends on the record created earlier. That setup lets the authors test which strategic abilities matter because the same agent can be run with or without each ability while the market rules stay fixed.

The paper's move in one sentence. Turn agent competition into a repeated market simulation, then isolate which strategic abilities raise profit, reputation, and market share.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Create a repeated gig market

Simulate many rounds of job posting, bidding, execution, payment, and reputation updates.

Give agents different strategic abilities

Turn self-assessment, rival modeling, and long-horizon planning on or off so that each ability can be tested separately.

Let agents compete for the same work

Agents choose bids under uncertainty because they do not know exactly how rivals will act or how future rounds will unfold.

Track market outcomes

Measure profit, reputation, market share, and job success across many rounds.

Compare strategies

See which abilities raise long-run outcomes because they improve the sequence of choices, not just one isolated action.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes how an agent chooses work in a competitive market. Instead of reacting only to the current job reward, the agent reasons about self-fit, rival behavior, and future consequences.

the object it moves  =  Job-selection and bidding strategy
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  Strategic fit of a job

Strategic fit means how good a job is for one specific agent after you account for reward, chance of success, rival competition, and future effects on reputation. A job with a large posted reward can still have poor strategic fit because failure may be likely or because rivals make the chance of winning very small.

The paper shows that strong agents act as if they estimate this hidden value before bidding. They do not need the market to state it directly. They recover it from self-knowledge, market state, and longer-term planning because those signals together tell the agent whether taking the job moves it toward better future options.

Why it works

Why this works: good market play is a repeated choice problem

Metacognition helps because an agent that knows its own strengths avoids overbidding on jobs it is likely to fail. That preserves reputation and keeps effort available for better matches. Competitive awareness helps because the value of a bid depends on who else is likely to chase the same work. Long-horizon planning helps because some choices pay later rather than now, such as taking a lower-pay job that strengthens reputation with a client segment.

These abilities compound across rounds. A good bid today increases profit and hiring chances tomorrow, while a bad bid can lower both. The market therefore rewards strategies that manage sequences of choices well because later opportunities depend on the history created earlier.

Math after the picture

A simple strategic value score

First in words:

One way to summarize the paper is to score each job by expected payoff minus expected damage from failure and wasted competition. The agent should prefer jobs with higher strategic value because that combines present reward with future effects.

Then the compact form:

strategic_value(job) = win_prob * reward - fail_prob * reputation_cost - contest_cost + future_access_gain

Every symbol:

win_probchance that the agent wins the bid and completes the job well
rewardpayment from the job if the result is accepted
fail_probchance that the agent harms its record through poor execution or overreach
reputation_costfuture loss caused by a poor result
contest_costeffort lost by bidding into a crowded job
future_access_gainlater benefit from taking this job now, such as better client trust
A tiny worked example
Job A: win chance 0.7, reward 10, fail chance 0.1, record cost 8, contest cost 2, future access gain 3.
Strategic value = 0.7 * 10 - 0.1 * 8 - 2 + 3 = 7.2.
Job B: win chance 0.4, reward 16, fail chance 0.3, record cost 10, contest cost 4, future access gain 1.
Strategic value = 0.4 * 16 - 0.3 * 10 - 4 + 1 = 0.4.
Job B pays more on paper, but Job A is the better move because the combined chance, risk, and future effect are stronger.

The paper runs a full market simulation rather than this exact formula, but the formula captures the decision tradeoff the agent must handle.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The market rules may be too simple. A simulation can miss real pricing, contract, or trust behavior. If an important market rule is absent, the measured strategy may look stronger than it would in deployment because the world gives fewer ways to fail.
Self-assessment can be wrong. An agent that overestimates itself bids too aggressively and harms its record because it takes jobs it cannot execute well enough.
Rival modeling may age badly. If other agents change their behavior, an old picture of the market can mislead planning because yesterday's strong bid may be a weak bid today.
Done right. The useful lesson is not one exact market forecast. It is the causal point that strategic abilities matter because repeated competition turns local mistakes into long-run economic loss.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small repeated market with 20 jobs per round, 5 agents, job rewards, difficulty levels, and a reputation update rule. Give one agent accurate self-assessment and planning, and give another agent only myopic reward chasing. Run at least 100 rounds with the same job stream. The demo should record profit, reputation, and market share over time.

The result should show that the strategic agent pulls ahead because it avoids poor-fit jobs and protects future access. To make that claim credible, log the actual decisions that created the gap: overbids avoided, crowded jobs skipped, and lower-risk jobs chosen to preserve reputation.

success = strategic_profit > myopic_profit and strategic_reputation > myopic_reputation

The demo must prove that better strategic judgment wins the market because repeated decisions shape later opportunity.
