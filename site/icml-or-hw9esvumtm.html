RoCA: Robust Cross-Domain Autonomous Driving

Learn prototype driving scenarios from a source domain and use them as anchors to predict trajectories in new domains without extra computation.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Autonomous driving models trained in one city often fail in another because they miss rare or unusual scenarios. This paper proposes RoCA, which finds a small set of prototype scenarios (called basis tokens, which are learned representations of key driving situations) that cover the diversity of driving, then uses those prototypes to work well in new cities.

Zero assumption version

Models trained in one place fail in another

Autonomous driving models learn to predict what cars should do by watching video and steering decisions from humans. But the training data is uneven: common highway scenes appear thousands of times, while rare events (heavy rain, sudden obstacles, unusual road layouts) appear in only a handful of examples.

A naive approach: train the model to minimize error on all scenarios equally. This fails because the frequent scenarios dominate the training, so the model gets good at highways but brittle elsewhere. When you move the model to a new city with different road patterns or weather, it fails.

The core problem: Frequent scenarios drown out rare ones, and models that work in one city fail in another.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Collect basis prototypes

Sample representative driving scenarios from training data to form a small codebook.

Train a Gaussian process

Learn a probabilistic model that maps any ego and surrounding vehicle information to these basis prototypes.

Constrain base model with prototypes

Use the Gaussian process to nudge the main driving model's predictions toward the prototypes, keeping it focused on learned patterns.

Adapt to new domains

On a new city, retrain the driving model but keep the prototypes and Gaussian process fixed.

Prediction is fast

When the model runs on a new scene, the prototypes add no extra computation because the Gaussian process is already learned.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

RoCA introduces basis tokens: a small set of learned prototype scenarios (captured as learned numerical representations) that represent the diversity of driving. During training, these prototypes are learned; when the model runs on a new scene, they guide the driving model toward predictions that fit one of these known patterns.

the object it moves  =  basis tokens
The hidden quantity

Prediction uncertainty

measured quantity  =  prediction variance

As the model predicts a trajectory (the future path of a car), it should also express how confident it is. Uncertainty (or variance) is high when the current scene does not match any learned prototype well, and low when it does. This is the hidden quantity the paper measures.

RoCA estimates this uncertainty using a Gaussian process, a probabilistic model that naturally outputs both a prediction and a confidence level. When variance is high on a scenario, the model learned that scenario is rare or ambiguous, so it should be weighted more during training.

Plain reading. The model is less confident (high uncertainty) on rare scenarios like sudden obstacles or complex intersections, which is good—it learns to focus on these hard cases.
Paper reading. RoCA multiplies the training loss by prediction variance, so difficult scenarios (high variance) get stronger updates during learning. This pulls the model toward the learned basis prototypes because high-variance scenarios are forced to matter more in the final weights.
Why it works

How learning from prototypes fixes the imbalance

Normally, training loss is the same everywhere: the model sums up errors equally across frequent and rare scenarios. This means rare scenarios barely move the weights because their errors are a drop in the bucket.

RoCA inverts this: for each scenario, it computes how well it matches the learned basis prototypes. If the scenario is rare or ambiguous, the match is uncertain (high variance). Then it multiplies the training loss by this uncertainty, so rare scenarios get amplified. A rare scenario with high uncertainty now contributes more to training, so the model learns to handle it better. This is not a trick—it is mathematically sound because uncertainty is information: high uncertainty means the current learned prototypes do not explain this scenario well, so the model should update harder to cover it.

Math after the picture

The variance-weighted loss

First in words:

Loss = trajectory error + (Gaussian process variance) times (error of model prediction against prototype prediction). The variance term multiplies the penalty so rare scenarios contribute more.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_traj(y_pred, y_true) + sigma_squared * L_gp(y_pred, mu_gp)

Every symbol:

L_trajstandard supervised loss (difference between predicted and true trajectory)
y_predthe driving model's trajectory prediction
y_truethe ground-truth human trajectory from data
sigma_squaredthe Gaussian process variance (prediction uncertainty)
L_gploss measuring how far model prediction is from the GP's prototype-based prediction
mu_gpthe Gaussian process mean prediction based on basis prototypes
A tiny worked example
Scenario A (highway, common): L_traj = 0.3, sigma_squared = 0.1, L_gp = 0.2. Total = 0.3 + 0.1 * 0.2 = 0.32.
Scenario B (rare obstacle): L_traj = 0.4, sigma_squared = 0.8, L_gp = 0.3. Total = 0.4 + 0.8 * 0.3 = 0.64.
Same traj error (highway vs. obstacle both have error ~0.3–0.4), but rare scenario B has 2x the total loss because variance is high—so it pulls training harder.

The variance from the Gaussian process (which measures prototype diversity coverage) directly weights how much each scenario matters during training, ensuring rare situations get learned thoroughly.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Prototypes do not cover a new domain's unique scenarios. If the target city has driving patterns entirely absent from the source (e.g., a unique intersection layout or weather), the learned prototypes cannot represent it, and predictions will still be brittle. RoCA assumes diversity within the source domain generalizes to the target.
Gaussian process computations can be expensive at scale. Although inference is fast (prototypes are learned), if the basis codebook is too small, it may underfit; if too large, computing the correlation structure inside the Gaussian process becomes expensive. The paper does not deeply explore this trade-off.
Variance estimation is noisy on small target domains. When adapting to a new domain with few unlabeled samples, the Gaussian process variance estimates may be unreliable, causing the method to mismatch the true difficulty of new scenarios.
Done right: use RoCA when source and target domains share conceptual scenarios but differ in frequency or style. RoCA works best when a new city has the same kinds of driving situations (highways, intersections, obstacles) but in different proportions or visual styles. It fails if the new domain has completely new types of scenarios the source domain never saw.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A minimal implementation: take a simple driving simulation (or a small real dataset split), train a baseline model equally on all scenarios, then train RoCA with variance weighting. The demo should show that rare scenarios (e.g., sudden obstacles, unusual maneuvers) improve more with RoCA than with the baseline because the variance-weighted loss forces the model to learn them thoroughly. It should also show that adapting the RoCA model to a new domain requires fewer training steps and achieves lower collision rate than retraining from scratch, because the prototypes already cover the scenarios.

improvement = (collision_rate_baseline - collision_rate_roca) / collision_rate_baseline

The demo must show rare scenarios improving much more with RoCA than the baseline (because variance weighting makes them matter during training) and prove that RoCA adapts faster to a new domain because the learned prototypes already cover the range of real scenarios.
