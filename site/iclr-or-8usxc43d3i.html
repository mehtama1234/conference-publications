HardcoreLogic: Testing Long-Tail Logic Puzzles to Expose Memorization

When you test reasoning models on unusual puzzle variants they have not seen before, their scores plummet, revealing they have memorized stereotypes instead of learning to reason.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Large reasoning models perform well on standard Sudoku and logic puzzles, but only because those puzzles follow predictable formats. When you ask the model to solve a puzzle with irregular shapes, extra rules, or an unsolvable setup, it fails badly. This benchmark reveals that the model is not actually reasoning — it is matching against patterns it memorized during training.

Zero assumption version

Why current benchmarks do not measure real reasoning

Existing logic puzzle benchmarks use only canonical puzzles: standard 9x9 Sudoku with nine 3x3 zones, ordinary Nonogram grids, regular letter-encoding puzzles. Reasoning models do very well on these.

But a naive explanation would be that the model has memorized stereotypes, not learned to reason. When you take a standard Sudoku and make the zones irregular, or add a diagonal constraint, or change the rule slightly, does the model still solve it? No. It fails dramatically. The model has learned short-cuts: if it sees Sudoku-like numbers and a grid, it applies the memorized 9x9 Sudoku reasoning path. It does not actually understand the rules; it recognizes the surface form and plays back a learned response.

The core problem. Models perform well on standard puzzles not because they reason, but because they memorized the canonical form and the reasoning path.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Define canonical puzzles

Start with standard logic puzzles (9x9 Sudoku, regular Nonogram, letter-encoding).

Step 2: Generate long-tail variants

Transform puzzles three ways: make them bigger and more complex, change rules to uncommon forms, or make them unsolvable.

Step 3: Ensure variants are rare

These non-canonical puzzles are so unusual they are unlikely to appear in training data.

Step 4: Test reasoning models

Ask the models to solve variants; measure accuracy drop from canonical to variant.

Step 5: Diagnose the gap

Analyze error patterns to show the model misunderstood rules or missed new constraints.

What the paper changes

What the paper measures

The paper does not change model weights or training recipes. Instead, it constructs a new evaluation dataset: puzzles that differ from the canonical form in specific, controlled ways. By comparing model accuracy on canonical versus variant puzzles, it exposes how much performance comes from memorized patterns versus actual reasoning.

the object it moves  =  The test set: canonical versus variant puzzles
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: memorization reliance

measured quantity  =  Memorization reliance

The hidden quantity is how much of the model's accuracy comes from matching memorized stereotypes versus applying general rules. You cannot directly see this in a model, but you can infer it: if accuracy drops sharply when you change the puzzle form, then the model was relying on memorization, not reasoning.

HardcoreLogic estimates this by measuring performance on three kinds of transformations: increased complexity (bigger grids, more constraints), uncommon elements (irregular zones, different rules), and unsolvable puzzles (no valid solution). A model that genuinely reasons should adapt to all three. A model that memorized only the canonical form will fail on the variants.

Why it works

Why the performance drop reveals true reliance on memorized patterns

The mechanism is straightforward. During training, the model saw mostly canonical puzzles: 9x9 Sudoku, standard Nonogram, regular formats. It learned to recognize the surface form (grid shape, constraint language) and apply a canned reasoning path. This works perfectly on canonical puzzles because the model has seen thousands of examples.

When you change the puzzle form — irregular zones, diagonal constraints, uncommon rules — the surface signal that triggers the memorized path no longer matches. The model cannot fall back on its learned pattern. Now it must actually parse the new rules, understand the constraint graph, and deduce solutions from first principles. This is much harder. Most models fail.

Unsolvable puzzles test a different kind of memorization: the model has learned that puzzles have solutions, so it invents an answer rather than saying no solution exists. This breaks the reasoning chain from the start.

Measuring the gap

First in words:

memorization_reliance = accuracy_canonical - accuracy_variant

Then the compact form:

M = A_canon - A_variant

Every symbol:

Mmemorization reliance (how much of the accuracy comes from memorized patterns)
A_canonaccuracy on canonical puzzles (standard 9x9 format)
A_variantaccuracy on variant puzzles (irregular zones, novel rules, unsolvable)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a model solves 95 percent of canonical 9x9 Sudoku correctly.
On variant Sudoku with irregular zones: the model solves only 52 percent.
Memorization reliance = 95 - 52 = 43 percentage points.
This means 43 percentage points of the canonical performance came from memorized stereotypes, not reasoning.

The gap quantifies how much the model relies on having seen the exact puzzle form during training.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Model ignores new constraints. When you add a diagonal rule to Sudoku, the model solves the 9x9 zone constraints but ignores the diagonals, producing an invalid answer.
Model assumes a solution always exists. On unsolvable puzzles, the model invents a filled grid rather than saying no valid solution, because its training data never showed unsolvable cases.
Model fails on scaling. A 16x16 Sudoku (double the size) confuses the model even though the rules are identical, because training data had only 9x9 puzzles.
Genuine reasoning passes all three. A model that truly understands the rules understands that they apply regardless of grid size, zone shape, or whether a solution exists.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a script that takes a reasoning model (GPT, Qwen, Claude, or a fine-tuned LRM), feeds it 10 canonical Sudoku puzzles and 10 variant Sudoku puzzles (irregular zones, or diagonal constraints), and measures accuracy on each. Show the performance drop.

memorization_gap = accuracy_canonical - accuracy_variant

The gap should be large (≥30 percentage points). If a model claims to reason, but its performance collapses on variants, then it was memorizing, not reasoning.
