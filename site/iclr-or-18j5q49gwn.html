SPG: Sandwiched Policy Gradient for Masked Diffusion Language Models

Use two honest estimates of how good an answer is (one too high, one too low) to teach a language model via reward signals.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Diffusion language models generate words by gradually unmasking letters in parallel, making them faster than traditional word-by-word models. But teaching them to follow human feedback or task rewards is stuck: standard reward-based learning does not work because the model's true success score (log-likelihood) is hidden and intractable. SPG solves this by building two bounds around that true score—one that overestimates and one that underestimates—and uses both to train.

Zero assumption version

Why reward learning for diffusion models is broken

Diffusion language models work like a artist filling in a portrait gradually: start with every pixel masked (hidden), then step by step unmask real words until a complete sentence appears. They are fast because many words unmask in parallel, not one at a time like traditional models.

To make them follow human preferences or solve tasks, we want to use reinforcement learning (RL)—reward good answers, penalize bad ones. But here is the trap: RL methods need to know exactly how likely each answer is, measured on a clear scale. For diffusion models, that true likelihood is buried deep in the math and too expensive to compute directly. Prior work punts: they use a one-sided guess (the ELBO, an estimate from pre-training) pretending it is the true score. But a one-sided guess biases the learning, especially when punishing bad outputs.

In one sentence. SPG builds both an overestimate and an underestimate of how good an answer is, then uses both to train, eliminating the bias of a one-sided guess.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a pre-trained diffusion model

The model is trained to unmask sequences but not yet tuned to follow rewards.

Sample multiple completions and grade them

For each prompt, sample several candidate answers and score each with a reward function (e.g., task accuracy).

Compute advantage (did this answer beat the average)

For each completion, compute how much better or worse it is than the average of the samples, because this tells the model which answers to emphasize and which to discourage relative to typical performance.

Build upper and lower bounds of likelihood

Instead of one guess, compute two: a score that overestimates and a score that underestimates the true success.

Blend and update

Mix the two bounds to form an unbiased estimate, then update the model's weights to increase scores for good answers.

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

The paper changes how the model learns from rewards. Instead of using a single biased estimate of answer quality (the ELBO), it swaps in a mixture of an upper and lower bound. This mixture is unbiased (not systematically too high or too low) because one bound is provably too high and the other too low, so their average cancels the bias, allowing the model to learn the right lesson from each reward.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: evidence bounds

measured quantity  =  Upper and lower bounds of log-likelihood

The core challenge is computing the true log-likelihood (how likely the model assigns to each answer). This is intractable—too expensive to calculate directly. SPG works around this by computing two approximations: an upper bound (an estimate that is provably too high) and a lower bound (an estimate that is provably too low). The truth lies between them.

Prior work used only the lower bound (ELBO), biasing the learning. SPG computes both, then mixes them. The paper estimates the bounds using block-wise masking: corrupt chunks of the sequence at random, predict the missing parts, and use this prediction to derive an evidence upper bound (EUBO).

Why it works

Why sandwiched bounds work

When you train a model with rewards, you nudge the weights in a direction that increases the score for good answers and decreases it for bad ones. But that nudge must be *correct*—it should point toward answers the reward function truly likes. If your score estimate is biased (always too high or always too low), the nudge goes in the wrong direction.

SPG's trick: use two estimates that bracket the truth. The upper bound (EUBO) overestimates; the lower bound (ELBO) underestimates. When you mix them, their biases cancel out, giving an unbiased estimate. This means the training nudge points in the right direction. Moreover, SPG adapts the mixture weight for each weight in the model—some weights benefit from trusting the upper bound more, others trust the lower bound more. This per-weight adaptation further reduces noise in the weight-update direction, making training stable and efficient.

Math after the picture

The unbiased mixture equation

First in words:

Gradient from mixture equals (one-minus-mixture-weight times lower-bound weight) plus (mixture-weight times upper-bound measure), all multiplied by the change in log-likelihood of the answer

Then the compact form:

g_{ω,k} = ((1-ω)w(t,z_t) + ω*ρ_β) * ∂_θ_k log(π_θ(x|z_t,c))

Every symbol:

g_{ω,k}the gradient (nudge direction) for weight k using mixture weight ω
ωmixture weight (how much to trust the upper bound versus lower bound), chosen to minimize variance
w(t,z_t)the pre-training weight from the ELBO (lower bound)
ρ_βthe upper bound measure derived from one-step block predictions
∂_θ_k log(π_θ)how much the log-likelihood changes when weight k changes
A tiny worked example
Suppose a weight w1 has ELBO weight w(t)=0.8 and upper-bound measure ρ_β=1.2.
Optimal mixture for this weight is ω=0.5 (balancing both equally).
Then g_{ω,k} = ((1-0.5)*0.8 + 0.5*1.2) * (change in log-likelihood).
= (0.4 + 0.6) * (change) = 1.0 * (change).
With ω=0: gradient would be 0.8*(change), biased low. With ω=1: 1.2*(change), biased high. ω=0.5 gives unbiased 1.0.

The mixture weight ω is adapted per weight to minimize variance in the gradient, ensuring the nudge is stable.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Block masking too coarse. If the masked blocks are too large, the model cannot predict the missing words accurately, breaking the upper-bound estimate. The blocks must be sized to the model's capacity.
Mixture weight diverges. If the upper and lower bounds diverge wildly (e.g., one is 10x the other), the adaptive mixture weight ω can become extreme and unstable, amplifying noise. This happens when the model sees data very different from what it was trained on.
Negative rewards swamp the learning. The paper shows that negative-advantage penalties are critical. Without them, the model does not learn to avoid bad outputs, only to chase good ones. Removing negative-reward pressure causes a large performance drop.
Done right: block size tuned to model capacity, mixture weight constrained, and negative rewards included. SPG works when all three pieces are in place: block-wise masking that the model can reason about, variance-minimizing mixture weight per layer, and explicit penalties for bad outputs.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

To show SPG works, build a small diffusion model (or use a pre-trained one at reduced size) and fine-tune it on a simple reward task like GSM8K math problems. Compare three training runs: (1) ELBO-only baseline, (2) upper-bound-only baseline, (3) SPG with both bounds. Track the reward score over training steps and measure final accuracy on held-out math problems. SPG converges faster because the unbiased mixture estimate has lower variance than either one-sided bound alone, so each training step nudges the model more confidently. SPG reaches higher accuracy because the unbiased estimate prevents the model from learning false preferences: a one-sided guess biases the learning direction, causing the model to chase phantom reward signals.

Accuracy on math problems = number correct / total problems; Convergence speed = steps to 90% of final accuracy

The demo must make it impossible to miss that both bounds together (SPG) learn better and faster than either bound alone, because canceling the bias and reducing variance both matter for training efficiency and final performance.
