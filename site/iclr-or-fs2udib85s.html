DataMask: Joint Data Selection via Policy Gradient Learning

Instead of picking training data by only quality or only diversity, DataMask learns a smart blend by treating selection as a learning problem with its own reward signal.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

When training giant language models on trillions of words, picking which pieces to actually use is expensive. Real data has a hard tradeoff: pick only high-quality writing and the model gets bored and stops learning; pick only diverse topics and it misses the best examples. DataMask solves this by learning which samples to keep through policy gradient learning (a technique where you repeatedly try random choices, measure how well they work, and nudge the random generator toward better choices—the same trick that trains game-playing AI). It works 99% faster than the old exhaustive method because it only samples a few hundred masks instead of checking all 2^N combinations, and it finds subsets that beat both quality-only and diversity-only approaches because it optimizes both metrics simultaneously rather than picking one at the expense of the other.

The dataset selection trap

When you train a large language model, you need billions or trillions of words of text. You don't want to use ALL of it — much is redundant, some is low quality, and computing time costs money. So you filter: keep only the best writing, or keep only the most different kinds of writing.

But here is the trap: keep only high-quality writing and the model learns its patterns perfectly, then stops improving (the learning curve flattens; this is called diminishing returns). Keep only diverse, different kinds of writing and you throw away many of the best examples — the model trains on too much weak material and ends up weaker overall. A naive person would try to use both filters at once: rank by quality, rank by diversity, pick the top of both lists. But those lists almost never overlap; the hard filtering logic (pick this AND that) either picks nothing or requires looking at every possible combination, which on a trillion-word dataset takes weeks of computation.

So today's models use one filter or the other, and live with the weakness.

The method in one sentence. DataMask learns a mask (which samples to keep) by repeatedly trying random masks, checking how they score on a combined quality-and-diversity objective, then gradually nudging its random-mask generator toward better choices because the masks that score higher guide the learning process, much like how a game-playing AI learns by trying moves and rewarding successful ones.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Start with a random mask generator

A simple learned program that flips a coin for each sample (keep or drop) but biased to learn.

Sample a random mask

The generator makes a random choice for each sample; assemble the chosen subset from the dataset.

Score the masked subset

Check how good that subset is by computing quality and diversity scores; combine them into one number.

Measure the improvement gradient

Compare to the previous mask: if this one scored higher, nudge the generator to favor similar choices.

Repeat and converge

Repeat until the generator settles on a consistent, good subset (often in hundreds of tries, not millions).

What the paper changes

The one thing the paper changes

DataMask learns a binary choice per sample: keep or drop. Those choices are encoded in a learned mask, a list of yes/no flags. Unlike traditional data selection, where the mask is computed by running a formula (e.g., if quality_score > threshold, keep it), the mask here is learned as a distribution (a learned program that can adjust its bias). The paper nudges this learned distribution toward masks that score higher on a joint objective.

the object it moves  =  the data selection mask
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Joint objective value

measured quantity  =  combined quality-diversity score

The paper measures one hidden quantity: how good is a chosen subset at being both high-quality AND diverse at the same time? This is not quality alone or diversity alone; it is a single score that rewards you for finding samples that score high on both fronts. Think of it like a school report card that grades both attendance and test scores together, not separately. The paper estimates this by running the model on the chosen subset and measuring its accuracy across 12 different tasks, but it also measures the diversity of topics in the subset and the quality of the text, and combines those into one number.

The mask learning works by repeatedly sampling masks and measuring this combined score. Masks that get higher combined scores nudge the probability distribution toward similar masks. Over iterations, the learned generator converges to a mask that balances both metrics.

Plain reading. The paper is trying to answer: which 10% of a trillion-word dataset should I actually train on? It learns by sampling random 10% subsets, scoring them on quality and diversity, then adjusting its random-sampling to favor the good subsets.
Paper reading. The paper frames data selection as a random optimization problem: maximize a joint objective (combining quality and diversity metrics) over the space of binary masks, using policy gradient learning to navigate that space efficiently.
Why it works

Why it works: the learning loop

Here is the real reason DataMask beats quality-only and diversity-only: it learns a mask by treating mask choice as a decision that has a reward (the combined score). The method works by sampling random masks, measuring their reward, and then nudging the sampling probability toward the rewarded choices. If a mask with lots of high-quality diverse samples scores well, the generator learns to favor that pattern. If a mask drops all high-quality samples in pursuit of diversity, it scores poorly, and the generator learns to avoid that. This is how you avoid checking all possible masks: you learn which pattern of choices tends to score high, without explicit enumeration.

Why is this faster than the old greedy exhaustive method? Because greedy search checks every possible choice sequentially (keep or drop this sample, then the next, then the next), running 2^N checks for N samples. This method samples a small set of random masks — maybe a few hundred — and converges. The learned mask generator learns to gravitate toward the shape of good masks (keep high-quality diverse ones, drop redundant weak ones) without trying every combination.

Math after the picture

The equation: policy gradient objective

First in words:

expected combined score = sum over all masks of (probability of mask times combined score of that mask)

Then the compact form:

max_theta E_m ~ p_theta(m) [ alpha * Q(S_m) + beta * D(S_m) ]

Every symbol:

thetathe learned mask generator's parameters (what we update)
p_theta(m)the probability the generator assigns to mask m given parameters theta
S_mthe selected subset when mask m is applied (which samples are kept)
Q(S_m)the quality score of the subset (how well-written the samples are)
D(S_m)the diversity score of the subset (how many different topics appear)
alphaweight on quality (how much to care about high-quality samples)
betaweight on diversity (how much to care about covering different topics)
A tiny worked example
Suppose we have 4 samples and two random masks: Mask A (keep samples 1,3) and Mask B (keep samples 1,2,3,4).
Quality scores: Sample 1=0.9, Sample 2=0.3, Sample 3=0.8, Sample 4=0.2.
Diversity scores: Sample 1=0.5 (common topic), Sample 2=0.9 (rare topic), Sample 3=0.6 (common), Sample 4=0.8 (rare).
Mask A: Q(S_A) = (0.9+0.8)/2 = 0.85, D(S_A) = (0.5+0.6)/2 = 0.55.
Mask B: Q(S_B) = (0.9+0.3+0.8+0.2)/4 = 0.55, D(S_B) = (0.5+0.9+0.6+0.8)/4 = 0.7.
Combined with alpha=1, beta=1: Mask A scores 0.85+0.55=1.4. Mask B scores 0.55+0.7=1.25.
Mask A wins; the generator learns to favor the pattern 'keep high-quality samples preferentially, even if it slightly reduces diversity'.

The gradient tells the generator how to nudge theta to increase the expected combined score. This is why it converges: each update is a small step toward masks that balance quality and diversity.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Weights poorly chosen. If alpha and beta (the importance of quality vs. diversity) are set wrong, the learned mask will ignore one dimension. For example, if alpha is 10 times beta, the mask will drop diverse samples to maximize quality, falling back to the quality-only problem.
Convergence too early. If the policy gradient updates stop too soon, the mask may settle on a locally good solution (e.g., 'keep samples from only the top three diverse topics') and miss a better global one ('keep a smarter mix of top and second-tier samples'), because the learning process has found a valley of good choices and without enough iterations cannot escape it to find the deeper, better valley.
Initialization bias. If the random mask generator starts with a strong bias (e.g., 'keep everything' or 'drop everything'), it takes many iterations to correct, and convergence slows. Bad initialization can make the method nearly as expensive as greedy search.
Done right. With good hyperparameter tuning, careful initialization, and enough iterations, the method converges in hundreds of updates, yielding a subset that beats both single-metric approaches by significant margins.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dataset of 10,000 text snippets, each with a manually assigned quality score (0–1) and a topic label (for diversity). Implement a simple mask generator: a list of 10,000 learned probabilities, one per snippet. Implement quality and diversity scorers. Run policy gradient updates: sample 100 random masks per step, compute combined scores, and adjust the learned probabilities toward masks with higher scores. Run for 500 steps. Track the combined score across steps (it should rise smoothly) and the composition of the final mask (it should be a mix of high-quality and diverse snippets).

final_combined_score = (average_quality_of_selected) + (average_diversity_of_selected)

The demo proves the method works if: the combined score is higher than either metric alone, the convergence is smooth (no wild oscillations), and the final mask contains both high-quality and diverse samples.
