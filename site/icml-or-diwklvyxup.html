Optimal Transport Group Counterfactual Explanations

A method learns a reusable map that turns any member of a group into a counterfactual example, while keeping the group intact.

Paper explainersAll themesInterpretability

Imagine you want to explain why a group of loan applicants were rejected. You could show each applicant what would have to change to get approved. But if you have 100 applicants, computing 100 separate explanations is expensive and messy. This paper proposes learning one smooth map that works for any member of the group without asking the model to retrain or make strong assumptions about linearity. It uses a mathematical tool called optimal transport to find the cheapest way to move the entire group to approval, all at once.

Zero assumption version

The cost of explaining groups

When you want to explain why a model rejected a group (denied loans, flagged accounts, filtered applicants), you need counterfactual examples: what would have to change for them to pass? A naive approach optimizes each person separately, which is expensive and brittle. If a new person joins the group, you start over. Other methods assume the model works in a straight line (linear), which rarely holds for real models. A third problem: as you move people toward approval, the group loses its shape. A tight cluster of rejected applicants spreads out into a cloud.

The core tension: you want one reusable rule that turns anyone in the group into a counterfactual, keeps the distances between people roughly the same, and works without strict assumptions about the model.

In one sentence. The method learns a single smooth map (optimal transport) that generalize to any group member without re-running the model.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper shifts from computing one explanation per person to learning a single smooth mathematical function (a map) that works for anyone in the group. This map takes each person's current numbers and pushes them toward approval, keeping the group clustered.

the object it moves  =  The learned transport map
The hidden quantity

The transport cost: how much movement is needed

measured quantity  =  Total transport cost

The hidden quantity is how much total movement is needed to send the entire group from rejection to approval. Formally, it measures the sum of distances traveled, weighted by how densely the group is packed at each point. Think of it like the cost of moving a crowd: moving one person a long distance is expensive, but moving a dense group means you can spread the cost across many. The paper estimates this cost by setting up an optimization problem: what smooth function minimizes total movement while keeping everyone in the same relative positions?

The paper uses this cost to guide learning. It does not compute the cost explicitly for each person; instead, it learns a map that makes the cost as small as possible across the entire group.

Why it works

Why moving the group together works

The paper exploits a key insight: if you move a tight cluster toward approval, you can use one smooth function instead of separate tweaks for each person. This works because optimal transport is designed to move probability distributions (clouds of points) efficiently. It computes the cheapest way to slide one cloud onto another by matching points greedily: the closest rejected person pairs with the closest-matching approved profile, and so on. When you learn this pairing once, it applies to any new person in the group without re-running the expensive matching.

For linear classifiers (like a loan-scoring formula), the paper proves that this learned map is simply a linear transformation: multiply each person's numbers by a grid of values, then add a fixed shift. This shift moves the group uniformly without stretching or squashing it. Because of this, the group stays tight and the changes remain interpretable. The shared map also means fewer numbers to explain; instead of describing 100 individual changes, you describe one set of multipliers that applies to everyone.

The core equation

First in words:

The total cost is the integral of the distance each person travels under the learned map, summed across all group members, plus a penalty term that preserves group geometry (constraint on how much the map can stretch or squash).

Then the compact form:

minimize E[||T(x) - y||] subject to T preserves Wasserstein distance between group pairs

Every symbol:

T(x)The learned map applied to a person with features x
xA rejected applicant's original numbers
yAn approved applicant's numbers (the target)
||...||The distance between two people's numbers
E[...]Average over all people in the group
Wasserstein distanceThe cost of moving one distribution to another (captures group geometry)
A tiny worked example
Suppose a rejected group has 3 applicants with income scores [30, 32, 28]. Approved group looks like [70, 75, 68].
The optimal transport map learns to add 40 to each score (on average).
Person 1: travel distance 30 to 70 = 40 units. Person 2: 32 to 75 = 43 units. Person 3: 28 to 68 = 40 units.
Total cost = (40 + 43 + 40) / 3 = 41 units average. The group stays tight (gaps of 2-4 are preserved in both before and after).

The map moves the group as a rigid unit, preserving internal distances, which is why the optimization focuses on the average transport cost plus a penalty that keeps gaps intact.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Group changes over time. If the rejected group evolves (new applicants with different profiles arrive), the learned map may not fit as well. Re-learning is needed for a very different population.
Model is highly nonlinear. The method works best when the decision boundary is simple (linear or smoothly curved). If the model is chaotic or has sharp discontinuities, the map may be warped and fail to preserve distances between people.
No feasible recourse exists. If the group cannot realistically reach approval (e.g., loan rejection due to factors outside anyone's control), the map will be forced to push people far out of the reasonable range, making explanations unrealistic.
Done right. When the group is stable, the model boundary is smooth, and realistic recourse exists, the method generalizes to new members with minimal additional cost.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple classifier (e.g., decision boundary is a tilted line separating approved from rejected loans). Create a group of 10 rejected applicants clustered together. Compute the optimal transport map once using the group. Then: (a) apply it to the original 10 and measure whether they all cross the approval line while staying close to their original positions; (b) sample 5 new rejected applicants (not in the training group) and apply the same map without re-optimization, checking if they still cross the line; (c) measure the group cohesion before and after (average pairwise distance) and confirm it is preserved.

Cohesion preservation = (average distance after recourse) / (average distance before) — should be >= 0.9 (group stays tight). Generalization success = fraction of new members who cross the approval boundary when the learned map is applied.

The demo makes impossible to miss: one learned map, reused instantly on new people, keeps the group together while sliding everyone to approval.
