Faster and more efficient generation

Papers on speeding up generation while keeping quality high, including distillation (learning from larger models), few-step sampling, and compression techniques.

What this is

Faster and more efficient generation means reducing both time and computational resources because standard generative models need many steps (often 50-1000) to go from noise to a real output, and each step is computationally expensive. Generative models face a core tradeoff: quality versus speed. Most models can produce beautiful output, but often very slowly, making them impractical for real work. A beautiful image that takes 30 seconds to generate is not useful for interactive applications because users want real-time feedback and iteration. Neither is a video that takes an hour, because video production already consumes time and waiting additional hours for generation makes it impractical. Efficient generation is a practical necessity—it's the difference between a research demo and something millions of people can use. The research question is: can we get good quality in fewer steps, so users get outputs in seconds rather than minutes?

There are several approaches. Distillation means training a small, fast 'student' model to mimic a large, slow 'teacher' model. The student learns to do in 5 steps what the teacher does in 50 steps. Few-step sampling means rethinking the mathematics so that you can jump multiple noise-removal steps at once—instead of removing 1% of noise 100 times, can you remove 10% of noise 10 times? Quantization compresses the model itself—storing numbers with lower precision—so it runs faster and uses less memory. Pruning removes parts of the model that don't contribute much. Caching means remembering intermediate computations rather than redoing them.

The research here is a mix of mathematical work (proving you can use bigger steps while still converging to good outputs) and systems work (engineering models to be fast without sacrificing quality). A key insight is that there's often a tradeoff curve: you can be very fast but lower quality, or slower but higher quality. Papers here explore where that curve lies for different methods and how to push it forward. The practical impact is huge because faster generation means more interactive applications (you can iterate in real time), lower energy use (the system spends less time computing, which matters both for cost and for the environment), and accessibility on lower-end devices like phones (a model that needs a data center is only useful for large organizations with big budgets, while a model that runs on a phone is useful for everyone).

How the work breaks down

Faster generation splits into five directions: using mathematical insights from flow matching to take bigger steps through the generation process; training small models to copy large ones through distillation; getting extreme speed by squeezing generation into one or two steps; applying efficiency techniques to text and other discrete data; and optimizing how we schedule the generation process and use guidance.

Flow Matching Foundations for Efficient Paths

Flow matching is a mathematical framework for turning noise into real data by flowing smoothly from random to structured. The core insight is that you don't have to follow any particular path—you can take any path from noise to data, as long as the math is consistent. This opens up the possibility of taking bigger steps or different paths than traditional diffusion methods, which means fewer function evaluations (times you run the model) to get good results. The challenge is doing this on diverse data types and proving it actually works.

Functional Rectified Flow removes unnecessary mathematical restrictions that earlier work imposed. By using a more general mathematical framework, this work shows you can be more flexible about how you design the flow, which means better results across different types of data without being locked into specific assumptions. Generalised Flow Maps takes this further by handling data that lives on curved surfaces (like geographic coordinates on Earth or sequences of molecules)—on these surfaces, standard flow matching doesn't work because you can't just add numbers like you can in flat space. This paper extends flow matching to work on these curved surfaces and shows it achieves one-step or few-step generation that matches or beats multi-step methods, because the right path depends on the shape of the data space itself. Finite-Time Convergence Analysis provides theoretical guarantees—proving that if you use these flow-based methods, you'll actually converge to good outputs in finite steps, which gives confidence that the speedups are real and not just lucky. These three papers show that better mathematics makes faster generation possible by letting you design smarter paths through the space of possibilities.

Distillation: Learning Speed from Larger Models

Distillation is the idea that a small, fast model can learn to copy what a large, slow model does. The student model watches the teacher solve problems and learns to do it faster by finding shortcuts—not by retraining from scratch, but by learning the teacher's patterns. The hard part is doing this without losing quality, because if the student cuts too many corners it produces bad outputs. It's also hard to scale to very large models because they're expensive to run repeatedly for training.

Optimizing Few-Step Generation with Adaptive Matching Distillation improves the best image generation models like SDXL by using a smarter matching loss—instead of just trying to make the student output look like the teacher output, it learns where the student's mistakes are and focuses training there, which gets 31.25 HPSv2 score (a measure of image quality and alignment with text descriptions), beating the previous best of 30.64 without needing expensive GAN training. pi-Flow takes a different angle: instead of copying the teacher's samples directly, it learns a policy—a strategy for what to do at each step—that imitates how the teacher would solve the problem. On ImageNet 256x256 at one step, it achieves FID of 2.85 (lower is better), and on large production models like FLUX.1-12B it gets substantially better diversity than competing one-step methods, because imitating strategy is more effective than imitating samples. Universal Inverse Distillation provides the theoretical foundation for all of this—it proves that you can distill different types of generative models (diffusion, flow matching, and others) using the same underlying principle, and it enables training directly from real data instead of needing a teacher model for every scenario, which is more practical for production use. Large Scale Diffusion Distillation with Score Constraints and Continuous-Time Consistency scales distillation all the way up to 14 billion models and videos that are 5 seconds long, getting competitive quality while avoiding mode collapse (where the model only generates a few types of outputs instead of diverse ones), because the method keeps diversity without needing to carefully tune adversarial tricks. These four papers show that distillation is the most practical path to few-step generation at scale, because you reuse work already done by large models instead of starting from scratch.

One-Step and Ultra-Fast Generation

One-step generation means running the model just once and getting a final output—that's the extreme of speed. This is nearly impossible with traditional diffusion because one step has to undo hundreds of steps of noise corruption. The only way to make it work is through mathematics (flow matching with smart scheduling) or distillation (copying a fast solution), or both together. The challenge is maintaining quality when you have so little time to generate.

Soft-DiMO achieves one-step FID of 1.56 on ImageNet-256 by using discrete flow models (which handle categorical data like image tokens) with GAN refinement—it combines flow matching with adversarial training to get both fast and high-quality generation in a single forward pass. FACM combines flow matching with consistency models (a technique that forces the model to give the same answer no matter how many steps you use), achieving FID of 1.32 with two steps and 1.70 with one step on ImageNet-256, because forcing consistency is a mathematical way to reduce the solution space to only the good answers. BézierFlow learns to schedule the generation process—instead of removing noise evenly, it removes noise following a Bézier curve, which means it can be more aggressive early and conservative late, getting 2-3x better results for very few steps with only 15 minutes of training. Primal-Spectral Generative Modeling uses a different mathematical trick: instead of iterating step by step, it uses spectral inversion to jump directly to the answer in one analytical step, achieving competitive FID of 1.66 on ImageNet-256 while using only 26 Gflops—170 times less compute than a 25-step method. StreamFlow accelerates standard rectified flow generation through careful implementation and specialized computation, achieving 611 percent speedup on 512x512 images by engineering the actual computation rather than changing the algorithm, which shows that even at high resolution, one-step is achievable in practice. These six papers represent different angles to the same goal: Primal-Spectral and BézierFlow use mathematical innovation to shrink steps, Soft-DiMO uses distillation to copy a fast solution, FACM forces mathematical consistency, and StreamFlow uses systems engineering, which together show that one-step or two-step generation is no longer research fantasy—it's achievable with different trade-offs between quality, architecture, and engineering effort.

Discrete, Specialized, and Structured Generation

Not all generation uses continuous vectors—text uses discrete tokens, molecules use graphs, and speech uses structured data. Distillation and flow matching work differently on discrete data because you can't just add numbers to a discrete token the way you can add noise to a pixel. The question is whether the same speedup ideas transfer to these domains and how to adapt them.

PairFlow tackles discrete flow models by learning a direct source-to-target coupling—instead of following a path through the noise-to-data space, it learns a function that tells you which noise maps to which data output, so you can solve the inverse problem directly. It matches two-stage finetuning performance with only 1.7% training overhead, which is orders of magnitude more efficient. Categorical Flow Maps extends flow matching to work on categorical (discrete) data by using the right mathematics for discrete spaces—it achieves best few-step results on images, molecular graphs, and text because the same principle (smart paths through space) works across all these data types, you just have to respect that the space has different structure. Consistent Diffusion Language Models (CDLM) applies consistency to language generation—the challenge with text is that each token is discrete and depends on all previous tokens, making few-step generation much harder than for images, but CDLM shows you can get quality text generation in far fewer steps by learning consistency directly in discrete space. Inverse-Distilled Diffusion Language Models (IDLM) reduces inference steps 4 to 64 times on text by using inverse distillation—instead of having a teacher and student, it trains the model to predict its own better version through an intermediate representation, which means text generation can now happen in seconds instead of minutes while staying fluent. M-Star uses a mathematical transformation (projection) to enable few-step generation while maintaining flexibility across multiple generative tasks, because some domains naturally break into simpler components you can solve faster. These five papers show that the speedup principles work across text, graphs, and other discrete domains, but they require careful adaptation because the mathematics of discrete spaces differs from the smooth, continuous case.

Scheduling, Guidance, and Solver Optimization

Even with smart mathematics, how you schedule the generation matters enormously—do you remove noise evenly or aggressively at first? Do you follow the direction of steepest change exactly or approximate it? These scheduling and solver choices can make the difference between needing 50 steps and needing 10, so a bad schedule wastes steps while a good one saves them. The challenge is finding better schedules and solvers without huge computational overhead, because testing each schedule candidate requires running the full generation pipeline multiple times.

Decoupled DMD separates classifier-free guidance (a technique that steers generation using an external signal, like text for image generation) from the core generation process, and it decouples the noise schedules for the main model and the guidance model. By allowing different schedules for different pieces of the system, it improves performance and gives more principled control, because different parts of the problem have different optimal paces. Decoupled MeanFlow turns flow models into flow maps by learning the optimal mapping function—this shifts from thinking step-by-step to thinking about the overall transformation, which achieves 1-step FID of 2.16 on ImageNet-256 and 125x faster inference than standard flow models by getting the mapping right instead of iterating. GAS (Improving Discretization of Diffusion ODEs via Generalized Adversarial Solver) learns better solvers for the differential equations that underlie generation—instead of using fixed step schemes, it trains an adversarial solver that adapts to the specific problem, showing superior performance under the same training budget. Shortcut Diffusion Training with Cumulative Consistency Loss takes an optimal control view—it treats generation as a control problem and uses math from control theory to design training that gets better one-step and few-step results than consistency loss alone, because viewing generation through the control lens reveals structure you can exploit. These four papers show that the solver and schedule matter as much as the model—even if your model is good, choosing how to step through time or what guidance to apply can double or triple your effective speed.
