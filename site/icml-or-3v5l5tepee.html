PRISM: Synergizing Vision Foundation Models via Expert Specialization

Route different image understanding tasks to specialized expert models instead of forcing one model to do everything at once.

Paper explainersAll themesComputer Vision

When you have several strong models that each understand images well, simply trying to merge them into one often backfires—each model's way of seeing gets trampled by the others. PRISM sidesteps this by letting each model become a specialist: one gets really good at texture, another at shapes, a third at depth. A router then picks the right specialist for each task, avoiding the traffic jam.

Zero assumption version

The trap of merging too many models at once

You have three really good models for understanding images. One learned to spot objects. Another learned to measure depth. A third learned to find textures. Each is excellent at its own job.

The natural idea: combine them into one model. Squash all their knowledge into a single neural network so you get one box that does all three things. In practice this fails. Each model's internal patterns clash with the others. The depth expert wants to look for smooth changes in brightness; the texture expert wants sharp edges. Training the merged model, you end up with a weakened compromise—better at nothing, worse at everything—because the learning signals for depth and texture pull on the same learned numbers in opposite directions, canceling each other out.

You naively try forcing a single model to absorb all three experts at once. The networks collide. Gradients wash each other out. The result is negative transfer—the merged model becomes dumber than any original.

In one sentence. PRISM avoids the collision by giving each expert its own slice of the feature space, then routing tasks to the right specialist.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Carve the feature space

Give each expert its own slice of the learned numbers so they cannot overwrite each other.

Step 2: Train experts to specialize

A router guides each expert to become really good at one kind of image pattern (depth, texture, objects).

Step 3: Learn the routing rules

The router builds an internal map: when I see this kind of image, call expert A; when I see that, call B.

Step 4: Compose on the fly

For a new image, the router picks the right expert or blends multiple experts in real time.

Step 5: Task-specific improvement

Because each expert stayed sharp at its job, every downstream task gets a stronger foundation.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of mashing all models together, PRISM parcels out the learned numbers (called weights, or the internal knobs of the network) so each expert has its own labeled slots. Expert A uses slots 1 through 100. Expert B uses slots 101 through 200. They stay out of each other's way.

the object it moves  =  the assignment of feature slots to experts
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: routing probability

measured quantity  =  how often each expert activates

The paper measures a quantity that does not show up in accuracy numbers: the routing probability—for each image, what fraction of computation flows to expert A, B, C, and so on. A high routing probability to one expert means that expert is specialized and critical. A low probability means it is not being used.

The paper estimates routing probability by running images through the router and counting: out of all test images, how many times does the router pick expert A? This tells you whether the specialization is working—if expert A always gets picked, it might not be specialized at all. If expert A gets picked only for depth tasks, that is the goal.

Why it works

Why partitioning experts stops the collision

The core problem in merging models is feature collision: when you stack expert A and expert B into one network, their learned patterns compete for the same learned numbers. Expert A wants weight-slot 5 to detect edges. Expert B wants weight-slot 5 to measure depth. They fight, and training spins weight-slot 5 in circles.

PRISM fixes this by physically separating the experts. Expert A gets slots 1–100; expert B gets 101–200. Now they cannot collide. During training, when the router decides expert A should handle depth, expert A's learning signal flows through slots 1–100 only. Expert B's learning signal flows through slots 101–200. Each expert becomes really good at its own job because its learning signal is not drowned out.

The router then learns: when I see an image that looks like a depth task (certain colors, gradients), I pick expert A; when I see texture, I pick B. This routing decision is learned end-to-end, so over time the router becomes a specialist itself—it learns to send the right image to the right expert.

The composition equation

First in words:

Output equals the sum over all experts of: the probability the router assigns to that expert, times that expert's answer.

Then the compact form:

output = sum_k (g_k * expert_k(x))

Every symbol:

outputthe final prediction
g_kthe routing probability—how much weight goes to expert k
expert_k(x)expert k's answer for input x
sum_kadd up all experts weighted by their routing probability
A tiny worked example
Suppose expert-A answers 85 and gets routing probability 0.6. Contribution: 0.6 * 85 = 51.
Expert-B answers 92 and gets routing probability 0.4. Contribution: 0.4 * 92 = 36.8.
Final output: 51 + 36.8 = 87.8.
Expert-A dominated because the router sent more probability to A for this image. That means the router learned A is better for this kind of image.

The routing probabilities come from the router's learned pattern-matching, so the mixture adapts per image instead of being fixed.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Router ignores an expert. If the router learns to always pick expert A and never expert B, then B's training signal vanishes. B stays random. The paper tracks this with routing probability and adds a load-balance loss to discourage it.
Experts learn the same thing anyway. If the teacher signal from the original foundation models is not clear enough, experts may still duplicate each other even in separate slots. PRISM uses a teacher-conditional router that explicitly steers each expert toward different patterns.
Too many experts with too few slots. If you have five experts competing for only fifty slots each, the slot space becomes crowded and specialization collapses. You need enough capacity for true separation.
Done right. Router maintains balanced load across experts. Each expert's slots let it specialize. The teacher signal guides each expert toward a distinct feature subspace. Result: emergent specialization with no collision.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a tiny PRISM on two simple vision tasks (e.g., CIFAR-10 for objects and a synthetic depth dataset). Train two lightweight models separately, each good at one task. Then merge them with PRISM: give each expert its own slots, and learn the router end-to-end on a mix of images from both tasks. Measure accuracy on each task before and after. Show that PRISM keeps both tasks sharp, while a naive merge weakens both.

improvement = (accuracy_after_PRISM - accuracy_before_merge) / accuracy_before_merge

The demo proves that routing to a specialist stays sharper than forcing one model to handle everything.
