MoRGen: Mixture-of-Resolutions for Medical Time Series

Medical forecast models work better when they stop forcing all predictions into one clock speed, and instead blend predictions from experts trained at the clock speeds that match each specific medical outcome.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

Hospital patient data arrives in drips and bursts—some measurements every hour, others once a day, and heart rates every few seconds. A generative forecasting model (one that predicts future measurements by imagining the next value word by word) needs to decide how many time steps to break each hour into. Pick too fine and it wastes effort on meaningless detail; too coarse and it misses the real rhythm of the illness. MoRGen solves this by running multiple expert models in parallel, each trained at a different clock speed, and blending their forecasts to match whatever rhythm the current medical task requires.

Zero assumption version

One clock speed does not fit all medical outcomes

A generative model that predicts patient measurements generates numbers one at a time: it sees past data and produces the next number, then uses that prediction to forecast the one after, step by step. Before it starts, it must choose a temporal resolution—how many time steps to break each hour into. If it chooses 60 steps per hour (one per minute), it is forced to imagine the blood-pressure value for every single minute. If it chooses 1 step per hour, it can only forecast at hourly granularity.

The problem is that different medical outcomes need different resolutions. Heart rate changes rapidly (fast dynamics), so a model trained to predict one measurement per minute makes sense. But sepsis risk (whether an infection is spreading) unfolds over hours or days (slow dynamics). The same model trained at one-per-minute becomes bogged down, wasting prediction effort on meaningless intermediate states.

A naive approach is to just pick one resolution and hope it works for all outcomes. It does not. The paper shows that accuracy crashes when the model resolution is mismatched to the outcome's true temporal dynamics.

The method in one sentence. Train separate expert models at different clock speeds and blend their forecasts for each task.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Train expert models

Run generative model training three times, each at a different clock speed (e.g., 1, 10, and 60 steps per hour).

Prepare task descriptions

For each medical outcome you want to forecast, collect examples showing when it occurred and when it did not.

Learn a mixer

Train a small network to predict how much each expert should contribute to the forecast, based on what outcome is being predicted.

Predict by blending

For a new patient, run all three experts in parallel and combine their predictions weighted by the mixer's decision.

Measure accuracy

Compare the blended forecast to ground truth. The mixture adapts automatically to the right blend per task.

What the paper changes

What the method changes

MoRGen changes how much weight each expert contributes to the forecast. Instead of one fixed generative model, it uses multiple models trained at different temporal resolutions and dynamically shifts the blend for each medical task.

the object it moves  =  expert mixture weights
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: task-resolution mismatch

measured quantity  =  temporal mismatch penalty

Before MoRGen, each outcome suffered from a temporal mismatch penalty: the accuracy loss that happens when the generative model's clock speed does not align with the outcome's true temporal dynamics. When you forecast heart rate (fast) with a model trained at the hourly clock, the model generates nonsensical intermediate states, and its predictions degrade. Conversely, forecasting slow-moving sepsis risk with a minute-by-minute clock wastes modeling capacity.

MoRGen estimates this mismatch penalty implicitly by learning how much each expert contributes to reducing prediction error. The mixture network learns that for fast outcomes, the high-resolution expert contributes more; for slow outcomes, the low-resolution expert contributes more. The paper measures this by tracking the final mixture weights per task.

Why it works

Why it works: matching clock speed to outcome rhythm

A generative model forecasting at too coarse a resolution forces the outcome to follow a discretized tick-tock that does not match reality. For heart rate, which changes every few seconds, being told to imagine only hourly values is like trying to draw a curve using only a few giant dots—you get the broad shape wrong. For sepsis, which develops over days, imagining every-minute transitions creates a problem that is too complex, requiring the model to memorize which intermediate states are physically possible.

MoRGen runs three parallel experts, each learning a different problem class: one learns short-time patterns (fast clock), one learns medium patterns, one learns long-time patterns (slow clock). When you ask for a forecast, the mixture network (a small learned function) looks at which outcome you are predicting and blends the experts. For a fast outcome, the fast expert contributes most of its prediction because its learned patterns match reality; for a slow outcome, the slow expert leads because its learned patterns avoid the memory burden.

Mathematically, this is a weighted average of three independent generative forecasts. The weights are computed by a task-specific learned network that runs once per outcome, not on every prediction. This keeps the compute cost low—you do not retrain the experts, only the tiny mixer.

The blending equation

First in words:

The final forecast is a weighted sum of three expert forecasts, where the weights depend on the task and are learned by a small neural network.

Then the compact form:

forecast_final = w_1(task) * forecast_1 + w_2(task) * forecast_2 + w_3(task) * forecast_3, where w_1 + w_2 + w_3 = 1

Every symbol:

forecast_1prediction from the slow expert (1 step per hour)
forecast_2prediction from the medium expert (10 steps per hour)
forecast_3prediction from the fast expert (60 steps per hour)
w_1(task)learned weight for the slow expert, specific to this outcome
w_2(task)learned weight for the medium expert, specific to this outcome
w_3(task)learned weight for the fast expert, specific to this outcome
A tiny worked example
Heart-rate outcome: Expert 1 predicts 72 bpm, Expert 2 predicts 73 bpm, Expert 3 predicts 71 bpm.
Mixer learns: w_1=0.1 (slow expert, low weight), w_2=0.3, w_3=0.6 (fast expert, high weight).
Final forecast: 0.1 * 72 + 0.3 * 73 + 0.6 * 71 = 7.2 + 21.9 + 42.6 = 71.7 bpm.
Sepsis outcome: Expert 1 predicts 8.5%, Expert 2 predicts 7.2%, Expert 3 predicts 6.1%.
Mixer learns: w_1=0.7 (slow expert, high weight), w_2=0.2, w_3=0.1 (fast expert, low weight).
Final forecast: 0.7 * 8.5 + 0.2 * 7.2 + 0.1 * 6.1 = 5.95 + 1.44 + 0.61 = 8.0%.

The same three expert predictions are blended differently for each outcome because the mixer weights are task-specific, not global.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Not enough data to learn the mixer If you only have a few examples of an outcome, the mixture weights are poorly estimated, and the blended forecast becomes noisy. The mixer is small but still needs enough task-specific training data to learn reliable weights.
Three experts are not enough Some outcomes might have temporal dynamics that fall between the chosen resolutions (e.g., changing every 15 minutes, but experts are at 1, 10, and 60). A mismatch between the available experts and the outcome can still degrade accuracy.
Computational cost of three models Running three independent generative models in parallel costs roughly three times as long to generate predictions as running one. For real-time clinical use, this may be too slow, and you would need to pre-compute all forecasts offline.
Mixer solves the real problem When the outcomes' temporal dynamics are truly diverse and each expert is trained sufficiently, the mixture learns to assign high weight to experts that match the outcome's rhythm. The paper shows this works consistently across sepsis, glucose, lactate, and heart-rate forecasting on real clinical datasets.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small synthetic dataset of patients with two outcome types: one that changes rapidly (e.g., blood-oxygen saturation, which responds within minutes to breathing changes) and one that changes slowly (e.g., kidney-function decline, which unfolds over days). Train three generative experts at three temporal resolutions. Learn the mixture weights for each outcome type. Forecast the next 24 hours for held-out patients and measure prediction accuracy using a standard metric that compares forecasts to ground truth (like the area under a true-positive-vs-false-positive curve). Show that the mixture-based forecast beats any single fixed-resolution expert on both outcome types.

improvement = (baseline_error - mixture_error) / baseline_error * 100 percent

The demo must show that one expert excels at the fast outcome and fails at the slow one, another expert reverses this, and the mixture wins at both.
