OmniVinci: Omni-Modal Understanding LLM

OmniVinci aligns vision and audio embeddings in a shared space so the model can reason across sight and sound together, and encodes time so it knows when things happen.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

Humans understand the world by seeing, hearing, and watching how sight and sound fit together in time. Existing models treat each sense separately, missing the connections. OmniVinci brings vision and audio into one shared embedding space (a list of numbers that represents both), lines them up by their timing, and teaches the model to reason across all senses at once.

Zero assumption version

Why separate senses break understanding

When you watch a video, sight and sound arrive together and tell a unified story. A dog barks and jumps at the same moment. If a model sees the jump but hears the bark a split second late, it is confused about what is cause and effect.

Existing omni-modal models treat vision (images and video frames) and audio (sound and speech) as isolated streams. They encode them separately into lists of numbers, and the model never learns that the bark and the jump are the same event happening in the same place at the same moment. It misses the bridge.

A naive approach tries to just concatenate all the numbers from both senses and train the LLM. But this doesn't work because the embeddings (the lists of numbers) speak different languages. Vision embeddings were learned to describe pixels; audio embeddings were learned to describe frequencies. They do not naturally align.

In one sentence. OmniVinci builds a single shared embedding space and teaches vision and audio embeddings to point to the same concepts, so the model can reason across both, because when embeddings live in the same coordinate system, the model can compare them directly and spot when a bark and a jump are the same event.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Separate encoding

Video frames go through a vision encoder, audio goes through an audio encoder, each produces a list of numbers.

Alignment via contrastive learning

OmniAlignNet uses a similarity game: correct vision-audio pairs get pulled close, wrong pairs pushed apart, until both types live in the same space.

Temporal grouping

Embeddings from the same moment in time are grouped together in sequence, because when the model sees that frame N and audio chunk N are consecutive, it learns to treat them as a single event.

Absolute time encoding

Constrained Rotary Time Embedding rotates each embedding by an amount based on its absolute timestamp, because rotations in embedding space encode distance, so a larger rotation means more elapsed time, and the model learns to use this signal.

Joint training

The LLM learns to read this unified, time-stamped embedding space to answer questions about vision, audio, and their relationship.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

OmniVinci changes the embeddings (the lists of numbers that represent the input). Instead of separate vision and audio embeddings that live in different coordinate systems, both are rotated and repositioned into one shared space, and each one gets a timestamp attached.

the object it moves  =  embeddings into one aligned, time-tagged space
The hidden quantity

The alignment score: how much do vision and audio point to the same thing?

measured quantity  =  Cross-modal alignment strength

The paper measures how well vision and audio embeddings point to the same concepts. This is not accuracy on a task; it is a proxy for how tightly the two modalities are bound together.

OmniAlignNet estimates this using contrastive learning (a similarity game). For each video-audio pair, it computes how close the vision embedding is to the audio embedding in the shared space, versus how close it is to audio from a different video. If correct pairs are much closer than wrong pairs, alignment is strong.

Why it works

Why it works: three ideas that teach the model to bind senses

The mechanism has three parts working in concert. First, OmniAlignNet uses contrastive learning to teach vision and audio encoders to emit embeddings that point to the same concepts. This works because when two encoders try to predict each other, they must learn what both modalities have in common. A bark is a bark whether you see lips moving or hear the sound; the embedding learns to encode the bark itself, not the specific pixel or frequency.

Second, Temporal Embedding Grouping preserves the relative order of events. By keeping embeddings in sequence and allowing the model to compare each embedding to all others that come before and after, the model learns that a wag follows a bark because it sees them in that order in the embedding stream. This is free information from the data; the model just has to not scramble the sequence.

Third, Constrained Rotary Time Embedding adds absolute time. Instead of just knowing that event B comes after event A, the model can count how long the gap is. A rotation applied to each embedding (proportional to its timestamp) encodes absolute time, like a clock hand pointing to the exact moment. This helps the model learn that if audio pauses for 2 seconds, something important may have changed in the video, because gaps in audio often signal scene changes or moments when something shifts focus, and the visual stream reflects those changes.

The alignment equation

First in words:

The OmniAlignNet alignment loss pulls matching vision-audio pairs together and pushes mismatched pairs apart. The similarity between a vision embedding and an audio embedding is computed, and the loss penalizes when the correct pair ranks lower than wrong pairs.

Then the compact form:

L_align = (1/2) * (L_v->a + L_a->v), where L_v->a = cross-entropy over (sim_matrix_V_to_A), L_a->v = cross-entropy over (sim_matrix_A_to_V)

Every symbol:

L_alignAverage alignment loss (lower is better)
L_v->aVision-to-audio directional cross-entropy loss
L_a->vAudio-to-vision directional cross-entropy loss
sim_matrix_V_to_AMatrix of similarity scores between each vision and each audio embedding
cross-entropyPenalty that encourages correct pairs to have high scores
A tiny worked example
Batch of 4 videos, each with paired audio.
Video 1 embedding similarity to Audio 1 (correct): 0.92; to Audio 2,3,4 (wrong): 0.15, 0.18, 0.12
The loss penalizes when wrong pairs rank high and rewards when the correct pair ranks highest.
L_v->a for this video: [0.92, 0.15, 0.18, 0.12] concentrates the penalty on the three wrong predictions.
Same computation in reverse (L_a->v): Audio 1 embedding matched against all video embeddings.
Average of both losses: (L_v->a + L_a->v) / 2 guides the encoder update.
After many batches, vision embeddings drift closer to audio embeddings for matched content, farther for mismatches.

The symmetric design (bidirectional loss) ensures both encoders pull equally; neither one is passive.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Temporal mismatch. If video frames and audio are not properly synchronized when the contrastive pairs are created, the alignment will learn bad associations. A bark on frame 10 must match audio at the same real time, not audio 5 frames ahead.
Modality imbalance in training data. If training data has many more videos than audio clips, or vice versa, the alignment will bias toward whichever modality has more examples. The contrastive loss then overfits to one modality's encoding choices.
Timestamp granularity too coarse. If the Constrained Rotary Time Embedding has a maximum time horizon that is too large, nearby events become indistinguishable in time. The model cannot learn that a 0.5 second gap matters differently than a 2 second gap.
Done right. Carefully curated paired vision-audio data, balanced across modality types, with accurate timestamps and reasonable time granularity, ensures the alignment learns true cross-modal concepts.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small multi-modal dataset: 10 short video clips with synchronized audio and text descriptions. Examples: a person speaking while gesturing, a dog barking while jumping, music playing while objects move. Train OmniVinci (or a toy version) on modality-specific data for 100 steps, then joint training for 200 steps.

At the end, measure: (1) whether cross-modal retrieval works (give the model a sound, can it pick the right video frame?), because if alignment works, the model has learned to map both modalities to the same space; (2) whether the alignment score (similarity in the shared space) is higher for matched pairs than mismatched pairs, because this directly measures the contrastive learning objective; (3) whether the model's reasoning about what happened when is more accurate than a model without time encoding, because rotary embeddings encode temporal distance and the model should use it to reason about causality.

Cross_modal_accuracy = (videos_correctly_matched_to_audio) / (total_videos); Alignment_gap = (sim_correct_pairs) - (sim_wrong_pairs)

The demo must show that a question like 'Why did the dog jump?' now draws on both the sight (frame) and sound (bark) together, and assigns them to the right moment in time.
