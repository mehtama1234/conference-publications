Beyond Binary Rewards: Training LMs to Reason About Their Uncertainty

Train reasoning models with rewards that chase both correct answers AND confident guesses, so they stop hallucinating.

Paper explainersAll themesLLMs & Foundation Models

Language models trained with standard reinforcement learning learn to reason better and get more questions right. But they develop a blind spot: they become overconfident, spitting out wrong answers without hesitation. This paper shows how to fix that by changing the reward signal itself. Instead of just rewarding right answers, reward right answers that come with appropriate confidence, measured by a standard confidence score (called a Brier score). Models trained this way stay accurate while becoming genuinely calibrated—they learn to say 'I'm not sure' when they should.

Zero assumption version

Why do reasoning models get overconfident?

When you train a model with reinforcement learning using simple rewards ('right or wrong, 1 or 0'), the model learns to maximize that score. It learns to say the right answer. But the reward signal says nothing about how confident to be. A model trained this way can answer a hard question correctly one day and confidently hallucinate (make up a plausible-sounding but false answer) the next day, because there's no penalty for guessing when you're unsure.

The naive fix would be: add a confidence classifier after the model finishes, a separate post-hoc tuner that tries to guess how confident the model should sound. But that's like a student who memorizes answers perfectly then tries to figure out which answers to fake confidence about. The confidence is bolted on, disconnected from the real reasoning.

The problem. RL training for reasoning optimizes only correctness, ignoring confidence, so models become overconfident and hallucinate.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The paper changes the reward function from a single binary score to a combined score that rewards both correctness and calibration. The model still generates reasoning chains, outputs, and confidence estimates—the architecture doesn't change. Only what we measure and reward changes.

the object it moves  =  reward function
The hidden quantity

Calibration: the mismatch signal

measured quantity  =  calibration error (squared gap between confidence and correctness)

Calibration is a simple idea: if the model says it's 70 percent confident, it should be right about 70 percent of the time on those statements. If it says 99 percent confident but is only right 50 percent of the time, it's badly calibrated (overconfident). The Brier score measures this by taking the difference between stated confidence and actual correctness, squaring it, and averaging over many examples.

The paper computes this Brier score during training and includes it in the reward. A model that says it's 90 percent sure about a wrong answer gets heavily penalized. One that says 55 percent sure about an answer it gets right also gets penalized, but much less. Over many training steps, the model learns to output confidence numbers that actually match reality.

Why it works

Why mixing correctness and calibration works

A proper scoring rule like Brier has a mathematical property: if a model learns to minimize it, the model must output confidences that match reality. There is no shortcut—the model cannot cheat by always guessing 0.5 or always saying 99 percent. The only way to lower the Brier score is to output confidence numbers that honestly reflect how often the model is correct.

During training, the RL algorithm (GRPO, a method that adjusts the model's outputs to maximize reward) uses this combined reward to nudge the model. When the model gets an answer right and outputs high confidence, the training adjustment says 'good, do more of this.' When it gets an answer wrong and outputs high confidence, the adjustment says 'bad, fix this—either get it right or admit uncertainty.' Over thousands of training steps, the model learns to route its confidence through the same reasoning process that produced the answer. The reasoning and the confidence become coupled, not bolted on.

Math after the picture

The combined reward equation

First in words:

Reward equals 1 if the answer is correct else 0, minus a penalty strength times the Brier score (squared gap between stated confidence and actual correctness)

Then the compact form:

reward = correctness - lambda * (confidence - correctness)^2

Every symbol:

correctness1 if the answer is correct, 0 if wrong
confidencemodel's stated probability from 0 to 1
lambdapenalty strength: how much to punish miscalibration
(confidence - correctness)^2squared gap: Brier score
A tiny worked example
Scenario A: Model answers correctly and says 90% confident. Correctness = 1, confidence = 0.9. Gap = (0.9 - 1)^2 = 0.01. With lambda = 1, reward = 1 - 1*0.01 = 0.99.
Scenario B: Model answers incorrectly but says 80% confident. Correctness = 0, confidence = 0.8. Gap = (0.8 - 0)^2 = 0.64. With lambda = 1, reward = 0 - 1*0.64 = -0.64.
Scenario C: Model answers correctly but says 20% confident. Correctness = 1, confidence = 0.2. Gap = (0.2 - 1)^2 = 0.64. With lambda = 1, reward = 1 - 1*0.64 = 0.36.
The key: A and C both sacrifice points for miscalibration, but A (right + high confidence) loses less than B (wrong + high confidence). The model learns the optimal tradeoff: get it right AND say so.

The formula enforces an honest trade-off: accuracy alone gives a higher reward than either high confidence without correctness or low confidence with correctness. Over training, the model learns that the safest path is to align confidence with its actual reasoning quality.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If lambda is too high, model learns to be uncertain. Calibration improves but accuracy drops—the model trades correctness for the safety of low confidence. Tuning lambda balances the two.
If the training data is mislabeled, confidence becomes meaningless. The model learns to match its confidence to corrupted labels. In that regime, Brier score rewards false confidence on wrong answers.
Confidence on very different data may still be miscalibrated. If test questions are very different from training, the model's learned confidence estimates may not hold up. The paper shows out-of-domain improvement, but extreme shifts remain a risk.
Done right. Tune lambda on a validation set, clean your labels, and test on data similar enough to training that reasoning skills transfer. Confidence will track accuracy in the domain where the model was trained.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small reasoning model trained on a dataset like GSM8K or HotpotQA. Split your data into train and test. Train one copy with ordinary binary-reward RL and another with RLCR (binary plus Brier). For each model, compute: (1) accuracy (fraction of questions answered correctly), (2) average confidence on correct vs incorrect answers, and (3) Brier score (mean squared gap across all test examples). The visualization should show: (a) a chart of accuracy for both models (they stay close), (b) a chart of calibration on test (RLCR's confidence and correctness track each other; ordinary RL's don't), and (c) a few example questions with side-by-side confidences from each model, highlighting cases where RLCR says low confidence on a hard question while ordinary RL confidently hallucinated.
