Moving Out: Physically-grounded Human-AI Collaboration

Robots learn to collaborate with humans by generating many different behavior styles, simulating what each will cause, and picking the one that works best.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Robots often need to work alongside humans in the real world—moving furniture, coordinating around tight corners, adapting to how a person naturally moves. Most training methods ignore physics, giving agents no way to predict what their actions will actually cause when they run into real-world constraints. This paper introduces Moving Out, a benchmark with real humans and physics, and BASS, a method that teaches agents to collaborate by trying many different behavior patterns, simulating each one's outcome, and choosing the move most likely to succeed with that particular human partner.

Zero assumption version

Why robots struggle to work alongside humans

Most robot-training benchmarks are games—pick an action, get a reward, try again. Real human-robot tasks are different: a robot and a person have to coordinate their movements while handling physical constraints—a heavy sofa gets stuck on corners, bodies collide if timing is off, and the human partner keeps changing strategies. A robot trained on game-like tasks has no way to know what a real action will do. It has never simulated the outcome of pushing at different angles or speeds. It has never learned that some humans prefer to lead and others prefer to follow.

A beginner might try training a robot on human videos alone, hoping it learns by imitation. But videos do not show the physics—which movements actually work, which do not, and why. A robot watching humans move furniture has no way to know that a certain angle fails because the sofa hits the doorway, or that coordinating the lift-height matters because one person is shorter. The physics stays hidden.

The gap. Robots need to learn physics-aware collaboration—how their actions will actually play out when two bodies work together under real constraints—not just copy human videos.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Create a repertoire

The agent generates many different behavior styles using data augmentation, not just the optimal one.

Simulate forward

Each style is played out in a physics simulator to see which actions lead to success or failure.

Score by outcome

Behaviors are ranked by how likely they succeed: did the sofa reach the target without jamming?

Select best for this human

When deployed, the agent picks the highest-scoring behavior that matches how its human partner is currently moving.

Handle surprises

If the human partner suddenly changes strategy, the agent switches to a different pre-tested behavior instead of freezing.

What the paper changes

What changes

Instead of training one single best policy (a fixed decision rule), the agent maintains a collection of many different policies. Each one embodies a different collaboration strategy. The paper's key move is to not discard the weaker strategies—keep them, simulate them, and use them as backup plans when the primary strategy fails.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Simulation-predicted success

measured quantity  =  Success likelihood under physics

A robot working with humans cannot know in advance whether a move will work—whether the sofa will jam, whether the timing will cause a collision, whether the human partner will feel comfortable with the speed. Normally, the agent learns this only through trial and error on the real humans. BASS introduces a hidden quantity the agent can measure without trying: how likely is a behavior to succeed when simulated in physics, given what the human partner is currently doing?

The paper estimates this by running each behavior through a physics simulator paired with a model of human motion. For example: simulate the robot pushing at angle A while the human lifts at angle B—does the sofa clear the doorway? Does anyone collide? This simulation happens in microseconds, allowing the agent to score dozens of behavior candidates before picking one.

Why it works

Why simulation + selection works

Here is why this method succeeds where simple imitation or one-policy training fails: A single behavior learned from averaged human data is a compromise—it works okay with most people but surprises nobody and adapts to nobody. A robot trained on average human motion will push at average speed and angle, which is often suboptimal. But humans vary. Some people lead, some follow. Some are strong, some weak. Some move cautiously, others aggressively. A single policy cannot excel at all variations.

BASS solves this by first expanding the behavior space through augmentation—creating versions that are bolder, more cautious, more leading, more following—without throwing away the weak ones. This is the opposite of typical training, which discards unsuccessful attempts. Then, instead of picking one randomly, the paper uses physics simulation to filter: Which of these behaviors will actually work given the current human's motion? A behavior that would fail physically (get jammed, cause collision) is ranked low. A behavior that physics predicts will succeed is ranked high. On deployment, the agent simply picks the top-ranked behavior.

This works because physics simulation is fast and accurate—it reveals what videos and imitation cannot: the actual outcome of actions. The agent does not need to learn from reward signals; the simulator is the oracle. And because the agent chose many behaviors upfront, when a human partner changes strategy mid-task, the agent has a pre-tested backup behavior already scored and ready to go. It does not need to retrain or freeze—it just switches.

The selection equation

First in words:

For each candidate behavior, compute how likely it succeeds by running it through physics paired with the human motion model, then pick the behavior with the highest success score.

Then the compact form:

b_selected = argmax_b Success(simulate(b, human_motion_now))

Every symbol:

bone of the pre-generated behaviors in the repertoire
simulate(b, human_motion_now)run behavior b in a physics engine paired with predicted human motion; returns a trajectory
Success(...)a score measuring whether the trajectory achieves the goal without collision or jamming (e.g., sofa position within target, no stuck events)
argmax_bpick the behavior b that makes Success highest
A tiny worked example
Behavior 1 (cautious): Simulate paired with this human -> sofa reaches target, no collision -> Success = 1.0
Behavior 2 (aggressive): Simulate paired with this human -> sofa hits doorway jamb -> Success = 0.2
Behavior 3 (follow-only): Simulate paired with this human -> sofa reaches target, no collision -> Success = 0.95
Behavior 4 (lead-only): Simulate paired with this human -> collision mid-task -> Success = 0.0
Best is Behavior 1 with Success = 1.0. Robot executes Behavior 1.

The robot does not solve an equation during training; instead, for each moment, it simulates all behaviors, scores each by predicted success, and picks the winner.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Simulator does not match reality. If the physics engine is inaccurate—friction coefficients wrong, collision detection miscalibrated, or human motion model too simple—behaviors will be ranked wrong. A behavior rated high by simulation might fail on real humans. The method is only as good as the simulator.
Augmentation creates impossible behaviors. If the behavior augmentation step generates styles that are physically unrealistic (e.g., a human lifting faster than humanly possible), the simulation will score them as if they were real, wasting computation and causing rank errors, because the simulator has no way to flag them as impossible—it just runs the physics. The augmented behaviors must stay within plausible human motion.
New task defeats pre-training. BASS works well if the new task (human partner, environment) is similar to the simulation training scenarios. If the task is completely different—new object shape, new environment layout, new human height or strength profile—the pre-generated behaviors and their simulated ranks may all be wrong, and the method has no way to adapt except to retrain.
Done right. When the simulator is tuned well, augmentation is realistic, and the new task is within the training domain, BASS delivers immediate adaptation to new human partners and unforeseen changes by selecting the best pre-tested behavior without retraining.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A convincing demo would pick one collaborative task—say, moving a heavy box around a corner in a simulated room—and show that BASS adapts faster than a baseline, because BASS has pre-scored multiple behaviors while a baseline must learn from scratch. The demo should include at least three human-partner profiles: a strong partner who prefers leading, a weak partner who prefers following, and a neutral partner. For each, the demo runs BASS and a baseline (e.g., a single fixed policy, or pure imitation). Measure success: did the box reach the goal without jamming or collision? How many of the partner's unexpected direction-changes did the agent handle without pausing?

Adaptation speed = steps_until_first_success_baseline - steps_until_first_success_BASS

The demo must make it impossible to miss that BASS picks different behaviors for different human partners, and switches behaviors mid-task without freezing, while a baseline learns once and fails to adapt.
