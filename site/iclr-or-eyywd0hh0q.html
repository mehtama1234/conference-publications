DeepPrim: Physics-Driven 3D Weather Forecasting

A weather forecaster that learns the actual physics equations governing the atmosphere instead of just memorizing patterns.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

Weather forecasting is hard because the atmosphere is complicated — countless gases, temperature, moisture, and wind all interacting. Traditional forecasts use simplified physics (numerical weather prediction, or NWP) that skip hard details to run fast, while modern AI models usually ignore the physics entirely and just learn from data. DeepPrim does something different: it teaches a neural network to actually learn and solve the primitive equations — the core physics that describes how air moves and weather forms — instead of memorizing patterns or faking physics.

Why old forecasts miss the weather

Weather happens because gas, heat, and water vapor follow the laws of physics. But solving those laws perfectly is too slow — checking every tiny patch of air every second would take weeks to forecast tomorrow. So meteorologists use a shortcut: they solve the simplified laws on a coarse grid (maybe one square kilometer per point) and skip hard details they assume don't matter much. This works okay, but it misses storms, shifts rain timing, and fails when conditions are weird.

A beginner might try: 'Just train a neural network on past weather and recent conditions — it will learn the real patterns.' But that pure learning approach has a problem — the network learns surface correlations (when humidity is high, rain often follows) but does not learn the actual mechanism (air rises, cools, water condenses). When conditions are new or extreme, the network guesses wrong because it never learned the rules, just the most common stories.

So the real problem is: we can either use simplified physics (fast but incomplete) or pure pattern learning (flexible but unreliable), but we cannot easily have both.

In one sentence. DeepPrim teaches a neural network to learn and solve the actual physical equations that govern how the atmosphere moves.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Take recent weather conditions

Temperature, wind, humidity at many heights above ground.

Run the network

It learns to compute the primitive equations: how air accelerates, how temperatures change, how moisture moves.

Output the change

Not a direct forecast, but the rate of change (how much wind will shift, how much temperature will rise, how humidity evolves).

Step the forecast forward

Add those changes to the current state to get the next hour or day.

Repeat

Run the network again on the new state to forecast the next step.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

Instead of having the network directly guess 'what will the weather be in 6 hours', DeepPrim makes it learn to compute the rates of change — how much each quantity (wind, temperature, humidity) shifts each hour — by solving the actual differential equations that physicists write down. The network becomes an equation solver, not a pattern memorizer, because solving equations is more reliable when conditions are new.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: physical consistency

measured quantity  =  How well the learned equations conserve physics

The paper measures whether the network's learned equations obey conservation laws — rules that say certain quantities (like total mass, total energy) cannot be created or destroyed. For example, if air rises in one place, it must descend elsewhere; if the atmosphere heats up in one region, it must cool elsewhere. A pure AI model can violate these rules everywhere; the physics-trained network respects them.

The paper estimates this by checking: after the network predicts, do the conservation laws still hold? Do the predicted wind patterns conserve mass? Do the energy equations balance? When they do, the forecast stays realistic even far into the future.

Why it works

Why it works: teaching physics beats pure pattern learning

A pure neural network trained only on 'input conditions -> output weather' learns to recognize common patterns, but it has no reason to respect physical laws. It might predict wind shifts that violate conservation of mass or temperature changes that break energy balance. In rare or extreme situations where the training examples do not cover the case, the network fails catastrophically because it never learned the actual rules.

DeepPrim embeds the primitive equations into the network's structure. Each layer of the network performs a piece of the physics calculation — computing how pressure changes drive wind, how wind advects temperature, how water vapor condenses. Now the network cannot output nonsensical predictions because its own design forbids it. When conditions change dramatically, the network still respects the laws, so its forecast stays plausible.

The mechanism is this: by teaching the network to solve equations instead of memorize examples, the network learns the deep structure of weather dynamics. It can extrapolate to new situations because it learned the rules, not just the patterns. Physics acts like a strong constraint that forces the network to learn only what is real and necessary, filtering out spurious correlations that look good in data but do not exist in nature.

The core equation: solving primitive equations

First in words:

The rate of change of wind equals the pressure gradient force plus the Coriolis force; the rate of change of temperature equals the heat from rising air and solar input; the rate of change of humidity equals the transport of water vapor minus losses to condensation. The network learns the coefficients and feedbacks in these three coupled equations.

Then the compact form:

du/dt = -(1/rho) * dP/dx + f*v + F_wind; dT/dt = -u*dT/dx - (omega/Cp)*dT/dp + Q; dq/dt = -u*dq/dx + E - C

Every symbol:

uwind speed in the x direction (east-west)
du/dthow fast the wind changes each hour
rhoair density (how tightly packed the air is)
Patmospheric pressure
dP/dxhow pressure changes over distance (the pressure gradient)
fCoriolis parameter (Earth's rotation effect)
vwind speed in the y direction (north-south)
F_windfrictional drag the network learns
Tair temperature
dT/dthow fast temperature changes each hour
omegavertical pressure velocity (how fast air rises or falls)
Cpheat capacity of air at constant pressure
Qheating rate from solar radiation
qwater vapor mixing ratio (how much water is in the air)
dq/dthow fast humidity changes
Eevaporation the network learns
Ccondensation that forms clouds and rain
A tiny worked example: how wind changes in one place
Suppose pressure drops 1 unit over 100 km distance to the east, air density is 1.2 kg/m^3, and the Coriolis factor (Earth's rotation effect) is f = 0.0001.
Pressure gradient force: dP/dx = 1/100 = 0.01 units/km; (1/rho)*dP/dx = 0.01/1.2 = 0.0083 m/s per hour.
If no other forces, wind accelerates eastward by 0.0083 m/s each hour.
But Coriolis force also acts (Earth rotation turns moving objects): f*v term adds 0.0001 * (current northwind speed).
The network learns both terms and combines them to predict how the wind field evolves realistically over 24 hours.

The network does not just memorize these equations; it learns to compute them faithfully given data, so new conditions still produce physically consistent forecasts.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

If the training data is biased (missing rare storms or very cold days). The network learns correct equations only for the situations it saw. A heatwave or freak snowstorm outside the training envelope will still baffle it, because the equations are learned from data.
If the network is too small to express the full equations. The primitive equations are three-dimensional, coupled, and nonlinear. If the network is not deep or wide enough (not enough neuron layers or neurons per layer), it cannot learn all the feedback loops, and the forecast drifts off reality fast.
If the pressure coordinate system is wrong. The network is trained to solve equations in pressure coordinates (a vertical grid based on air pressure, not altitude). If the pressure coordinate is not set up right, the network's equations do not match reality.
Done right. If the network is large enough, trained on diverse weather (common and extreme), and the pressure coordinates are set correctly, the learned equations become trustworthy even on new conditions — because it learned the laws, not just the patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A good demo would train DeepPrim on historical weather data (say, 10 years of global observations), then test it on a year it never saw. Compare: (1) the traditional simplified NWP forecast (from official weather services), (2) a pure neural network baseline (trained on the same data but with no physics constraints), and (3) DeepPrim. Measure accuracy as mean absolute error in temperature, wind speed, and precipitation at each forecast lead time (6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, 3 days, 5 days). The point is to show that DeepPrim stays accurate longer than the baseline, especially on extreme events (storms, heat waves) that the pure neural network got wrong.

accuracy = 100 * (1 - mean_absolute_error / typical_weather_variance); for each lead time, compare traditional NWP vs. pure NN baseline vs. DeepPrim

The demo should make it impossible to miss this: DeepPrim forecasts stay physically plausible and accurate even far into the future and even for rare weather events, while a pure neural network drifts off into nonsense because it never learned the actual rules.
