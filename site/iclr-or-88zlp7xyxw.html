Seeing Through the Brain: PRISM

Decode what a brain sees by translating brain signals into text descriptions, then composing objects to reconstruct the image.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When neuroscientists measure brain activity (called fMRI), they see electrical signals but no pictures. Researchers want to know: could we reverse-engineer the image the brain is looking at? The challenge is bridging brain signals to images. This paper discovers that brain signals look more like language patterns than pure image patterns. PRISM uses that insight: it reads the brain as if it were narrating (turning signals into text descriptions), then builds images from those descriptions, object by object.

Zero assumption version

Brain signals are not images

fMRI records brain activity as numbers (activation levels in thousands of locations). To reconstruct the seen image, you need a bridge between those brain numbers and pixels. A naive guess: use a pure image model (a network trained to understand visual patterns). But brain signals turn out to be organized differently than images—they emphasize what objects are and their properties, not the low-level patterns of light and shadow.

The real problem: which middle language should translate brain signals to images? Should it be image patterns? Or something else? Nobody knew. And even if you pick the middle language, how do you make sure the generative model captures what the brain cares about—objects, their colors, their relationships—rather than trying to match pixels exactly?

The core move. Map brain signals into text descriptions (what objects are there, what they look like, how they relate), then use those descriptions to steer a diffusion model to compose images object by object.

What it actually does

Extract text labels from images

Use a vision-language model to list each object, its colors, size, relationships; learn optimal prompts.

Train an encoder on brain-to-text mapping

Feed brain signals through neural network (MLP); teach it to predict those text descriptions.

Fine-tune language model on the text

Adjust a pre-trained language model so it encodes brain-aligned descriptions better.

At test time: brain signals become text

Run new brain signals through trained encoder; predict what objects and attributes are being seen.

Diffusion model generates image from text

Feed text to diffusion model; it composes each object independently in space, avoiding blur.

What the paper changes

What the paper changed

The paper changed the intermediate representation—the middle language that bridges brain signals to images. Instead of going brain to image features to pixels, it goes brain to text descriptions to pixels composed from objects.

the object it moves  =  Intermediate representation from image space to text space
The hidden quantity

Brain-to-space alignment

measured quantity  =  Alignment similarity (text vs image vs joint space)

The hidden quantity is how well the brain's neural code matches the structure of different middle languages. Imagine brain signals as patterns made by different brain areas firing at different strengths. Text embeddings (lists of numbers that represent words), image embeddings (lists of numbers that represent visual features), and joint embeddings are organized differently. The closer the brain's firing pattern matches one of those organization schemes, the easier it is to train a network to translate brain signals into that space. The paper measures this alignment using a technique called canonical correlation analysis (CKA)—a test that asks: do two sets of number-patterns have similar structure?

The key finding: brain signals have structure much closer to language model text embeddings than to vision model embeddings. This matters because if the structures are close, a simple MLP (a small neural network with just a few layers) can learn the translation easily. If they are far apart, translation is harder.

Why it works

Why text descriptions work

The mechanism has two parts. First, text descriptions capture what the brain actually encodes. The brain does not store pixel values; it stores interpretations—object identity, color, pose, relationships. Text is the natural language for these interpretations. When you ask an image a question, the brain uses stored object knowledge to answer. Text descriptions directly encode that knowledge.

Second, object-centric diffusion breaks image generation into smaller, independent pieces. Instead of the diffusion model trying to paint the whole image at once (which often blurs or distorts objects because the model must balance many constraints simultaneously), it generates each object from its own description, then spatially composes them. This works because the generation model can focus deeply on one object's description at a time—all the transformer layers (the neural network that reads the description) work on just that one object—rather than splitting resources across all objects at once. The result: cleaner objects, fewer detection errors.

Together: text captures brain intent, and object-centric composition preserves that intent into pixels.

The training loss

First in words:

Minimize text-description loss plus image reconstruction loss: loss = description_error + lambda times diffusion_reconstruction_error

Then the compact form:

L = L_desc(encoder(fMRI), text_labels) + lambda * L_diff(diffusion(predicted_text), ground_truth_image)

Every symbol:

L_desccross-entropy loss between predicted and true text tokens

A tiny worked example
Training sample: brain signals for an image of a red ball on grass.
Ground-truth text: 'red ball, sphere, sitting on green grass, centered'.
Encoder predicts: 'red ball, sphere, on green grass' (missing 'centered').
Text loss L_desc: penalizes missing the word 'centered'; say 0.3.
Diffusion model generates from predicted text: ball is there, grass is there, but placement is slightly off.
Perceptual loss L_diff (measuring image difference): 0.15.
Total loss: 0.3 + 1.0 * 0.15 = 0.45 (if lambda = 1.0).
Training algorithm adjusts encoder weights to predict 'centered' next time, reducing total loss.

Text loss teaches the encoder what objects are there; diffusion loss teaches it to predict descriptions that lead to good images. Together they align brain signals with image content.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Text description generator misses real objects in the image. If the vision-language model does not extract a key object (e.g., a person in shadow), the encoder never learns to predict it from brain signals. The diffusion model cannot generate what was never in the text description. Result: reconstructed image is missing objects.
Brain signals for abstract or spatial relationships are weak. Some brains emphasize what things are; others emphasize where they are. If the training data is biased, the encoder learns object labels well but spatial descriptions poorly. A dog-centered image reconstructs well, but swapping left and right might not trigger an error.
Diffusion model bias toward frequent attributes. If most training images show red balls, the diffusion model learns to default to red when the text description is unclear or contradictory. Uncommon colors or rarely-seen objects may be missed or replaced with the most common version the model has learned.
Done right: multi-modal labeling + fine-tuned diffusion. Use multiple vision-language models to cross-validate object extraction, include spatial layout in text descriptions (left, right, foreground, background), and fine-tune the diffusion model on brain-aligned data.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

A demo should show: (1) collect fMRI signals while a person views 20 different images (mix of objects, colors, spatial arrangements); (2) train an encoder on 15 images to predict text from brain signals, and test on 5 held-out images; (3) at test time, take held-out brain signals, predict text descriptions, feed to a pre-trained diffusion model, and generate images; (4) compare reconstructions to ground truth by visual inspection and a perceptual loss metric (LPIPS); (5) show that adding object-centric composition reduces object blur vs baseline diffusion.

perceptual_loss = LPIPS(reconstructed_image, ground_truth_image); object_blur_score = average_edge_variance_inside_objects (higher means clearer)

The demo must make impossible to miss: text-guided reconstruction preserves object identity that direct image prediction loses; and object-level composition gives sharper, more correct reconstructions than whole-image diffusion.
