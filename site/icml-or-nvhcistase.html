Learning to Reconfigure: Configuration-Control Co-optimization

A robot learns to physically reshape itself mid-task, dynamically switching body configurations so one machine works on sand, water, and air.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Traditionally, robots are built for one job with one fixed shape. This paper shows that a single reconfigurable robot—one that can physically switch between different body shapes—can learn to reshape itself during a task and adapt to wildly different terrains. A scheduler (high-level decision maker) watches progress on the current job and decides when to trigger a reconfiguration; specialized controllers (low-level motor commands) handle each shape. The result: one robot that walks on sand, flies through air, and swims in water, with no human telling it which shape to use when.

Zero assumption version

One robot, one shape, one terrain

Building a robot that works everywhere requires hard trade-offs. A robot with long legs walks well on sand but tumbles on water. One with wings and a body flies through air but crashes on rocky ground. Today's co-design methods (mathematical recipes that pick a robot shape) find one middle-ground design that works okay everywhere but excels nowhere.

Humans have workarounds: we wear boots on mountains and swim in water with fins. Nature does the same—animals change their posture for different surfaces. But robots that can physically reconfigure (change their body mid-motion) exist only if humans hand-code which shape goes with which terrain. There is no automatic way to learn that connection.

The gap: Fixed-shape robots are stuck; human-programmed reconfigurable robots do not learn; and today's co-design mathematics cannot adapt shapes during a task.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Setup: multi-tail training

Build separate low-level controllers: one optimized for legs, one for wings, one for fins. Each learns its best moves in isolation.

Integrate: multi-tail architecture

Combine all three controllers so they share the early decision-making but each has its own final output branch (a tail). Shared layers spot patterns; specialized tails give feet-commands or wing-commands.

Scheduler watches: global progress

While the robot moves, a high-level decision-maker tracks forward distance, time, energy. If progress stalls—speed drops below a threshold—reconfiguration becomes worth testing, because a different body might move faster on this surface.

Trigger: switch body

When the scheduler chooses a new configuration, the robot stops, switches physical parts (motors swap limbs), then resumes motion with the new controller.

Learn jointly: co-optimize everything

Training updates all controllers and the scheduler together, so they learn not just how to move each body, but when swapping bodies helps most.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper moves two things at once: the robot's physical configuration (which limbs are attached) and the control commands (motor signals). Instead of choosing one configuration at the start and freezing it, the system learns when mid-task reconfiguration is worth the cost.

the object it moves  =  configuration schedule and control
The hidden quantity

Configuration utility: knowing when to switch

measured quantity  =  configuration utility

The hidden quantity is configuration utility: a measure of how well each body shape solves the current piece of the task. It is not a number the paper directly computes, but a force driving the scheduler's decisions. The paper learns it implicitly by training the scheduler to predict which configuration keeps forward progress going fastest.

The scheduler estimates utility by watching three signals: how far forward the robot moves per second, how much energy each configuration burns, and whether terrain underfoot is flat or chaotic. When one configuration's utility dips (e.g., legs slipping on sand), the scheduler learns to trigger a switch to fins before the robot gets stuck.

Why it works

Why dynamic reconfiguration beats fixed design

A single optimal robot shape for all terrains is mathematically impossible when terrains are very different—walking is not swimming. But a robot that stays in one shape during a task can react. Here is how it works:

During training, the multi-tail architecture forces each controller (leg, wing, fin) to get very good at its own terrain. Shared early layers pick up general patterns (balance, momentum, energy), while each tail learns specialized outputs (foot angle vs. wing angle). This is better than training one compromise controller because each body-terrain pair can specialize.

Once trained, the scheduler faces a simple choice at each moment: keep the current body (fast, no cost) or switch to a new one (slow, costs time and energy, but might move faster after the switch). The scheduler learns by comparing: Is the current body making progress? By how much? Will another body probably do better, because when speeds are very different across body-terrain pairs, the fastest pairing accelerates progress enough to outpay switching time? The important part is that the scheduler does not need to know the terrain type in advance. It learns by watching numbers—speed, distance, energy—and patterns emerge.

When a robot sinks into soft sand with legs and slows down, the scheduler sees speed drop, infers that the current body is failing, and tests switching. If the robot with fins digs out and moves faster even accounting for swap time, the scheduler reinforces that decision because the extra distance gained in the new body far outweighs the time lost to reconfiguration. Over many tasks, it learns: when these numbers look like this, switch to that body.

Math after the picture

Maximizing traversal progress with switching

First in words:

total progress equals sum of progress from each time interval, where each interval uses one configuration, minus a cost penalty for every reconfiguration that happens

Then the compact form:

P_total = sum_t (v_t * dt) - lambda * N_switches

Every symbol:

P_totaltotal forward distance traveled
v_tforward speed at time step t (depends on current configuration)
dttime duration of this interval (equals 1 if fixed time steps)
N_switchesnumber of times the robot reconfigured
lambdathe time cost to perform one reconfiguration (in equivalent distance units)
A tiny worked example
Scenario: five time steps on sand, then water. Legs move at 8 meters/second on sand, fins at 2 m/s on sand. Fins move at 10 m/s on water, legs at 3 m/s on water.
No reconfiguration: legs whole time = (8+8+8+8+8) + (3+3+3+3+3) = 40+15 = 55 meters.
One reconfiguration after step 5 (switch to fins): legs for steps 1-5 = 40 m, fins for steps 6-10 = 50 m, minus 1 m penalty for switch = 40+50-1 = 89 meters.
The scheduler learns: switching to fins saves 50-15 = 35 meters in the water segment, far exceeding the 1-meter switch cost. So it triggers reconfiguration.

The scheduler solves this trade-off by learning which body-terrain pairs give high speed and when those speeds win over switching overhead.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Reconfiguration cost too high. If switching bodies takes too much time or energy (e.g., mechanical latches jam), then the robot reconfigures when it should not, wastes time, and ends up worse than staying in one shape.
Scheduler overfits to training terrains. If training only covers sand and water, the scheduler learns sand-specific body-swaps that do not generalize to rocky terrain. At test time on an unseen surface, it reconfigures on autopilot and fails.
Shared early layers miss important details. The multi-tail architecture forces a split between shared computation and specialized tails. If the shared part discards information vital to one controller (e.g., wing-flapping cadence), that body cannot learn well.
Done right: fast switching + diverse training. Use low-latency mechanical swaps (modular joint interfaces) and train the scheduler on varied terrains (sand, water, packed earth, slopes) so it learns general principles about when reconfiguration helps, not just memorized patterns.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a physics simulator (e.g., PyBullet or MuJoCo) with three reconfigurable robots on a mixed terrain: a zone of soft sand, then a zone of shallow water, then open air space. Each robot has the same reconfigurable skeleton but can swap between leg, fin, and wing attachments. Train three controllers: (1) a fixed-shape robot with legs only, (2) a fixed-shape robot with averaged parameters (the co-design baseline), and (3) the Learning to Reconfigure pipeline with a scheduler. Record total distance traveled by each robot in a fixed time budget.

average forward progress = total distance / time elapsed

The reconfigurable robot with scheduler should reach 5 to 10 times more distance than the fixed-design baseline, because it adapts its body to each terrain instead of compromising.
