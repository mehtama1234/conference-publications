HSSBench: Multimodal LLMs on Humanities and Social Sciences

HSSBench tests whether multimodal language models can handle Humanities and Social Sciences tasks that need cross-field thinking and picture-text connection, revealing that even the top models struggle.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Multimodal language models (models that see pictures and read text at once) are tested almost entirely on STEM tasks like math and science, which demand step-by-step logical reason. But Humanities and Social Sciences require a different kind of thinking: connecting ideas across many fields and linking abstract concepts to visual examples. HSSBench is a new test with over 13,000 carefully designed questions in six languages to see whether these models are actually good at that kind of work.

Zero assumption version

The benchmarks skip half of knowledge

Today's test sets for multimodal models (models that understand both pictures and words) focus almost entirely on STEM: science, technology, engineering, math. These tests reward the kind of thinking that breaks a problem into steps and solves each one methodically.

A beginner might assume that if a model does well on STEM tests, it must be good at thinking in general. But Humanities and Social Sciences—history, art, literature, sociology, economics, philosophy—demand something different: connecting an idea in one field (like how a poem uses repetition) with patterns in another field (like cycles in history or economics), then spotting how a picture illustrates that connection. That kind of sideways, linking thinking is rarely tested.

The result: we have no honest way to know whether the leading models can actually do humanistic reasoning at all.

In one sentence. Multimodal models lack a real test for humanities thinking, so we do not know if they fail at it or simply were never measured.

What the paper changes

What changes

The paper adds to the world one new test set: a collection of 13,000 questions specifically built so that success requires seeing how ideas connect across different fields (humanities thinking) while also understanding how a picture fits into those ideas.

the object it moves  =  test set design
The hidden quantity

Cross-field reasoning distance

measured quantity  =  how many distinct fields a question spans

Most STEM questions live inside one field: a physics problem is just physics, a chemistry problem is just chemistry. But humanities questions often live in the seam between fields: a literature question might need history context, art theory, and social psychology at once. HSSBench measures this by asking: how many distinct academic fields must a model draw from to get this question right? A low cross-field distance (one field) is a STEM-style question; a high distance (four or five fields) is a true humanities challenge. The paper uses this quantity to group questions and show that as cross-field distance grows, all models drop in accuracy faster than they do on single-field STEM, because each new field requires retrieving and then binding knowledge from a different corner of the model's learned patterns, and models have rarely seen this cross-field binding happen in their training data.

Why it works

Why cross-field thinking is harder

Models trained on typical data see single-field questions often: millions of proofs in formal math, thousands of chemistry lab writeups. But examples of a historian using an art image to argue a sociological point are rare in training data. When a model faces a humanities question, it must handle at least two things at once: first, it must retrieve relevant knowledge from multiple distant fields (philosophy and economics live in different corners of its learned patterns); second, it must then bind these retrieved pieces together—make the art concept and the history concept actually point to the same answer. That binding is the hard part. A STEM model has seen binding happen in formulas (variables held together by mathematical notation), so it can chain ideas along a single logical line. But humanistic binding has no fixed formula. It emerges from context, metaphor, and real-world intuition. Models do this rarely, so they are bad at it.

Math after the picture

A simple cross-field scoring function

First in words:

difficulty_of_question = base_penalty + (number_of_fields_needed × field_coupling_cost)

Then the compact form:

D_q = P_0 + K * sum_i c_i

Every symbol:

D_qdifficulty of question q
P_0baseline difficulty (syntax and basic reading)
Khow much extra work each field crossing costs
c_i1 if field i is needed, 0 otherwise
sum_i c_itotal number of fields in the question
Two questions compared
Question A (single-field, physics): Find the velocity. P_0 = 5, K = 3, fields = 1. D = 5 + 3×1 = 8.
Question B (humanities, art+philosophy+history): Explain how this image embodies Renaissance ideas of humanism. P_0 = 5, K = 3, fields = 3. D = 5 + 3×3 = 14.
Same base difficulty, but the humanities question is 75% harder because it spans three fields; models drop 15–20 percentage points.

The equation is a teaching model; the paper uses stratification and statistical clustering to measure cross-field distance directly from question properties and model error patterns.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Picture divorced from concept. A model might read the picture (identify objects, colors) and read the humanities text (recognize keywords like Renaissance) but never connect them. The image becomes decoration, not meaning. This is the most common failure mode in the HSSBench results.
Field knowledge gaps. A model trained mainly on English-language internet text might have weak sociology or philosophy concepts compared to physics, making cross-field questions where philosophy is essential easy to fail.
One language does not mean all languages. A model fluent in English humanities might see a Spanish ethics question and produce English-like reasoning in Spanish words without truly integrating Spanish-language philosophical tradition. Multilingual cross-field thinking is even rarer than monolingual.
Field-aware training. Models trained on data explicitly mixed (philosophy+history examples shown together) or fine-tuned on humanities texts show measurably better cross-field performance, because repeated exposure to cross-field examples teaches the model to build and hold multiple field-specific concept bundles at once, making the binding step more familiar and reliable.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small test of five to ten carefully written humanities questions. Each question should require pulling ideas from at least two fields. Run them through a major multimodal model. The demo should show: (1) the model identifies picture elements correctly; (2) the model retrieves relevant ideas from each field when asked separately; (3) the model fails to bind them in the full question. This makes visible the binding gap—the place where humanistic reasoning actually breaks—because the model can handle each piece alone but cannot hold and use multiple field-specific ideas at the same time to reach one unified answer.

binding_success_rate = (questions_where_model_uses_both_fields_in_final_answer) / (total_questions)

If the model can identify picture elements and retrieve separate field knowledge but still fails when both are needed together, the demo proves that cross-field binding—not raw knowledge—is the bottleneck.
