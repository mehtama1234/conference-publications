DTO-KD: Dynamic Trade-off Optimization for Effective Knowledge Distillation

When training a small model to copy a large one, balance the two conflicting pull signals by checking their directions and letting them both push fairly.

Paper explainersAll themesEfficiency & Systems

Training a small model to copy a large model is called knowledge distillation. It is like teaching an apprentice by showing them finished work, not just raw numbers. The problem: the small model gets two conflicting instructions at once — get answers right on the test data, AND copy what the large model outputs. These two demands often push the training in different directions, so one goal ends up ignored. This paper solves that by checking whether the two signals point the same way, and if they clash, rotating one so both can pull fairly.

Zero assumption version

Two masters pull in different directions

When you train a small model to copy a big one, you give it two separate goals: (1) answer questions correctly on real test data, and (2) match what the big model outputs exactly. These are not always the same thing. The big model might give a soft answer that hints at the right direction even when not perfect, but the small model might be training toward a sharper answer based on ground truth.

A naive approach just adds the two losses together: train on task loss (get it right) plus distillation loss (copy the big model). But here is the problem: when you calculate how to nudge the weights to improve, these two paths often push in nearly opposite directions, because the task (correct on ground truth) and the teacher output (which might be softer or take a different path) are solving different problems. Imagine being pulled north and south at the same time — you do not move anywhere useful. The result: training gets stuck, one goal wins, and you lose the benefit of distillation.

In one sentence. Two training signals (get the answer right, and copy the large model) often point in opposite directions, causing the model to ignore one of them.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Compute both gradients

Calculate the direction to move weights for the task, and for copying the teacher.

Check the angle between them

Do the two directions point the same way (aligned) or opposite ways (in conflict)?

Project and blend

If they clash, rotate the distillation gradient so it points closer to the task gradient.

Apply the balanced update

Move weights in the blended direction, letting both goals contribute fairly.

Repeat per batch

Recalculate angles and rebalance on every batch, adapting as training progresses.

What the paper changes

The thing that changes: gradients

The paper does not change the weights or the data. It changes how the weight updates themselves are computed. Specifically, when you calculate the direction to nudge each weight, you now blend the task direction and the distillation direction instead of just adding them. If they point the same way, both push together. If they clash, you rotate one to point closer to the other.

the object it moves  =  gradient direction and weight
The hidden quantity

Gradient conflict and dominance

measured quantity  =  the angle between task and distillation gradients

The paper measures something invisible: the angle between the two push-signals. Imagine two ropes pulling a box. The angle between them tells you how much their forces interfere. A small angle means they agree; a large angle means they fight.

In knowledge distillation, the paper computes: how many degrees apart are the task gradient and the distillation gradient? If this angle is large (say, 120 degrees), the two goals are in direct conflict. The paper then estimates how much learning progress is being wasted by this mismatch, and adjusts by projecting one gradient to reduce the angle. This lets both objectives push the model in a coherent direction.

Plain reading. At each training step, the paper calculates the angle between the two gradients. If it is large (conflict), it adjusts them so they point more the same way. This is like straightening two ropes so they pull in unison.
Paper reading. The paper formulates multi-objective optimization where task and distillation objectives may have gradient conflicts. It detects high-angle misalignment and applies gradient projection (projecting the distillation gradient onto the task gradient subspace or into its orthogonal complement) to ensure neither objective is completely suppressed.
Why it works

Why balancing gradients fixes distillation

Here is why this works. When you add two forces that point in opposite directions, they cancel each other out. The model does not move anywhere. By checking the angle between the two gradients and rotating one to align closer to the other, you ensure both forces are pulling the model in a direction that makes sense. The rotation does not throw away either goal; it bends one so that both can contribute.

Think of it like this: imagine you want to go north and northeast at the same time. If you just average the directions, you go north-northeast. But if the two goals were truly opposite (north and south), averaging would leave you stationary. By projecting, the method says: keep as much of both goals as possible, but remove the exact conflicts. The model learns faster because the updates are no longer canceling out, and it learns better because both the task and the teacher input actually steer it.

Math after the picture

Balancing two competing gradients

First in words:

The update is a weighted combination of the task gradient and the projected distillation gradient, where the weighting is adapted based on how aligned they are.

Then the compact form:

w := w - lr * (alpha * g_task + (1 - alpha) * g_distill_projected)

Every symbol:

wthe model weight being updated
lrlearning rate, how big each step is
alphathe balance factor, adapted per update
g_taskgradient that points toward correct task answers
g_distill_projecteddistillation gradient rotated to align with the task gradient
A tiny worked example
Suppose the task gradient points at angle 0 degrees (directly north). The distillation gradient points at 150 degrees (northwest). The angle between them is 150 degrees.
A naive blend: (g_task + g_distill) / 2 would point at 75 degrees, somewhat northwest, but dilutes both signals.
DTO-KD projection: rotate g_distill from 150 degrees to align closer, say to 15 degrees. Now both point mostly north (0 and 15 degrees). The blended update (0.6 * g_task + 0.4 * g_distill_projected) points at 6 degrees, north-northeast, and both objectives actually contribute.
Result: neither signal is wasted; the model moves faster in a coherent direction.

The math is just a weighted average of the gradients, but the key is that one gradient is first rotated so the two are no longer in conflict.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Overfitting to the teacher. If the teacher is overfit on its own data, the small model will copy those mistakes. Balancing the gradients reduces but does not eliminate this. The teacher should be a good one.
Excessive projection. If the projection is too aggressive (rotating the distillation gradient all the way to match the task gradient), you lose the benefit of the teacher. The method must keep both signals. The paper uses careful blending factors.
Orthogonal gradients. If the task and distillation gradients are completely perpendicular (90 degrees, meaning they are at a right angle to each other), neither can be rotated to align with the other, because rotating one 90 degrees will never point it toward the other. In this case, the method must simply balance their magnitudes and move diagonally. This is rare but is a hard boundary.
Done right. When the gradients are well-aligned or the projection is tuned to preserve both signals, the model learns faster because the update steps are no longer canceling each other out, and it learns better because both the task truth and the teacher guidance pull it toward a smarter region of weight-space.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small neural network and train it on a standard dataset like CIFAR-10 (32 by 32 pixel images, 10 classes). Train a large teacher model on the same data first. Then train a small student model in three ways: (1) only on the task (baseline), (2) with naive knowledge distillation (add both losses), and (3) with DTO-KD (balance the gradients). Measure accuracy on both the held-out test set and how well the student copies the teacher's outputs on new examples.

benefit = (accuracy_DTO - accuracy_naive) + (copy_accuracy_DTO - copy_accuracy_naive)

The demo should show that DTO-KD maintains task accuracy while copying the teacher better than naive distillation, and converges faster because both gradients are pulling in the same general direction.
