Understanding Truncated Positional Encodings for Graph Neural Networks

When graph neural networks use truncated positional encodings instead of complete ones, they lose expressive power in unexpected and different ways depending on the encoding type.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

Graph neural networks use positional encodings to help distinguish nodes in a graph—each node gets a list of numbers that encodes its position in the structure, letting the network tell similar nodes from different ones. Complete encodings use all the information available, but in practice, engineers truncate them to use only the first few strongest signals because computing all signals takes time proportional to the graph size cubed. This paper shows that truncation breaks the theoretical guarantees—different encoding types lose signal-spotting ability in different ways, and some become unable to recognize graph patterns they used to see.

Zero assumption version

Truncation breaks theory without warning

A graph neural network must learn which nodes are similar and which are different. Positional encodings are signals that attach to each node (a list of numbers per node) that encode where the node sits in the graph structure, so the network can distinguish nodes that play different roles. Two popular families—spectral (based on the graph's structural vibrations) and random walk (based on paths the network traces through the graph)—are mathematically proven to see the same range of patterns when used completely because both capture the full structural information just in different orders.

In practice, computing full encodings is expensive—work time and memory grow as you multiply the graph size by itself three times. So engineers always truncate them—use only the first k strongest signals instead of all n. Naive intuition says truncation just trades accuracy for speed, but the paper proves this is wrong: truncation changes what the network can see at all. Worse, spectral and random walk encodings degrade differently. A complete encoding might see the same patterns as its paired twin, but truncate both and they suddenly diverge.

In one sentence. Truncated positional encodings lose different amounts of pattern-spotting ability depending on the encoding family, and spectral encodings drop to the level of a basic graph neural network.

The hidden quantity

Expressivity class under truncation

measured quantity  =  expressivity ceiling after truncation

Expressivity is the ceiling of patterns and distinctions a network can make. A network at the 1-WL level can perform simple graph comparisons; at the 3-WL level it can see more nuanced relationships. The Weisfeiler-Lehman test is a formal way to measure this ceiling. It asks: can the network distinguish every pair of structurally different node sets? The higher the WL level, the more patterns it sees.

The paper measures how far truncation pushes each encoding family down the expressivity ladder. Spectral encodings drop from somewhere between 1-WL and 3-WL all the way to 1-WL alone. Random walk encodings stay higher. This is the hidden quantity: where does each method land after truncation?

Why it works

Why truncation breaks spectral but not random walk

Spectral encodings are built from the graph's structural frequencies—like the natural vibrations of a drumhead. The first few frequencies (low-energy modes) describe coarse, global structure: is the graph connected? Are there big clusters? Later frequencies (high-energy modes) capture fine detail: which specific nodes are neighbors? When you truncate and keep only the top few, you keep the big-picture frequencies but throw away the fine details. This is like looking at a photograph through thick frosting—you see whether it is bright or dark overall, but not which person is smiling.

Random walk encodings work differently. They are built from powers of the adjacency matrix: first you ask which nodes are one step away, then two steps, then three. These encode local neighborhood structure directly. The first few powers already carry the most useful information for distinguishing nodes. When you truncate, you are just stopping the journey earlier, but you have already collected the most important signals. The later powers add refinement but are not critical.

Because spectral modes are ordered by frequency, not by usefulness for node discrimination, truncation removes information the network actually needs. Random walk encodings are naturally ordered by distance-from-node, so truncation removes only gradually less-useful signals. That is why they degrade differently.

Expressivity as signal ranking

First in words:

The expressivity level of truncated encodings depends on how many independent patterns can be distinguished. For spectral methods with k eigenvectors, the expressivity does not increase beyond 1-WL no matter how large k is. For random walk methods, expressivity improves with k. A mixed strategy averaging both encodings recovers higher expressivity.

Then the compact form:

expressivity(truncated_spectral, k) = 1-WL (constant for all k) expressivity(truncated_random_walk, k) = min(k, between_1-WL_and_3-WL) expressivity(mixed, k) = max(spectral_k, random_walk_k)

A tiny worked example
Consider a graph where node A and node B are structurally different only in their fine-grained neighborhood details (the types of nodes at distance 3+).
Spectral truncated to k=3: keeps only coarse frequency modes. Cannot distinguish A and B. Result: 1-WL expressivity.
Random walk truncated to k=3: keeps 1-step, 2-step, 3-step neighborhoods. CAN distinguish A and B because 3-step detail is captured. Result: between 1-WL and 3-WL.
Mixed truncated to k=3: uses both methods. Random walk part catches the difference. Result: higher expressivity than spectral alone.

The equation shows that spectral truncation creates a hard expressivity ceiling, while random walk scales up gracefully, and mixing both recovers lost power.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Choosing spectral for truncated encodings. If you truncate spectral encodings, they drop to basic graph network power no matter how many signals you keep. Spectral truncation is inherently limited because the signal ordering (by frequency) does not align with usefulness for node discrimination.
Assuming all truncation is equal. The paper proves different encoding families degrade at completely different rates. Truncating to k=5 might preserve random walk power but wreck spectral power. No universal answer.
Using harmonic distances without checking. Even closely related spectral variants like k-harmonic distances show different expressivity under truncation. Related does not mean identical.
Using mixed encodings. Combining spectral and random walk signals (truncated) recovers most of the power of complete encodings at a fraction of the compute cost. This is the reliable strategy.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple graph-classification task (e.g., predict whether a graph is a tree, cycle, or grid). Implement three node-encoding strategies: spectral truncated to k=5, random walk truncated to k=5, and mixed truncated to k=3 each. Train the same graph neural network backbone on each encoding. Run on a set of test graphs and measure the accuracy of node-pair discrimination (can the network tell apart nodes that should be different by structure?).

discrimination_power = fraction_of_node_pairs_correctly_distinguished_by_network

The demo should make it impossible to miss that random walk encodings retain discrimination power while spectral encodings hit a wall, and that mixing both recovers it.
