KANO: Kolmogorov-Arnold Neural Operator

KANO learns physics laws by combining wave-space and position-space rules, so it works on position-dependent dynamics where earlier methods fail.

Paper explainersAll themesAI for Science

When you train a model to learn physics equations, earlier methods like FNO (Fourier Neural Operator) crash when the equation has coefficients that change with position. KANO solves this by learning in two parallel spaces at once: the frequency space (which catches repeating patterns) and the physical space (which catches position-specific rules). This dual approach lets KANO work on any position-dependent physics problem and even reconstruct quantum equations as pure symbolic formulas.

Zero assumption version

Position-dependent physics breaks earlier methods

When a physics rule (a differential equation) has coefficients that change depending on position, earlier neural operators fail badly. FNO (Fourier Neural Operator), the prior standard, learns to represent physics in wave-space only—like reading a song only as pure frequencies (bass, treble, harmonics) and never looking at how it changes from start to finish. FNO assumes the physics is the same everywhere, so it learns spectral patterns (frequency content) from the training data. But when you ask it to predict on new physics that varies with location, it crashes because it never learned position-specific behavior.

A beginner might try: train FNO on a few examples of position-dependent physics and hope it generalizes. But FNO's frequency patterns are locked to the training data's spatial structure. The moment you test on new spatial layouts or position-dependent coefficients outside the training distribution, FNO's spectral patterns (the frequency off-diagonals it learned) become wrong, and accuracy plummets. The paper calls this the spectral bottleneck: FNO ties its learned patterns to the training data's spatial footprint, so generalization breaks.

The core problem. FNO learns only frequency patterns from training data and cannot adapt to position-dependent physics it hasn't seen before.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: Split the learning task into two jobs

One part of the model learns frequency patterns (how repeating waves behave). The other learns spatial rules (how things change with position).

Step 2: Encode the input physics problem

Compress the input function into a small list of numbers, just like FNO does.

Step 3: Run both pathways in parallel

Frequency pathway learns Fourier coefficients (wave content). Spatial pathway learns position-dependent coefficients using KAN networks (networks that interpolate smoothly).

Step 4: Combine both answers

Merge the frequency and spatial predictions to get the final learned operator that works anywhere.

Step 5: Decode and output

Reconstruct the predicted solution function from the final operator prediction.

What the paper changes

What the paper changed

KANO adds a second learning pathway: instead of learning only in frequency space (like FNO), it learns simultaneously in frequency space and position space, with the position-space part built from KAN networks (networks that learn smooth, interpretable functions). This dual parameterization means the model can capture both global wave patterns and local position-dependent variations.

The hidden quantity

The spectral off-diagonal stability: how learned frequencies change on new spatial domains

measured quantity  =  spectral off-diagonal stability

In FNO, when you look at the learned frequency responses, the off-diagonal terms (the cross-talk between different frequencies) become locked to the training data's spatial distribution. This is invisible but catastrophic: it means the frequency model memorizes the spatial structure of the training set, not the true physics. KANO measures this by tracking what happens to the learned spectral patterns when you shift to a completely different spatial layout. If the patterns stay valid, the model learned the physics; if they shatter, the model was just memorizing the training domain.

KANO estimates this stability by testing on new spatial domains (Group B), not just interpolating within the training domain (Group A). The paper computes a generalization ratio: average loss on new spatial domains divided by average loss on training domains. FNO's ratio is very high (fails badly on new domains), while KANO's stays close to 1 (succeeds equally on new domains).

Why it works

Why dual-domain learning fixes the bottleneck

FNO learns a fixed mapping from input wave-space to output wave-space. This is like learning to convert a song's frequency content directly, with no idea that the song's volume changes in the middle. The learned mappings (spectral off-diagonals) encode assumptions about the spatial structure baked into the training data. When you move to a different spatial structure, those assumptions break.

KANO runs two learners in parallel. One (the spectral learner, built from spectral convolutions) captures global frequency behavior. The other (the spatial learner, built from KAN networks) captures how things change with position. These two pathways are complementary: the spectral path handles smooth, repeating patterns everywhere; the spatial path handles local coefficient variations. When you test on new spatial layouts, the spectral path remains valid (frequencies are universal), and the spatial path learns to adapt to new position-dependent rules because it is explicitly trained to be position-aware, not position-agnostic like FNO.

The mechanism: by splitting the learning into spectral (position-independent) and spatial (position-dependent) parts, each part is forced to solve only its own half of the problem. The spectral part never needs to memorize spatial distribution because the spatial part handles that. This decoupling makes both parts generalizable.

The combined operator equation

First in words:

Combined operator equals spectral convolution part plus KAN spatial part, both applied to the encoded input and summed.

Then the compact form:

Op_m(phi_sym) + KAN(x, z) where phi_sym are the learned Fourier multipliers and KAN is a smooth position-aware function.

Every symbol:

Op_m(phi_sym)spectral convolution operator: filters the input using learned frequency multipliers phi_sym at wavenumbers up to cutoff m
KAN(x, z)Kolmogorov-Arnold network: a smooth function that depends on position x and latent representation z
xspatial position coordinates
zlatent encoded representation of the input function
mfrequency cutoff (only frequencies up to m are represented)
A tiny worked example
Example: input is a heat distribution at position x=0 to 1.
Spectral path encodes it as Fourier coefficients: [12.0 (constant), 3.5 (first harmonic), 1.2 (second)]. Convolution filters these: [12.0*0.8, 3.5*0.6, 1.2*0.2] = [9.6, 2.1, 0.24].
Spatial path: KAN network learns that at x=0.2, the coefficient is 1.5; at x=0.5, it is 2.1; at x=0.8, it is 1.8. Interpolates smoothly between them.
At position x=0.5: spectral part outputs [9.6+2.1+0.24=11.94] fused with spatial adjustment 2.1 gives combined output about 12.04.
FNO would give only [11.94] everywhere (position-blind), breaking when coefficients change.

The dual paths work because spectral part stays valid universally (Fourier modes are basis-independent) while spatial part adapts to local structure.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Spatial mesh is too coarse. If the spatial discretization is too coarse (too few grid points), the KAN spatial network cannot learn fine position-dependent structure. Use at least as many spatial samples as the feature complexity requires.
Position-dependence is truly mild. If the physics problem has almost no position-dependence (like truly periodic boundary conditions everywhere), both KANO and FNO work about equally; KANO just pays overhead for nothing. Check if the problem is genuinely position-dependent first.
Mixing train and test spatial domains accidentally. If test examples use the same spatial domains as training (Group A only), both FNO and KANO will generalize. The advantage only shows on genuinely new spatial regions (Group B). Design experiments to include out-of-distribution spatial shifts.
Done right: out-of-distribution spatial test Generate training examples from one spatial domain or shape (Group A), test on a second, distinct spatial region with the same underlying physics (Group B). KANO will outperform FNO because its spatial learner explicitly captures position-dependent rules, while FNO has no way to adapt to new domains.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple position-dependent PDE: a heat equation with thermal conductivity that changes smoothly with position (e.g., left half is insulating, right half conducts well). Generate 50 training examples by solving it on domain 1, and 30 test examples by solving the same physics on domain 2 (shifted or scaled). Train FNO and KANO on the training set. Measure accuracy on domain 2. KANO should show 3–5x lower error on domain 2 than FNO. Then, freeze KANO's symbolic representations (the KAN networks' interpolation coefficients) and retrain only the spectral part; verify it recovers similar loss, proving KANO's position-aware learned rules are the key to success.

generalization_ratio = (mean loss on domain 2) / (mean loss on domain 1); aim for KANO ratio ~0.96, FNO ratio ~0.25 (FNO fails, KANO holds)

The demo must make undeniable that KANO's position-space learning is what lets it generalize to new spatial layouts, not just luck.
