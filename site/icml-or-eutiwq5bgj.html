Feedback Control for Multi-Objective Graph Self-Supervision

Instead of blending multiple learning objectives at every step, use a feedback controller to train one objective at a time and schedule which one gets the next time block.

Paper explainersAll themesGraph & Geometric Learning

When you teach a graph encoder multiple tasks at once (mutual information, reconstruction, contrastive learning), they fight over the same weights—like a team where every person has to move in all directions at once. This paper replaces that chaos with a simple scheduling idea: train one objective per time block, and use feedback control (like a thermostat) to decide which one runs next based on how hard it is and how much it conflicts with others.

Zero assumption version

Multiple objectives in one brain fight each other

Graph encoders—models that turn graphs into dense summary vectors—need to learn from multiple self-supervised (unlabeled) objectives at once. Each objective is a task like 'predict missing graph edges' or 'keep similar nodes close together.' A beginner combines them naively: every training step, blend the losses (the error signals) using weights, and update the parameters (numbers in the model) toward all objectives at once.

This breaks in three ways. First, conflicting objectives pull the parameters in opposite directions on the same step (Disagreement)—one objective wants to slide a weight up, another wants to slide it down, so the update goes nowhere or gets shuffled around. Second, some objectives are useful early in training but useless later (Drift)—the weight that looked right at step 1,000 is stale by step 10,000. Third, one objective silently starves (Drought)—weaker objectives lose to stronger ones in the blended sum, so the model never actually trains hard on the weak one because its signal is drowned out by the louder voices.

The naive solution fails because it forces every step to compromise. Instead of blending objectives every step, pick one objective per block of time and use feedback control to decide which one runs next.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Sense the state

Compute how hard each objective is right now (spectral demand: are gradients scattered or aligned?) and how much they conflict (do they push the same weight in opposite directions?).

Measure loss and interference

Each objective produces an error signal (loss). Estimate which objectives are antagonistic (pulling in opposite directions on shared parameters).

Plan the target allocation

Use a Pareto planner—a decision rule that asks 'if I run objective X next, will I make progress on ALL objectives or sacrifice some?'—to decide what fraction of the remaining time each objective should get.

Track with feedback control

Use a PID controller (a standard engineering device that steers based on error) to decide which objective to train next. The controller compares the current allocation to the target plan and nudges it back on track.

Execute and repeat

Run the chosen objective for a time block, then loop back and re-measure. The schedule adapts as the model learns.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper replaces per-step loss blending with a time-dependent schedule: which objective gets trained in the next time block. Instead of mixing loss1 * w1 + loss2 * w2 + loss3 * w3 at every step, the parameters are updated for one pure objective at a time, and a controller decides which one gets the next turn.

the object it moves  =  the training schedule (which objective runs each block)
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantities: conflict and demand

measured quantity  =  Per-objective difficulty and pairwise antagonism

The paper measures two hidden signals from the training data. The first is spectral demand: how scattered or varied are the gradients (error-direction arrows) for objective K across the graph nodes? A scattered signal (high demand) means the objective is pulling hard and needs training. A coherent signal (low demand) means the objective is mostly satisfied.

The second is pairwise antagonism: when objective A pulls a shared weight up and objective B pulls it down at the same step, that is antagonism. The paper computes this by checking the inner product (dot product, a measure of alignment) of gradient vectors—if two gradients point in opposite directions, antagonism is high.

Plain reading. The paper senses two numbers per objective: how hard is it (spectral demand) and which other objectives does it conflict with (antagonism). These are computed from the model's current summary vectors (dense representations of the graph) and loss gradients on the full training graph.
Paper reading. Demand is quantified via the spectral properties of the representation-gradient field (the matrix of per-node gradient vectors stacked). Antagonism is detected by sign of the inner product of gradient vectors across objectives; the log-hypervolume formulation then trades off these signals.
Why it works

Why separating objectives in time stops the tug of war

When two objectives contradict on a single step, the parameter move is the average of the two pulls—suboptimal for both. Separating them in time (one objective per time block) means each gets a pure signal. After block 1 (objective A), the weights move exactly in the direction A wants. In block 2 (objective B), they move in B's direction. The total path makes progress on both.

The PID controller ensures fairness: it measures how far behind each objective's current 'allocation budget' is from the target plan. If objective A's allocation is behind (it hasn't gotten its scheduled time yet), the controller picks A next. If it is ahead, the controller picks someone else. This is exactly how a thermostat works—it measures the temperature gap and decides whether to heat or cool. Here it measures the time-allocation gap and decides which objective to train. Because the signal adapts (the plan changes as training progresses), the schedule handles nonstationarity (drift) automatically: if objective A stops being useful mid-training, the planner detects it (its loss stops improving) and shrinks A's future allocation.

Math after the picture

The core scheduling rule (simplified)

First in words:

deficit for objective k = target allocation minus current allocation. pick the objective with the largest deficit. repeat every T steps.

Then the compact form:

deficit_k = target_alloc_k - current_alloc_k; pick k* = argmax_k(deficit_k)

Every symbol:

target_alloc_kplanned fraction of time for objective k (set by Pareto planner)
current_alloc_kactual fraction of time spent on objective k so far
deficit_khow far behind objective k is on its time budget
k*the objective selected to train next
A concrete time allocation example
Say the plan says: obj1 gets 40% of time, obj2 gets 35%, obj3 gets 25%.
After 100 steps: obj1 ran 50 steps (50%), obj2 ran 30 steps (30%), obj3 ran 20 steps (20%).
Deficits: obj1 = 40 - 50 = -10 (ahead), obj2 = 35 - 30 = +5 (behind), obj3 = 25 - 20 = +5 (behind).
Controller picks the largest deficit: obj2 and obj3 both have +5, so the controller picks obj2 (or uses a tiebreaker). Next 10 steps train obj2.
After 110 steps: obj1 = 50/110 = 45%, obj2 = 40/110 = 36%, obj3 = 20/110 = 18%. Deficits are now -5, -1, +7. Next turn goes to obj3.

The deficit rule ensures all objectives get time; the planner adapts the target allocation based on difficulty and conflicts, and the controller tracks toward it.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Planner miscalibrates demand. If the demand estimation is noisy or the graph structure is pathological, the planner may allocate time to the wrong objectives. Fix: smooth the demand estimates over a rolling window.
Block size is too large or too small. If each time block has too few steps, the controller thrashes (switches objectives too often, gains nothing). If blocks are huge, nonstationarity (drift) is not tracked. Fix: tune block length experimentally; typically 10–100 steps.
Objectives are truly orthogonal (no conflict). If the objectives do not actually interfere, per-step mixing and temporal allocation both work fine. ControlG does not hurt, but it does not buy much. The paper's main win is when antagonism is real.
Done right. The controller handles nonstationarity (changing usefulness over time) because it re-measures demand and antagonism every sensing step and adjusts the plan. The Pareto planner ensures no objective is sacrificed entirely (no drought). Temporal blocks eliminate instantaneous tug of war (no disagreement).
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a toy graph encoder with three self-supervised pretext tasks (e.g. mutual information, edge reconstruction, contrastive). Start with a small graph (10–50 nodes). Implement naive per-step mixing and ControlG side by side. Train both for 1,000 steps and log the three objective values over time.

final performance = average of all three objective losses at step 1000. temporal spread = standard deviation of objective losses at step 1000 (lower is fairer). starvation = minimum of the three objective values (higher is better—no objective dropped).

The demo must show: ControlG's three objective losses converge more evenly; no objective is starved to < 50% of the other two; and the final average is higher than naive mixing.
