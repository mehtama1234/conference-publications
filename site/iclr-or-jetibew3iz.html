Memorization Through Sample Gradients

A cheap way to detect which examples a neural network will memorize by tracking how gradients change during training.

Paper explainersAll themesLearning Theory

Neural networks sometimes memorize outliers and mistakes instead of learning the general pattern. Knowing which examples are memorized is important for privacy and fixing bad data, but measuring it exactly is prohibitively expensive. This paper uses a simple side effect of training—the gradient signals—to spot memorization fast, without storing data separately.

Zero assumption version

Why memorization is hard to measure

A neural network learns by adjusting millions of weights to minimize error on its training data. Some examples are easy (a dog that is clearly a dog); others are hard (a dog photo that is mislabeled as cat). Hard examples often get memorized—the network stores exact pixel patterns or peculiar features tied to that one sample, instead of learning what makes a dog a dog.

A beginner might think: just train without that sample, then train with it, and see which samples hurt the model more. But that requires training twice for every sample you want to measure. On a billion-sample dataset with a model taking days to train, you'd need billions of training runs. It is prohibitively expensive.

The method in one sentence. Track how much the loss gradient (the error signal) changes as training progresses for each sample, and use that signal to flag memorized examples without retraining.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

During training, collect error signals.

For each sample, save the gradient (how much the error would change if you tweaked each weight).

Add up gradients across all epochs.

Cumulative Sample Gradient (CSG) is the sum of all gradients for that sample from start to finish.

Larger sum means more memorization.

Samples that stay hard (high error signal) throughout training accumulate bigger gradients.

Stop training at weight-norm peak.

Watch the total size of all weights combined; stop when it peaks (no validation set needed).

Rank samples by memorization risk.

Use CSG to identify which samples are being memorized, mislabeled, or are outliers.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper introduces Cumulative Sample Gradient (CSG): the sum of loss gradients with respect to each sample over the full course of training. Instead of training a model twice for each sample, the paper collects the gradient signal—the error signal—from a single training run and accumulates it.

the object it moves  =  Gradient accumulation over time
The hidden quantity

The memorization proxy

measured quantity  =  Cumulative Sample Gradient (CSG)

The hidden quantity is how much a sample resists learning. When a sample is easy, the model learns it quickly and the error drops to near zero. When a sample is hard or an outlier, the error stays high throughout training, and the gradient (the error signal trying to fix it) stays large. Cumulative Sample Gradient is the sum of these error signals over all training steps, so outliers and memorized examples have larger CSG values.

The paper shows that CSG is mathematically bounded by true memorization, and CSG can be computed with negligible overhead—just by saving the gradients that are already being computed during training's error-correction step.

Plain reading. CSG adds up all the error signals one sample produces during training. A big cumulative signal means the network kept struggling with that sample, a sign it was memorized.
Paper reading. CSG for sample x is the sum over all training steps t of gradient-t (loss for sample x) with respect to all the numbers the model learns (the weights and biases), providing a computationally efficient estimate of memorization aligned with Feldman & Zhang.
Why it works

Why tracking gradients reveals memorization

Memorization happens when a sample is hard to fit and the network keeps adjusting weights to try to match it. Each time the network fails to match that sample during training, the gradient (error signal) is large. Easy samples get memorized early—their error drops, and their gradient shrinks. Hard samples and outliers never get learned well—their gradient stays large throughout.

By accumulating all those error signals over the full training run, you get a number that reflects how much the network struggled with that sample. The more struggle, the more memorization. The mechanism is simple: memorized samples produce persistent large error signals, and persistent large signals accumulate to large sums.

Math after the picture

The equation

First in words:

CSG of sample x is the sum over all training steps t of the gradient of the loss for that sample.

Then the compact form:

CSG(x) = sum_{t=1}^{T} ||grad_t( L(f_t(x), y) )||

Every symbol:

CSG(x)Cumulative Sample Gradient for sample x
Ttotal number of training steps
grad_tthe gradient computed at step t
Lthe loss function (error measure)
f_t(x)the model's prediction for sample x at step t
ythe true label for sample x
||...||the magnitude (total size) of the gradient vector
A tiny worked example
Suppose a sample trains for 100 steps. At each step, the loss-gradient has some size.
Easy sample: step 1-10 gradient=5, step 11-100 gradient=0.1, sum = 10*5 + 90*0.1 = 50 + 9 = 59.
Hard outlier: step 1-50 gradient=8, step 51-100 gradient=6, sum = 50*8 + 50*6 = 400 + 300 = 700.
The outlier's CSG (700) is much larger than the easy sample's (59), flagging it as memorized.

Samples that keep producing high error signals throughout training accumulate large CSG; samples learned early stop producing signals and accumulate small CSG.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Gradient-free learning methods break CSG. Methods like evolutionary algorithms or some reinforcement-learning settings do not use gradients. CSG requires gradient-based training and won't work elsewhere.
Batch effects hide individual sample signals. When many samples are trained together, per-sample gradients must be isolated carefully. Careless batching mixes signals and weakens the memorization signal.
Stop-point matters. CSG depends on when training stops. Stop too early and you miss memorization; stop too late and the signal blurs. Weight-norm peak helps because memorization intensity rises as weights grow, but it is not perfect because dataset and architecture variations change when the peak occurs.
Done right. Use per-sample gradients throughout training, track the weight-norm peak to know when to stop without a validation set, and aggregate CSG values to rank samples by memorization.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple image classifier on a small dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10), intentionally mislabel a few samples, and train the model for 50 epochs. At each epoch, compute the per-sample gradient magnitude for each training sample and accumulate it. Plot the cumulative sum over time for three groups: correctly labeled easy samples, correctly labeled hard samples (near decision boundary), and mislabeled samples. Show that mislabeled samples accumulate much larger gradient sums, and that monitoring the weight norm shows a clear peak that tracks with memorization onset.

CSG(mislabeled) should be 5+ times larger than CSG(easy samples); correlation between CSG and true memorization score should exceed 0.85.

The key visual: mislabeled samples' gradient curves stay high throughout training, while correct samples' curves drop early. The cumulative sums diverge sharply, making memorization unmissable without expensive retraining.
