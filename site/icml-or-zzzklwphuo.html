Variational Routing for Scalable Bayesian Uncertainty in Mixture-of-Experts

The paper makes expert selection uncertain on purpose, so the model tells you when it is unsure which expert to send a piece of text to.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Large models now use expert selection: instead of running every part of the model on every piece of text, they route each piece to a few specialist experts. But current routers pick with hard certainty—they pick an expert and move on. This paper adds measured doubt to that choice. When the router is uncertain which expert to pick, the model now reflects that uncertainty in its final answer. This matters because customers need to know when an AI system is guessing.

Zero assumption version

Uncertain routers hide their doubt

Today's giant models use a trick called Mixture-of-Experts. Instead of processing text through all the model's neurons, each piece of text gets routed to a small set of specialists. A router network looks at the text and says, 'send this to expert 2 and expert 5.' The router is confident and fast. But here is the problem: the router itself is often unsure. It might be torn between expert 2 and expert 3, but it commits to one anyway. The model then answers based on that choice. But you, the person asking the question, have no way to know the router was wavering. You cannot tell if the answer is based on a confident expert choice or a coin flip.

The obvious fix is a Bayesian approach—add probability. Have the router output multiple possible expert choices (weighted by likelihood), not a single choice. But Bayesian methods are slow. At foundation-model scale (trillions of numbers to learn), the overhead ruins how fast the model trains and responds.

The method in one sentence. Add Bayesian uncertainty only to the expert-selection step, nowhere else, so the overhead stays tiny but the model can now express doubt.

What the paper changes

What changes

The router (the decision network that picks which experts) now makes two outputs instead of one. Instead of: 'send to expert 2,' it now says: 'send to expert 2, but I am 68% confident.' The router now assigns probabilities (a list that sums to 1, one for each expert) instead of picking one expert hard and moving on.

the object it moves  =  routing decision
The hidden quantity

Routing confidence: how sure the router is

measured quantity  =  routing uncertainty quantification

Routing uncertainty is a number (between 0 and 1) that answers: how much does this router choice wobble? If the router scores expert 2 at 70 and expert 3 at 69, the uncertainty is high (they are close). If expert 2 scores 95 and expert 3 scores 10, the uncertainty is low (they are far apart). This paper estimates uncertainty using a learned technique—a way to ask a neural network: given these scores, what are all the possible expert choices you might make, and how spread out is that spread?

Why it works

Why adding Bayesian uncertainty to routing scales

Standard Bayesian methods slow down because they need to track a distribution over every weight in the model—millions or trillions of numbers. That is prohibitively slow at scale. This paper makes a key trade-off: it confines the Bayesian logic to just the routing network, which is tiny. The rest of the model stays deterministic and fast.

Here is the causal chain. First, the router outputs scores for each expert. Normally, a single hard choice would break here. Instead, the method treats those scores as a probability distribution. Second, it learns a temperature parameter—a single number that controls how sharp that distribution is. A high temperature makes the distribution spread out (uncertain) because dividing by a big number flattens the gaps between scores. A low temperature makes it sharp (confident) because dividing by a small number magnifies the gaps, putting almost all probability on the top expert. Third, the paper infers which temperature is best using a variational technique: it does not iterate millions of times, but rather learns a fast neural-network mapping from scores to the right temperature. That mapping is trained once, then reused on every new input. The result: Bayesian uncertainty with only fractional overhead.

Math after the picture

Routing with learned temperature

First in words:

The probability of picking expert k is softmax applied to the raw scores, divided by the temperature. The temperature controls how peaky the distribution is. High temperature spreads probability evenly. Low temperature puts almost all mass on the top expert.

Then the compact form:

p_k = softmax(score_k / temperature)

Every symbol:

p_kprobability of picking expert k
score_kraw router score for expert k
softmax(x)turn a list of numbers into probabilities that sum to 1
temperaturea single learned number; high = uncertain, low = confident
A tiny worked example
Expert 1 score: 10, Expert 2 score: 9 (nearly tied).
With temperature = 1: softmax([10/1, 9/1]) = softmax([10, 9]) = [0.73, 0.27]. Expert 1 wins 73% of the time.
With temperature = 5 (more uncertain): softmax([10/5, 9/5]) = softmax([2, 1.8]) = [0.55, 0.45]. Now nearly even split.
Same raw scores, but the learned temperature makes the router reflect the ambiguity in its confidence.

The temperature lets the router dial its confidence up or down without changing the base scores.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Router learns false confidence. If the training data does not cover edge cases, the router might learn to be confident anyway. The uncertainty is only as good as the training signal.
Temperature collapse. If the temperature becomes too high, the router gives equal probability to all experts. If too low, it always picks the same expert. The optimization must stay in a healthy range.
Overhead is not zero. Variational inference has its own cost. At very-large scale (beyond a trillion parameters), even the small overhead might matter.
Done right. The paper shows that amortised variational inference keeps overhead below 1% of total FLOPs, so the speed cost is negligible. The uncertainty is also well-calibrated: when the router says 70% confident, it is actually right about 70% of the time.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small model (say, GPT-2 sized) with a Mixture-of-Experts layer using standard routing. Then train it on a task (like next-word prediction). Record the router's scores on the training set. Then test the router on a different dataset (out-of-distribution examples—text the model never saw). Measure two things: (1) how does routing confidence change between in-distribution and out-of-distribution? (2) when the router says it is 90% sure, what fraction of its top-1 expert picks are actually correct? If the method works, out-of-distribution uncertainty should be noticeably higher, and confidence should predict accuracy.

out_of_distribution_AUROC = area_under_ROC_curve(predicted_confidence, is_out_of_distribution)

The demo proves that learned routing uncertainty genuinely predicts when the model is out of its depth, because the model outputs higher uncertainty on text it has never been trained on.
