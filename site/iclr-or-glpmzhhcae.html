Fairness Without Harm: When Simple Training Beats Specialized Algorithms

A rigorous benchmark reveals that a carefully tuned baseline model and simple data augmentation often outperform specialized bias-mitigation algorithms, and shows which training choices matter most for fairness.

Paper explainersAll themesDatasets & Benchmarks & Evaluation

Machine learning models trained on real-world data inherit and amplify biases - they predict worse for some groups than others. Many researchers have proposed specialized algorithms to fix this. But comparing these methods fairly is almost impossible: different papers use different datasets, different fairness metrics, and different training settings. This paper builds one standard testing ground (called NH-Fair) that reveals a surprising finding: many fancy debiasing methods don't actually beat a simple baseline that is just trained very carefully, and the real win comes from augmenting your training data with more examples.

Zero assumption version

Why comparing debiasing methods feels broken

Imagine you want to buy a debiasing algorithm to make your model fair. You search the literature and find ten different methods—each paper claims their approach is best. But here's the trap: paper A uses Dataset 1 with a fairness metric called Demographic Parity and trains for 50 epochs. Paper B uses Dataset 2 with a different metric (Equalized Odds) and trains for 100 epochs with a different optimizer. They are not measuring the same thing. A beginner might think: just pick the one that sounds smartest, or train all of them on my data. That fails, because the algorithms were never tested under identical training conditions—the optimizer choice, the number of training steps, the learning rate, and even how the baseline model was trained can swing fairness numbers by 20 percent or more.

The second trap is that most papers only test classical image classifiers (like ResNet), not the newer large vision-language models (like CLIP). And almost no paper compares whether their fancy algorithm actually beats just training the standard baseline (ERM, or plain risk minimization, which means training without any special fairness trick) very carefully. So you don't know whether the algorithm really helps, because the comparison was unfair: if the baseline was trained with a default learning rate and the new method was tuned exhaustively, of course the new method looks better.

The core idea. Run all debiasing methods on the same datasets, with the same metrics, the same training procedure, and extensive hyperparameter tuning—and see which ones actually win compared to a well-trained baseline.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Set up the benchmark

Collect multiple datasets, standardize fairness metrics, define exactly how to train the baseline.

Tune the baseline exhaustively

Test many learning rates, optimizers, epochs for the standard model; pick the best one by fairness+accuracy.

Apply each debiasing method

Train data-augmentation methods, algorithmic methods (adversarial training, regularization), and post-processing methods under identical conditions.

Measure subgroup disparities

For each method, compute how much accuracy differs between majority and minority groups.

Compare and rank

Ask: does this method beat the baseline without losing accuracy? Simple augmentation wins most often.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes: training procedure and hyperparameters

The paper does not propose one new debiasing algorithm. Instead, it changes how we evaluate and train all of them. Specifically: (1) it uses systematic hyperparameter sweeping (trying many learning rates, optimizers, regularization weights) so that every method—including the simple baseline—is given its best shot; (2) it keeps everything else constant (same dataset, same fairness metric definitions, same epochs, same hardware setup), so that comparisons are honest; and (3) it tests on both classical vision models and large vision-language models, not just one or the other.

the object it moves  =  training procedure + hyperparameter choices
The hidden quantity

Which training choices matter most

measured quantity  =  High-impact training choices

The paper identifies a hidden quantity: not every hyperparameter affects fairness equally. Some training choices—like the learning rate, the number of training epochs, and whether you use momentum in your optimizer—swing disparities by 5-10 percent. Others (like batch size within reason) barely matter. By measuring how much each choice influences both accuracy and fairness, the paper discovers which knobs practitioners should actually spend time tuning versus which ones are safe to leave on a default value.

The paper estimates this via a full factorial or systematic search: for each dataset-method pair, it tries multiple values of each hyperparameter and records both the average accuracy and the gap in accuracy between groups. This tells you: to improve fairness on this dataset, focus your tuning effort on the learning rate and optimizer, not the batch size, because the paper's experiments directly show that changing the learning rate swings disparities by 5-10 percent, while batch size barely moves the needle.

Why it works

Why careful tuning beats specialized algorithms

Most papers on debiasing use weak baselines. They train the standard model (ERM) with just one or two hyperparameter settings—say, the default learning rate—and then compare it to their new method, which was tuned carefully. Naturally, their method looks better, because the comparison is rigged in their favor. But if you tune the baseline just as carefully, it is much stronger. The reason: the baseline is already quite effective at reducing bias if you give it the right conditions to train. A well-chosen learning rate avoids overfitting to the majority group because it prevents the model from memorizing just the common examples; the right number of epochs ensures the model has seen enough diverse examples to learn all groups equally well; a good optimizer (like Adam with momentum) provides stable updates that don't bounce wildly and accidentally favor one group.

Data augmentation works through a different mechanism: by adding more training examples, you increase the diversity and quantity of data from minority groups. This shifts how common each group is in the training data toward balance. The model no longer sees only 90 percent majority-group examples; it sees, say, 70 percent majority and 30 percent minority. With more balanced data, the standard training process naturally learns to predict well for all groups, not just the common one. No explicit fairness constraint is needed. Specialized algorithms add explicit fairness objectives (like adversarial training to make representations group-agnostic, or a regularization term that penalizes accuracy gaps). These add complexity, require their own hyperparameter tuning (how strong should the fairness constraint be?), and often introduce overhead (slower training, more memory). If the baseline is tuned well and augmentation is applied, the additional complexity rarely pays off.

Fairness without harm: the equation

First in words:

For each method, measure the gap between group accuracies, and accept the method only if it doesn't sacrifice accuracy for the majority group.

Then the compact form:

FWH: method M beats baseline if (accuracy_minority_M >= accuracy_minority_baseline) and (accuracy_majority_M >= accuracy_majority_baseline - tolerance)

Every symbol:

accuracy_minority_Maccuracy on minority examples with method M
accuracy_minority_baselineaccuracy on minority examples with baseline ERM
accuracy_majority_Maccuracy on majority examples with method M
accuracy_majority_baselineaccuracy on majority examples with baseline ERM
tolerancemaximum acceptable accuracy loss for the majority group (e.g., 1-2 percent)
A tiny worked example
Baseline ERM: minority group accuracy = 72%, majority group accuracy = 94%.
Method A (adversarial): minority accuracy = 78% (gain 6), majority accuracy = 90% (loss 4).
Method B (data augmentation): minority accuracy = 79% (gain 7), majority accuracy = 93% (loss 1).
With tolerance = 2%: Method A fails (loses 4%, exceeds tolerance). Method B wins (loses only 1%).
Data augmentation respects fairness-without-harm. Specialized algorithms trade off one group for another.

The fairness-without-harm criterion ensures that when we try to help minorities, we don't accidentally punish the majority, because it only accepts methods that improve or keep-stable the accuracy for the majority group; methods that improve fairness via data augmentation tend to achieve this naturally (more diverse data helps everyone), while explicit fairness objectives sometimes trade off one group against another (they may boost minority accuracy but drop majority accuracy below its baseline).

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Insufficient hyperparameter tuning If you do not tune the baseline, specialized methods appear to win when they actually don't. You must try many learning rates, optimizers, and epoch counts for every method to make a fair comparison.
Mixing datasets or metrics across comparisons If paper A uses Demographic Parity and paper B uses Equalized Odds, you cannot directly compare their numbers. A single benchmark must standardize all metrics.
Data augmentation at saturation If your training set is already very large and balanced, augmenting further may help very little, because there is already enough minority data for the model to learn their patterns well. Data augmentation works best when minorities are severely underrepresented, because the original imbalance leaves the model without enough examples to learn minority patterns accurately.
Overhead and latency costs Some specialized algorithms (adversarial training, complex regularization) slow down training or require more memory, because they add extra computations (like adversarial objectives that train competing models, or loss terms that require computing how to move the weights in many different directions). A method that improves fairness by 2 percent but doubles training time may not be practical, because practitioners would rather spend that compute budget on better data or tuning simpler methods.
Done right. Use a standardized benchmark, tune all methods under identical conditions, measure both utility and fairness for all groups, and report overhead. Then practitioners can make an informed choice.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small end-to-end experiment on one dataset (e.g., CelebA for face attribute prediction with a protected attribute like gender). Train four versions: (1) a baseline model with default hyperparameters; (2) the same baseline but tuned carefully (sweep learning rates from 0.0001 to 0.01, try Adam and SGD); (3) the baseline plus data augmentation (oversample minority examples or create synthetic augmentations); and (4) an adversarial fairness method with its own hyperparameter tuning. Measure accuracy separately for each group and compute the disparity (difference in accuracy). Report numbers like: Baseline default disparity 18%, Baseline tuned disparity 6%, Tuned + augmentation disparity 3%, Adversarial fairness disparity 2% (but majority accuracy drops 4%). The demo should make clear that tuning the baseline narrows the gap (because good hyperparameters make the model learn all groups equally), augmentation helps more (because more diverse data naturally balances learning), and specialized algorithms often trade off one group against another (because they explicitly push the model to favor one group even if it hurts the other).

disparity = max(accuracy_group_A, accuracy_group_B) - min(accuracy_group_A, accuracy_group_B); lower is fairer

The demo proves that careful tuning + data augmentation wins more often than specialized fairness algorithms, and that many papers were comparing against weak, un-tuned baselines.
