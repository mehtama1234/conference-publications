Query-Guided Spatial-Temporal-Frequency Interaction for Music Audio-Visual Question Answering

Use the question to guide how you listen to and watch a music video from the very start, not just at the end.

Paper explainersAll themesMultimodal

When you watch a music video and answer a question about it, your brain listens to the audio and watches the video at the same time. But most AI methods separate these: they first grab visual information, then at the very last moment try to understand the audio and the question. QSTar flips this—it uses the question as a guide from the beginning, asking the model to focus on audio frequencies (pitch patterns) and visual motion that matter for answering the specific question.

Zero assumption version

Audio and text come too late

Today's audio-visual question answering works like this: grab the video, extract all the visual details (shapes, motion), then at the very end ask the question and try to make sense of the audio. That's backwards. A beginner watching a music video would hear the question first (Where is the cello?) and immediately listen for cello sounds and watch for cello players.

Existing methods bury the question deep in the model. They treat audio as an afterthought—just something that sits next to the video. They also ignore a key fact: sounds have patterns across frequencies (high pitches vs low pitches), not just patterns over time. A cello has a specific frequency signature that a model never learns to exploit.

The core problem. The question does not guide what the model listens for and watches for until it is far too late, and the model never learns what makes different sounds sound different (their pitch patterns).

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Extract raw features from all three inputs

Pre-trained models pull audio waveform, video frames, and text separately.

Question shapes audio and visual focus early

Instead of ignoring the question, the model asks it immediately: which audio frequencies matter? Which parts of the video?

Refine spatial and temporal patterns

For each frame, the model learns which objects move together and which are still (spatial-temporal refinement).

Highlight question-relevant frequencies

The audio is transformed into frequency bands (like a graphic equalizer), and the model learns which bands (pitch ranges) the question is asking about.

Fuse all three modalities to predict the answer

Audio frequency patterns, visual motion, and question context combine into a single reasoning step.

What the paper changes

What the paper moves

The core change: instead of extracting audio and visual features separately and then combining them, QSTar forces the question to influence audio and visual feature extraction from the very beginning. The question becomes a filter on what the model focuses on.

the object it moves  =  feature extraction order and focus
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Question-relevance score

measured quantity  =  Question-relevance (how much a frequency or spatial region matters for this question)

The paper measures a quantity called a question-relevance score: for each audio frequency band and each visual region, how much does this particular piece contribute to answering this specific question? A cello question has high scores at low frequencies (where cellos live); a visual object-finding question has high scores on the objects that move.

The model estimates this by computing attention weights—numerical scores between 0 and 1 that say 'this audio frequency is important' or 'this image region matters.' The paper's innovation is to compute these scores early and use them to steer everything that follows.

Why it works

Why filtering by question works

A video has thousands of visual details and millions of frequency components in the audio. If the model tries to reason about all of them, it drowns in noise. By filtering early using the question, the model throws away useless information before it accumulates.

For example: you ask 'Where is the cello?' The model immediately computes importance scores that suppress high frequencies (where violins and flutes live) and boost low frequencies (cello territory), because those frequency bands are where cello sounds appear. It also boosts visual regions where someone is holding a large stringed instrument, because large instruments are typically shown in wider shots. Every later step then works on a much cleaner, smaller problem. The question has pruned the search space.

Additionally, audio is weak in standard time-domain representations. A cello and a violin produce visually identical waveforms (up-and-down squiggles), but they occupy different frequency bands (like different radio stations). By explicitly analyzing frequency-domain audio early and filtering it by question-relevance, the model learns the pitch patterns that distinguish instruments.

Math after the picture

The filtering equation

First in words:

Refined audio equals original audio features multiplied by attention weights, where attention weights come from comparing the audio to the question.

Then the compact form:

F_audio_refined = softmax(Q_embedding * F_audio_T / sqrt(d)) * F_audio

Every symbol:

F_audio_refinedthe audio features after filtering by question-relevance
Q_embeddingthe question converted into a list of numbers
F_audiothe raw audio features before filtering
dthe dimension (how many numbers in each feature)
softmax(...)turn raw scores into probabilities (all positive, sum to 1)
*element-wise multiply (scale each audio feature by its relevance)
A tiny worked example
Question: 'What instrument?' -> Q_embedding = [0.2, 0.8, 0.3] (learned representation)
Raw audio feature at frequency band 1: F_audio_1 = [0.1, 0.5, 0.2]
Attention score (dot product): Q · F_1 = 0.2*0.1 + 0.8*0.5 + 0.3*0.2 = 0.46
After softmax over all frequencies, this band gets weight 0.75 (high relevance)
Refined feature: F_audio_refined_1 = 0.75 * [0.1, 0.5, 0.2] = [0.075, 0.375, 0.15]
A frequency band irrelevant to 'instrument' would get weight 0.05, nearly zeroed out.

The model learns that certain audio frequencies 'agree' with the question, so it amplifies those frequencies and suppresses noise.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Polysemous questions (same word, different meanings). If a question uses a word like 'lead' (lead singer vs lead guitar vs playing in the lead), the model might focus on the wrong instrument. Frequency attention alone cannot disambiguate meaning.
Audio and video completely disagree. If the audio says cello but the video shows a violinist, the model has to resolve the conflict. Early fusion helps because the question-relevance scores steer both modalities the same way, but does not guarantee consistency if the training data contains such contradictions.
Question uses vague language. Asking 'What is happening?' is harder than 'Where is the cello?' because many frequencies and regions could be relevant.
Done right. The model succeeds when the question names a specific instrument or property that maps clearly to frequency bands or spatial regions, and the audio-visual ground-truth agrees.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple audio-visual dataset: 10 video clips (instrumental solos—a cello, a violin, a flute, etc.), each 5 seconds. Pair each with three question types: 'What instrument?' (instrument identification), 'Where is the player?' (spatial), 'When does the note rise?' (temporal). Run a baseline method that concatenates audio and visual late, then run QSTar. Measure accuracy on each question type.

Accuracy = (correct answers) / (total questions); compare baseline vs QSTar per question type.

The demo must show that QSTar's accuracy gain is biggest on 'What instrument?'—the question type that benefits most from frequency-domain attention—and smallest on vague questions like 'What happens?'
