Information Estimation with Discrete Diffusion

Estimate information on discrete data by learning discrete noise processes directly, instead of forcing sequences or symbol arrays through a continuous embedding pipeline first.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

Mutual information, Kullback-Leibler divergence, and entropy, meaning uncertainty in a probability law, all compare probability laws. On truly discrete data such as DNA motifs, text tokens, meaning discrete text symbols, or spin configurations, the clean object is the probability law over symbols themselves. Many recent estimators avoid that direct route. They first map the symbols into continuous embeddings, meaning learned continuous feature lists, and then apply tools designed for continuous variables. That can work, but it introduces two avoidable burdens: the embedding model becomes another learned object that can distort the probability law, and the estimator must cope with high-dimensional continuous representations that were not part of the original problem. This paper builds the estimator around a discrete diffusion process instead. By learning how discrete states are corrupted and denoised in continuous time, InfoSEDD can estimate the log-probability ratios needed for information quantities on the original symbol space. The result is a method that stays closer to the data-generating domain and plugs into pretrained discrete generative models when available.

Zero assumption version

Why discrete information is awkward for embedding-based estimators

Information quantities ask how much probability mass one law places where another does not, or how uncertain one law is by itself. On symbol data, those questions are about counts and combinations of symbols.

Embedding-based neural estimators change the domain before answering the question. They turn symbols into continuous features, then estimate information in that new space. That adds modeling choices that are hard to justify from first principles. The embedding may blur distinctions between symbol patterns, and the continuous estimator must work in a space that can be much larger than the original alphabet structure.

The paper's move. Learn a discrete diffusion model whose denoising scores recover log-probability ratios on the original symbol space, because those ratios are the core ingredient behind many information measures.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Define a discrete corruption process.

Use a continuous-time Markov chain, which means a random jump process over symbol states, so the model has a controlled path from clean data to noise.

Train a denoiser at different noise levels.

Predict which clean symbols could have produced each noisy state, because reverse-time information is what reveals the underlying probability law.

Recover log-probability ratios.

Translate denoising predictions into ratios between data laws, because information measures are built from those ratios.

Average the right log-ratio under samples.

Form the desired divergence, entropy, or mutual-information estimate by integrating or averaging the recovered log terms over the relevant data law, meaning the probability rule that generated the samples.

Reuse pretrained discrete models when available.

Plug in an existing denoiser if one already models the symbol domain well, because the estimator needs an accurate reverse process more than a task-specific embedding.

The denoiser itself is not the final quantity of interest. The key hidden quantity is the log-probability ratio between laws, or between a law and its factorized counterpart, that the reverse process lets us estimate. Mutual information and divergence formulas depend on exactly those ratios.

This matters because a good symbol generator is not automatically a good information estimator. The method must recover the right ratio at the right noise levels. The paper's construction uses discrete diffusion theory so the denoising model carries information about how likely each clean state is under the law being studied.

Why it works

Why discrete diffusion gives information estimates

A continuous-time corruption process provides many paired views of the same law: one clean, one partially noised. If a model learns the reverse process accurately, it has learned which clean states remain plausible behind each noisy state and at each noise level.

That reverse knowledge is enough to recover log-probability differences, because the probability of reversing toward one clean state rather than another depends on how much mass the original law assigns to those states. Once those log differences are available, standard information formulas can be written as expectations of them.

The practical advantage is that the estimator never has to invent a continuous hidden feature space for the data. This matters on long text or biological sequences because the main challenge is already the combinatorics of discrete patterns. Avoiding an extra embedding model removes one source of distortion and one source of tuning.

Math after the picture

Ratio-based information view

First in words:

Estimate an information quantity by averaging a recovered log-probability ratio. For mutual information, the ratio compares the joint law with the product of its marginals.

Then the compact form:

information_measure = mean over samples of recovered_log_ratio(relevant laws at the sample)

Every symbol:

recovered_log_ratio(relevant laws at the sample)the log-probability difference inferred from the learned reverse diffusion process
relevant lawsfor example the joint law and the product of marginals for mutual information
mean over samplesthe average under data drawn from the law being studied
information_measurethe final divergence, entropy, or mutual-information quantity
A tiny worked example
Suppose a text bigram appears often under the true joint probability law but much less often under a product-of-marginals baseline.
A correct reverse diffusion model should assign a larger recovered log ratio to that bigram than to a near-independent pair.
Averaging those recovered log ratios over samples yields a positive mutual-information estimate.
If the denoiser cannot distinguish the joint law from the factorized baseline, the recovered ratios shrink toward zero.
That is why denoising quality directly affects the quality of the information estimate.

The method estimates information through recovered probability ratios, not through distances in an embedding space.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

A poor reverse model gives poor ratios. If the denoiser fails to model the reverse jump process accurately, the recovered log-probability ratios will be biased, which means every downstream information estimate inherits that bias.
The corruption process must suit the data domain. A badly chosen noise process can erase useful structure too quickly or in the wrong pattern, so the reverse model learns a task that does not reflect the true discrete law well.
Rare symbol patterns remain hard. Information measures are sensitive to low-probability events. If the training data contain too few rare but important patterns, the reverse process may estimate their ratios poorly.
Done right. When the reverse diffusion model fits the symbol law well, the estimator works because the needed probability ratios are read directly from the discrete generative process.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build synthetic discrete datasets where the true mutual information or divergence is known, such as short binary sequences with controlled dependence strength. Compare an embedding-based estimator against InfoSEDD.

The demo should report estimation error across sequence length and dependence strength, and it should include a consistency check on held-out discrete domains. A convincing result shows InfoSEDD staying accurate as the symbol structure grows because the estimator models the discrete law itself rather than a continuous surrogate.

ratio_error = mean over test samples of absolute(recovered_log_ratio - true_log_ratio)

The key contribution is not diffusion by itself. It is using diffusion as a direct route to the probability ratios that define information on discrete data.
