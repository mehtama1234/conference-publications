Computational Arbitrage in AI Model Markets

If model providers charge different prices for different success rates, a broker can buy model calls from them, combine them, and resell verified answers for less while still making money.

Paper explainersAll themesOther

This paper studies a simple market fact. A customer does not care which model solved a task; the customer cares whether the answer is correct and what it costs. If several model providers sell access at different prices and with different success rates, another company can sit in the middle, buy model calls from those providers, and choose where to spend each dollar. That middle company is an arbitrageur. It does not train its own model. It earns money by routing each task to the cheapest path that is still likely to produce a verifiable answer under the customer's budget. The paper shows that this is already possible in a real coding benchmark, and then traces what happens to prices, profits, and entry barriers when such brokers appear.

Zero assumption version

Selling model access creates room for middlemen

Suppose two providers sell access to models for a task with a verifiable outcome, such as fixing a GitHub issue and checking whether the tests pass. One provider may be cheap but miss often. Another may be costly but succeed more often. A customer may be willing to pay up to a fixed amount for a correct answer, but the customer may not know which provider gives the best cost-to-success tradeoff on that particular task.

If a broker can estimate those tradeoffs more carefully than the customer, the broker can buy calls from the providers and resell only the successful outcome. The key fact is that the broker prices a completed solution, while the providers price raw model calls. Those are not the same product. Once the products differ, price gaps appear. The paper asks when those gaps are large enough to support profit, how stable they remain outside one benchmark, and how they change the broader market.

The core problem. Providers price model usage one call at a time, but customers buy completed answers. That mismatch creates room for arbitrage.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes the unit of sale. Instead of treating a model call as the product, it treats a verified solution as the product and uses provider calls only as ingredients.

the object it moves  =  the pricing layer between customers and model providers
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: expected profit per task

measured quantity  =  Expected margin after paying for all model calls

The broker has to reason about a hidden average, not a single outcome. A cheap provider may fail on many tasks, and a costly provider may succeed often enough to justify its price. The broker therefore tracks expected profit per task, which means average customer payment minus average spending on all attempted calls. This number decides whether arbitrage exists at all.

That quantity matters because the broker does not need every task to be profitable. The broker needs the portfolio of tasks to be profitable on average. A routing plan that loses money on hard tasks can still work if easy tasks earn enough to cover them. The paper measures this in a verifiable domain, so success and failure are not vague judgments; the task either passes the check or it does not.

Why it works

Why it works: resell success, not attempts

Arbitrage works here because model providers and customers value different things. Providers charge for access to computation. Customers pay for a completed answer. If the broker can predict which provider sequence gives the cheapest path to a correct answer, the broker can transform raw calls into a higher-level service and keep the gap as profit.

The routing step matters because price alone is not enough. A model that costs half as much but succeeds one tenth as often is not actually cheaper per solved task, because many more failed attempts have to be paid for before one success arrives. The broker therefore combines price and success chance into one decision rule. In the case study, even simple rules earn money because they avoid paying premium prices on tasks that the cheaper model already handles well, while still keeping a fallback path for harder cases.

The market analysis matters because arbitrage changes incentives. If many brokers can undercut providers, customer prices fall because brokers compete away part of the gap. At the same time, smaller providers gain a path into the market because a broker can include them in a bundle before they are strong enough to win customers directly.

Math after the picture

The equation: revenue minus routed call cost

First in words:

A broker earns money on a task when the payment for a verified answer is larger than the average amount spent across all provider calls used to get that answer.

Then the compact form:

expected_margin = customer_price - sum_over_providers(call_cost * probability_call_is_used)

Every symbol:

expected_marginaverage profit per task for the broker
customer_pricewhat the customer pays for a successful verified answer
call_costprice of one call to a chosen provider
probability_call_is_usedhow often that provider is actually invoked under the routing rule
A tiny worked example
Customer pays 10 dollars for a verified fix.
Cheap provider costs 2 dollars and solves 60 of 100 tasks.
Premium fallback costs 5 dollars and is used only on the 40 remaining tasks.
Average spending is 2 + 0.4 times 5 = 4 dollars.
Expected margin is 10 - 4 = 6 dollars per task before other overhead.

The point is not a single task. The point is the average over many tasks under one routing policy.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Success estimates can drift. If the broker learned provider quality on one task mix and the market shifts, the routing rule can spend money in the wrong places because past win rates no longer describe current tasks.
Verification may be incomplete. The setup works best when success is easy to check. If the customer cannot verify quality cleanly, the broker can resell weak answers and the pricing logic becomes harder to trust.
Providers can react. Providers may change prices, bundle products, or limit access when brokers start extracting margin, because the source of profit is the gap between provider pricing and solution pricing.
Done right. Use a verifiable task, estimate success rates carefully, set a firm stop rule, and update the routing policy as prices and models change.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simulator with two providers, a task set with known success rates, and a customer who pays only for verified solutions. Let a broker choose between cheap-first, premium-first, and fallback routing rules. Track customer price, broker spending, and broker profit across many tasks. Then add a second broker and show that customer prices move downward because the brokers compete away part of the margin.

margin_rate = total_customer_payments / total_provider_spend

The demo should make one point visible: the broker wins when it prices solved tasks while buying raw model calls below that solved-task value.
