Video Generation for Robots: a Benchmark and Dataset

Build a precise evaluation standard that exposes why video models fail at robot tasks, then solve it with a carefully curated dataset.

Paper explainersAll themesRobotics & Control

Video models trained on internet video can generate convincing-looking robot videos—but they often show movements that are physically impossible or that complete the wrong task. This paper measures this gap with a robotics evaluation benchmark (RBench), then fixes it by curating and annotating 4 million robot videos (RoVid-X) with physical property labels.

Zero assumption version

Video models learn from the wrong training diet

A robot video generation model learns by watching thousands of training videos and predicting the next frame. If you feed it internet videos (humans dancing, cars driving) plus uncurated robot footage, it learns average pixel patterns—smooth movements, realistic lighting. But robots are different: they have hard joint limits, they cannot defy gravity, and a small gripper cannot lift a 10-pound object. A beginner might think: 'Just train on more data.' The problem is that more data is not the same as better data, because a neural network learns by averaging the examples it sees. If your training set contains videos where robots perform impossible tasks (e.g., lifting 10 pounds with a 2-pound gripper), the model learns to predict those impossible tasks, because that pattern exists in the data it is averaging. Just adding more bad examples does not fix this.

The second problem: how do you even know your model is failing? Traditional metrics measure pixel similarity (does the generated frame match a real frame?) and they score high on videos with natural-looking movements, even if those movements are physically impossible or complete the wrong task. You need different metrics that actually test whether the robot could perform the predicted action.

In one sentence. Precise task-level evaluation reveals that video models fail at robot realism, and the fix is training on carefully curated robot videos labeled with physical constraints.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes two things together: the evaluation standard (what you measure to judge success) and the training data (what videos the model learns from). The evaluation is necessary to spot the problem; the data is necessary to fix it.

The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity: Physical plausibility

measured quantity  =  Physical plausibility

This is the degree to which a generated robot video respects the physical laws and constraints that a real robot obeys. A robot arm has joint limits (it cannot rotate its wrist more than 180 degrees). A gripper can only lift objects up to its weight limit. A humanoid robot cannot teleport. Pixel-based metrics do not measure this; they measure whether pixels look realistic.

RBench measures physical plausibility by explicitly checking whether the generated motion respects these constraints. For each video, evaluators verify that joint angles stay within bounds, that contact forces are physically realistic, and that the task is actually completed. The paper's core insight is that you cannot know if your model is learning physical realism until you measure it directly.

Why it works

Why it works: signal from clean examples

A neural network is a pattern-matching system. It learns from examples. If your training examples are a soup of internet videos and uncurated robot footage, the model learns to predict whatever is most common in that soup—which includes impossible movements, because nobody filtered them out. The model has no way to know that a gripper cannot hold 10 pounds if the training data shows a gripper holding 10 pounds.

Curating and labeling the data sends a different signal. When 4 million robot videos are filtered to remove physical impossibilities and then annotated with their constraints (e.g., this gripper can hold up to 2 pounds), the model learns a different pattern: what is possible given the embodiment. The physical property annotations act as explicit labels for what the model should learn. The model sees that when a heavy object is present, the gripper does not attempt to lift it. It learns the constraint because the data embodies it.

Measuring the signal: the evaluation metric

First in words:

Overall score is a weighted sum of physical plausibility checks (joint validity, contact realism, task completion) divided by total possible points, plus a term that penalizes pixel-level hallucination if it conflicts with physical constraints.

Then the compact form:

score = (J_valid + C_real + T_complete) / max_points - alpha * hallucination_penalty

Every symbol:

J_validnumber of joint angles within physical limits
C_realnumber of contact forces within realistic range
T_completetask is completed correctly (binary: 1 if yes, 0 if no)
max_pointstotal possible points (sum of all constraints)
alphaweight on hallucination penalty (how much to penalize physical impossibilities)
hallucination_penaltycount of physically invalid frames in the generated video
A tiny worked example
Robot: single-arm gripper with 3 joints (limits: 0-90 degrees each).
Generated video has 10 frames.
Frames 1-7: all joints stay within limits. Frame 8: joint 1 rotates to 110 degrees (invalid). Frames 9-10: valid again.
J_valid = 9 valid frames / 10 = 9 points.
Contact forces all realistic: C_real = 10 points.
Task was to move a light block; frames 1-7 move it, frame 8 stops (hallucination), frames 9-10 resume: T_complete = 1 (task done) = 10 points.
max_points = 30. alpha = 0.5. hallucination_penalty = 1 (one invalid frame).
score = (9 + 10 + 10) / 30 - 0.5 * 1 = 0.97 - 0.5 = 0.47.
A model with no physical violations would score (10+10+10)/30 = 1.0. This one lost points for the one invalid joint angle.

The metric makes physical plausibility visible and measurable, so you know whether your training data has worked.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Evaluation is expensive. RBench requires fine-grained human annotation of physical constraints for each sample. If you cut corners on annotation quality, the benchmark will not catch real failures.
Data curation has a bottleneck. Filtering 4 million videos and annotating physical properties requires automation (automated pose estimation, object detection), and these tools have errors. Errors in annotations leak back into training.
Benchmark covers only five task categories. RBench evaluates common manipulation, long-horizon planning, multi-entity collaboration, spatial reasoning, and visual reasoning across four embodiments. A model trained and tested only on these may fail on a sixth task type never seen in either set.
Evaluation catches what training data cannot fix. A model cannot learn from examples it never sees. If RoVid-X is missing a task (e.g., deformable object manipulation), the model will fail on it, because there is no training signal in the data showing what deformable-object actions should look like. No amount of finetuning on RoVid-X will help, because the knowledge is not there to learn from. This is not a failure of the method; it is a reminder that evaluation and data collection must be aligned.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a video generation model, split it into two versions: one finetuned on random uncurated robot videos, one on RoVid-X. Generate videos for the same five test prompts with both. Measure each output on RBench metrics (joint validity, contact realism, task completion). The demo should show that the RoVid-X version has higher physical plausibility (e.g., 0.71 vs 0.34) and task success (e.g., 0.78 vs 0.41), while pixel-realism metrics stay similar.

improvement = (plausibility_RoVidX - plausibility_uncurated) / plausibility_uncurated

The demo must make impossible to miss: a model trained on curated data with physical constraints outperforms one trained on uncurated data when measured on physical realism and task success (0.71 vs 0.34 plausibility, 0.78 vs 0.41 task completion). This happens because the constraints in the labeled data teach the model to avoid impossible movements, whereas uncurated data contains these mistakes and the model learns to replicate them.
