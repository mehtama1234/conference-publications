Solving equations and modeling systems

Training neural networks to act like mathematical equations to predict how physical systems evolve over time—faster and cheaper than traditional computer simulations.

All themesField mapMathematicsAll explainers
What this is

Physics and engineering describe how things change using equations — differential equations, to be precise — that say: given the current state, what is the next tiny step? A ball falls because gravity pulls it down. A chemical reaction speeds up as temperature rises because molecules move faster and collide more often. These equations are exact, but solving them by hand is impossible for most real systems. Scientists use computers to simulate step by step, but for large systems (fluids, weather, climate), even computers take days or weeks to run one simulation.

The bottleneck is that every tiny step costs computation time. So researchers ask: could we train a neural network to act like the equation itself? Feed it the current state, and it predicts the next state instantly — thousands of times faster than a real simulation, because a forward pass through a network is just arithmetic on the GPU instead of solving differential equations. The tricky part is that the network must obey the physics exactly: energy cannot disappear, momentum cannot create itself, and the same physical law must apply everywhere. Standard neural networks do not enforce these rules — they just fit the data, and they often break the laws of physics in ways that cascade into garbage predictions.

The papers in this cluster train neural networks in ways that respect physics. Some encode the equations directly into the network architecture so it cannot violate the laws. Others train on simulated data from known equations, then test whether the network really learned the equation or just memorized specific scenarios. Some networks learn to handle multiple time scales at once — a fast reaction coupled to a slow change — which is where most naive models fail. The goal is a surrogate model: a fake version of reality that is fast, accurate enough for the next discovery, and trustworthy because it respects the laws that we know are true.

How the work breaks down

Training neural networks to solve physics equations splits into five natural directions. First, encoding known physics directly into the network architecture so it cannot violate conservation laws. Second, learning operators that map initial conditions to solutions anywhere in time, rather than step-by-step prediction. Third, handling systems with wildly different time scales (fast reactions mixed with slow changes) without losing accuracy. Fourth, building foundation models that transfer across different equations and systems. Fifth, making predictions stable and accurate over long horizons without error cascade.

Physics-Informed Architecture: Enforcing Laws by Design

The core problem: standard neural networks fit data without caring about physics. A network trained on molecular dynamics might predict a state where energy disappeared, or where momentum appeared from nowhere, because the network only memorized patterns in the training set. These violations cascade: one bad step leads to garbage predictions a few steps later. The solution is to encode the physical laws into how the network is built, so it cannot violate them no matter what weights the training finds.

Harmonized Cone solves this by constraining how the network weights change during training so they never conflict with physics constraints. When training pulls the network in two directions at once (fit the data versus obey the law), Harmonized Cone finds a direction that satisfies both, guaranteeing the training will converge even in hard non-convex problems. Euler-Poincaré Neural Dynamics builds the network structure itself from geometric mechanics principles, so momentum and energy conservation happen automatically in the math. FluxNet restricts the network to only learn local transport that preserves total quantity (mass, energy, particles), making sure the predictions stay bounded and conserve totals everywhere. Mitigating Training Instabilities removes instabilities during training by aligning the loss surfaces, stabilizing the network as it learns complex dynamics. Together these papers show that designing the network architecture around physics constraints is more reliable than teaching it to behave afterwards because the physics-first design prevents the training from creating violations in the first place.

These papers compete on different aspects of the same goal: make the architecture itself embody the law, not just the training. Harmonized Cone and Mitigating Training Instabilities both fix how the training works; the first guarantees convergence while the second focuses on stability because instabilities during training can cause the network to diverge or get stuck. FluxNet goes further by making conservation happen in every local piece, enabling long rollouts without error creep because each step preserves totals locally. Euler-Poincaré is the most ambitious, rethinking the whole network around symmetry groups because symmetries in physics are the deepest constraint. Together, these approaches are more reliable than trying to teach the network physics rules after it is trained, since encoding physics upfront prevents misguided training from creating errors.

Operator Learning: Direct Solution Mapping

The bottleneck in traditional simulation is the time step: to predict what happens next, you solve the equation step by step, which takes hours. The insight is that you can train a neural network to act as an operator: given the current state (a grid of temperature values, a fluid velocity field), instantly output the state at any later time without stepping through intermediate times. This is thousands of times faster because a network forward pass on a GPU is just arithmetic, while solving an equation takes many iterative steps.

KANO (Kolmogorov-Arnold Neural Operator) generalizes to position-dependent equations where older methods failed by using special basis functions that adapt to the problem's structure. Riesz Neural Operator achieves superior accuracy by building on functional analysis foundations, handling complex real-world data that simpler operators cannot parse. Simple Yet Effective paper reduces computation by learning which parts of the domain matter for each prediction, cutting error 17% on benchmarks. Locally Subspace-Informed combines the operator with multiscale coarse-grain methods, speeding up the expensive offline setup phase while keeping accuracy higher than either method alone. All four are fundamentally learning: given the current state, what is the state later, all at once.

These papers differ in their core mechanism. KANO and Riesz both map functional spaces but use different mathematical foundations. Simple Yet Effective focuses on efficiency by cutting the spatial scope because most grid points do not matter for the final answer. Locally Subspace-Informed is the only one that couples with classical multiscale methods (GMsFEM), showing operator learning works as a faster replacement for the costly tuning stage because it learns the subspace automatically instead of hand-tuning it.

Multi-Scale and Chaotic Dynamics: Capturing All Time Scales

Many real systems have two clocks ticking at once: a fast clock (a chemical reaction that completes in milliseconds) coupled to a slow clock (a temperature change over minutes). Most neural networks trained on this data fail catastrophically. They learn to predict the slow parts accurately but explode on the fast parts, or vice versa. The same problem appears in chaotic systems where tiny errors amplify exponentially. The challenge is that a single network must handle vastly different timescales without breaking.

Panda trains on low-dimensional ODEs (simple systems with few variables) and discovers it spontaneously learns to forecast PDEs (complex systems with spatial variation) without retraining. More surprisingly, it preserves short-term accuracy and long-term statistics even in chaotic regimes where tiny errors normally blow up. RecurrFNO handles this by using depth (deeper networks) as a knob: at test time, you can trade accuracy for speed by running fewer layers, and it still beats much larger fixed-depth models on high-dimensional fluids and active matter. Hypergraph Neural ODE models disease progression by treating time as continuous, capturing when fast biochemical changes happen within a slow disease timeline. Spectral-guided Physics Distillation learns from physics simulation outputs by decomposing them into frequency bands, so slow trends are accurate and fast fluctuations are stable across molecules, proteins, and human motion. The result is that it stays accurate for long predictions without errors cascading.

All four papers solve the cascade problem differently. Panda achieves it through architectural choices that preserve statistics. RecurrFNO uses adaptive depth to handle variable timescale separation. Hypergraph Neural ODE treats time continuously instead of in discrete steps. Spectral-guided Physics Distillation explicitly breaks the dynamics into frequency components so each scale is handled separately. They all show that multi-scale dynamics requires explicit design for the problem.

Foundation Models and Transfer: One Network for Many Equations

Training a new neural network from scratch for every new equation is expensive. The breakthrough is that a single pretrained network can adapt to new problems quickly, the way large language models adapt to new tasks. This requires learning representations that capture the structure common to all equations: the network must understand that temperature diffuses the same way in metals as in fluids, that momentum is conserved in shocks as in water waves.

ATOM pretrains on a large database of molecular dynamics and then transfers to new unseen molecules with zero retraining, matching or beating task-specific networks on three standard benchmarks. PINFDiT bridges general-purpose (foundation model on broad forecasting) and domain-specific (physics constraints), showing it learns physics by incorporation into the diffusion process itself, enabling zero-shot transfer across domains. DisentangO learns latent features that are both predictive and interpretable, meaning the network discovers which hidden variables (energy, how density changes in space, concentration) actually matter, bridging prediction and physical understanding. Foundation Inference Models for Ordinary Differential Equations (FIM-ODE) achieves strong zero-shot performance on unseen ODE systems and can be refined quickly with a few examples per new equation. Together these show that pretraining on physics simulation data teaches the network reusable concepts.

These papers tackle transfer at different levels. ATOM is the most direct: one network, many molecules. PINFDiT uses the diffusion structure to encode physics so it transfers across domains because diffusion is a universal process. DisentangO focuses on interpretability as the key to transfer because when the network learns what actually changes, it can transfer that knowledge to new systems where the same quantities change. FIM-ODE is the most pragmatic because even light pretraining teaches the network enough structure that it can adapt quickly with few new examples. They all suggest physics has enough regularities across systems because energy, momentum, and other conservation laws apply everywhere, so learning once and adapting quickly is better than learning each new problem from scratch.

Training and Inference: Fixing Numerical Stability and Convergence

Traditional PINNs (physics-informed neural networks) have a training curse: it takes forever to converge, and when it does, the solution is often wrong because multiple objectives pull the training in conflicting directions. The problem is that the loss function has many terms: fit the data, satisfy the differential equation at scattered points, obey boundary conditions. These pull the training in opposite directions, stalling convergence. Fast training requires rethinking the whole approach.

Fast Training of Accurate Physics-Informed Neural Networks Without Gradient Descent uses completely different solver (least squares instead of gradient descent) to train a thousand times faster with better accuracy on extreme cases like shocks because least squares avoids the conflicting objectives problem. Iterative Training of Physics-Informed Neural Networks with Fourier-Enhanced Features shows that encoding high-frequency structure into the network from the start (using random Fourier features) dramatically improves the network's ability to capture wiggly, oscillatory solutions because the network does not have to learn these patterns from scratch. TINNs (Time-Induced Neural Networks) embeds time structure into the network architecture itself, achieving 4x better error and 10x faster convergence than standard PINNs by making time a first-class citizen of the network design because this forces the network to respect how time actually flows. Learning to Think in Physics improves generalization by aligning internal representations with actual physics, reducing residual errors 66%, improving performance on unseen data 49%, and converging 2x faster because enforcing physics alignment prevents the network from memorizing shortcuts. All four papers attack the core training problem: the network learns fast and solves hard PDEs that standard methods fail on.

These papers represent four distinct improvements to training. Fast Training changes the solver algorithm entirely (away from gradient descent) because the algorithm itself was creating the conflicts. Fourier-enhanced Features improves the network's expressiveness for oscillatory solutions because high-frequency patterns are built in from the start. TINNs makes time explicit in the architecture so the network cannot violate causality. Learning to Think in Physics enforces alignment between what the network learns and what physics actually says because this prevents the network from taking shortcuts that break physics. All show that training is a major bottleneck and fixing it matters more than architecture for these problems because even the best architecture cannot overcome bad training dynamics.
