COGNOS: Making Anomaly Scores Honest

Train a model to produce noise that looks like ordinary random scatter, then use that property to denoise the anomaly signal.

Paper explainersAll themesTime Series & Sequential

When a time series model reconstructs a normal pattern, the leftover error should be pure random noise. But most models are trained to minimize error size, not to make that error actually random. This means the anomaly scores jump around wildly, making it hard to spot real problems. COGNOS fixes this by adding a constraint during training that forces the errors to truly look like random noise, then applies a denoiser at the end to smooth them out.

Zero assumption version

Noisy, unreliable anomaly signals

Time series anomaly detection works by having a model learn to reconstruct normal patterns. When you feed it a normal data point, the model should output something close to it; when you feed it an abnormal point, the gap between input and output (called the residual) should be large. That gap is the anomaly score.

But here is the catch: the model is trained using a simple rule called Mean Squared Error, which just says make the error as small as possible. This rule does not care whether the error looks like random noise or structured weirdness. So the residuals end up messy and jump around unpredictably, even for normal data. This noise in the score makes it hard to set a threshold that reliably separates normal from abnormal, because when the background is jagged any threshold is either missing real anomalies (set it too high) or flagging normal noise as an anomaly (set it too low).

The beginner's try. Just use error size as the anomaly score, but the noise ruins the signal.

The algorithm as a flow

What it actually does

Step 1: During training, track the errors.

As the model learns to reconstruct, record what the errors look like.

Step 2: Add a constraint to the training rule.

Modify the training objective to penalize the model if errors do not look random.

Step 3: Require errors to be statistically Gaussian.

Force the pattern of error sizes to match a bell curve (most small, few large).

Step 4: Require errors to be flat across frequencies.

In the frequency domain, make sure no frequency is stronger than others, because if low or high frequencies dominate, the error contains a hidden pattern that a denoiser can exploit.

Step 5: At test time, smooth the raw anomaly scores.

Apply a filter to reduce noise and expose the true anomaly signal.

What the paper changes

The one thing that changes

The model's reconstruction errors. Before, they were whatever the MSE training produced. After COGNOS, they are constrained to look like random noise: centered around zero, spread consistently, and with no pattern across time.

the object it moves  =  reconstruction errors
The hidden quantity

The statistical shape of the noise

measured quantity  =  How random the reconstruction errors are

The paper measures how close the model's errors are to true random noise. This has two parts: first, whether the errors follow a Gaussian bell curve (the shape of the distribution), and second, whether all frequencies in the errors are equally strong (flat power spectrum, meaning no hidden pattern). Most methods never check this; COGNOS makes it explicit because only when the noise is truly random can you be sure that a big jump in the error score means an anomaly, not a coincidental spike in the noise itself.

During training, COGNOS estimates this by looking at how much the actual error distribution diverges from a perfect random pattern. It uses two measures: one that checks the shape using a mathematical distance called Maximum Mean Discrepancy (a way to compare two distributions), and one that checks whether the frequency spectrum is flat by comparing it to a uniform distribution using information theory.

Why it works

Why making errors random reveals anomalies

When the model is forced to produce random-looking errors on normal data, you know that any big jump in the error is not just noise—it must mean something strange happened. This is the key: randomness is boring and predictable. Anomalies are not.

During training, COGNOS adds a penalty that punishes the model if its errors do not match the properties of random noise. This penalty has three parts. First, it checks whether the size of errors follows a bell curve (Gaussian constraint). Second, it checks whether the errors contain no repeated pattern across frequencies (spectral flatness). Third, it keeps the overall error scale free to adapt to each channel (allowing for different sensor noise levels). Once trained this way, at test time you apply a Kalman filter (a standard technique that combines what you expect to see with what you actually see) to smooth the anomaly score. The filter works better when the noise underneath is already random, because the filter assumes the background is clean randomness; when it sees a big jump, it can tell right away this is an anomaly and not just noise.

Math after the picture

The training rule with added constraints

First in words:

The training loss is: original error loss, plus a penalty for errors not looking random (Gaussian shape), plus a penalty for errors not being flat in frequency domain.

Then the compact form:

L_total = L_MSE + lambda_gaussian * L_Gaussian + lambda_spectral * L_spectral

Every symbol:

L_MSEsquared error between input and reconstruction
L_Gaussianhow far the error distribution is from a bell curve
L_spectralhow uneven the frequencies in the error are
lambda_gaussianhow strongly to enforce Gaussian shape
lambda_spectralhow strongly to enforce flat frequencies
A tiny worked example
Say a model reconstruction error has three components:
(1) MSE contribution: squared error = 4.5
(2) Gaussian shape penalty: distribution distance = 0.8
(3) Spectral flatness penalty: frequency unevenness = 1.2
With lambda_gaussian=2.0 and lambda_spectral=3.0:
L_total = 4.5 + 2.0*0.8 + 3.0*1.2 = 4.5 + 1.6 + 3.6 = 9.7
If the error distribution becomes more random, penalties drop:
L_Gaussian becomes 0.3, L_spectral becomes 0.4
L_total = 4.5 + 2.0*0.3 + 3.0*0.4 = 4.5 + 0.6 + 1.2 = 6.3
Training prefers the second state (lower loss), so it shapes errors toward randomness.

This penalty biases the model to produce random-looking errors, which become a reliable baseline for spotting true anomalies.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

The noise floor changes over time. If sensors get noisier or quieter as you operate (sensor degradation), the assumed noise level becomes wrong. The Kalman filter assumes a fixed noise covariance (learned from training), but real systems drift.
The method only works with MSE-trained models. COGNOS is built on the assumption that reconstruction errors should be Gaussian. If the model uses a different loss (contrastive, discrepancy-based), the framework does not apply; the constraint and filter are not designed for those objectives.
Computational cost during training. The spectral and Gaussian checks add about 187 milliseconds per training iteration. For large datasets or long sequences, this can be slow.
Done right: test before deployment. Validate that the noise is actually random on your specific sensor data, set the lambda weights carefully, and verify that the Kalman filter is tuned to your operational noise floor.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a simple reconstruction autoencoder on a multivariate time series (e.g., synthetic sine waves with injected anomalies, or a real dataset like power consumption or network traffic). Train it twice: once with standard MSE, once with COGNOS. For each, compute the anomaly scores and apply the Kalman filter. Measure the stability of the scores by computing how much they vary on pure normal data (lower is better). Plot the scores for a window that includes both normal and anomalous points, and verify that anomalies stand out clearly above the noise in the COGNOS version but are buried in noise in the standard version.

anomaly_recall = (true_anomalies_detected) / (all_anomalies), false_alarm_rate = (normal_flagged_as_anomaly) / (all_normal_points), score_stability = std_dev_of_scores_on_normal_data

The demo must show that COGNOS produces a flatter baseline on normal data, making a fixed threshold far more reliable than standard MSE training, because when normal data produces consistent small scores and anomalies produce rare large scores, you can pick one threshold that catches anomalies without false alarms.
