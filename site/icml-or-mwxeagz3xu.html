Measuring Agents in Production

Study how deployed language-model agents are really built, checked, and kept under control once they are running in real organizations.

Paper explainersAll themesAgents & Tool Use

This paper is not a new agent training method. It is a measurement study of systems that are already in use. The authors interview builders and survey practitioners to find out what kinds of agent designs survive contact with real work, what teams care about when they judge success, and what keeps failing after deployment. The main message is that production agents are usually short-horizon systems with strong human oversight because teams trade freedom for control.

Why production agent practice is hard to see from papers alone

A research paper can show a strong demo, yet that does not tell you what companies actually keep in production. Real deployments face approval rules, cost limits, handoff needs, and users who will stop trusting the system after a few bad outcomes.

Without direct evidence from deployed systems, researchers can aim at the wrong target. They may study long open-ended agents while most real teams use shorter flows with checkpoints because those flows are easier to check, pause, and repair.

The paper's move in one sentence. Measure live deployment practice directly through interviews and surveys so that agent research can be compared against the constraints that real teams face.

What the paper changes

What the paper changes

The paper changes our picture of what an agent system usually looks like after deployment. Instead of assuming that high autonomy is the default target, it shows that many teams purposely constrain the agent because constraint makes behavior easier to predict and easier to review.

the object it moves  =  the field's estimate of the autonomy level that production teams actually use
The hidden quantity

The hidden quantity

measured quantity  =  how much uncertainty a team is willing to tolerate from an agent

The central hidden quantity is the tolerance for uncertain behavior inside a real organization. A deployed agent is not judged only by whether it can finish a task once. It is judged by whether teams can trust it to behave acceptably over many runs.

This quantity does not appear as one number in a dashboard. You infer it from design choices such as step caps, required human approval, and reliance on prompt control because those choices reveal how much freedom the team is comfortable granting.

Why it works

Why this works: deployment choices reveal the real target

If you ask teams what they want, they may describe an ambitious long-term vision. If you inspect the system they actually run, you see the design compromises they accepted because those compromises are what passed operational review.

That is why direct field measurement matters here. Interviews and surveys expose which choices repeat across domains. When many teams independently limit step count, favor prompt control, and keep human review, the pattern suggests that these choices solve a shared problem: they reduce unpredictable behavior by keeping the system easier to inspect and interrupt.

Math after the picture

Summarize production practice as weighted evidence from teams

First in words:

A simple summary is to treat each survey or interview response as evidence about one deployment choice, then average those choices across teams. The larger the average for a choice, the more common that choice is in production practice.

Then the compact form:

practice_score(choice) = (1 / N) * sum_{i=1..N} indicator_i(choice)

Every symbol:

practice_score(choice)average prevalence of one deployment choice across teams
Nnumber of teams or respondents included in the measurement
indicator_i(choice)1 when team i uses that choice and 0 otherwise
A tiny worked example
Suppose 86 respondents are surveyed.
If 60 of them require human review for their main agent flow, the score for human review is 60 divided by 86.
That gives about 0.70.
The high score matters because it shows the choice is common across deployed systems.

The paper reports several percentages directly, and this average captures the basic measurement idea behind those summaries.

What can go wrong

What can go wrong

Self-reports can miss silent failures. A team may underreport problems or may not notice certain user harms, so the study can inherit blind spots from the practitioners it surveys.
Current practice is not the final destination. A common design choice today may reflect present tool limits rather than an ideal long-term design, so the study should guide research without freezing it.
Domain mix can skew the picture. If some industries are overrepresented, the aggregate view can lean toward their needs because those teams contribute more evidence to the summary.
Done right. The study is useful because it tells the field what builders are actually optimizing for once the agent has to survive routine use.
What a runnable demo should prove

What a runnable demo should prove

Build a small dashboard that compares two kinds of agent deployments: a short tightly controlled flow and a longer more autonomous flow. For each one, log step count, required human approvals, output error rate, and how often operators intervene.

The demo should make the tradeoff visible by showing that tighter control often reduces operational surprises because the system has more checkpoints where a person can stop or redirect it. Pair the dashboard with a short practitioner survey so that technical measurements and operator judgment can be viewed together.

control_gap = intervention_rate_long_flow - intervention_rate_short_flow ; review_share = reviewed_runs / total_runs

The point is to show how deployment teams choose designs that stay manageable under daily use.
