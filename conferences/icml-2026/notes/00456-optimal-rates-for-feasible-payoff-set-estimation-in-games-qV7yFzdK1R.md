## Abstract

We study a setting in which two players play a (possibly approximate) Nash equilibrium of a bimatrix game, while a learner observes only their actions and has no knowledge of the equilibrium or the underlying game. A natural question is whether the learner can rationalize the observed behavior by inferring the players' payoff functions. Rather than producing a single payoff estimate, inverse game theory aims to identify the entire set of payoffs consistent with observed behavior, enabling downstream use in, e.g., counterfactual analysis and mechanism design across applications like auctions, pricing, and security games. We focus on the problem of estimating the set of feasible payoffs with high probability and up to precision $\epsilon$ on the Hausdorff metric. We provide the first minimax-optimal rates for both exact and approximate equilibrium play, in zero-sum as well as general-sum games. Our results provide learning-theoretic foundations for set-valued payoff inference in multi-agent environments.

## One-Sentence Claim

Feasible payoff sets in bimatrix games can be estimated from observed equilibrium play with minimax-optimal Hausdorff rates, giving inverse game theory a sample-complexity foundation.

## Problem

In inverse game theory, a learner observes players' actions but not the underlying game, equilibrium, or payoff functions. A single inferred payoff matrix can be misleading because many payoff functions may rationalize the same observed behavior.

The goal is therefore set-valued inference: estimate the entire set of payoffs consistent with exact or approximate Nash equilibrium play. This matters for counterfactual analysis and mechanism design, where downstream decisions should account for identification uncertainty.

## Core Contribution

The paper provides the first minimax-optimal rates for estimating feasible payoff sets in bimatrix games, under both exact and approximate equilibrium play. It covers zero-sum and general-sum games and measures estimation error in Hausdorff distance.

The contribution is to put inverse game theory on learning-theoretic footing. It identifies how many observations are needed to recover the payoff-consistent set to precision epsilon with high probability.

## Method

The setting assumes two players repeatedly play a possibly approximate Nash equilibrium while the learner observes only actions. From the empirical action distribution, the learner estimates the set of payoff functions for which the observed behavior is equilibrium-consistent.

The analysis derives upper and lower sample-complexity rates for this set-estimation problem. Hausdorff distance provides a natural metric because the target is a set, not a point estimate.

## Experiments and Evidence

This is a theory paper. The evidence consists of minimax upper and lower bounds for exact and approximate equilibrium play across zero-sum and general-sum games.

Full-paper reading should verify the dependence of rates on action-space size, approximation level, confidence, and payoff normalization, as well as the construction used for lower bounds.

## Limits and Failure Modes

The setting assumes behavior is generated by Nash or approximate Nash play in a bimatrix game. Real agents may use bounded rationality, learning dynamics, correlated strategies, or nonstationary preferences that violate this rationalization model.

Set-valued inference is honest about ambiguity but can produce large feasible sets when observations are weak. Downstream mechanism design must propagate that uncertainty rather than collapse it prematurely.

## Deep Themes
