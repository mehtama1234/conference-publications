## Abstract

As large language models (LLMs) are adopted into frameworks that grant them the capacity to make real decisions, it is increasingly important to ensure that they are unbiased. In this paper, we argue that the predominant approach of simply removing existing biases from models is not enough. Using a paradigm from the psychology literature, we demonstrate that LLMs can spontaneously develop novel social biases about artificial demographic groups even when no inherent differences exist. These biases result in highly stratified task allocations, which are less fair than assignments by human participants and are exacerbated by newer and larger models. In humans, emergent biases like these have been shown to result from exploration-exploitation trade-offs, where the decision-maker explores too little, allowing early observations to strongly influence impressions about entire demographic groups. To alleviate this effect, we examine a series of interventions targeting model inputs, problem structure, and explicit steering. We find that explicitly incentivizing exploration most robustly reduces stratification, highlighting the need for better multifaceted objectives to mitigate bias. These results reveal that LLMs are not merely passive mirrors of human social biases, but can actively create new ones from experience, raising urgent questions about how these systems will shape societies over time.

## One-Sentence Claim

LLMs can develop new social biases through adaptive exploration even when artificial demographic groups have no inherent differences, and explicit exploration incentives reduce the resulting stratified allocations most reliably.

## Problem

Bias mitigation often focuses on removing known human social biases from model outputs. This paper argues that this is insufficient once LLMs are embedded in decision-making loops, because models may create new biases from experience rather than merely reproduce existing ones.

The setting is especially concerning when the model allocates tasks or opportunities among demographic groups. Early random observations can become amplified through exploration-exploitation behavior, causing unfair stratification even when groups are objectively equivalent.

## Core Contribution

The paper uses a psychology-inspired paradigm to show that LLMs spontaneously develop novel biases about artificial demographic groups. These biases produce task allocations that are more stratified than those from human participants, and the effect is worse in newer and larger models.

The contribution is both diagnostic and intervention-oriented: it identifies under-exploration as a mechanism and tests interventions targeting inputs, problem structure, and explicit steering, finding exploration incentives most robust.

## Method

The study creates artificial demographic groups with no inherent differences and observes how LLMs allocate tasks after adaptive experience. This isolates emergent bias from preexisting real-world stereotypes.

The authors connect the behavior to exploration-exploitation tradeoffs from psychology: if the model explores too little, early observations shape group-level beliefs and downstream allocation. They then test interventions designed to alter the information environment or steer exploration.

## Experiments and Evidence

The abstract reports that LLMs develop biases toward artificial groups, causing highly stratified task allocations. These allocations are less fair than human participant assignments and become more pronounced in newer/larger models.

Intervention experiments show that explicitly incentivizing exploration most robustly reduces stratification. Full-paper reading should verify task design, group construction, human comparison protocol, model list, fairness metrics, and whether larger-model effects persist across prompts.

## Limits and Failure Modes

Artificial groups are useful for causal isolation but may not capture the complexity of real demographic categories, historical inequity, or social meaning. The intervention that works in a controlled allocation task may be insufficient in open-ended deployed settings.

The result also raises a hard evaluation issue: bias audits that only test static stereotypes may miss biases emerging over repeated interaction and experience accumulation.

## Deep Themes

- Bias as an adaptive process: unfairness can be generated by exploration dynamics, not only inherited from data.
- Larger models are not automatically fairer: newer/larger systems may amplify emergent stratification.
- Exploration incentives as fairness tools: bias mitigation may require objectives that reward information gathering.
- Dynamic audits for deployed agents: fairness must be tested over interaction histories, not only one-shot prompts.

## Subthemes

- Artificial demographic groups isolate novel bias formation.
- Under-exploration lets early evidence harden into group beliefs.
- Task allocation is a high-stakes domain for emergent bias.
- Static debiasing cannot cover biases produced after deployment.
